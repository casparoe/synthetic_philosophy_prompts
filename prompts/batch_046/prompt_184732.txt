Okay so I've been stuck in this privacy philosophy reading group for about two years and we keep circling the same drain. One side thinks privacy violations are basically failures of control — you wrong someone when they can't decide what information flows where, like with that smart thermostat that learns your sleep patterns and sends them to the cloud without clear consent. The other side thinks that's too focused on the individual agent, and that privacy is more like maintaining a low-entropy informational enviroment around yourself — it's violated whenever the predictability of your behavior spikes for outsiders, even if you technically consented or never find out. They both agree the thermostat case is bad, they agree scraping employee social media is bad, but they can't agree on whether the harm is they took away my choice vs my informational environment got corrupted. My hunch is that this isn't about privacy at all but about whether moral wrongs need a subject who can feel greived, or whether they can be purely structural. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a hidden empirical disagreement about how people actually experience these harms? I want an honest diagnosis — is this verbal, is it about bedrock intuitions, is there a formal distinction we're missing — and what would actually move the needle? A conceptual split, a study, or is the question just ill-posed? Also please say how sure you are about all this and flag the point you're least confident in.
