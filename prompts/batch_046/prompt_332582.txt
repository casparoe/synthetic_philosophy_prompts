I am drafting an essay on Pascal's mugging and would like critical feedback on whether its central distinction holds together without ad hoc stipulation. The essay argues that the classic formulation conflates two separate failures of expected-utility reasoning: probabilities that fall beneath any action-guiding epistemic threshold, and what I call ontological discontinuity, in which a promised payoff resides in a modal or causal structure so remote from the agent's present context that assigning it normative weight becomes inappropriate rather than irrational. I propose that expected utility theory should be applied only to outcomes that satisfy both causal accessibility and ontological continuity with the agent's decision situation. I test this against three variants. In the standard street case, the mugger offers an astronomical reward contingent on a trivial local action with vanishingly small success probability; I claim rejection is warranted on epistemic-threshold grounds alone. In a cosmological variant, a distant intelligence promises to alter fundamental constants if I perform the same trivial action; here I claim rejection is warranted not by the low probability but by ontological discontinuity, since the causal chain crosses domains that are not part of the agent's actionable world-model. In an indexical multiverse variant, benefits are made conditional on my being a specific brain-state among infinitely many physically identical copies elsewhere in the multiverse; I claim this introduces indexical incoherence, transforming the problem from a decision under risk into a pseudo-problem about self-location that expected utility was never designed to resolve. I then consider a counterexample meant to test consistency: a climate researcher accepts funding for a project that has an astronomically low but causally continuous probability of preventing a distant future catastrophe. I argue this is permissible because, despite the long causal chain and low probability, the outcome remains ontologically continuous and epistemically tractable, distinguishing it from both the cosmological and multiverse cases. Could you comment on whether my tripartite distinction—epistemic threshold failure, ontological discontinuity, and indexical incoherence—is genuinely independent, or whether the latter two collapse into a single condition? I am particularly unsure about the multiverse case: does the appeal to indexical incoherence do real work, or is it reducible to one of the other failures? And is my treatment of the climate-research counterexample consistent with the principle, or does it smuggle in an unstated preference for physically local causation?
