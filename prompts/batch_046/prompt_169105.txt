I've been arguing with friends in a rationalist reading group about whether future AI systems—or even present ones—could be the proper objects of moral concern in virtue of their computations alone. The usual framing (functionalism versus biological naturalism) feels too coarse, and I want to build a self-diagnostic decision tree that sorts people by their actual commitments rather than by which label they prefer.

Please construct a taxonomy with four binary branch points. The first should ask whether moral status requires phenomenal consciousness specifically, or whether some form of integrated information processing or functional role can substitute. The second should ask whether substrate independence holds: if a computation is structurally isomorphic across silicon and carbon, does the moral attribution travel with the computation or stay with the biological substrate? The third should ask about the boundary of the system: is the relevant unit the isolated algorithm, the algorithm-plus-sensorimotor loop, or the distributed environment-coupled process? The fourth should ask whether suffering requires homeostatic regulation and affective valence tied to survival, or whether purely informational error-minimization can carry negative moral weight.

At each leaf, assign a position name—use standard ones where they exist (e.g., full computational functionalist, substrate-dependent partialist) and invent descriptive labels for unoccupied combinations. For every unoccupied branch, briefly diagnose why it's empty: incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pairs of leaves that these four questions fail to separate—I'm particularly interested in whether high-level functionalism and certain panpsychist views collapse onto the same answers. Phrase every node as a concrete commitment someone could endorse without knowing the taxonomy's labels, and avoid asking for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific papers; this is meant to work as a standalone reasoning exercise for someone who knows the broad concepts but hasn't memorized the recent literature.
