In a seminar on historical geography, a colleague dismissed my argument that a particular nineteenth-century land-reform ordinance permanently reshaped a city’s street grid and property boundaries. He said only, “The long term always swallows the event—geography and climate set the real boundaries,” implying that whatever policy had done, the slow structures of terrain and weather eventually reasserted the same spatial pattern. He seemed to be reaching for something like Braudel’s distinction between histoire événementielle and the longue durée, but the phrase was doing heavy argumentative work: it was meant to show that the reform was causally inert, not worth studying, and perhaps not even genuinely possible to begin with.

Could you lay out the precise theses that slogan might actually be expressing—causal determinism by geography and climate, statistical averaging-out of discrete interventions over centuries, epistemic invisibility of events at long temporal scales, normative priority of structures over human agency, or something else—indicating for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive claim defensible within Braudel’s framework, or plainly false? Then tell me which of these readings he actually needs to make his dismissal of the land reform stick, and whether that reading survives contact with the fact that the reform altered legal tenure and built form for generations before any “reassertion” occurred. I want a disambiguated menu I can take back to the seminar, not a single authorized interpretation.
