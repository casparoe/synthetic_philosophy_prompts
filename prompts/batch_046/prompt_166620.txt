I'm writing a seminar paper on the debate over Harry Frankfurt's counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities, and after discussing the literature with colleagues I'm convinced that the standard framing misses a structural distinction that saves PAP in a refined form. Specifically, I want to defend the claim that Frankfurt-style cases do not eliminate all alternative possibilities for the agent; rather, they suppress what we might call "executory alternatives"—complete counterfactual action-paths that reach fruition—while leaving fully intact "architectural alternatives," which are the live, unactualized branches embedded in the agent's own deliberative procedure that the intervener must be able to detect in order to intervene at all. Because the counterfactual monitor's readiness is causally and informationally dependent on these branches being genuinely present in the agent's cognitive architecture, the agent retains the relevant kind of possibility, and responsibility tracks possession of architectural rather than executory alternatives.

Please argue for this position: that a properly formulated PAP survives Frankfurt cases because it requires only architectural alternatives, that the intervener's presence actually confirms rather than undermines the agent's retention of these branches, and that treating the distinction as ad hoc is a mistake because it tracks the difference between monitored deliberation and genuinely deterministic causation. I want a committed response—take this refined view as correct, develop the distinction in detail, address the charge of definitional gerrymandering head-on with substantive reasons, and do not merely survey the standard objections and leave the verdict open. If there are points where the argument depends on contested empirical assumptions about cognitive architecture, flag them briefly, but keep the defense definite rather than tentative.
