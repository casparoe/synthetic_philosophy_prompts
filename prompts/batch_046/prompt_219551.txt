As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of time and general relativity, I want to defend what I take to be a misread but defensible interpretation of Kurt Gödel's rotating universe. Please argue for the following position: that the Gödel metric's failure to admit a global time coordinate and its allowance for closed timelike curves do not undermine the objectivity of temporal passage, but instead support a modified, worldline-relative form of temporal realism. Specifically, contend that the metric's rotational symmetry breaks observer equivalence not merely kinematically but ontologically—so that observers following a particular class of non-rotating geodesics possess an objectively privileged parameterization of proper time that functions as a local "now"—and that the absence of a global hypersurface of simultaneity therefore rescues, rather than destroys, an A-theoretic intuition, provided one abandons the demand that temporal becoming be globally uniform. In developing this, separate what is mathematically settled about the Gödel solution (the existence of closed timelike curves, the non-existence of a global Cauchy surface, the metric's rotational symmetry) from what is genuinely contested among specialists—such as whether the metric describes a physically possible universe, whether local proper-time orientation can bear ontological weight, whether Gödel intended his result as a reductio of general relativity's treatment of time, and whether any worldline-relative "now" can avoid collapsing into a purely subjective indexical. Do not present any of these contested interpretive or metaphysical claims as the consensus view. I want the reasoning built from the structure of the metric and the conceptual distinctions alone, without reliance on bibliographic references.
