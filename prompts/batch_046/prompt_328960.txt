I have recently begun experiencing prolonged lucid intervals during REM sleep that feel phenomenologically continuous with waking consciousness, and this has left me genuinely uncertain whether my ordinary, non-lucid dreams are best understood as experiences, as reconstructed narratives, or as something in between. For a small philosophy-of-mind graduate seminar, please construct a diagnostic taxonomy — structured as a decision tree with exactly seven branching nodes and eight terminal leaves — that sorts positions on whether dreams are experiences, without requiring students to recognize authors' names in advance. The root node should pose the question of whether retrospective first-person report upon waking can ever be authoritative about mental states occurring during sleep. From there, branch on whether robust sensory-motor cortical activation during REM is sufficient for phenomenal consciousness in the absence of working-memory access; then on whether the absence of a continuous, unified ego-perspective during a dream undermines the claim that there was a subject of experience; and finally on whether functional explanations of dreaming — memory consolidation, emotional regulation — are compatible with treating dream content as genuinely experienced rather than merely processed information. At each leaf, provide either a recognized label (for instance, a Malcolm-associated denial, a Dennett-style narrative-construction view, or an unnamed hybrid) or mark it unnamed, followed by one sentence describing its most awkward commitment — such as having to deny that nightmares cause real suffering, or having to treat lucid and non-lucid dreams as ontologically distinct despite phenomenological overlap. Identify any combinations of answers that are logically available but currently unoccupied in the literature, and for each gap indicate whether it is likely incoherent, unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves that share identical answers but diverge only on hidden premises that the tree does not capture. The audience consists of philosophy graduate students with no specialized background in consciousness studies, and the instrument should be answerable through reasoning about concrete cases rather than by naming theories.
