I serve on the advisory review board for a university strategic-forecasting office, where every month we evaluate long-range institutional reports—demographic, economic, climate, technological—that often embed implicit claims about historical inevitability. As a philosopher of science, I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Popper’s critique of historicism, that my board can run in roughly thirty minutes on any forecast claiming a “law of history.” The board includes economists and climate scientists who are not philosophers, and our verdict is advisory but must be signed by the chair, so the procedure must be usable without specialist philosophical training.

Design the procedure as an ordered set of diagnostic questions or tests, specifying what counts as passing or failing each step, where the procedure halts with a definitive rejection, and where it must hand the decision back to human judgment because the issue is one of values or interpretation rather than logic. Translate Popper’s core distinctions—between trends and unconditional laws, between open futures and closed predictions, and between methodological individualism and holistic determinism—into practical checks a non-philosopher can apply to a report’s language and structure. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure, and state clearly what kinds of forecasting errors the procedure is not designed to catch.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: a forecast asserting that global fertility decline is an irreversible historical law guaranteeing economic stagnation by 2050; a scenario derived from historical-materialist premises predicting inevitable institutional crisis from present wealth concentration; and a climate-adaptation plan that frames sea-level rise as a deterministic historical trajectory rather than a conditional physical projection. Include one case where the procedure returns a verdict that defenders of the forecast will find unwelcome. Conclude by stating the procedure’s hard limits—what it cannot settle, and what decisions remain outside its scope. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
