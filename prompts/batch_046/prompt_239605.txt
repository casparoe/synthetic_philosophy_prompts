I facilitate an upper-level philosophy seminar of 14 students, with 75 minutes available. Design a structured in-class game that makes the group experience Buridan's problem not as a fable about a donkey, but as a live collective deadlock: every consideration that favors option A is structurally mirrored by an identical consideration favoring option B, and the group must reach a unanimous decision within a time limit or face a small, real collective penalty (for example, both options are implemented in sequence, doubling their workload). Create specific roles—such as a Reason-Register who may only state paired, symmetric arguments; a Will-Proxy who must cast the deciding vote but is forbidden from citing any reason not already on the register; and an Asymmetry-Hunter who secretly receives one hidden, genuinely distinguishing feature that they may or may not reveal. Provide the rules, the payoff and stake structure, a 60-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps the procedural crisis onto the standard philosophical distinctions: rational choice versus arbitrary will, first-order versus second-order preference, and the metaphysics of volition independent of intellect. Specify what will predictably fail the first time—likely that hidden social status or rhetorical framing breaks the symmetry before the formal rules do—and how the facilitator should intervene without destroying the experience. Be frank about which feature of the metaphysical view (the causal independence of will from reason) the role structure can only simulate, not demonstrate, and must therefore be supplied in the closing discussion. No lecture up front; participants should not hear the phrase Buridan's ass until the debrief.
