In my interdisciplinary environmental ethics seminar, a recurring argument about habitat destruction has stalled in a way that feels genuinely unproductive, and I am trying to understand its underlying structure rather than win it. One faction argues that reducing wild populations through habitat loss is, from a suffering-focused perspective, morally permissible or even beneficial: because wild ecosystems generate enormous quantities of predation, starvation, parasitism, and exposure-related death, preventing future generations from coming into existence avoids a net surplus of suffering. The opposing faction accepts these empirical premises—acknowledging high wild-animal suffering and recognizing that destroyed habitats lower future population densities—but insists that habitat destruction remains profoundly wrong. Their objections combine direct harm to currently existing sentient individuals with the claim that intact ecosystems possess a structural or relational value that cannot be collapsed into an aggregation of individual welfare states, regardless of how negative those states might be. Over several months we have refined the biological details—replacement dynamics, density-dependent mortality curves, and cross-species welfare estimates—without anyone changing position.

Given this stalemate, I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is our disagreement substantive but obscured by shared vocabulary, verbal—turning on incompatible uses of 'value,' 'harm,' or 'intervention'—or are we answering different questions entirely, with one side framing the issue within population ethics and the other within ecological preservation? Could there be a hidden empirical disagreement about the quality of life in degraded versus intact habitats that we have failed to articulate, or is the deadlock instead rooted in bedrock intuitions about the moral significance of 'naturalness' versus suffering-reduction that neither side can justify to the other? I am particularly interested in whether injecting a conceptual distinction—perhaps between the moral status of preventing existence versus ending it, or between ecosystem integrity and summed welfare—would actually move the debate, or whether the disagreement is of a kind that no new distinction or empirical finding can resolve. What should I treat as a genuine weakness in my own position that is likely to come up, and what should I concede is probably unresolvable?
