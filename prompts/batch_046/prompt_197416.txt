I have encountered a summary of Michael Walzer's well-known discussion of dirty hands in democratic politics, and I should like your help in assessing its fidelity. The account runs as follows. Walzer holds that officeholders are entitled to breach ordinary moral constraints—deceiving citizens, sanctioning coercion, or suppressing dissent—provided the anticipated public benefit outweighs the private moral injury. On this reading, Walzer offers a consequentialist license framed by institutional role: the politician performs a cost-benefit analysis, executes the decision, and is thereby discharged from further moral obligation, since democratic authorization absorbs the wrong. What remains is merely subjective remorse, which Walzer allegedly treats as a psychological frailty rather than as an enduring moral stain. Accordingly, the summary concludes that Walzer denies any need for public penance, symbolic punishment, or institutional accountability; the democratic mandate itself operates as a kind of moral cleansing, leaving the actor with clean hands in the only sense that matters. I am inclined to think this collapses Walzer into an act-utilitarian and obscures his insistence on the persistence of moral conflict, but I am not certain where the distortion ends and the interpretation begins. Would you audit this characterization? Specifically, please distinguish those elements that fairly represent Walzer's account of role morality and the irreducibility of moral cost from those that import consequentialist reasoning he explicitly rejects; clarify whether Walzer indeed regards guilt as non-moral residue; and assess whether the notion of democratic moral cleansing has any textual basis, or whether it is a fabrication that vitiates the summary's conclusion about accountability.
