I was hashing this out with my roommate after our philosophy of ethics seminar and I think I have a good exam idea, but I want it sharpened. We're looking at Stoicism's modern revival — all the popular books and apps about the dichotomy of control, where you sort your worries into what you can and can't change. Most students can parrot Epictetus and maybe mention CBT, but I want them to see a real crack in the revival: modern Stoic practise often tells you to 'manage your thoughts' and suppress reactive feelings before they fully form, which sounds like you're controlling the initial impression itself, not just your assent to it. In the classical framework, impressions hit you unbidded — you only control whether you assent. So imagine a specific case: a modern practitioner uses a mindfullness protocol to stop a flash of anger from even arising, before there's anything to assent to or reject. Is this still Stoic, or has it quietly replaced the dichotomy with a kind of cognitive-behavioral monism where every mental event is up for management?
