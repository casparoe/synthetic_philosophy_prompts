I have been working through an older philosophical text on my own—something composed in the late eighteenth century by a German moral philosopher who holds that only rational, self-legislating beings possess direct moral standing. In the portion I am struggling with, the author discusses our obligations regarding animals. He maintains, in substance, that we have no direct duties to animals themselves, since they lack reason, autonomy, and the capacity to recognize moral law; rather, any obligations we have toward them are indirect, arising solely from our duties to humanity. He warns that cruelty to animals hardens our hearts and weakens the sympathy we ought to extend to other people, comparing animals in some respects to instruments or tools that we may use for human ends, yet he also insists that unnecessary suffering should be avoided—not because the animal is wronged in its own right, but because such cruelty corrupts the moral character of the agent.

What I cannot grasp is whether this reasoning is genuinely consistent, or whether it quietly relies on exactly the kind of moral recognition it officially denies. If the danger of cruelty lies in its effect on our sympathy, why does cruelty to an animal diminish our moral faculties in a way that damaging an inanimate object does not? It seems to presuppose that animals occupy some intermediate position: not full persons with rational dignity, but also not mere things, since they are capable of suffering in a manner that can train or deform our moral sensibility. So my question is this—can one coherently defend the view that animals have no direct moral rights and yet are protected only through indirect duties to humanity, without implicitly granting them a derivative or proto-moral status? Or does the argument necessarily collapse into a hidden form of direct consideration once we ask why animal suffering specifically, rather than damage to any non-rational object, is morally relevant to the cultivation of human virtue? I would appreciate help understanding whether the passage allows for a stable middle ground, or whether the tension I see is unavoidable.
