I serve on the university's Committee on Civic Education and Public Memory, and we must reach a decision by next Friday on a proposal for a mandatory first-year seminar titled Parallel Failures: Moral Education Through Unvarnished Lives. The proposal, submitted jointly by History and Philosophy, contends that standard biography courses encourage what the authors call hagiographic extraction—students isolate admirable traits from narrative context and construct impossible moral ideals. To counter this, the course adapts Plutarch's parallel structure but inverts its purpose: each unit pairs a traditionally exemplary figure with a contemporaneous peer of comparable achievement whose documented moral failures were not peripheral but structurally entangled with those achievements. Rather than asking students to judge or rank the pair, the instructors require them to construct what they term a moral topology—a continuous, non-chronological spatial mapping of how specific virtues and vices occupied the same institutional and emotional terrain—using only primary-source fragments that have been deliberately left unannotated and without synthetic narrative framing. The pedagogical goal is not to provide role models but to cultivate discomfort tolerance: the capacity to hold moral admiration and moral disappointment in sustained, unresolved tension. A minority faction led by the Dean of Undergraduate Studies objects that asking eighteen-year-olds to generate unsupervised interpretive frameworks about real people's moral failures, without scaffolding, closure, or explicit ethical framing, violates our duty of care and risks producing either cynical detachment or uncritical identification with vice. A second faction, drawn from the Philosophy Department's moral epistemology working group, argues that stripping biography of synthetic narrative and refusing evaluative closure abandons the very conditions that make moral learning from lives possible, reducing the exercise to an aesthetic ambiguity that teaches nothing. Draft the committee's formal written opinion recommending approval, conditional approval with specific revisions, or rejection, including a concrete implementation timeline or revision deadline if conditions are attached. The opinion must treat the strongest objection—whether the duty-of-care concern or the epistemological ambiguity concern—as a serious consideration that genuinely shapes the reasoning rather than being dismissed. Please present the entire opinion in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other structural formatting, as one might write in a formal letter or email.
