In arguments about capital punishment I keep running into a specific asymmetry I can't name. A speaker insists that because execution is irreversible, any nonzero risk of executing an innocent person is categorically unacceptable—no amount of deterrence or retributive justice can outweigh it. The same speaker, however, treats wrongful life imprisonment as a serious but ordinary cost that can be weighed against benefits: years of lost liberty are also irreversible, and exoneration data show wrongful imprisonment happens too, yet this never triggers the same conversation-stopping precaution. I've seen this in debates over DNA exonerations, in discussions of procedural safeguards, and in direct responses to the challenge "if you're willing to risk one innocent execution, why not one innocent life sentence?" The move isn't just severity-sensitive or act-omission bias in general; it seems to switch a deontological side-constraint on specifically for state-inflicted death while leaving structurally identical errors in the alternative policy inside consequentialist calculus. Is there an established concept for this selective activation of irreversibility-based precaution? How does it differ from omission bias, status quo bias, or the asymmetry between killing and letting die? If it hasn't been named, can you define the phenomenon precisely—specifying whether the key factor is the finality of death, the state's direct agency, or the rhetorical function of the argument—together with boundary cases where the pattern does and doesn't apply? Please don't just invoke broad biases; I want the carving of this specific pattern, including what it is not. End with a one-sentence summary as the last line beginning "Verdict:".
