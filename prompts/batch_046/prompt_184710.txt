I hold that in gradual mind uploading—where biological neurons are replaced one by one with functionally equivalent silicon substrates—personal identity is preserved not by substrate continuity, nor by brute psychological continuity, but by what I shall call the preservation of diachronic predictive integration: the maintenance, across the replacement interval, of a hierarchical generative model whose counterfactual self-representations remain continuously coupled to first-order experience, even as the physical realiser changes. On this view, identity is not an all-or-nothing relation but a gradient property that degrades only when the system's capacity for integrated error-correction across temporal slices falls below a threshold permitting coherent autobiographical narration. I want to know what would genuinely count against this. Not merely the standard intuition pumps—the Ship of Theseus, fission cases, or the claim that silicon cannot feel—but the specific defeaters: which empirical findings about neural plasticity or about discontinuities introduced by digital emulation would undermine the claim that predictive integration can survive substrate substitution? Which conceptual results—concerning, say, the individuation of dynamical systems, or the conditions for genuine counterfactual reasoning in artificial architectures—would show that the proposed gradient is either illusory or collapses into either pure psychological continuity theory or pure substrate-dependent essentialism? I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by qualifications—if every possible failure of preservation can be redescribed as merely falling below the threshold—and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a symptom of unfalsifiability. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate which ones a defender of diachronic predictive integration ought to concede as genuinely damaging rather than dismissible.
