I've been trying to wrap my head around Nāgārjuna's tetralema for a philosphy course, and I keep hitting the same wall. I get the basic move: apply the four corners — it exists, it doesn't exist, both, neither — to anything with svabhāva and show that inherent existence collapses under its own weight. But then there's this second-level problem about emptiness itself. If śūnyatā just means everything lacks inherent existence, then emptiness is also lacking inherent existence, right? So the statement that all phenomena are empty is itself empty. Some texts call this the emptiness of emptiness, and it's supposed to stop Madhyamaka from becoming a kind of essentialism about absence. But conceptually I'm not sure how this avoids being self-defeating. If I reject all four positions regarding svabhāva, and then I turn around and say that the correct view is emptiness, haven't I just parked my view in that fourth corner — neither existence nor non-existence — while pretending I rejected it?

Can you explain the actual mechanics of how śūnyatā-śūnyatā is supposed to work here? Specifically, is the distinction between conventional truth, samvṛti, and ultimate truth, paramārtha, doing all the work — like, emptiness is conventionally affirmed but ultimately empty? Or is there a more structural point about how the tetralema applies to its own conclusions without generating a hiden fifth position? I want to understand whether Madhyamaka is genuinely view-less, or whether emptiness functions as a stealth metaphysical claim that just happens to deny itself in a clever loop.
