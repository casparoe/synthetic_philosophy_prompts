I was recently appointed to the ethics review board of a regional hospital, and in our case conferences I have noticed a recurring structure in the hardest decisions that I do not have a name for. A clinician or family member is torn between two obligations—say, honoring a patient’s explicit refusal of treatment and preventing foreseeable suffering to a dependent child—where the obligations seem genuinely incommensurable, with no clear lexical priority or weighing procedure available. Before the decision, both demands feel equally mandatory; yet the moment one is acted upon, the deliberator almost invariably reconstructs the situation so that the fulfilled obligation now looks strictly required, while the unfulfilled one is recast as regrettable but not obligatory—often by appealing to some subtle feature, such as the temporal order in which the obligations were acknowledged, the institutional role that was “active” at the time, or a distinction between causing and allowing, that seemed entirely neutral before the choice was made.

This is not quite rationalization in the usual sense, because the agent genuinely experienced symmetric moral pressure beforehand, and it is not the discovery of a pre-existing hierarchy, because the breaking feature feels contingent rather than principled—if the situation had unfolded in a slightly different order, the opposite reconstruction might have occurred. It also differs from the standard discussion of moral residue or tragic choice, where the agent acknowledges that both obligations remain binding and bears guilt for the unfulfilled one; here, the residue disappears through retrospective restructuring rather than being endured. I am trying to determine whether this pattern—where an apparent moral dilemma dissolves not through prior reasoning but through a post-decisional manufacture of asymmetry—has an established concept in moral philosophy or moral psychology. If there is a term for it, how precisely does it differ from ordinary rationalization, from accounts that deny genuine dilemmas by appealing to hidden lexical ordering, and from cases of practical indeterminacy? And if it has no established name, how should it be defined so as to distinguish it both from true tragic choice and from situations where a hidden principle was present all along?
