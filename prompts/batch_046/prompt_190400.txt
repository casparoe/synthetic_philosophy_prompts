My profesor just passed and I tried writing a piece about what he taught me, but I smoothed over all the ugly parts — the unfair grading, the grudges he held, the time he ignored a student in crisis. I want people to learn from his life, but now I'm worried I turned him into a cardboard saint. A friend walked me into this and I cant find the exit.

(1) Moral learning from a life only works if there's a contingency gap you can actually bridge — you need to be able to imagine yourself facing the same choice with the same uncertainty.

(2) Hagiography closes that gap by presenting virtue as inevitable, the natural expression of a sanctified character.

(3) So hagiography doesn't teach morality; it only produces admiration.

(4) But any biography that actually teaches has to do some hagiographic compression — selecting moments, hiding contradictions, framing failure as redemption — otherwise it's just raw chronicle with no pattern.

(5) Plutarch's Parallel Lives do exactly this: he pairs Greeks and Romans to highlight single virtues, suppresses context that complicates the moral, and makes character look like destiny.

(6) Therefore either Plutarch's Lives don't teach morality, or all biography is useless for it, or (1) is false.

(7) Yet people have clearly learned from Plutarch for centuries, and if I'd included my profesor's flaws, they might have made his good choices more imitable, not less.

He says the slip is between (4) and (5) — that narrative framing isn't the same as closing the gap. I want to keep (1) because it explains why smoothing things out felt like cheating, but then I'm stuck with the conclusion that biography is useless. Which step do you actually reject, and what does that rejection cost? Don't hedge — take a side and defend it.
