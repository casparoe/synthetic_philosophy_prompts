In my school district and across the media I constantly see institutions treating “Gen Z,” “Millennials,” and “Gen Alpha” as if they are stable kinds of people with shared psychological essences—designing wellness curricula, hiring protocols, and political strategies around these labels. At the same time, demographers often insist that observed differences are mostly age effects or period effects rather than true cohort effects, and sociologists treat the labels as marketing constructs. I am trying to understand what philosophical commitments this widespread institutional practice actually presupposes.

Specifically, when a district creates a “Gen Z mental-health initiative” that assumes this birth-cohort carries a distinctive, enduring vulnerability profile, what ontology of cohorts does that presuppose? Must it be committed to generations as real kinds—perhaps as historical kinds analogous to biological species, with diachronic identity conditions that persist as the members age—or can the practice be explained by treating them as merely pragmatic, indexical groupings? Here is the puzzle that confuses me: the same institution sorts all students into grade levels by chronological age, which treats maturity as a continuous, universal life-phase (like being an adolescent), yet the generational framework treats narrow birth-year windows as carrying essential, cohort-specific traits that remain fixed even as the cohort grows older. Is the practice implicitly committed to a kind of diachronic essentialism—where belonging to Gen Z is like belonging to a natural kind—while simultaneously relying on age-based sorting that assumes human development is uniform and non-cohort-dependent? Or can a constructivist or nominalist view about generations accommodate both practices without contradiction?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of cohort-ontology that makes these two institutional patterns consistent, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with, and say whether replacing that ontology—say, with a pure life-stage model that rejects cohort-specific essences—would force a change only in rhetoric or also in the substance of the policies themselves. I am especially interested in whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival ontologies could license the same programs.
