I am preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher—let me call her Dr. Elena Voss, an unnamed composite figure—who defends what she terms pragmatic framework relativism about ontological disputes: the view that debates over whether composite objects, moral properties, or social institutions exist are not substantive disagreements about the world but rather mismatches in the conversational goals that govern quantifier use, and that recognizing this dissolves the need for metaontological arbitration altogether. The topic has become personally relevant to me in recent months: after a prolonged, emotionally charged disagreement with a close family member about whether our deceased relative’s presence in family rituals is literally real or merely symbolic, I realized that we were not disputing facts but negotiating what kind of acknowledgment the conversation required, and I want to know whether philosophy’s tools for diagnosing verbal disputes can illuminate lived disagreements of this kind without reducing them to mere confusion. I need eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate the view. Specifically, I want at least one question on the shared-target problem—how she handles cases where interlocutors sincerely believe they are talking about the same thing—followed by a reply keyed to her likely appeal to implicit pragmatic framing; one question on how her taxonomy distinguishes a genuine verbal dispute from a substantive disagreement that merely happens to be intractable, with a follow-up pressing the risk of over-application; one question on what her position says about higher-order disputes, particularly whether metaontology itself is verbal, and a follow-up anticipating her answer that metaontology is just another framework with its own pragmatic aims; one question on how she responds to critics who claim that dissolving existence questions abandons the normative force of scientific realism, with a follow-up on whether she owes an account of why science succeeds if not because it tracks ontology; and one final question asking what kind of evidence or argument could convince her that at least some ontological disputes are genuinely substantive rather than framework-relative. Each question should be phrased to press where the actual literature on metaontology and verbal disputes presses, anticipate the standard reply, and include the follow-up to it.
