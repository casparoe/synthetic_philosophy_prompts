I'm writing a novel about an art restorer who starts wondering if her work is destroying value rather than saving it, and I've got stuck in this philosophical knot. Here is the argument my character keeps turning over:

(1) If a perfect forgery is visually identical to an original, then any extra value the original has must come from something non-visual—provenance, history, the artist's touch.
(2) But that sounds like we're treating paintings as relics rather than as art, which feels wrong.
(3) So artistic value proper should be the same for both.
(4) Yet we preserve damaged originals with only fragments of original paint, and call them the real thing, while a perfect reconstruction using AI to guess missing parts—closer to the artist's intent—is dismissed as fake.
(5) That means we care about being the actual object the artist worked on, not about matching the artist's vision.
(6) But if "being the actual object" matters, then heavy restoration that replaces 90% of the canvas should destroy that status, and yet it doesnt.
(7) So the criterion cant be original material.
(8) If it's not material and not appearance, it must be some kind of historical continuity—like Theseus's ship.
(9) But if continuity is enough, then a forger who slowly swaps in copies over centuries could eventually claim the original's status, which is clearly absurd.
(10) So either artistic value is just irrational relic-worship, or I'm missing a hidden distinction. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I'd rather hang onto (5) if possible. Could you answer in British English, please?
