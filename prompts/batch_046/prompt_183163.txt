Consider the institutional and medico-legal framework governing personal identity across severe neurological disruption. In current practice, a patient who suffers total retrograde amnesia together with a radical transformation of personality, values, and memory is still treated as the identical rights-bearing subject: prior contracts, marital obligations, property holdings, and criminal liability all persist unchanged, provided the living biological organism continues. By contrast, when irreversible cessation of whole-brain function occurs—so that no psychological life remains—the same legal system declares the person dead, terminates all rights and duties, and permits organ procurement, even though mechanical support preserves bodily life and circulation. Now imagine an emerging therapeutic protocol in which, over months, induced pluripotent stem cells gradually replace every neuron in a patient’s cortex and subcortical structures in order to treat progressive neurodegeneration; the body never dies, consciousness is maintained continuously through the transition, yet by the end no original neural tissue remains.

What theory of survival—of what matters in the persistence of a person—does this triad of practices presuppose? Does it commit us to a Parfit-style psychological-continuity view, according to which survival tracks relations of memory and intention rather than bodily or neural substance? Or does it presuppose a bodily-continuity criterion, explaining why amnesia changes nothing but brain death ends everything? Or is there a hidden hybrid—perhaps that practical administrability, not metaphysics, drives the distinctions, so that the practices presuppose no consistent philosophical view at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the institutional pattern strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined—i.e., which rival theories of personal identity could license exactly the same legal and medical rules without contradiction. Then tell me what a policy or doctrine built on the view that survival consists in the continuity of conscious experience, rather than in brain or body, would have to change about death certification, liability, and therapeutic replacement protocols. Finally, say what would change your mind: which argument or institutional detail would lead you to reject your reconstructed theory, and where in your reasoning the risk of error is greatest—whether in reading practical convenience as metaphysical commitment, or in assuming coherence where the law is genuinely fragmented.
