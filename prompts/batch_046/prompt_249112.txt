I'm drafting a column on why the 35-hour week feels philosophically shaky—maybe you've seen the debates about karoshi and Weber's Protestant ethic floating around lately. I got into a back-and-forth with a friend who covers labor law and she layed out this argument that I can't find the exit from. She thinks any defense of leisure as a real right proves too much and ends up undermining the labor movements that won the 35-hour week in the first place. Here's her reasoning, numbered:

(1) A right to leisure is only defensible if leisure has intrinsic moral value, not just instrumental value as rest.
(2) Weber's Protestant work ethic denies intrinsic value to leisure: unstructured time is sloth unless it's recuperative for more labor.
(3) The modern 35-hour week was won historically by labor movements that appealed to worker dignity and health—preventing karoshi-style death by overwork—not to leisure as a sphere of human flourishing.
(4) So in practice, the 35-hour week is an instrumental harm-prevention rule, not a recognition of leisure's intrinsic worth.
(5) Now consider "ma," the Japanese concept of negative space or structured pause that makes action meaningful. Apply it to labor ethics: moral agency requires "ma" between work periods, not just physical rest but a kind of structural emptiness.
(6) If "ma" is necessary for moral agency, then a society that eliminates it—through karoshi culture or always-on digital labor—is not just harming people but actively undermining their capacity for moral choice.
(7) Therefore, the right to leisure is actually the right to "ma," and it has intrinsic value because without it you're not fully an agent.
(8) But if leisure/"ma" has intrinsic value, then the Protestant ethic is false: work is not the primary sphere of moral formation, structured absence is.
(9) Yet the labor movements that secured the 35-hour week depended on the idea that workers deserve dignity through control over their labor, not through escape from it—implicitly accepting the ethic's framing of work as central.
(10) So defending leisure rights requires rejecting the Protestant ethic, but rejecting it undermines the conceptual framework that made labor rights like the 35-hour week politically coherent in the first place.
(11) Hence any robust defense of leisure proves too much: it either collapses back into harm-prevention (and can't condemn voluntary overwork like a surgeon's long shift or a monk's vigil) or it destroys the moral basis of labor protection itself.
