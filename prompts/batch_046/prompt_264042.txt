As a doctoral student in philosophy of history, I am trying to formalize the adequacy conditions for any theory that addresses whether historians can escape the normative and conceptual standards of their own epoch—what the literature frames as the problem of presentism, though I want to move past that label’s usual baggage. Rather than a survey of named positions, I need the desiderata themselves: the full specification of what an adequate account must deliver to count as successful.

Please provide a requirements list for such a theory. For each requirement, specify the phenomenon it must explain (e.g., the attribution of anachronistic moral categories to past agents; the recovery of conceptual schemes that lack modern equivalents; the institutional and disciplinary pressures that embed historians in contemporary norms), the distinctions it must preserve (such as between describing a past belief system and endorsing it, or between epistemic presentism—using present categories for understanding—and axiological presentism—using present values for evaluation), and the constraints from adjacent fields (hermeneutics, sociology of knowledge, cognitive science of categorization) with which it must remain consistent. Include the motivating case or consideration behind each, and indicate which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable versus which reflect the prejudices of one camp—say, the camp treating all historical judgment as irredeemably parochial versus the camp assuming a view-from-nowhere is available.

Next, address whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. In particular, examine whether the demand for complete normative neutrality conflicts with the hermeneutic claim that understanding requires some shared evaluative horizon, and whether that tension is fatal or productive for the project.

Then identify which single requirement does the most damage to leading candidates, without exhaustive scoring. I am especially interested in whether the theory must accommodate cross-tradition historiography—for instance, a historian formed within Confucian moral frameworks interpreting European Enlightenment practices without reducing them to Confucian analogues—and whether this eliminates accounts that treat presentism as merely a local, Western disciplinary habit.

Finally, include a judgment on how the theory should handle what I will call recursive presentism: the fact that a historian’s awareness of her own present-bound position is itself articulated through the conceptual resources available in her present. Is this reflexive loop a fatal circularity, or can it be incorporated as a manageable feature? Keep the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and their interrelations rather than on cataloguing specific theorists; I want the standard of evaluation itself, not a scorecard.
