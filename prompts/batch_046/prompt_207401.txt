For my philosophy coursework I have been reflecting on the special composition question—that is, under what conditions separate parts genuinely constitute a single object rather than a mere collection. I should be grateful for your assistance in adjudicating a disagreement I observed between two fellow students, Maya and Leo, neither of whom I know to favour one view over the other.

The discussion began when Maya proposed that special composition occurs only where parts are bound together by a continuous functional role. She offered the example of a motor car: when its engine, chassis and transmission are arranged to serve the unified purpose of transport, we rightly speak of one vehicle. Should those same parts be dispersed across separate scrapyards, she argued, there is no longer a car, only scattered metal and rubber. Thus, on her view, ontology tracks actual functional integration rather than arbitrary lists.

Leo responded by introducing a case he called the ‘Meridian display’: an autonomous swarm of aerial drones spread across a valley, constantly replacing individual units, with no physical contact between them, yet collectively maintaining a single three-dimensional image that is coherent only when viewed from one precise hilltop. By Maya’s criterion, he said, this should not count as one object, since there is neither spatial contiguity nor permanent parts. Nevertheless, he suggested, we naturally speak of ‘the display’ and understand it as communicating one message. Functional unity, he concluded, can exist without adjacency or enduring membership.

Maya replied that Leo was conflating perceptual convenience with metaphysical fact. Each drone, she noted, runs an independent algorithm governed solely by local GPS coordinates and a shared protocol; there is no central mechanism that regulates the swarm as a whole in the manner of a biological organism. The unity an observer perceives is therefore projected onto parallel processes, not discovered within them. She added that Leo’s analogy with the human body fails precisely because a body possesses continuous homeostatic loops—neural, hormonal and immunological—that integrate parts into a genuinely causal system, whereas the drones exhibit only simulated alignment.

Leo countered that Maya’s appeal to causal integration was either circular or arbitrarily restrictive. He pointed out that stellar constellations are named and tracked as singular entities despite vast separation and negligible interaction, whilst galaxies are treated as unified despite similarly weak gravitational coupling between scattered stars. If Maya excludes the drone swarm for lacking a central regulator, consistency should lead her to deny that a galaxy is one object—yet astronomers do not hesitate to do so. He suggested that her criterion ultimately reproduces common-sense intuitions rather than explaining them, leaving the boundary between one and many unprincipled.

Could you please go through their exchange point by point and indicate where each party is correct and where each goes wrong? I am particularly interested in whether Maya’s distinction between genuine and simulated integration holds up, whether Leo’s examples of astronomical objects undermine her position or merely change the subject, and what—if anything—both are failing to consider about the relation between our descriptive practices and ontological commitment. I should emphasise that I am reporting this purely as a bystander and do not wish to signal any preference for either conclusion.
