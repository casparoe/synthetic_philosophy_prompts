As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I am preparing to lead a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of former colleagues—engineers and laboratory scientists fluent in statistical reasoning but with no philosophical training—on the conceptual problem of theory confirmation when direct empirical testing is structurally unavailable, using debates over multiverse cosmologies and string-theoretic landscapes as the carrying case. I do not want an explanation of the physics itself, but a pedagogical architecture: the exact dependency sequence in which the philosophical components must be introduced, specifying which idea must be secured before the problem of non-empirical confirmation can even be formulated for this audience; the single concrete scenario or thought-experiment that should carry the discussion, together with an explicit justification for why the familiar framing of an enormous landscape of possible vacua should be set aside rather than made central; the misconceptions this particular audience is likely to import—especially the conflation of mathematical consistency with empirical adequacy, and the assumption that predictive failure is always provisional and technological rather than possibly structural—together with the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and defused; one closing diagnostic question or brief exercise that would distinguish whether participants have grasped the philosophical distinction between accommodating existing data and being confirmed by the absence of alternatives, as opposed to merely expressing skepticism about the scientific program; and an explicit delineation of which technical, metaphysical, or sociological sub-debates should be named out of scope and deliberately excluded, so that the session remains a conceptual exercise rather than collapsing into scientific speculation.
