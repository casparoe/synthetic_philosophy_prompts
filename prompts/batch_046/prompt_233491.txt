I've been trying to clarify my own view on eliminative materialism—specifically, which mental-state attributions survive if we take the eliminativist challenge seriously—by testing my intuitions against concrete cases, and the pattern is messier than I expected. In my philosophy of mind seminar we discussed whether propositional attitudes are just useful fictions, and I found myself with the following verdicts. When a contemporary large language model generates the sentence that it believes the capital of France is Paris, I see no genuine belief: there is only statistical prediction over token distributions, with no integrated representational architecture behind it. When a dog salivates upon hearing a can opener, I am inclined to deny that it believes food is coming; the response is too tightly coupled to the stimulus and lacks the kind of decoupled, revisable representation that belief requires. By contrast, when a patient with Capgras delusion insists that her husband has been replaced by an identical impostor—despite overwhelming counterevidence—I do think she genuinely holds that belief: it is systematically integrated into her reasoning, guides her emotional responses, and resists revision in ways that seem genuinely representational, even if the underlying mechanism is pathological. Yet when a blindsight patient navigates around obstacles while sincerely denying any visual awareness, I do not think she believes there is an obstacle in her path; she possesses the information in some subpersonal form, but it is not available to her as a belief that could enter into deliberation.

The case that breaks my tentative principle is a chess engine that evaluates that the knight is pinned, integrating this assessment across long-term planning and counterfactual reasoning; it lacks consciousness and emotional life, yet its representational state seems more belief-like than the dog's and perhaps comparable to the Capgras patient's. So my question is whether there is a coherent principle—perhaps centered on functional integration across a rational economy, or on availability to a broader system of inference and action—that delivers all of these verdicts, or whether I must drop one. What would such a principle predict for the case I keep returning to: a distributed collective intelligence, composed of many local nodes that each process information without awareness, but whose collective behavior exhibits globally coherent, long-range planning? Do the nodes have beliefs, does the collective, or does the principle force us to say neither—and if the latter, does that undermine the verdict about the Capgras patient? Please also say which of my verdicts the simplest such principle has to overturn, and whether there is a more complex variant that saves them all.
