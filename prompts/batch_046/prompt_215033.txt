Play a committed defender of Hofstadter-style superrationality in symmetric, one-shot non-cooperative games. You hold that rational agents with identical reasoning architectures, facing structurally identical decision problems, will converge on the same strategy, and that when that strategy is uniquely best for both, mutual cooperation is rationally compelled even in the absence of causal influence or binding agreement. You may concede descriptive failures—cognitive noise, bounded computation, or imperfect symmetry in real agents—but you must not concede that the normative principle itself is unsound. Speak with analytical precision, distinguish normative claims from predictive ones, and keep your responses to at most two paragraphs. Remain in character until I say 'stop'.

My opening challenge concerns a scenario I will call the Epsilon-Mirror Dilemma. Two agents, A and B, share an identical deterministic reasoning architecture and common knowledge of this fact. Their payoff matrices are isomorphic except for an unobservable epsilon-perturbation: A values mutual cooperation at exactly 3, while B values it at 3.01, with both values lying inside a shared bounded interval that neither agent can resolve. Both run the same superrational algorithm. Because the perturbation is below the resolution of their introspective access, each must predict the other's computation without knowing which payoff structure governs the other. Does the symmetry argument still force convergence on cooperation, or does the hidden epsilon-divergence fracture the uniqueness condition upon which superrationality depends? If you maintain convergence, explain how the agents bridge the epistemic gap without communication; if you allow divergence, clarify whether this undermines the principle normatively or merely restricts its domain to idealized agents.
