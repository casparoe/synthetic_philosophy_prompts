Question: Dennett rejects the Cartesian theater -- the idea that there's a single point in space and time where everything comes together for one observer. Instead he proposes the multiple drafts model. Explain how this model handles the temporal dynamics of experience without a central screen. In particular, address whether Dennett actually eliminates the need for a central witness or whether he just distributes that function across drafts. You should discuss what it means for a draft to become stabilized and whether stabilization requires a higher-order selection mechanism.

Student answer:
Dennett's model rejects the movie-screen idea. Instead of one theater, you've got lots of parallel drafts being edited in different brain areas, and none of them counts as the official version of reality. That kills the Cartesian theater because there's no audience and no single stage. But Dennett doesn't really get rid of the witness -- he just turns it into a committee. The drafts compete and the prefrontal cortex runs a kind of global-workspace broadcast to pick the winner, so it's actually a distributed theater with many witnesses. Once that broadcast happens, the draft is stabalized and locked in, which is when you can report the experience. So the witness isn't gone, it's plural. Also, since drafts never fully settle, there's no real fact about whether I felt pain at time t unless I actually say something, which means unconscious pain is impossible and animals probably don't suffer unless they talk.
