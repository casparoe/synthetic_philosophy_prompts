As a novelist drafting an essay for a literary review, I want to examine whether Daniel Dennett's framework of the physical, design, and intentional stances—together with his notion of real patterns—can illuminate how fiction produces the appearance of autonomous minds. My working thesis is that the novelist necessarily operates in a hybrid of the design and intentional stances: we construct characters to serve precise narrative functions, yet we render them so that readers treat their beliefs and desires as genuine patterns worthy of predictive interpretation, despite the complete absence of any underlying physical substrate. I am especially interested in whether free indirect discourse serves as the technical mechanism that sustains this hybridity, allowing an author's design intentions to be experienced as a character's genuinely autonomous mental life without breaking the fictional illusion. I worry, however, that granting fictional minds the status of loci for real patterns might commit me to a broader fictionalism about mental states in general, rather than confining the stance to aesthetic artifacts. Could you comment on these notes? Specifically, does applying the intentional stance to deliberately engineered narrative entities differ in kind from applying it to biological agents, and am I right to think that Dennett's emphasis on predictive utility rather than ontological commitment is precisely what permits the stance to remain philosophically respectable across both domains? I would also welcome any observation on whether I am conflating the functional explanation of a character's behavior with the interpretive stance a reader adopts toward it.
