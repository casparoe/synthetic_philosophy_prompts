I recently concluded a lengthy and somewhat heated discussion with a close friend about Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model and its relationship to the Cartesian theater he so vigorously rejects. My friend insisted that abandoning the theater does not merely redistribute consciousness across parallel, competing narratives, but dissolves the very idea of a unified subject to whom anything is presented; she argued that without a central observer, first-person authority collapses entirely, leaving only subpersonal processes that cannot coherently belong to anyone. I tried to counter that the drafts are subject to editorial integration over time, yet we reached an impasse when I raised what strikes me as an underexplored consequence of the model: if later drafts can retroactively restructure the temporal indexing of earlier sensory content—so that the 'present' is not a single frame but a continuously revised editorial construct—then there may be no determinate fact of the matter about what was experienced at any particular instant, only a family of post-hoc stabilized narratives.

Before I resume this debate, I want to think systematically rather than polemically. Could you please describe the most important implications of Dennett's multiple drafts model—specifically when taken together with this idea of retroactive editorial restructuring of temporal indexing—for our understanding of three related issues? First, whether the Cartesian theater can be rejected without also rejecting a minimal, distributed subject of experience, or whether the dissolution of centralized presentation inevitably dissolves subjecthood altogether. Second, whether first-person reports retain any special epistemic status if they are always reports on already-revised drafts rather than on an original, unmediated presentation, and what this means for the reliability of introspection. Third, whether the model undermines, or can be reconciled with, the normative and legal practices that presuppose a single, continuous agent responsible for actions that were, at the time of initiation, genuinely 'mine.' I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want a disciplined conceptual analysis that treats the model seriously on its own terms, without forcing it to address concerns it never intended to solve, but also without ignoring the genuinely disruptive force of abandoning a centralized present.
