Over the past month I have become personally preoccupied with whether an ant colony can genuinely possess a mind, a concern sharpened by daily observation of an enormous colony in my garden and by my recent encounter with the classic dialogue in which the colony Aunt Hillary debates an anteater about her own consciousness. I am drafting a short essay arguing that ant colonies do realize a minimal form of distributed cognition, but not through brain-like neural integration; instead, I propose a niche criterion I call “synchronic integration,” according to which cognition requires information processing within a genuinely shared temporal window rather than mere functional connectivity. Because pheromone diffusion in the colony creates overlapping yet non-synchronous feedback loops, the colony achieves adaptive homeostasis and problem-solving without meeting this temporal condition, yielding what I term “temporally diffuse cognition”—real mental activity that lacks any unified narrative locus of experience. I contrast this with the dialogue’s implicit assumption that self-awareness requires a single, continuous subject. I would appreciate your comments specifically on whether “synchronic integration” is conceptually coherent, whether the distinction between functional cognition and narrative selfhood avoids both anthropomorphism and eliminativism, and whether the essay inadvertently smuggles in human-centered intuitions about unity while claiming to reject them.
