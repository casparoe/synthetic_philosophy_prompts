As a novelist constructing a long narrative whose structure depends on cyclical rather than linear time—where a protagonist's origin and terminus coincide, separated only by perspective—I have come to believe that Kurt Gödel's rotating-universe solution offers something more rigorous than a physical curiosity. Please argue for the position that the presence of closed timelike curves in Gödel's metric does not undermine but actually strengthens his idealist claim that temporal passage is unreal. Treat the rotating universe not as an empirically discredited cosmological model but as a conceptual laboratory: demonstrate that if a globally extended present can curve back upon itself, then the A-theory's required distinction between an objectively real past and an objectively real future collapses, leaving only a B-theoretic block in which change is a local orientation effect rather than a fundamental feature of reality. In developing this, draw on the novelist's intuition that circular narratives—where causation loops without contradiction because the story's logic is not globally linear—are not mere metaphors but structural analogues for how closed timelike curves function once we abandon the assumption that causation must be arranged in a single, non-intersecting sequence. Be precise enough that someone acquainted with Gödel's metric and his philosophical essays on the unreality of time could recognize the stakes, yet explain specifically why a circular narrative form might illuminate a feature of the physics that standard linear-causal interpretations obscure.
