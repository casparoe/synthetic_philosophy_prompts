Next Thursday I defend a reconstructed version of Berkeley's master argument in a philosophy-of-mind seminar, applied specifically to the ontology of virtual environments. My thesis is that if Berkeley is correct that nothing can exist without being perceived or conceived by a mind, then the engineering notion of a "substrate-independent" virtual world—one running on unrendered server architectures that are never experienced, never rendered, and never enter any conscious state—commits precisely the abstract idea of matter that Berkeley criticized. The virtual objects users interact with exist only as ordered ideas governed by rules graspable by minds, not as causal effects of hidden computational substrates. The audience consists of eight direct-realist philosophers of perception who reject representationalism about ordinary experience and are inclined to treat digital environments as straightforwardly present-to-hand, plus two computer scientists who design VR systems and treat substrate independence as an unproblematic working assumption; none are Berkeley specialists, and the philosophers will be suspicious that idealism collapses when confronted with technological artifacts that seem to function independently of any observer's mental state.
