After a long and still-unresolved argument with a friend about Derek Parfit's teletransporter and whether psychological continuity can genuinely substitute for personal identity in accounting for survival, I want to lay out for adjudication a sharper exchange I heard between two colleagues in our philosophy reading group. They were not debating the standard case—where a replica emerges on Mars with near-perfect psychological overlap—but a modified version that introduces what they called a parameterized fidelity setting. In this variant, the scanner destroys the original and reconstructs the replica with a configurable suppression of specific psychological clusters. The particular case they fixed on was this: every cognitive and affective trait is preserved except one structurally central emotional disposition—the replica retains full factual and autobiographical knowledge of the original's grief over a sibling's death, but the grief itself, with all its phenomenological texture and motivational pull, is absent. All other preferences, memories, and self-referential beliefs remain intact. Their disagreement turned on whether this omission breaks survival, and if so, why.

The first interlocutor began by defending the continuity view. She argued that if Parfit's central claim holds—that what matters in survival is psychological continuity with the right kind of causal origin—then this replica should clearly survive. Normal human life includes gradual emotional attenuation: trauma can flatten affect, aging can dull grief, therapy can reframe loss. If such endogenous changes do not amount to death and replacement, then an exogenous suppression of a single emotional module, leaving the rest of the architecture untouched, should not either. The missing grief is quantitative, not qualitative; it reduces the similarity coefficient without breaking the causal and psychological chain that grounds first-person concern. To treat this as non-survival, she said, is to reintroduce through the back door an all-or-nothing identity condition—the very Cartesian or soul-based metaphysics that the teletransporter was designed to displace.

The second interlocutor resisted by distinguishing quantitative similarity from structural coherence. He granted that Parfit's Relation R captures something important, but insisted that psychological continuity is not a statistical aggregate; it is the preservation of a unified narrative thread with specific load-bearing joints. The grief in question was not an accessory trait like a preference for tea over coffee, which could be altered without disrupting the self's temporal orientation. Rather, it was a pillar of the original's practical identity—shaping commitments to family, patterns of moral attention, and the very framework through which past and future were experienced. The replica, he argued, relates to the sibling's death as an informed observer rather than as a grieving participant. That is not merely a reduction in similarity; it is a discontinuity in the self's internal architecture. Moreover, he emphasized that endogenous emotional change transforms the same narrative thread, whereas the scanner's suppression creates a copy with a deliberate omission at a structurally central point. Even without branching—since only one replica exists—the causal process here is not continuation but reconstruction with a designed gap.

The first speaker replied that this "structural centrality" criterion is both vague and question-begging. If survival depends on identifying which psychological features are "load-bearing," then survival judgments become subjective or conventional, varying with how an observer weights different traits. Parfit sought to avoid precisely this ambiguity by grounding survival in direct causal connection plus broad psychological similarity. Furthermore, she noted that people often report feeling "like a different person" after profound trauma or religious conversion, yet we do not treat them as dead. If endogenous transformation can preserve survival despite radical emotional restructuring, then the exogenous reconstruction—operating through the same direct causal chain from brain-state to scan to body—should be evaluated by the same standard: does the overall pattern of psychology continue?

The second speaker conceded the risk of vagueness but maintained that the ambiguity is ontological, not conventional. The task is to discover, for a given psychology, which features are structurally central—not to legislate them arbitrarily. He proposed a diagnostic comparison: alter the parameter to suppress only the preference for tea, and both parties would agree survival holds; alter it to suppress the entire affective orientation toward the sibling, and disagreement emerges. This divergence reveals that survival is feature-sensitive in a way that pure similarity metrics cannot capture. He also challenged her analogy to trauma: post-traumatic change is the self adapting within its own continuous history, whereas the scanner produces a new history that mimics the old except at a point of deliberate design. The causal chain is singular, yes, but its direction is replication rather than transformation.

The first speaker then pressed a deeper objection. If the second speaker's view requires that continuity include not just similarity and causation but also the preservation of every structurally central feature, then the teletransporter's practical implications collapse. In any real-world reconstruction—whether through gradual neural replacement or rapid scanning—some micro-features will inevitably differ. If each such difference threatens survival, then survival becomes fragile to the point of non-existence in ordinary life. Parfit's insight was precisely that identity is not what matters because it is too brittle; psychological continuity is robust precisely because it allows minor variation. By insisting on structural integrity at load-bearing points, the second speaker restores the brittleness Parfit sought to eliminate, only now disguised as "narrative coherence" rather than "numerical identity."

The second speaker answered that robustness does not require unlimited tolerance, but rather the right kind of tolerance: variation within a continuous thread is compatible with survival, but replication with a targeted omission is not. He suggested that both of them were missing a distinction between two forms of psychological continuity—what he called "horizontal" continuity, which tracks similarity across time within one life, and "vertical" continuity, which tracks the causal and narrative integration of a single thread. The teletransporter provides vertical continuity in form—one cause, one effect—but may fail to provide it in substance if the reconstruction is parameterized to omit a feature that integrates past and present. The missing grief is not just one missing data point; it severs the vertical link between the original's past suffering and the replica's present self-understanding.

Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point? Specifically, is the first speaker correct that suppressing a single emotional module is analogous to normal forgetting or trauma-induced change, and therefore compatible with survival under a continuity account? Is the second speaker correct that structural centrality and narrative integration matter more than aggregate similarity, and that exogenous suppression differs metaphysically from endogenous transformation? Where does each argument overreach or conflate causal form with causal substance? And what deeper principle—perhaps about the unity of practical identity, the directionality of psychological change, or the conditions for non-branching continuity—are both interlocutors missing or leaving unarticulated?
