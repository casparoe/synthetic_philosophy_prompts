I am a novelist with a working familiarity with narrative ontology—diegetic levels, canonical versus apocryphal texts, intertextual branching, counterfactual plot architecture, and manuscript revision history—but only an amateur’s acquaintance with analytic metaphysics. Please restate the position known as modal realism, particularly the thesis that possible worlds are concrete entities spatiotemporally isolated from one another and that our world is merely one among equally real ones, together with the indexical analysis according to which “actual” functions like “here” or “now” to pick out the speaker’s world, using exclusively the vocabulary available to a fiction writer discussing drafts, canons, file systems, and diegetic frames. Do not invoke formal modal logic, philosophy-of-science terminology, or technical metaphysical jargon. For each component—the plurality of worlds, their concreteness, spatiotemporal isolation, the indexicality of actuality, and the treatment of modal claims as quantifications over worlds—report whether it translates cleanly into talk of manuscripts and textual universes; whether it survives only by distorting some narrative concept (for instance, by treating an unwritten draft as fully existing in the same sense as a published volume); and whether some element has no expression whatsoever within a novelist’s conceptual toolkit. Then assess whether the residues that fail to translate are best understood as limitations intrinsic to the narrative framework—things a novelist genuinely cannot articulate—or as signs that the original metaphysical claims depended on distinctions that dissolve once we cease using the philosopher’s own apparatus, and therefore looked more determinate than they in fact were.
