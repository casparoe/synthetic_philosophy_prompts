You are the senior methodological auditor for an evolutionary biology funding body. Every quarter you must evaluate ten to fifteen grant proposals that claim a specific morphological, physiological, or behavioral trait in a study population is an adaptation shaped by natural selection, rather than a spandrel, exaptation, or structural byproduct. The board requires a reusable, documented decision procedure that a non-specialist auditor can run in approximately forty-five minutes per proposal, with clear escalation points where the auditor must hand the case to a specialist evolutionary biologist or to the board for a judgment that involves research values rather than evolutionary fact.

Design the procedure. Derive its logic from the spandrels critique—specifically, the insistence that correlation with an environmental factor is insufficient, that structural and developmental constraints generate null expectations which must be ruled out, and that phylogenetic inertia must be addressed before invoking selection. For each step, specify the exact evidentiary question; what counts as a pass, a fail, or an indeterminate result; whether a fail is a hard stop (claim rejected) or a soft flag (claim downgraded); and whether the step can be executed by the auditor or requires specialist input.

The procedure must include an explicit branch marking where the decision becomes one of methodological values—such as whether incomplete but expensive comparative data should be demanded—rather than a determination of fact. It must also specify two classes of evolutionary claim for which it is not designed, and one structural limitation that could cause it to misclassify a genuine adaptation as a non-adaptive byproduct.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three submitted claims:

A. A proposal arguing that the unusually thick cuticle of a high-altitude beetle population is an adaptation to desiccation, based solely on a correlation between cuticle thickness and elevation, with no comparative data from low-altitude sister populations and no developmental study of cuticle formation pathways.

B. A proposal arguing that the loss of pigmentation in a cave salamander is a spandrel of selection for reduced metabolic rate, supported by comparative phylogenetic analysis showing that pigmentation genes are downregulated in all low-metabolism lineages regardless of light environment.

C. A proposal arguing that cooperative food-sharing in a primate group is an adaptation for reciprocal altruism, supported by extensive long-term behavioral records but lacking any phylogenetic comparison with non-cooperative sister species and lacking analysis of whether the behavior is developmentally coupled to kin-recognition mechanisms.

For each case, show the evaluation path, indicate where the procedure produces a verdict the submitting authors will reject, identify any point that forces escalation to a specialist or to the board, and note where the auditor’s personal judgment about evidentiary standards is unavoidably involved. Conclude by stating what the procedure explicitly cannot do.
