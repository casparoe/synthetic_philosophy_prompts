So I've been teaching epistemicism versus supervaluationism about vagueness for like fifteen years, and honestly the debate never moves. The epistemicists—Williamson-style—say there's a sharp boundary for being bald or being tall, we just can't know where it falls because of margain-for-error principles. The supervalutionists—Fine, Keefe, that crowd—say there's no single correct sharpening; every admissible precisification is equally good and that's why tolerance intuitions hold. But every argument about higher-order vagueness and the sorites paradox is totally rehearsed. Nobody budges.

So I'm asking: is this stall actually substantive? Or is it verbal, with both sides using 'truth' differently—one about ontology, one about semantic rules? Or are they just answering different questions under one heading?

But here's my own hunch, and I want you to wrestle with it: maybe the real problem is that both camps treat vagueness as a defect that needs solving, when actually it's part of our conceptual architechture for social coordination. Like, we need terms like 'rich' or 'poor' to stay vague so communities can negociate status without explicit cutoffs. If that's right, then asking 'where is the boundary?' or 'how many precisifications exist?' is already the wrong move. The disagreement might be bedrock: epistemicists want hidden facts, supervaluationists want pluralist semantics, but neither can accommodate the idea that vagueness does functional work for us.
