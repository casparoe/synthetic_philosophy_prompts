I read a summary in a philosophy of science newsletter that tried to capture the current state of the debate about causation by omission. The author distinguished between normative omissions, in which an agent fails to discharge an expected duty, and structural omissions, in which a system lacks a component that would have blocked an outcome. The summary then claimed that both varieties have now been shown to reduce without remainder to positive causal processes—normative omissions to the psychological and social causes of inaction, structural omissions to the presence of interfering conditions—so that statements like the missing guardrail caused the crash are merely pragmatic shorthand, not descriptions of genuine causal relations. It went further, invoking what it called a causal exclusion principle for absences to argue that if omissions were real causes they would have to occupy spatiotemporal locations, which they cannot do, and therefore omission-causation must be eliminated from ontology rather than merely reinterpreted. I have a nagging feeling that this flattens an important distinction between metaphysical and explanatory questions, and that the exclusion argument may be misapplied, but I am not sure where the errors lie. Could you audit the summary: which parts fairly represent standard positions in the literature, which parts subtly conflate separate claims or overstate modest theses, and does the eliminativist conclusion actually follow from the accurate premises?
