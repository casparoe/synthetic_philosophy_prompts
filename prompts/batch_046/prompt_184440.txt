I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been trying to understand hinge epistemology—particularly the idea in Wittgenstein's late work On Certainty that certain propositions form the framework of inquiry rather than being ordinary claims we verify. I want to apply this distinction to the certainties I actually operate with in my work, but my intuitive judgments about which ones are hinges and which are merely empirical are inconsistent, and I hope a clear principle can organize them.

Here are my verdicts. That boolean logic applies consistently to the circuit I am debugging is a hinge for me: I never verify it in practice, and without it the activity of debugging collapses into incoherence. That Earth is older than my deployment pipeline is also a hinge, in the classic sense. But that my compiler has not been backdoored feels ambiguous: in practice I treat it as standing fast, since auditing every binary is impossible, yet in principle I could run reproducible builds and inspect signatures, so it seems like it ought to be empirical. That my teammate genuinely attended yesterday's stand-up and is not a convincing simulation strikes me as not being a hinge—it concerns a specific, contingent person—yet I realize I have no direct proof of it, and my collaboration depends on treating it as fixed. That two plus two equals four in base-ten arithmetic is a hinge, but that it equals four in this particular floating-point implementation is not, since rounding errors can violate it. Finally, that there exists an external world beyond my monitor is a hinge, whereas that there is specifically a server in Virginia responding to my API call is not.

The sharpest tension is between compiler-trust and teammate-presence: both involve trusting something I cannot continuously verify, yet I want to classify one as a hinge and the other as not, without being able to articulate why. Is there a precise principle—perhaps about what must stand fast for a given practice to be rational, or about the structural role a belief plays within inquiry—that captures all these judgments? Or must I surrender either the compiler case or the teammate case? I also keep returning to one further instance: that my own memory of writing this module three months ago is reliable enough to guide refactoring. Is that a hinge, or merely a well-supported empirical belief? I would like one or more precisely stated candidate principles, each clause tied to the case that motivates it, together with their verdicts on cases I have not considered, and an honest admission of which of my judgments they cannot accommodate.
