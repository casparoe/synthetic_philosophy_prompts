A colleague has pressed me into an argument about Weber's Protestant ethic thesis that I cannot escape, and I need help locating the faulty step. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Weber's claim that culture can be an independent cause rests on the idea that value-rational action—action taken because an actor subjectively attaches ultimate meaning to it—can initiate behavioral patterns that material interests alone would not predict.

(2) To avoid reducing culture to a mere reflection of economic conditions, Weber insists that we must document the value independently of the behavior it explains, and then show an elective affinity between that value and specific material interests rather than others.

(3) But if we apply this standard rigorously, then for any observed behavior we can always construct a post-hoc value-rationalization that makes the behavior meaningful, because human actors can always be interpreted as acting for reasons.

(4) Therefore, without an independent documentary record, cultural explanation is always available and never falsifiable; it proves too much by explaining everything.

(5) Weber avoids this by requiring that the value be attested prior to the behavioral shift and that it selectively align with particular material interests rather than others.

(6) Yet this requirement makes cultural causation depend on a prior causal story about why those particular values, rather than others, were available to be adopted—a story that usually appeals to material conditions, institutional structures, or power relations.

(7) So either cultural explanation is circular (it uses behavior to infer values and then values to explain behavior) or it is parasitic on non-cultural explanations (it needs material conditions to account for the availability and selection of values).

(8) Hence Weber's defense of culture as a cause either collapses into redundancy or violates its own prohibition against reducing culture to material conditions.

I feel as though something slips between steps (5) and (7), because Weber's standard of elective affinity seems designed precisely to avoid circularity without making culture redundant. But I cannot tell whether the error lies in (3)—that it overstates the availability of rationalizations—whether (6) smuggles in a false dichotomy between cultural and material explanation, or whether the argument conflates the logic of discovery with the logic of justification. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the broader project of explaining historical change through values?
