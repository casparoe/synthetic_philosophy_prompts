I'm trying to isolate a recurring pattern in debates about logical revision—especially when intuitionistic or other non-classical systems are defended against classical ones, though I think it generalizes. A critic argues that a particular inference rule should be abandoned—say, double-negation elimination—because applying it in some domain produces results the critic takes to be unacceptable. But the argument that the results are unacceptable itself proceeds by reasoning that employs that very rule, or rules of comparable strength, in the metalanguage. If pressed, the critic often says that the metalanguage is merely a practical tool, not part of the formal system under evaluation. Yet the critic then uses that same metalanguage reasoning to establish that the object-language system is inadequate, precisely because it cannot support the reasoning just deployed. So there is a loop: the logic is attacked with tools it does not authorize, the reliance on those tools is dismissed as pragmatic, but that dismissal is withdrawn whenever it serves to demonstrate the object-language's poverty.

I know this sits near Carroll's dialogue in which a tortoise asks Achilles to justify modus ponens and every justification appeals to a further rule of the same form, but I'm unsure whether what I'm describing is the same phenomenon. Carroll's case seems to be an internal infinite regress of rules within one system, whereas my case involves using one logical framework to attack another while insulating the attacking framework from reciprocal scrutiny. Is there an established term for this specific move—perhaps in the literature on logical pluralism, on the metalanguage/objection-language distinction, or on circularity in normativity? How exactly does it differ from ordinary question-begging, from the pragmatic/semantic metalanguage distinction in formal logic, and from Carroll's original regress? And if there is no precise term, what are the right boundary conditions: must the critic explicitly disavow the metalanguage logic, or is implicit reliance on stronger rules sufficient?

Please separate what is well-established about Carroll's original paradox and about standard distinctions between object-language and metalanguage from what is genuinely contested—such as whether insulating one's reasoning framework in this way is a legitimate methodological stance or an evasion—and do not present either side of that dispute as the consensus view. If the pattern I have described has no established name, propose one with a clear definition and indicate the closest neighboring concepts and where they diverge.
