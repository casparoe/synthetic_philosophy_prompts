I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about Berkeley’s master argument — specifically, whether the claim that we cannot conceive of an object existing unperceived undermines not just material substance but also the modal realist’s picture of possible worlds as concrete, mind-independent totalities, as well as representationalist theories in philosophy of mind that posit unconscious mental contents. I have come to defend an extended Berkeleyan anti-realism: the master argument shows that “existing unperceived” is not merely false of physical objects but incoherent as a metaphysical predicate, and this should make us deeply suspicious of any framework — modal realism, Platonism about abstracta, or cognitive-scientific representationalism — that relies on entities whose defining feature is to lie outside the scope of any possible conception. On my view, if you can genuinely conceive of a state of affairs, you have already conceived it as present to some mind; therefore, the very idea of a maximally comprehensive possible world, or of an uninstantiated universal, or of a representation that is by nature inaccessible to consciousness, collapses into contradiction.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this position. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to the secondary literature; I want the arguments, intuitions, and conceptual results that should move someone who takes the master argument seriously. Which standard objections — that the argument confuses conceiving an object with conceiving it as perceived, that it illicitly moves from epistemic limitation to metaphysical conclusion, or that it proves too much by rendering mathematics or logic mind-dependent — are actually decisive, and which are merely awkward? Beyond the familiar repertoire, are there formal considerations from modal logic, results in the philosophy of mathematics, or distinctions drawn in contemporary philosophy of mind that expose this extended view to special damage? I want an honest assessment of whether the position can be qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, by redefining “conceivable” so narrowly that no counterexample could ever reach it — and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a fatal vice. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely embarrassing, distinguish what a committed defender of this Berkeleyan line would concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss, and tell me whether the view risks anything concrete or has already been protected into emptiness. Please answer in American English.
