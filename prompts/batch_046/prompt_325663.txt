As a retired natural scientist who has recently begun studying philosophy of physics, I have found the Born-rule problem in Everettian quantum mechanics to be both technically subtle and conceptually under-examined in popular accounts. To move beyond the standard formulations, I ask you to adopt the following distinction for the purpose of your analysis: call an ontology "thin-branching" if every decoherent history possesses equal ontological weight regardless of its wavefunction amplitude, and call it "thick-branching" if the squared modulus of the amplitude functions not merely as a predictive measure but as an intrinsic ontological density that makes high-amplitude branches more substantial or experientially prominent than low-amplitude ones. With this framing in place, write an essay that weighs whether the decision-theoretic arguments for the Born rule—those that derive rational credences from preference axioms applied to self-locating uncertainty in branching scenarios—actually resolve the problem or merely displace it onto the agent's reasoning. In particular, address whether such arguments can remain fully coherent under a uniformly thin-branching ontology, or whether they tacitly presuppose some form of thick-branching in order to justify why a rational agent should care disproportionately about high-weight outcomes. Do not end on an indecisive note: defend a specific verdict about whether the Born rule is best understood as a purely normative constraint on rational belief that requires no ontological weighting, or as an emergent statistical feature that demands thick-branching to be physically meaningful. Your justification should turn on conceptual analysis of probability, ontology, and rational agency rather than on appeals to particular authors, texts, or empirical findings.
