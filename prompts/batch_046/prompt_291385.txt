After a long argument with a friend about whether grounding moral rules in idealized deliberation saves or destroys indirect normativity, I need an outside eye on a student exam answer I am grading as a TA. The prompt I gave was: distinguish direct from indirect normativity using Railton’s criterion-of-rightness versus decision-procedure distinction, then evaluate the claim that if rules are justified through an idealized deliberative procedure—behind a veil of ignorance or via reasonable rejectability—indirect normativity collapses into direct evaluation applied one level up at the constitutional stage; is the collapse inevitable?

The student answer correctly defines direct normativity as act-level evaluation by value-promoting properties and indirect normativity as act-level assessment by conformity to rules that are themselves justified by value-promoting properties, with Railton’s distinction intact. The student then argues that in Rawls’s original position parties compare basic structures by their long-run distributive outcomes, so the veil does not eliminate direct evaluation but merely relocates it upward; therefore any deliberative foundation for indirect norms must be directly consequentialist at the rule-selection stage, making indirectness illusory. To avoid this, the student appeals to Scanlon and claims that because Scanlon replaces outcome-comparison with reasonable rejectability, contractualism is best read as a non-consequentialist form of indirect normativity—indirect in application but direct in justification.

Please grade this point by point, flagging the one or two subtle errors a rushed reader might miss—there is a mischaracterization of what the original position compares and a misclassification of Scanlon’s view, plus a possible conflation of psychological decision-procedures with constitutional justification—and say whether the collapse argument survives once those are corrected. Stay neutral: do not tell me whether my friend is right, and do not ask for bibliographies or citations. Keep the assessment precise and focused on the logic of the answer itself.
