You are preparing me for the Q&A after my seminar presentation to the metaphysics working group. I defend the view that a statue can survive the gradual, part-by-part replacement of its clay—provided the replacement is guided by what I call a diachronic structural script—but that this survival exhibits directional dependence: it holds when replacement is intentional and form-preserving (a sculptor or conservator substituting parts to maintain the work), yet fails when the same final shape and material result from accidental erosion followed by piecemeal repair, even though the spatiotemporal trajectory and the endpoint are materially identical. I call this formal persistence with directional dependence. The audience will include a Chisholm-style mereological essentialist who applies essential-parthood to artifacts; a constitution theorist in the Baker tradition who holds that constitution is always one-one and temporary; an art-conservation ontologist who thinks we should replace statue-talk with talk about object-events; and a graduate student working on a Wiggins-style dominant-kind essentialism who will press me on whether the replaced object still belongs to the kind statue. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will arise, each phrased in the vocabulary its speaker uses. For each: state the objection, rate its damage as fatal, serious, or awkward; provide a reply I can deliver in about two minutes that concedes what should be conceded; flag the weakest point in that reply; and note whether the objector is likely to accept the concession or will treat it as a retreat. Include the objection I most hope nobody asks—namely, whether my structural script is just psychological continuity smuggled into material-object metaphysics, and whether that collapses my view into a personal-identity theory applied to clay. End with the single reply that must land if the proposal is to survive, and identify the precise point in my own position that is most exposed and that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook.
