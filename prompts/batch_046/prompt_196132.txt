Draft the joint statement that an epistemic unilateralist and a coordination multilateralist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the unilateralist's curse in high-stakes collective-decision domains. The unilateralist holds that a rational agent should act on her own best all-things-considered judgment even in the face of broad expert disagreement, because institutional deferral systematically amplifies collective error. The multilateralist holds that agents facing widespread peer disagreement should bind themselves to collective decision rules or institutional procedures, precisely to prevent the negative externalities of unilateral action.

Structure the response in four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: cover the formal structure of the curse, the conditions under which it arises, observed patterns of expert disagreement in policy-relevant domains, and the known limitations of current game-theoretic models of the problem. Second, a disagreements table: for each camp, state its own position in wording that side would accept; then state the opposing view in neutral terms; and pinpoint the exact divergence. Third, identify at least three cruxes. One crux must concern second-order disagreement—disagreement not only about first-order empirical claims but about which epistemic norms should govern resolution of the dispute—and whether this undermines the unilateralist's appeal to private judgment. For each crux, specify a concrete historical or hypothetical case that both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each side predicts that case demonstrates. Fourth, a brief concluding assessment of whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal rather than substantive; if so, identify which part and explain why.

Write in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the analysis conceptually precise and avoid reliance on obscure empirical details or named scholars.
