I am a journalist writing on science and ideas, and in the course of researching a feature on the ethics of ecosystem management I witnessed a sharp exchange between two scholars at a conference. The first scholar (Move 1) argued that habitat destruction—whether accidental or deliberate—is ethically defensible, perhaps even obligatory, because wild animal life is structurally dominated by suffering: predation, parasitism, starvation, and chronic stress produce vastly more pain than pleasure across most populations. Reducing animal numbers through habitat loss therefore reduces aggregate suffering, however callous it may appear to conservationists. The second scholar (Move 2) replied that this neglects reproductive dynamics: in stable populations, individuals who die are replaced by offspring, and if the average wild life is even mildly net-positive in welfare terms, then destroying habitats eliminates future positive lives rather than rescuing animals from misery. The first then countered (Move 3) that empirical patterns of juvenile mortality and density-dependent stress suggest wild lives are net-negative, and that replacement merely perpetuates a cycle of suffering; furthermore, survivors in degraded habitats often face intensified competition and disrupted social structures, raising per-capita suffering rather than lowering it. The second then pressed (Move 4) a reductio: if reducing wild populations is genuinely good, then we ought to celebrate natural disasters that wipe out ecosystems and actively support global eradication programmes for wild species—yet no coherent conservation ethic accepts this, which suggests the initial suffering-based calculus misses something essential about ecological value, stability, or the moral significance of natural processes. Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move, rating each contribution from clearly mistaken to clearly sound and briefly justifying each rating. In particular, say who is more right overall, identify which premise or inference both parties are neglecting, and explain what accepting or rejecting that neglected point would cost each side. I should be grateful if you could reply in British English.
