I cover democratic reform and institutional design for a science-and-ideas desk, and I have been tracking the rulings of a citizens’ assembly tasked with proposing structural changes to local government. Its record so far: it endorsed random selection for climate-policy juries but rejected it for appointing appellate judges; it supported strict two-term limits for mayors and governors yet opposed them for senior career diplomats; it approved mandatory cooling-off periods before former legislators may lobby, but approved lifetime tenure for central-bank governors while rejecting it for members of an election-integrity board. The assembly must now rule on a hybrid proposal: fill thirty percent of legislative seats by lot, drawing only from citizens who have already served one elected term in local government, with those seats non-renewable after a single four-year term. Predict the verdict they ought to reach for consistency, identify the closest analog in their precedents, and lay out the two or three distinct rules that could jointly explain their track record—specifying which rule predicts approval and which predicts rejection. Then name the one further case that would best distinguish those rules, and be frank about whether their precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome: if they do, state precisely which principle the assembly would be adopting with an approval and which with a rejection.
