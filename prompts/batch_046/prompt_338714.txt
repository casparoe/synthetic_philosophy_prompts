Many democratic legal systems retain criminal statutes that prohibit private, consensual conduct widely regarded as immoral—conduct that causes no direct harm to non-consenting parties—while relying on prosecutorial discretion or judicial interpretation to keep them practically dormant. At the same time, these jurisdictions often extend constitutional protections, such as privacy rights or anti-discrimination guarantees, to the very groups and behaviors those dormant statutes condemn, without formally repealing the moral offenses. What philosophical commitments about law, morality, and harm does that institutional pattern presuppose? Must it be reconstructed as an implicit endorsement of Devlin’s claim that a shared morality requires legal expression to bind society together, even when enforcement is absent? Or can it be accommodated within Hart’s harm principle, treating the unenforced prohibitions as purely symbolic anomalies that do not commit the legal system to moralism? And if the practice is genuinely stable, does it presuppose a specific distinction between the expressive and coercive functions of law—one that allows citizens to treat an unenforced prohibition as normatively active despite its practical dormancy?
