I've ended up thinking lexical threshold negative utilitarianism only works if pleasure and suffering aren't just quantitatively different but structurally incommensurable — they sit in different evaluative dimensions, so no amount of good experience can ever trade against even a small breach of the suffering-threshold. That saves the view from collapsing into regular utilitarian calculus, but I'm worried I've just built an unfalsifiable bunker. So what'd actually count against it? Skip the tired kill-everyone-to-end-suffering line; I want serious defeaters: phenomenlogical cases where pleasure genuinely cancels pain, neurscience showing shared reward/pain circuitry, formal results about preference consistency when you reject all trade-offs, and analogies to other lexical orderings that show structural incommensurability is usually suspicious. Rank them fatal to merely awkward. Be straight: do defenders have real exposure here, or can they absorb any counterexample by saying wrong evaluative kind? Does the view predict anything its rivals don't, and if not, is that insulation a problem? I especially want to know if there's an emperical finding or conceptual result that can't just be dismissed that way.
