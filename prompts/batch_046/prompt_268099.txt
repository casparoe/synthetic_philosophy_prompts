I recently encountered a summary—presented by an older student in our philosophy discussion group—of P.F. Strawson's argument concerning moral responsibility and determinism. The summary claimed that Strawson reduces moral responsibility entirely to reactive attitudes, understood as instinctive emotional responses such as resentment and gratitude; because these responses are biologically fixed and operate independently of metaphysical beliefs about causation, the free-will debate is revealed to be a pseudo-problem. The student added that Strawson therefore adopts a straightforward compatibilist position, holding that determinism is likely true yet harmless because our emotional machinery cannot be suspended. Furthermore, the summary asserted that Strawson introduces the concept of an objective attitude merely as a clinical stance taken toward malfunctioning machinery, implying that he regards all moral judgment as ultimately reducible to psychological description. The student concluded that Strawson's view makes the traditional free-will debate obsolete by dissolving it into social psychology. I want to know whether this is a fair rendering. In particular, does Strawson identify reactive attitudes with bare emotional reflexes, or is there a more structured interpersonal framework that the summary omits? Does he actually endorse determinism, or does he treat it as an irrelevant hypothetical? Is the objective attitude correctly described as a clinical stance toward machinery, and does Strawson intend to reduce moral judgment to psychology? Finally, does his argument genuinely dissolve the free-will problem in the manner suggested, or does it bear a more nuanced relation to compatibilism?
