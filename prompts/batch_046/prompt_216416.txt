I have recently been working on a cognitive-architecture project that attempts to model self-monitoring without a central executive controller, and in a design review a colleague dismissed my concern about whether the system could suffer a divided subjective state by saying that there is no audience in the head and that Dennett settled the matter long ago. I realize the phrase is doing considerable argumentative work, but I cannot tell exactly what work, and I need to disentangle its possible meanings before I can respond. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that the claim there is no audience in the head — or its close relatives, such as the denial of a Cartesian theater — could actually be expressing? For each reading, please indicate whether it amounts to a trivial denial of a homunculus, a substantive empirical claim about the distributed architecture of consciousness, or a metaphysical claim about the absence of a unified self; say which are defensible, which are false or question-begging in this context, and which ones actually license dismissing worries about divided consciousness in an artificial system. I am particularly interested in whether any reading entails that there is no fact of the matter about the subjective character of being in a particular draft-state, or whether they merely deny that such character is presented to a single inner observer. Please treat this as a menu of interpretations rather than selecting one correct reading, and tell me which version my colleague likely intended and whether that version genuinely supports her dismissal of my concern.
