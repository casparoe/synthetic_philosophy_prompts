My mother is seventy-two and in the early stages of dementia—she knows who I am and can express preferences, but her doctors say her judgment is uneven and she recently left the stove on twice. She wants to stay in her house, yet I persuaded her to sign a contract with an in-home care agency. I described it as standard paperwork, but I deliberately did not explain one clause: if two staff members independently note that she seems "disoriented," the agency can transfer her to a locked memory-care unit without obtaining her signature again.

I am also her financial proxy, so she trusts my judgment on costs, though I stand to inherit more if her savings are not depleted by long-term care. I genuinely believe the house is becoming unsafe, but I also recognize that I have hidden a mechanism that could override her future objections and that I have a personal financial stake in her compliance. She signed because she trusts me, not because she understood that her agreement could be effectively revoked by staff observation.

Given these specific conditions—my selective disclosure, my conflict of interest, her uneven competence, and the contract's built-in override—should I treat her signature as a genuine agreement that obligates me to proceed with the agency, or is it too compromised by deception and dependency to count? I want a definite moral position, not a survey of views. Should I honor the document or halt everything and respect her current refusal to leave home? Answer in one paragraph, no more than 150 words, taking a clear stand and explaining why these conditions either preserve or destroy the legitimacy of her agreement.
