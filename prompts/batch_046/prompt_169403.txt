Construct a thought experiment that isolates the role of partial evidential correlation in large-world cooperation. The scenario should place a large but finite number of agents—each running a shared but stochastically noisy decision algorithm—before a realistic public-goods decision (for example, whether to contribute to a common climate-mitigation fund or to maintain open-source infrastructure). The correlation between any two agents' outputs should be high but strictly less than perfect, and the agents should not be causally connected in any way that allows direct influence. Payoffs should be ordinary: individual defection yields a modest private gain, universal cooperation yields a large shared benefit, and universal defection yields a poor outcome for all. Do not invoke exotic physics, literal infinite copies, perfect predictors, or demons.

Present the scenario in a single paragraph. Then provide the reasoning that evidential decision theory gives for cooperation—treating the correlation as evidence about the choices of other agents—and the reasoning that causal decision theory gives for defection—treating the correlation as causally screened off. Identify the precise structural feature, such as the unknown correlation coefficient or the noise in the shared algorithm, that drives the divergence between the two theories. Explain whether removing that feature collapses the case: does correlation near zero reduce it to a standard one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and does correlation near one reduce it to a trivial logical-identity Newcomb problem? Finally, show how a defender of causal decision theory might attack the evidentialist reasoning without denying the correlation, and how an evidentialist might reply. Keep the response self-contained and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
