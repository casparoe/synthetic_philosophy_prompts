The exam question asked: 'Plato's Meno asks why knowledge is more valuable than true belief if both guide action correctly. Discuss whether the "tethering" account—knowledge as true belief bound by reasoning (aitias logismos)—solves the problem, and compare it with virtue epistemology (Sosa's aptness) and the modal-tracking account (Nozick). Address whether a Gettier-style case undermines the tethering account, whether Sosa's achievement-view actually closes the value gap, and whether Nozick's conditions add anything beyond reliability. Conclude with your own view on whether Meno's problem survives these treatments.'

Please grade the following student answer with a specific numerical mark out of 100 and a point-by-point commentary. Be committed: take a definite position on its quality, do not merely balance praise and criticism neutrally, and say clearly where the reasoning succeeds and where it slips. Identify the visible strengths, then pinpoint the subtle errors—particularly any conflation of Plato's causal tether with modern internalist justification, any misreading of Meno's framing as purely instrumental, and any step that asserts redundancy between Sosa's competence conditions and Nozick's modal conditions without actually showing it. The assessment should be precise enough to return to the student.

Student answer:
'Plato's Meno asks why knowledge is more valuable than true belief, since both direct action correctly. His answer is that knowledge is true belief tethered by reasoning—aitias logismos—which prevents it from running away. This solves the value problem because a tethered belief is stable: it will not be lost by accident, and stability is an instrumental good. Modern epistemology has refined this. Gettier cases show that justified true belief can be true by luck, so modern internalist justification—like Plato's tether—is insufficient. Ernest Sosa's virtue epistemology improves on this by defining knowledge as apt belief: belief that is true because of the agent's intellectual competence. This makes knowledge an achievement, which is intrinsically valuable, like hitting a target through skill. Robert Nozick offers a different improvement: he requires that the belief track the truth across nearby possible worlds—if things had been different and the belief false, the agent would not hold it. Both Sosa and Nozick agree that knowledge excludes luck, but Sosa focuses on the agent's competence, whereas Nozick focuses on the belief's modal sensitivity. In my view, Meno's framing assumes that the value of knowledge must be purely instrumental—useful for reaching goals—whereas virtue epistemology shows it can be intrinsic. The tethering account therefore fails because it treats stability as a property of the belief, whereas Sosa shows it is a property of the agent. Moreover, Nozick's tracking conditions are redundant because any belief that is true through competence will automatically be sensitive to falsehood in nearby worlds. Thus the modern virtue-theoretic answer dissolves Meno's puzzle by relocating value from belief-stability to agent-achievement.'
