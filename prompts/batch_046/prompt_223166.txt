I'm writing a feature on embedded agency — the view that a reasoning subject is physically continuous with the world it acts upon, with no ontological firewall between mind and environment. A philosopher I interviewed put forward an argument that seems to show embeddedness makes normative failure impossible, yet I am convinced the conclusion is too strong.

The argument runs as follows:
(1) If agency is genuinely embedded, the agent's neural states and the environmental states form one continuous causal system; there is no boundary at which causation stops and independent justification begins.
(2) Any reason the agent offers for acting is therefore itself a state within that system, determined by prior conditions.
(3) So citing a reason cannot be an independent justification; it is merely a redescription of one causal link.
(4) From this it follows that there is no normative gap between what the agent does and what it ought to do — every outcome is causally correct relative to the chain that produced it.
(5) Yet we plainly distinguish rational from irrational embedded agents. Consider a climate modeller whose forecasts are fed back into the economic policies that alter the very emissions trajectory she is modelling: she may revise her assumptions when new data conflict with her predictions, or she may suppress the discrepancy to protect her institutional standing. The first course seems rational, the second irrational.
(6) Hence embedded agency either abolishes all normative evaluation, or it smuggles in a hidden boundary between reasoning and causation that contradicts the premise of embeddedness.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a way to preserve both the physical continuity of agent and world and the possibility that an embedded agent can genuinely go wrong?
