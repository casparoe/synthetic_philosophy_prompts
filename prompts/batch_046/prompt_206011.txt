I'm a student in an upper-level philosophy-of-action seminar, and I've noticed a recurring objection to compatibilism that doesn't line up neatly with the standard taxonomy we've been using. In discussion, a classmate will concede that an agent can satisfy Frankfurt-style hierarchical conditions—her second-order volitions align with her first-order desires, she is reasons-responsive, and she identifies with her actions. She will also concede that the agent could have done otherwise had her prior mental states differed. Yet she still insists that something important is missing, and she illustrates it with long-range character trajectories rather than with single choices. She asks us to compare two musicians: both practice rationally and become virtuosos, but in one case the entire arc—developing the desire to practice, valuing mastery over other goods, interpreting setbacks as challenges—is fully determined by initial temperament and environment, so that the musician can choose within that arc but cannot genuinely "rewrite" its governing values. In the other case, she imagines the same rational development occurring, except that the agent's deep evaluative framework itself remains open to rational revision in a way that doesn't trace back deterministically to prior psychological states. The classmate isn't demanding randomness or indeterminism at the moment of choice, and she isn't demanding the libertarian "ultimate origination" of every action. She is demanding a specific kind of long-term openness: the capacity for rational, non-arbitrary self-revision at the level of one's deepest character structure, such that one's future self is not merely the unfolding of a predetermined trajectory. I have seen this move made repeatedly with examples involving career choice, religious conversion, and moral reform—cases where the agent acts rationally and is clearly the "source" of her actions, yet critics say she lacks freedom because her capacity to become an altogether different kind of person was "locked in" from the start. Is there an established concept in the literature that isolates exactly this configuration—rational, non-random, deep-character openness that is neither momentary leeway nor causal self-creation? I know it borders on Kane-style self-formation and on developmental accounts of autonomy, but neither seems to capture the precise emphasis on the non-determined revisability of one's long-range evaluative architecture rather than on single actions or on being an uncaused cause. If there is a standard name, how does it differ from these neighboring ideas? And if there is no precise term, how should one carve the phenomenon so that it is clearly distinct from both hierarchical compatibilist sourcehood and from libertarian ultimate responsibility?
