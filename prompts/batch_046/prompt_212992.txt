I am a novelist constructing a multigenerational narrative centred upon three siblings who inherit the same estate, each holding a contradictory yet partially documented account of their deceased father's final intentions — not mere subjective biases, but genuinely incompatible legal and emotional truths, each supported by independent evidence. Rather than imposing an omniscient resolution, I wish to structure the novel so that every chapter fully inhabits one sibling's account, allowing details that directly contradict preceding chapters to stand without reconciliation or hierarchical ranking. My editor contends that this will appear as authorial evasion rather than philosophical discipline, and urges me to reveal a hidden, unified truth beneath the contradictions. I resist this impulse: I want the contradictions to remain structurally irreducible, as though the inheritance itself genuinely possesses incompatible properties depending on the standpoint from which it is assessed. Yet I worry that I am merely aestheticising confusion. Is there a rigorous philosophical framework — one that does not collapse into vulgar relativism — that validates holding contradictory descriptions of a single reality as simultaneously true? And if so, how might such a framework inform the architecture of fiction without sacrificing narrative coherence?
