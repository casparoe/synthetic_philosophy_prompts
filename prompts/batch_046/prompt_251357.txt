I've been trying to formalize the conjugal view for a debate and my argument keeps running off a cliff. (1) Marriage is a normative kind defined by reproductive complementarity: the state recognizes it because it stabilizes the form in which children are typically raised by both biological parents. (2) Belonging to a normative kind doesn't require actually performing the function; it requires having the characteristic capacity, even if accidentally blocked. (3) Opposite-sex couples have reproductive complementarity even when infertile or elderly, since the block is accidental to there kind-membership. (4) Same-sex couples lack complementarity altogether—no accidental obstacle is being removed. (5) So recognizing same-sex marriage is recognizing a union outside the kind marriage is meant to stabilize. (6) But apply this consistently: if the state should only recognize unions ordered toward reproduction, then it should deny licenses to couples who know in advance reproduction is impossible—elderly pairs, people post-sterilization, couples with known genetic incompatibilities. (7) Nobody accepts that, so one premise is wrong. I want to keep (1) and (5) if I can—where's the weakest link, and what does dropping it cost?
