I'm writing this scene for a novel about a publishing co-op called Northlight, and I've got them running this strange internal ritual. Every quarter they black out the names of their top-selling authors in the staff newsletter and print the line 'excess visibility harms collective craft'; meanwhile, in the foyer there's a big brass plaque listing every founding editor with birth years and quotes about excellence. One character thinks it's hypocritical — they smash current success but worship dead success — another says it's consistent because past achievement is settled and present achievement is competitive, and a third wonders if it's not about philosophy at all but just office politics wearing moral language.

I'm trying to figure out what philosophical commitments, if any, this pairing actually forces on them. Could you reconstruct the implicit theory behind anonymising present winners while venerating founders? I want to know whether that only makes sense if you think merit is purely systemic, or if a liberal egalitarian could defend it on seperate grounds, or whether it's coherrent to say achievement becomes harmless once it's historical. Also, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — compatible with rival views — and what a version of Northlight that genuinely celebrated individual success would have to change structurally, not just symbolically.
