I'm a retired natural scientist—used to run a biophysics lab—who got into philosophy late and now I'm stuck on Szasz's The Myth of Mental Illness (1961). My neighbour, a philosophy grad student, walked me through this argument and I can't find the exit. I think there's a hidden slip but I can't name it, and I want to keep thier idea that predicitive-processing errors are real biological states without accidentally saying IBS is a pseudoscience.

(1) Szasz's core claim is that mental illness is a myth in the literal sense: psychiatrists take problems in living—grief, bad habits, social failure—and treat them as if they were diseases with tissue-level pathology, like pneumonia or diabetes, but there is no such pathology.

(2) But that was 1961. Now we have predictive-processing models of psychosis, measurable interoceptive dysregulation, and documented allostatic load in major depression. So there IS biological dysfunction, just not at the level of a single lesion.

(3) So Szasz's factual premise—that mental conditions lack objective biological correlates—is false. The myth is debunked.

(4) Now apply the SAME standard consistently to the rest of medicine. Tension headaches, IBS, essential hypertension, and long-covid fatigue are also diagnosed mostly by subjective report and functional dysregulation; none have one clear tissue lesion you can point to in every case.

(5) Therefore, if mental illness is "real" because functional dysregulation counts as pathology, then IBS and hypertension are real too—fine. But if we stick to Szasz's stricter standard (no tissue lesion = not a real disease), then we have to call IBS, hypertension, and half of primary care "myths" too.

(6) He says this proves Szasz's argument is self-defeating: either you reject the strict standard and save psychiatry but also save gastroenterology, which makes Szasz wrong about mental illness being special; OR you keep the strict standard and end up saying internal medicine is mostly pseudoscience. Since nobody accepts the second horn, Szasz loses.

I feel like something slipped between (3) and (4), or maybe between (4) and (5). Is it that Szasz wasn't actually making an empirical claim about lesions but a conceptual/normative claim about medicalization? Or is there an equivocation between "functional dysregulation" in the brain versus the gut? I want to keep that predictive-processing errors count as real biology, and I also want to keep that IBS is a legitimate diagnosis, but I'm not sure if I can have both without giving up on Szasz entirely. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
