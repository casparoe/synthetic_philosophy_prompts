Draft the joint statement that two adversarial collaborators—one a universal-transparency advocate and one a contextual-harmony advocate—could both sign after sustained good-faith engagement.

The universal-transparency advocate holds that Dutch-style directness is a baseline moral obligation in communicative ethics, that so-called white lies are wrong regardless of social context, and that politeness norms requiring strategic ambiguity constitute institutionalized deception. The contextual-harmony advocate holds that Japanese-style indirectness is frequently a positive moral duty, that white lies can be obligatory expressions of respect and care, and that unmediated bluntness often functions as a form of social aggression that denies the interlocutor's face.

The deliverable should contain four parts. First, a numbered section of genuinely shared claims about the nature of speech acts, the psychology of taking offense, the cross-cultural variability of conversational norms, and the conditions under which deception erodes or preserves trust. Second, a disagreements table stating each side's residual positions in wording that its own proponents would accept and that avoids strawman representation; the table should cover, at minimum, whether indirectness can carry intrinsic moral value independent of its welfare consequences, and whether a speaker's implicit epistemic warranty—the standing commitment to be held accountable for the full propositional force of an utterance across all foreseeable contexts—is violated by face-saving ambiguity. Third, a cruxes section identifying the precise sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party; include whether social harmony or naturalness has non-instrumental value, and how far predictive confidence in cross-cultural pragmatics should extend before policy recommendations become irresponsible. Fourth, for each crux, specify the concrete case or institutional design both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's explicit prediction.

Use the following constructed case for the predictions: an oncological disclosure protocol in which physicians must choose between delivering unvarnished statistical survival curves to terminal patients and employing culturally calibrated metaphorical framing that preserves family harmony and the patient's social face, with outcomes measured not only by informed-consent comprehension but also by long-term institutional trust and the patient's reported sense of being recognized as a whole social being rather than as a data point. Each side should state in advance whether they predict the indirect-framing protocol will increase or decrease net harm relative to direct disclosure, and they should identify the specific metric—cognitive accuracy, emotional sustainability, or relational trust—on which their prediction turns.

Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by the disagreements table, followed by the cruxes with paired predictions.
