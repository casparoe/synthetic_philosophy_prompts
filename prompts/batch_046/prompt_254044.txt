Here are my verdicts on first-person reports across four domains, and I want to know whether one principle captures the pattern or whether I must accept a patchwork.

First, when I introspect a qualitative sensory difference—say, that crimson looks darker than scarlet—I treat the report as highly reliable; I do not think I am confabulating the phenomenology.

Second, when I introspect the absence of mental imagery—claiming my current thinking is purely verbal, with no pictures—I think this is unreliable. I can be surprised by how quickly an image arises when I deliberately try, which suggests absence reports are often false negatives.

Third, when I introspect the causal origin of a preference—say, that I chose a career for intellectual freedom rather than prestige—I take this as unreliable. Outside-view tracking of my past choices suggests status motives dominate, and the introspective story feels like post-hoc rationalization.

Fourth, when I experience a "feeling of knowing"—the sense that a fact (the capital of Australia) is available without being able to retrieve it—I treat the feeling itself as calibrated and trustworthy, even though the retrieval may fail.

The difficulty is that cases one and four both involve hard-to-verify internal signals, yet I trust one almost completely and the other only instrumentally, while cases two and three fail despite being equally internal. Is there a unified account—perhaps in terms of evolutionary function, predictive-error architecture, or access conditions—that delivers all four verdicts without ad hoc exceptions? If no single principle can save them all, which verdict should I overturn: should I downgrade the sensory-qualitative reports, or upgrade the absence and reason-attribution reports?

Also, please state what your best account predicts for a case I have not considered: what should it say about introspecting one's own calibration curve—feeling that I am about 80% accurate on a specific class of forecasts—when there is no external feedback loop available?
