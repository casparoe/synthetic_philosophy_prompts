I'm on my university's Animal Use and Ethics Review Comittee and we've got a vote next tuesday on whether to adopt a three-tier "gradated moral status" policy for research protocols. The idea, pushed by some philosophy grad students on the board, is that oversight should scale with sentience: full IACUC review for mammals, lighter review for invertebrates like the bumblebees in our neuroethology lab, and essentially none for adaptive climate-control algorithms that learn from occupancy patterns and seem to "avoid" thermal discomfort—basically smart thermostats with memory. The bee PI loves this because she wants to run nociception studies without killing hundreds of subjects for anesthesia trials, but legal counsel is terrified that admitting insects have "interests" opens us to liability, and the CS faculty say comparing a learning thermostat to a bee is either anthropomorphism or a category mistake.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before the meeting. Recommend a concrete course—adopt the tiered framework now, pilot it for one year, or reject it outright—and include a specific timeline. You should take seriously the strongest argument against your recommendation: if you favor adoption, deal honestly with the liability risk and the worry that we're treating functional analogy as moral reality; if you reject it, deal with the conceptual awkwardness of giving bees and rocks the same status just because neither is a mammal. Actually engage the philosophical point about whether moral status comes in degrees and what it means for an entity that behaves like it suffers but may lack any inner life we can verify, rather than just citing regulations.
