As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun working through philosophy of science in earnest, I have been meeting with a small discussion group of former colleagues to read about spacetime ontology. Two members—both with technical backgrounds but little formal philosophy—have reached a sustained impasse over a modified version of Newton’s bucket argument, and I want an expert philosophical adjudication rather than a general survey. Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher of physics with specific expertise in substantivalism, relationism, and their modern physical interpretations. Before delivering your verdict, take a moment to map the logical commitments of each side, and begin by briefly outlining the structure of the disagreement so I can see how you are parsing it.

Here is the exchange, reported as accurately as I can recall it:

Colleague A: Imagine Newton’s rotating bucket, but place it in a modified scenario: two identical cylindrical vessels are floating in deep intergalactic space, each containing a superfluid bath and an internal suspended gyroscope, with no stars, galaxies, or massive bodies within any causal horizon. One vessel rotates at angular velocity ω, the other at 2ω, both initially aligned with a shared reference orientation. The superfluid surfaces inside will show different parabolic curvatures, and the gyroscopes will register different precessional forces. Because there are no external bodies to serve as relational partners, this demonstrates that rotation is absolute: there is a genuine fact of the matter about how fast each vessel spins relative to space itself, not merely relative to other bodies. Substantivalism is therefore correct—spacetime is a substance-like entity that provides an absolute standard of rest and rotation.

Colleague B: That assumes exactly what needs to be proven. The curvature of the fluid and the behavior of the gyroscopes are fully accounted for by the relational dynamics among the superfluid atoms, the vessel walls, and the quantum fields present inside each container. You do not need to invoke space itself as an independent substance; you only need the consistent mathematical structure describing those internal relations. Even in an apparently empty region, gravitational vacuum fluctuations and quantum correlations provide the relational partners that Mach’s principle demands. Calling that partner absolute space is merely reifying a descriptive framework. The vessels differ because their internal part-to-part relations differ, not because they are embedded in different states of some container.

Colleague A: But consider that the internal relations are identical except for the rate of rotation—same atomic composition, same wall geometry, same quantum state distributions, only scaled by angular velocity. The relational account must then explain why one configuration produces curvature proportional to ω² and the other to (2ω)². That quadratic scaling refers to a parameter that is not defined by any purely internal relation among the parts; it is defined by the vessel’s motion through the absolute manifold. Without such a manifold, there is no reason for quadratic scaling rather than linear or constant scaling.

Colleague B: The quadratic scaling follows directly from the geometry of the relational configuration itself—specifically, from how inter-part distances transform under rotation. It requires no external stage. You are treating the mathematical description of relations as evidence for a container, which is a category error. Moreover, if we actually performed this experiment, general relativistic frame-dragging effects might couple each vessel to distant cosmic structures through gravitational waves, preserving Machian relationality even in apparent isolation.

Colleague A: Then you have conceded that rotation involves a physical fact exceeding purely local internal relations—whether you call the partner distant matter, gravitational waves, or the metric field. Once you admit that rotation is not reducible to instantaneous relations among parts inside the vessel, you have abandoned pure relationism. The remaining question is only whether that partner is best understood as a substance (spacetime substantivalism) or as an extended web of distant relations. But my core point stands: the bucket experiment, even in this isolated form, shows that rotation is not merely internal.

Colleague B: I concede only that rotation involves non-local dependencies, not that those dependencies require a substantival container. A web of relations can extend beyond the vessel without being a substance. Your argument demonstrates relationalism with non-local scope, not substantivalism.

Please adjudicate this dispute with precision. Specifically: (1) Does the isolated rotating vessel thought experiment successfully demonstrate that rotation requires an absolute reference frame, or does it merely establish non-local relational dependencies? (2) Is Colleague A correct that the ω² scaling law points to a parameter undefined within pure internal relations, or does Colleague B’s geometric reply fully dissolve that concern? (3) What are both parties missing—perhaps regarding how general relativity actually treats rotating frames, or regarding whether quantum vacuum states can genuinely serve as a Machian relational partner? Provide your verdict in clear, reasoned steps, defend the ordering of your assessments, and do not conclude with a bibliography, citation list, or suggested readings. I want a direct philosophical evaluation of the arguments as actually made.
