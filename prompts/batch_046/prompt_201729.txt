During a mandatory community disaster-preparedness audit, you serve as the emergency coordinator for a simulated flood scenario. The rules allow only one rescue route: either your teenage child, trapped on a lower floor, or two unrelated adults on an upper balcony, both reachable in the same window but not together. You direct the team to extract your child first. When filing the required justification report, you write that familial bonds carry greater weight than treating every life as interchangeable in triage allocation. Re-reading your explanation, you feel an unexpected discomfort—not with saving your child, but with having translated that choice into the language of competing moral weights, as if affection had been turned into a calculable entry on a ledger. You are unsure whether this discomfort reveals a genuine error in your reasoning, or whether applying any moral framework to such a choice is itself a kind of corruption. Should one defend saving one's own child in these terms? If not, what form—if any—should an honest account take?
