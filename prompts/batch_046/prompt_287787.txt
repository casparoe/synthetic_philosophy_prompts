I serve on an institutional review board that evaluates deployment proposals for autonomous systems operating in open-ended physical and social environments, and we are trying to enforce a "low-impact" or "side-effect avoidance" criterion without requiring every proposal to come with, or our board to agree upon, a complete formal specification of human values. The proposals we see claim that their impact can be bounded by comparing the world-with-the-system to a counterfactual baseline, but they disagree on whether that baseline should be defined via utility-weighted world-states, via invariant causal mechanisms, or via some structural divergence measure that does not depend on which outcomes are valued. I need a reusable decision procedure—something my team can run in a single review session with standard technical documentation—that operationalizes the idea that "avoiding side effects" might be defined through a value-agnostic divergence budget rather than through harm-benefit accounting. The procedure should take the form of an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a rejection, the point at which it must hand the decision to a human because the question is irreducibly one of values rather than measurable divergence, and an explicit statement of where the procedure is guaranteed to misfire. It should be grounded in whatever formal work exists on impact measures and counterfactual baselines—such as structural causal invariants, reference-class construction for non-deployed counterfactuals, and degrees-of-freedom budgets—without requiring my reviewers to be philosophers of causation. In particular, please inject a specific framing: define impact not as a change in some valued state, but as the removal of degrees of freedom from a reference class of world-states constructed by holding fixed the environment's causal graph excluding the agent's direct outputs, using mechanism-preservation rather than state-matching to fix the baseline.

Please apply this procedure to three concrete proposals. First, an urban logistics system that dynamically renegotiates traffic-light priorities to optimize delivery routes, altering the temporal structure of an intersection but not the physical infrastructure. Second, a research laboratory AI that proposes altering the chemical composition of a small batch of reagents in an open bench environment to test an emergent hypothesis, where the physical change is localized but the causal pathway into broader experimental outcomes is not fully mapped. Third, a conversational agent deployed at scale that subtly reframes user queries before generating responses, with the explicit design goal of reducing the likelihood that users will form or express certain categories of political opinion, where the divergence is in the social-information graph rather than in physical states. For at least one of these—preferably the second—I want the procedure to yield an answer that permits the proposal despite the fact that a reasonable reviewer relying on intuition rather than the procedure would want to reject it; then explain exactly which step of the procedure produces that counterintuitive result and whether that is a feature or a bug. Finally, conclude by stating clearly what this procedure cannot do: which categories of side-effect it is structurally blind to, which value-laden judgments it smuggles in under the guise of technical measurement, and under what conditions a board should disregard its output and decide on normative grounds alone. The deliverable must be something a non-specialist compliance officer could run with a ten-page technical proposal and a one-hour meeting, and it should never ask the user to supply a full utility function or moral framework as an input.
