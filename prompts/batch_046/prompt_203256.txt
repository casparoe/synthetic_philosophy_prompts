Write the full specification of desiderata that an adequate theory of rational choice must satisfy when values are genuinely incommensurable—not a mere decision algorithm, but an account of what rationality requires in deadlock. For each criterion, give the motivating case or conceptual distinction: (i) the small-improvement problem, in which one option is marginally enhanced yet remains neither better nor worse; (ii) normative residue, where a rationally chosen option leaves behind appropriate regret rather than satisfaction; (iii) the triadic resolution case, where introducing a third option breaks a stalemate between two without being superior to either; and (iv) the parity-versus-incomparability distinction, where parity denotes a positive relation of equal standing that permits choice, while incomparability denotes the absence of any comparative relation at all. Specify which distinctions the theory must preserve without collapse, particularly whether it can avoid reducing incommensurability to hidden commensurability via an implicit common metric or utility scale. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable—such as consistency with rational agency, accommodation of persistent rational regret, and refusal to treat all hard choices as merely epistemic gaps—versus which are camp-specific preferences, like whether resolution must come through an act of will-formation alone or may be guided by parity-breaking contextual reasons. State whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, especially whether honoring both the small-improvement constraint and the prohibition on a hidden common scale generates a contradiction. Conclude with a brief evaluation of how value-pluralist parity accounts, will-based resolution theories, and contextualist practical-reason views fare against the list, but keep the conceptual weight on the criteria and their interrelations rather than on the scorecard.
