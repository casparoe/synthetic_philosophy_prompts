I cover science and public policy, and I am preparing a long-form piece on the moral logic hidden inside everyday price controls. One institutional pattern puzzles me: in many economies there is a statutory minimum wage — a universal price floor on hourly labour — sitting alongside rent-stabilisation regimes that cap annual increases for sitting tenants but permit landlords to reset rents fully to market levels upon vacancy, inheritance, or a change in ownership. At the same time, governments impose no comparable price floors on food, energy, or transport, and they do not apply analogous caps to mortgage costs or to owner-occupied housing.

What philosophical commitments about value, desert, and property does that specific bundle presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the wage floor rest on the idea that labour is not a pure commodity, while the vacancy-reset clause presupposes that landlords hold a stronger entitlement to market returns once a particular tenancy relation is broken? Is there a coherent framework that protects incumbent tenants but exposes new entrants, and workers but not consumers of other necessities — perhaps a view that distinguishes continuity-based entitlements from entry-based ones? Or do different parts of the bundle quietly rely on rival theories — for example, a capability-oriented justification for wages versus a Lockean or utilitarian justification for property? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, assess whether the parts are philosophically consistent, and indicate what would have to change in institutional design if the underlying view were replaced with a straightforwardly market-based or, conversely, a fully egalitarian one.

Please answer in British English. Separate what is well established in the philosophical and economic literature from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting any disputed interpretation as the settled consensus.
