I am drafting a one-semester philosophy-of-mind term-paper proposal (around 6,000 words) on a specific reading of the thought experiment in which a printed volume contains the complete state-transition rules of Einstein’s brain, drawn from Hofstadter and Dennett’s The Mind’s I. In other words, is there a hidden commitment to the idea that consciousness requires not merely correct static structure (the printed rules and state mappings) but also a continuously updated first-person temporal orientation that is constituted through live interaction?

The proposal should present this question in a form that admits a determinate answer, explain why standard functionalist and anti-functionalist treatments leave it untouched (they debate whether the book has the right states or the right causal powers, but neither examines whether the diagnostic method—the conversational test—already assumes a process-based, dialogical condition), and lay out the conceptual method: separating (i) static information storage, (ii) causal dynamics, and (iii) interactive self-modeling, then comparing the book not only to a running computer simulation but also to a non-interactive digital archive that preserves all states without temporal updating. The scope must remain realistic for one semester: no empirical neuroscience, no required citation archaeology, just rigorous conceptual analysis using the tools already available in philosophy of mind and philosophy of language.
