I am a software engineer without any formal training in philosophy of science, but I recently sat in on a cross-disciplinary seminar about organizational change where a speaker tried to map Thomas Kuhn’s account of scientific revolutions onto software architecture. The speaker summarized Kuhn as holding that a paradigm shift is structurally identical to replacing a legacy monolith with a distributed microservices architecture: the old and new frameworks are incommensurable in the sense that they share no common interface or performance metric, which makes rational comparison between them literally impossible. According to this summary, scientists never switch paradigms because of evidence, but only through a collective conversion experience—like a development team abruptly deciding to rewrite an entire codebase in a new language after a crisis of confidence—after which they inhabit a different conceptual world and can no longer even understand the problems the previous paradigm was attempting to solve. The speaker concluded that Kuhn therefore denies scientific progress altogether, reducing it to arbitrary fashion cycles driven by social pressure, and that this makes Kuhn’s own view self-defeating: if paradigms are truly incommensurable, then Kuhn’s theory possesses no special authority that could be recognized across different scientific communities.

Some elements of this analogy feel intuitively useful—particularly the idea of crisis driven by accumulating anomalies that resist patching within an existing framework—but I suspect the summary overstates the irrationality of paradigm choice and conflates incommensurability with complete untranslatability. Could you audit this characterization for fairness? I would like a clear separation of what accurately reflects Kuhn’s claims about anomaly, crisis, and the limits of cross-paradigm evaluation, versus what is a common popular oversimplification or an explicit distortion. In particular, does the speaker’s conclusion that Kuhn’s view is self-defeating actually follow from the accurate parts, or does it depend on the misrepresentations? I am also trying to understand whether the engineering analogy to technical debt and architectural migration introduces hidden philosophical distortions—such as treating conceptual frameworks as interchangeable modules—so please address whether that metaphor helps or misleads when applied to Kuhn’s notion of incommensurability.
