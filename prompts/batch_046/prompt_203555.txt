Read Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go through the lens of the procreation asymmetry—the intuition that there is a decisive moral reason not to bring into existence a being who will suffer, paired with the absence of any corresponding reason to bring into existence a being who will be happy, together with the "no victim" defense that denies harm to the created suffering being because non-existence is not a deprivation. The novel's clone-narrator Kathy H. and her friends are engineered for lives that contain genuine affection, education, and art, yet are structurally destined for suffering and premature death through organ donation. Ask whether the novel takes a position on whether their creators violated an asymmetry-based duty not to create suffering beings, or whether it instead dramatizes the limits of that framework. Focus in particular on what we might call the testimony problem: Kathy's retrospective first-person account gives moral voice and narrative presence to beings whose existence is precisely the kind of case the asymmetry treats as lacking a victim. Does her testimony function as a rebuttal to the "no victim" claim, or does the novel suggest that first-person testimony cannot generate the kind of person-affecting harm the asymmetry requires? Point to specific scenes—such as the gradual discovery of their purpose, the gallery exhibitions meant to demonstrate that they have souls, and Kathy's final solitary drive through the English countryside—to show where the philosophical idea illuminates a character's choice or a structural feature of the plot, and where the narrative resists reduction to it. Be explicit about where an honest critic must concede that the novel is not merely illustrating the asymmetry but complicating it. End by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate about procreative ethics that a purely argumentative treatment of the asymmetry misses.
