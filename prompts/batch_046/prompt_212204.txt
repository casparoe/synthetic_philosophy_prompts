I am applying for a six-month independent research fellowship and need a concise one-page proposal on a narrowly framed variant of the Meno problem. Rather than asking in general why knowledge is more valuable than true belief, I want to investigate what happens to that value gap in cases of deep epistemic delegation—situations in which one node in a cognitive system (an expert, a submodule, a teammate in a divided-labor research group, or a human relying on testimony) genuinely knows that p, while another node ends up with the corresponding true belief about p through outsourcing rather than through its own cognitive achievement. The core question is this: does the recipient’s mere true belief carry less value because it lacks stability, because it fails to guide action with the right kind of authority, because it forfeits achievement-credit, or because it is defective in some further normative dimension—call it “epistemic accountability”—that only knowledge can supply? And if the deficit is real, does it disappear, diminish, or change its character when we move from individual minds to distributed cognitive systems?

The proposal should do the following. First, state the question with philosophical precision, distinguishing the delegated-belief case from ordinary ignorance and from simple unreliability. Second, explain why the canonical answers to the Meno problem—reliability-based anti-luck conditions, virtue-theoretic achievement accounts, and practical-guidance theories—do not by themselves settle whether the value gap survives, collapses, or transforms under delegation. Third, outline the philosophical (not empirical or sociological) approach that would make progress: for example, constructing a conceptual taxonomy of delegation-types paired with corresponding value-profiles, or demonstrating that one standard account implicitly solves the delegated case but only at the cost of an unattractive commitment elsewhere. Fourth, describe what a useful deliverable looks like for people actually thinking about expert systems, institutional design, or epistemic authority—this should be a conceptual framework, not data. Fifth, specify what would count as failure (e.g., showing that all candidate deficits reduce to unreliability, or that the value gap is purely illusory in distributed cases). Sixth, keep the scope realistic for six months of solitary philosophical work: no large-scale empirical collaboration, no historical survey of the entire literature. Do not include a bibliography, a literature review organized by citation, or a reading list; you may refer to positions and arguments by name or description, and I will fill in specific citations myself. Finally, include a brief paragraph arguing why a foundation should fund this philosophical project rather than an empirical study of trust, expertise, or social epistemology—what distinctive progress can conceptual analysis make that surveys and experiments cannot?
