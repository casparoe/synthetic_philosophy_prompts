I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying moral philosophy, and I have volunteered to lead a session for a small reading group of fellow retired scientists and engineers — none with formal philosophical training, but all accustomed to reasoning about systems, failure modes, and design ethics. Our topic is the paradox of deontology: the structural tension within agent-relative moral theories whereby an active prohibition on causing harm can appear stricter than the corresponding permission to allow a greater harm through omission, a feature that resists straightforward consequentialist resolution and is not merely an error of intuition. To avoid the overused trolley and footbridge analogies, I wish to carry the explanation through an engineered safety case — specifically, a redundant mechanical interlock in a cooling system that can be manually overridden to prevent a catastrophic cascade failure, but only by an agent directly violating the design protocol that forbids manual interference. The case must illuminate why the deontologist treats overriding the interlock and permitting the cascade as belonging to different moral categories, without allowing the audience to collapse the distinction into physical causation or risk analysis.

Please plan the explanatory arc rather than deliver the explanation itself. I need the precise order in which the conceptual components must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit — that is, what must be in place before the paradox can appear as a structural feature rather than a simple contradiction. Specify the properties that the interlock example must possess and the properties it must lack in order to carry the idea without misleading analogues. Identify the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring — for instance, that deontology is merely risk-averse consequentialism disguised as rule-following, or that the distinction between action and omission has no physical meaning in engineered systems — and indicate the point in the sequence at which each should be anticipated and defused. Provide a check for understanding that would show the group has grasped the structural feature rather than merely memorised the action-omission distinction. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level — such as threshold-based relaxations of absolute prohibitions, discussions of moral taint or residue, and refined formulations of double effect — and explain briefly why omitting each protects the clarity of the core idea.

The session should be constructed so that it could be delivered without access to external sources, and I require no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
