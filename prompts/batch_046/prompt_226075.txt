I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends a contextualist Bayesian resolution to the problem of old evidence. Her core claim is that whether a datum counts as old is not an intrinsic property of the datum but a relational feature of the agent's partition of epistemic labor at the moment of evaluation: in collaborative scientific settings, evidence can be old for the collective record yet new for the individual theorist who hasn't yet incorporated it, and she applies this specifically to the Mercury-perihelion case to explain confirmation without invoking subjective priors or counterfactual reasoning about what the agent would have believed. She also rejects the standard reply that distinguishes using evidence to construct a theory from using it to test one, arguing that this distinction collapses under conditions of distributed cognition where no single agent holds the full inferential chain. I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. I need at least one question pressing her on how her contextualism avoids collapsing into epistemic relativism about confirmation—specifically whether two researchers with different labor-partitions can rationally assign different confirmation values to the same theory-evidence pair—and a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that appeals to a normative structure of collaborative inquiry: the follow-up should probe whether she can maintain that normative structure without smuggling in a social ontology of scientific groups that she hasn't independently defended. I need at least one question on whether her account can accommodate retroactive confirmation in solitary researchers, where there is no division of labor to appeal to, and a follow-up keyed to her likely dismissal of standard thought-experiment methodology: the follow-up should ask what empirical or historical case study she would cite to show that solitary confirmation genuinely occurs, given her skepticism about armchair counterfactuals. I need at least one question about hierarchical Bayesian models in which the prior at one level is the posterior from another—does old evidence at the lower level remain old at the higher?—and a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to level-relative evidential states: the follow-up should press whether her view requires abandoning the likelihood principle at the level of model selection. The remaining questions should press on other pressure points in the literature: whether her contextualism merely relocates the problem rather than solving it, how her view compares to predictive Bayesianism that denies old-evidence confirmation altogether, and whether her account can distinguish genuine old-evidence confirmation from post-hoc rationalization in historical sciences such as paleontology or cosmology. Every question should turn on the actual content of her position; include no generic biographical or career-opening questions, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
