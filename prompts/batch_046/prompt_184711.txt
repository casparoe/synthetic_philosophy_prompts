I'm delivering the eulogy for my uncle, a logician who died last week at eighty-seven. He lived as a conventional atheist, yet in his final years he became privately convinced by Gödel's reasoning that a rationally ordered world in which human minds possess capacities far exceeding any biological need must include some form of continued rational existence—an afterlife not of judgment or angels, but of completion. Roughly half the room will be philosophers and logicians who knew this side of him; the other half are family members who did not. Write a eulogy of about 400 words that honors this specific intellectual conviction without asserting that I share it, without offering false comfort such as that he is watching over us, and without turning the occasion into a lecture. The philosophical framework should do structural work: it should determine what I can and cannot honestly say, what tone is available, and how to speak to both audiences at once.
