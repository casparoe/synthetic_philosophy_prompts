I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about wronging people in identity-affecting choices, working off gut reactions. Case one: a government picks a fossil-fuel path that shifts climate patterns, so fifty years out a particular person lives with worse asthma and wouldn't have existed under the green path. I think we haven't wronged that individual specifically, but the choice was still bad. Case two: a couple conceives now knowing the kid will have a mild disability avoidable by waiting a year, which produces a diferent child. Same verdict — not wronged, but bad call. Case three: a city rezones downtown, changing migration patterns so in two generations diferent people live near a noisy train line and suffer health issues. Here I don't even feel the policy was bad; nobody was wronged, and the harm is just distributed elsewhere. Case four: an archive accidentally shreds old correspondence that, through a long chain of meetings and moves, would have led to one specific child being born; instead a diferent child is born to thier own parents. Even though nobody is worse off, this feels like a real loss or wrong, unlike the train case. Case five, the one I keep going back and forth on: a mandatory genetic screening program causes widespread shifts in conception timing, replacing a cohort of people with disabilities with a cohort of healthy diferent people. Is this wrong? Sometimes I think it's like the train case, sometimes like the archive case. Is there a principle that gets all four earlier verdicts, and what does it say about case five? Or do I have to drop one of the earlier ones to keep things coherent? And if there is a principle, what should I think about a private decision where one person moves cities, changing not just who they have kids with but also, through some butterfly effect, which strangers eventually meet and reproduce — is that like case three or case four?
