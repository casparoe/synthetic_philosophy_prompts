Consider the institutional package common in liberal democracies where the state issues formal apologies for historical injustices—forced assimilation programs, internment regimes, or extractive colonial policies—while simultaneously relying on sovereign immunity and statutory limitations to block legal liability, framing the gesture as symbolic “reconciliation” rather than a prelude to material redistribution, and pairing it with commemorative monuments or revised curricula that leave the underlying economic and legal structures untouched. What philosophical commitments about collective moral agency, intergenerational responsibility, and the ontology of the state does this practice presuppose? Must it treat the state as a continuous moral person capable of inheriting guilt and discharging it through performative speech acts, or can a purely structural, non-agential theory of institutions explain why apology without redistribution counts as a coherent institutional response? Is the package internally consistent: if the state genuinely accepts moral responsibility for past actions, why does the same legal system generally reject present citizens being held liable for harms they did not personally cause, and does the distinction between symbolic and material redress depend on a tacit theory of harm that separates dignitary injury from distributive injustice? Reconstruct the implicit theory of collective identity and moral bookkeeping at work here, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and specify what institutional arrangements would be forced upon us if we replaced the underlying view with a strictly individualist, presentist account of moral obligation.
