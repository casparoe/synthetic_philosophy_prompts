I've been trying to work out my view on contingency in history, and I'd like a consistency check. Here is roughly where I've landed: (1) historical processes are genuinely chaotic in the technical sense—small perturbations can amplify exponentially, so long-range macro-prediction is impossible in principle; (2) the proverb about a lost nail leading to a lost kingdom misrepresents historical causation by modelling it as a linear chain rather than a convergent network, so that in most cases the absence of one factor is compensated by others; (3) counterfactual dependence—whether an event would have failed to occur absent a particular cause—is the correct criterion for identifying genuine historical contingency; (4) if chaos theory applies to history, then counterfactual reasoning is unreliable because we can never specify initial conditions with sufficient precision; (5) despite this, historians should employ counterfactuals, but only for short-range, local events, never for macro-patterns such as the decline of an empire.

Do these hang together? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate which single revision would clean up the most conflicts, and say whether there is an apparent tension that dissolves on closer inspection. I am not asking for a bibliography or references to particular scholars—just whether this package is jointly tenable.
