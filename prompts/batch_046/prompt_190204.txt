In a graduate seminar on objective chance that I'm taking, discussion keeps collapsing into the slogan that "belief should defer to chance," and it is being invoked to settle disagreements that it clearly cannot settle on its own. The ambiguity became acute with a hypothetical scenario I proposed: suppose an agent knows that the physical dynamics governing a particular decay process assign an objective chance of 0.6 to event E, but she also knows that if she were actually to form a credence of exactly 0.6 in E, an undetectable feedback loop in her cognitive-measurement apparatus would perturb the system and shift the objective chance to 0.3. One participant used the slogan to insist that rationality demands credence 0.6, treating the uncorrupted physical law as authoritative; another used the same slogan to demand credence 0.3, arguing that deference must be to the chance that actually obtains conditional on her real belief state; a third dismissed both moves by claiming that admissible evidence must exclude any information about one's own mental states, rendering the slogan inapplicable here. I am not asking you to adjudicate the toy model itself, but rather to understand the language we are fighting over.

Could you lay out the distinct precise principles that "belief should defer to chance" might express — distinguishing, for instance, an unconditional alignment thesis, a conditional thesis restricted to physically admissible evidence, a thesis that permits self-locating or mental-state information into the conditioning set, and a thesis that treats the agent's own credence-formation as part of the chance-generating mechanism? For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, substantive, defensible, or false; whether it actually supports the conclusion its advocate needs in my feedback scenario; and which version people typically reach for when they appeal to deference in debates about statistical mechanics or quantum interpretation. Present the options as a menu rather than selecting one correct interpretation, and indicate precisely where the ambiguity in "chance," "belief," and "should" does the philosophical damage.
