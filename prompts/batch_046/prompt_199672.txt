The long-running dispute over whether British rule in India was, all things considered, a net benefit or a net harm—exemplified by Niall Ferguson’s claim that empire bequeathed modern institutions and Shashi Tharoor’s claim that it engineered deindustrialization and extractive famine—has hardened into a repetitive exchange of rail-mile statistics, GDP reconstructions, and mortality figures. Despite decades of archival work, the two sides have not converged; they seem to talk past each other even when they agree on the numbers. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse, not another weighing of the evidence. Is the disagreement substantive—say, about the elasticity of indigenous capital formation or the counterfactual trajectory of Indian state-building without conquest—or is it a clash of incommensurable baseline assumptions, with one side measuring outcomes against a hypothetical stagnant pre-colonial past and the other against an imagined autonomous industrialization? Or is it partly verbal, turning on whether "balance sheet" can apply to a relationship that involved the suppression of sovereignty, and partly normative, about whether institutional benefits can offset violations of self-determination? Could a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about how to model path-dependent institutional effects—be driving the apparent moral deadlock? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate: a shared counterfactual model that both sides accept, a conceptual separation between institutional causation and moral accountability, or the recognition that the question asks for a single ledger entry where none is possible?
