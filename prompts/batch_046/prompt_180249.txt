I am writing up my philosophical profile for a graduate seminar on rationalist moral metaphysics and want a consistency audit before I defend the package. Here is the set of commitments I have arrived at, all within a broadly Leibnizian framework:

(a) Moral truths are eternal verities, necessary in exactly the same modal status as mathematical truths; they do not depend for their truth on any particular divine volition, since God chooses the good because it is good, not vice versa.

(b) At the same time, the specific content of human obligations is fixed by our location within the actual global architecture—the best possible world—so that what we ought to do is determined by how our actions contribute to or detract from the total harmony of perfections that God actualized.

(c) Given that the best-world calculus involves an infinite comparison of compossible goods that exceeds finite discursive reason, moral methodology should be fallibilist and empirically informed, closer to reflective equilibrium or progressive approximation than to pure a priori deduction.

(d) Justice consists in charity directed by wisdom: promoting the perfection of others counts as genuinely moral only when guided by adequate knowledge of the overall good, not by mere benevolent sentiment or local sympathy.

(e) I also maintain that there are genuinely moral possibilities—alternative worlds in which different specific obligations hold—because if moral necessity were absolute across all worlds, the divine selection of one best world would be redundant with respect to morality; yet I deny that this variation makes morality voluntarist.

Please examine these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what the least costly revision would be in each case. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. For each genuine conflict, specify exactly which commitment must be weakened or abandoned to restore consistency, and do not assume I am especially attached to any one of them. In particular, address whether the combination of (a) and (e) can be stabilized without collapsing into either moral voluntarism or a redundant conception of divine choice.
