i was arguing with a friend after our seminar on historical contingency — we kept going back to that old proverb about the nail and the kingdom and how people usually read it as pure chaos theory, like one tiny physical glitch exploding into catastrohpe. but i think the intersting version is institutional path-dependence: the nail gets lost, but then the system locks around the mistake instead of correcting it. i want a poem that works with that tension, maybe 16 lines of blank verse, with one hard formal rule: every third line must end with either 'locked' or 'lost', alternating (so line 3 is lost, line 6 locked, line 9 lost, line 12 locked, etc.). use the proverb's chain — nail, shoe, horse, rider, message, battle, kingdom — but treat each link not as inevitable physics but as a point where the error could have dissipated and didn't. there should be a turn around line 10-12 where the reader sees that the kingdom fell not because the nail was missing, but because the absence was never fixed; the mistake got baked in. don't resolve it with a sermon about fate or free will, leave the tension hanging since that's literally where our discussion ended. keep it serious enough to read aloud at a seminar closing but avoid sounding like a greeting card, and don't just fall back on cute rhymes like horse/course — i want the form itself, especially those repeating end-words, to do the conceptual work.
