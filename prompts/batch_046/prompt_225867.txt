here's the sketch:

Braudel splits history into nearly immobile geographical time (mountains, seas, climate), social time (economic and demographic cycles spanning generations), and individual time (battles, treaties, lives). climate change seems to sit in all three at once: it's physically slow like geography, driven by discrete policy and industrial choices like individual events, and also shaped by economic cycles like social time. but the twist is latency. carbon released in 2020 doesn't finish warming the planet until 2060 or so. during those decades, the cause is an event but the effect hasn't joined the geographical layer yet — the structure is 'pre-loaded' but still looks stable. that suspended state doesn't fit neatly into any of Braudel's three boxes.

i also worry i'm sneaking in determinism. the idea that we're 'baking in' a future climate sounds like it removes agency from people living now. i don't want to say that, but i do think the latency creates a moral asymmetry: we're choosing for people who won't exist yet, and Braudel's lens makes that temporal disconnect visible in a way event-focused history hides.
