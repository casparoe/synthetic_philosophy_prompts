After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a close friend about whether personal identity is genuinely continuous or merely a convenient fiction, I drafted the following notes for a short essay and would like your critical assessment. I take Dennett’s notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity as my point of departure, treating it not as a metaphysical substance but as an emergent focal point around which experiences, intentions, and social recognitions orbit. My friend objected that if the self is only a narrative fiction, then radical revisions—following severe trauma, religious conversion, or profound amnesia—ought to be as effortless as editing a manuscript; yet they are frequently experienced as existentially destabilizing, even physically disorienting. To account for this resistance, I propose distinguishing narrative mass, the accumulated weight of past commitments and relational obligations, from narrative spin, the inertial pattern of self-interpretive habits that maintain coherence across time. From these I derive a speculative concept I call narrative tidal locking: just as a moon can become gravitationally coupled to its planet so that one face remains permanently oriented inward, a self can become so tightly coupled to its mnemonic and social environment that counter-narrative experiences are not merely ignored but structurally resisted, producing the phenomenology of crisis rather than simple update. The essay then asks whether this coupling undermines Dennett’s anti-essentialism, or whether tidal locking is itself only a higher-order feature of narrative gravity—an orbit so stable that it mimics substance.

Please comment specifically on three points. First, does the tidal locking analogy genuinely explain the resistance to narrative revision, or does it merely redescribe the phenomenon in celestial vocabulary? Second, does the distinction between narrative mass and narrative spin add real explanatory value, or does it needlessly bifurcate what should remain a unified account of narrative inertia? Third, is the essay’s concluding claim—that tidal locking is compatible with, rather than contradictory to, the fictionalist view—defensible, or does it surreptitiously reintroduce a kind of essentialist core? I am not asking for references, bibliographies, or citations; I want rigorous conceptual critique of the argument’s coherence, the aptness of its central analogy, and whether it fairly represents the view it engages.
