I am working on a philosophical essay about the boundaries of Kuhn's model, and I want to examine whether his account of paradigm shifts and incommensurability can be extended rigorously from explanatory theories in physics to purely classificatory conventions in astronomy. Consider the 2006 resolution by the International Astronomical Union that reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet. Analyze this episode as a potential Kuhnian revolution in taxonomy rather than in cosmological explanation: treat the pre-2006 nine-planet framework and the post-2006 dynamical-definition framework as competing paradigms, identify the discoveries in the Kuiper Belt—including bodies comparable in size to Pluto—as the anomaly or crisis that destabilized the older scheme, and examine whether the disagreement between astronomers who insisted on retaining Pluto's planetary status and those who endorsed the redefinition exhibits genuine taxonomic incommensurability.

More specifically, assess whether the two sides shared common standards for an adequate planetary classification or whether they appealed to incommensurable theoretical virtues—such as dynamical dominance versus historical continuity and pedagogical simplicity—to defend their respective taxonomies. Does this dispute reveal that paradigm choice in taxonomy, like in physics, is never fully determined by shared algorithmic rules? At the same time, distinguish carefully between the empirical discoveries and the normative decision to redraw boundaries, and indicate where the sociology of the debate fits Kuhn's narrative and where it breaks down, particularly given that astronomical taxonomy may lack the predictive and explanatory load that Kuhn attributed to physical paradigms. Finally, say what this episode ultimately demonstrates about the limits of applying incommensurability to definitional conventions: is it a confirming case, an anomalous extension, or evidence that Kuhn's framework was never intended to govern classification rather than explanation?
