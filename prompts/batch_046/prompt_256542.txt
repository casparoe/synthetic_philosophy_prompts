Since my father's recent diagnosis with a progressive neurodegenerative condition, I have become personally entangled in the ethics of proxy medical decision-making, and this has led me back to Bernard Williams's critique of impartialist moral theory—specifically his claim that requiring an agent to justify saving a spouse by appealing to a permitted exception introduces one thought too many, and his broader integrity objection that treating one's ground projects as mere preferences to be weighed against impartial demands alienates the agent from her own life. From this I have drawn what seems to me an unavoidable inference: if ground projects are genuinely identity-constituting, then any institutional practice—such as a hospital ethics committee demanding that a family proxy articulate generalizable, principled reasons for prioritizing a parent's welfare—structurally forces that proxy to stand at an alienating distance from her commitment, thereby violating personal integrity in exactly the way Williams describes. Given this, I want to ask how clinical ethics committees ought to redesign proxy-consent protocols: should they recognize non-justifiable, particularistic commitments as legitimate grounds for medical decisions, and if so, by what criteria can they distinguish an authentic ground project from an arbitrary preference without invoking the impartial reasoning that Williams argues corrupts the agent's relationship to her own commitments?
