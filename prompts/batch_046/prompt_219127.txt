As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue in a historical-fantasy setting, I am attempting to derive a consistent governing principle from Maimonides’ apparent verdicts on four related cases. I take him to have held that the sacrificial cult was permitted not because the divine nature requires blood, but as an accommodation to a people habituated to idolatrous worship; that prayer was instituted precisely to wean humanity away from corporeal sacrifice toward an interior service of the heart; that the detailed regulations governing animal slaughter serve a humanitarian purpose of minimizing suffering rather than sanctifying the act of killing; and that prophetic rebuke is directed at those who perform ritual while neglecting justice, which implies that ethical conduct possesses an independent value not reducible to ceremonial observance. Given this track record, what verdict would he reach—or should he reach for the sake of consistency—regarding a hypothetical religious reform, already adopted by one sect in my fictional world, in which worshippers execute the full sacrificial liturgy using engineered organisms that possess the appearance, vocalization, and hemorrhage of real animals but are neurologically incapable of experiencing pain, thereby preserving every sensory, symbolic, and communal element of the rite while eliminating harm to sentient creatures? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in the competing rules that might unify his four earlier rulings—one emphasizing psychological accommodation to concrete, costly ritual; one emphasizing symbolic substitution and the pedagogical necessity of blood; and one emphasizing the cultivation of communal solidarity through shared sacrificial action. For each candidate rule, state its prediction about the engineered-organism case, identify which rule best coheres with Maimonides’ prior verdicts and why, and then specify the further borderline case—perhaps a reform that retains the communal structure but replaces the ritual entirely with silent meditation, or one that employs living but fully anesthetized animals—that would most clearly distinguish the leading interpretations from one another.
