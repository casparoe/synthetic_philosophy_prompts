I am taking a seminar on philosophy of mind that has been working through cases of distributed and persisting selfhood, partly under the influence of Douglas Hofstadter's suggestion that an I is essentially a pattern that might, in principle, be instantiated in substrates other than the brain that originally hosted it. My professor has rendered verdicts on a sequence of test cases, and I am trying to determine whether she is operating with a single implicit criterion or whether her judgments are overdetermined.

Her rulings so far are these. First, in a split-brain patient whose hemispheres have lost all communicative pathways, she holds that there are now two distinct selves, not one self distributed across both hemispheres. Second, in the case of conjoined twins who share a thalamic bridge and report a genuinely unified first-person perspective that neither can isolate as exclusively mine, she judges that there is one self spanning both bodies. Third, if a person's brain is maintained alive in isolation but all of its outputs are routed into a network of other brains that collectively process its signals, she says the original self is extinguished and the network does not inherit it. Fourth, in a destructive teletransportation scenario—where psychological patterns are scanned, the original body destroyed, and a replica constructed elsewhere—she denies that the replica is the same I. Fifth, when a dying person's complete mental patterns are mapped and then used to guide a committee of close friends who collectively approximate that person's reasoning and values, she describes the result as a distributed echo but insists it is not literally the same self persisting across the group.

Next week we are examining a case that seems to sit between the conjoined-twin and the committee examples. Two healthy adults are linked by a direct, high-bandwidth neural interface that makes their autobiographical memories, emotional responses, and reasoning styles continuously cross-accessible in both directions—each can draw on the other's memories as if they were her own—while each retains a distinct bodily perspective and a private sensory stream that the other cannot fully share. Over months they begin to use plural pronouns for joint decisions, yet each maintains a singular first-person reference for immediate bodily actions. The interface is then gradually withdrawn; afterward, each retains fragments of the other's patterns but reports feeling like a partially blended individual rather than a pure return to her pre-linkage self. Predict my professor's verdict: would she say that during the linkage there was one distributed self across both brains, two selves that had merged and then partially separated, or merely an unusually deep intersubjective bond with no literal distribution of the I? Identify the rule or rules—whether a unity-of-consciousness condition, a historical-causal continuity requirement, a Hofstadter-style pattern-persistence view, or some hybrid—that best accommodate her earlier rulings, state what each predicts about this new case, and give your best guess with reasons. Then tell me which further case would most sharply distinguish the leading candidates, especially if you think her track record does not yet commit her to a unique principle.
