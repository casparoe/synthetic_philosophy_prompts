As a philosophy professor preparing a module on semantic paradoxes, I am trying to clarify for my students why the liar paradox remains alive despite the familiar rejoinder, "The liar only bites theories that try to say everything in one breath." In my seminar, this line is routinely invoked—sometimes by me—to dismiss objections that a given account of truth, whether correspondence, coherence, or deflationary, cannot survive unrestricted self-application. Before I circulate it again, I need its logical anatomy. Could you disambiguate the precise theses that slogan could be expressing? I would like a structured menu that distinguishes, at minimum: (i) the purely syntactic claim about object-language and meta-language restrictions; (ii) the metaphysical claim that truth is essentially hierarchical and non-circular; (iii) the pragmatic claim that self-referential assertions violate conditions on meaningful speech; and (iv) any other substantive reading that might be hiding behind the phrase. For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or theory-specific; whether it genuinely protects correspondence, coherence, and deflationary accounts alike; and which interpretation the interlocutor in my seminar is most likely intending when using the line to close debate. Present this as a disambiguation with verdicts rather than as a defense of a single thesis. Finally, state explicitly what would change your assessment—whether a successful formalization of a unified language with a consistent truth-predicate, a demonstration that pragmatic failure does not resolve semantic contradiction, or some other consideration—and identify the point in your reasoning where failure is most probable.
