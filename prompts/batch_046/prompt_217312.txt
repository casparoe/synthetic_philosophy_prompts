I am a high school student enrolled in an advanced interdisciplinary seminar that bridges philosophy of science with environmental history, and over the past month we have been examining the thesis that non-human forces—germs, climate shifts, volcanic eruptions—should be treated as genuine "historical actors" rather than as passive background conditions. Our instructor emphasized the perils of environmental determinism, yet I am still unsure where precisely the boundary lies between recognizing real causal power and reducing complex social outcomes to natural triggers. While discussing this with friends, one argued that attributing agency to a volcano is merely metaphorical and risks obscuring human institutional responsibility, while another insisted that without such framing we systematically underestimate the structural violence of ecological events. To move beyond this disagreement, I want to apply the framework to a well-known episode and see whether it actually explains anything that a conventional narrative misses.

Consider the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora and the subsequent 1816 "Year Without a Summer." The familiar causal sequence runs as follows: the eruption injected massive sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere, global mean temperatures dropped, harvests failed across large parts of Europe and North America, grain prices spiked and food riots followed, and the unusually cold, dark summer at Lake Geneva is traditionally linked to the composition of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein. Through the lens of non-human historical agency, please analyze this episode with discipline: what does treating Tambora as an actor genuinely illuminate about the pathway from geological event to social crisis and cultural production, and where does the framing misdescribe or overreach—for instance, by downplaying human choices regarding grain distribution, poor relief, and market regulation, or by falling into what I have tentatively called "retroactive agency assignment," the tendency to grant an event prominent causal standing only because dramatic, well-recorded downstream effects happened to follow it? Does this case strengthen the actor-framework, expose a hidden form of environmental determinism, or suggest that volcanic agency operates differently from biological or climatic agency? In your answer, please clearly separate what is settled among historians and philosophers—such as the basic climatological and agricultural chain—from what remains genuinely contested, particularly regarding whether non-human entities can possess agency in any robust philosophical sense, and do not present a disputed interpretation as though it were the consensus view.
