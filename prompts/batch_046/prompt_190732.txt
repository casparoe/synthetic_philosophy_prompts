ok so im trying to write up my philosophical profile on memory and forgetting for a workshop next week and want a consistency check before i present. here's roughly where ive landed:

1. i basically buy Margalit's ethics of memory: societies have a standing obligation to remember specific past injustices, mainly as recognition owed to victims, not just as a prudential lesson. i think this duty doesnt diminish with time, though its form can change.

2. but im also convinced by Rieff's argument that forced remembrance harms survivors and descendants, and that some collective traumas should be allowed to fade from public consciousness to enable reconciliation. i take this as a real moral counterweight, not just an exception.

3. i think state archives should be maximally inclusive and resistant to political erasure—selective deletion is dangerous and usually serves power.

4. at the same time i think individuals and groups have a kind of right to be forgotten: they shouldnt be permanently fixed in public identity by past victimhood, and commemorative practices shouldnt be mandatory for descendants.

5. my proposed resolution is that the state should maintain full records but practice what i call mnemonic opacity—the archives exist but are not continuously activated in public identity—while formal acknowledgment is periodic and non-performative, not anchored in group identity.

6. finally, i think cross-case analogies like Munich or Vietnam in policy debates are usually misleading, but i also think remembrance is partly valuable because it teaches general moral patterns about state violence and exclusion.

do these hang together? im especially worried that 5 doesnt actually resolve 1 and 2, and that 3 and 4 are in deeper conflict than they appear. can you go through the pairs, rank the real tensions by severity, flag which ones only look bad but dissolve on inspection, and tell me the least costly single revision? please be specific about which commitment has to give if they cant all stay.
