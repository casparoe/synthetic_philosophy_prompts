Exam question: Explain Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the impossibility of true moral responsibility, and evaluate whether hierarchical compatibilist accounts—such as Frankfurt-style identification with second-order volitions—successfully answer it.

Student answer:
Strawson argues that true moral responsibility requires being causa sui, a self-creating origin of one's mental nature. If an action flows from an agent's mental states, and those states flow from prior ones, then responsibility for the action requires responsibility for the prior states, generating an infinite regress. Even indeterminism fails to solve this, because an agent does not choose her initial psychological constitution. Therefore no one is ultimately responsible. Strawson's reactive-attitudes framework then shows that ordinary practices of blame and resentment do not actually depend on this metaphysical condition; they operate as interpersonal social responses regardless of whether anyone is self-originating.

Hierarchical compatibilists reply that responsibility requires only proper internal structure, not self-creation from nothing. By identifying with second-order desires, an agent integrates her will. Crucially, the highest-order volitions are self-creating: they are not caused by any prior mental states, but are genuinely authored by the agent herself. This breaks the causal chain at the top, giving the agent a limited form of causa sui with respect to her will. Since the regress is blocked at this highest level, Strawson's argument does not apply, and hierarchical compatibilism successfully preserves moral responsibility.
