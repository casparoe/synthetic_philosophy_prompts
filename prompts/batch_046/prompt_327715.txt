I'm delivering the opening address at the annual gala of the Nordisk-American Leadership Foundation, an event that honors three entrepreneurs who built organizations from minimal capital: one in decentralized renewable-energy infrastructure, one in language-access education technology for recent immigrants, and one in cooperative grocery distribution. The audience is deliberately mixed. Roughly half are board members of Scandinavian descent for whom Jantelagen remains an active moral reflex—success should be quiet, collective, and never individually claimed—while the other half are American venture partners who treat the American Dream as a doctrine that requires loud, personal celebration of merit, and who regard cutting down the successful as a cultural pathology that kills ambition. A smaller but vocal group of cultural economists is also present, studying what Australians and New Zealanders call the tall poppy syndrome, and they are skeptical of both the American and Scandinavian narratives, viewing each as an ideology that conceals its own mechanism of social control. The philosophical problem must do real structural work in the speech: I want to argue that the difficulty is not simply whether to celebrate these three people, but whether any culture can construct a form of recognition that is genuinely deserved without being extractive—one that does not treat the honored individuals as resources for collective self-flattery, nor as targets for collective resentment. Because of the board's egalitarian commitments, I cannot deliver a conventional American meritocratic encomium; because of the partners' presence and the actual achievements being recognized, I cannot deliver a Scandinavian-style apology for success. The address needs to navigate this tension explicitly, using the specific work of the three honorees—the energy grid's collective ownership model, the education platform's refusal to monetize student data, the cooperative's profit-sharing architecture—as concrete illustrations of how success might be structured so that it does not violate the visibility contract implicit in Jantelagen, without collapsing into the American fantasy of the self-made individual. It should be precise enough that the economists recognize the conceptual argument about non-extractive recognition, but accessible enough that family members who are not philosophers understand why the awards matter and why the form of praise being offered is not the usual kind. Please write a fifteen-minute address that makes the tension between celebrating and cutting down the successful the backbone of the rhetoric, not its decoration.
