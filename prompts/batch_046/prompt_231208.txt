I teach an upper-level philosophy seminar on artificial minds and moral status — 12 graduate students, one 90-minute session. I want a structured activity, not a lecture, that makes participants feel the instability of attributing moral patienthood to computations. The core tension should involve graded, substrate-independent functional criteria: entities that are individually below any plausible sentience threshold can aggregate into systems that behave like unified minds, while apparently unified systems can decompose into sub-processes that are individually trivial. Design a role-play or staged deliberation in which students act as members of an oversight board allocating scarce protection slots among synthetic entities described by specific computational architectures, not just observed behavior. The rules should force real trade-offs — protecting more entities reduces the quality of protection per entity, and information revealed in stages should show that some protected entities are actually decomposed swarms of sub-critical loops while some rejected ones contain nested, temporally integrated architectures. Use a non-monetary payoff scheme that requires students to defend allocations to the group and revise them under time pressure. Include a timeline with roles or rules, the staged reveals, materials needed, a debrief that maps each decision point onto the distinction between graded attribution, nested computation, and aggregation, and an explicit prediction of what will go wrong on the first run — especially the tendency to fixate on behavioral coherence rather than internal architectural integration. Be frank about which feature of the boundary problem, such as whether moral status can be genuinely discontinuous rather than continuously graded, the activity cannot reproduce and I will need to supply in discussion. Keep the framing concrete and avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
