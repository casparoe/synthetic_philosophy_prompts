I'm taking a course on ancient biography and moral philosphy and I've been trying to work out whether my own views on reading lives actually hang together, or if I'm just smuggling in contradictions without noticing. So here's my profile, more or less as I'd tell it to a friend:

I think the main point of biography — especially in the Plutarch tradition — is moral self-improvement, using other people's lives as mirrors rather than just historical reports. Because of that, I tend to think it's okay if Plutarch gets some details wrong or smooths chronology; if the moral shape is right, a factual slip doesn't really damage the work. But I also think that for someone to count as a moral exemplar, they actually need to have done the things they're praised for; if the courageous act never happened, you're not emulating a real person but imitating fiction, and that seems to change the nature of the learning in a way that matters. I reject hagiography outright because stripping away all flaws makes the subject either unrelatable or impossibly perfect, both of which block real emulation. Still, I think some selective framing is inevitable — you have to choose which episodes to highlight — and that's narrative craft, not hagiography, so it's not the same problem. I think Plutarch pairs Greek and Roman lives to show that virtues like justice or courage are genuinely trans-cultural and recognisable across societies. At the same time, I don't believe virtues are universal in any strong sense; I think they're shaped by specific social practices, so you can't just lift a Roman idea of pietas into modern life without serious distortion. I learn best from people I can partly identify with, which means I want biographies to show doubts, failures, and moral ambiguity. But I also feel that if a biography dwells too long on failures, it stops being inspiring and just becomes another sad story, which seems to defeat the purpose entirely.

There's probably an apparent tension in there that dissolves once you look closely — point that out too, so I'm not fixing something that isn't broken. And if my whole framework rests on a confused premise — like if I'm asking for coherence where the concepts don't even apply — just say so plainly rather than trying to patch it up politely.
