I am preparing a graduate seminar on Confucian moral philosophy and have been reflecting on a cluster of case judgements I find myself making about role-based obligations—specifically, about when fidelity to a defined social role demands rigidity and when it permits, or even requires, departure from formal expectation. I should like to know whether there is a single coherent principle, perhaps drawing on the interplay of li (ritual propriety), yi (rightness), and ren (humaneness), that can accommodate all of the following verdicts, or whether theoretical elegance requires me to abandon one.

Consider these cases. First, a father refuses to intervene financially to rescue his adult son from bankruptcy caused by recklessness, arguing that genuine paternal care (ci) now requires allowing the son to bear the consequences and develop autonomous moral responsibility; I judge this a dereliction of the father role, which demands active, context-sensitive support rather than withdrawal disguised as respect for autonomy. Second, a senior physician in a multicultural hospital refuses to disclose a terminal diagnosis to the patient's extended family, insisting that the physician-patient dyad alone defines her obligations regardless of familial expectations about collective decision-making; here I judge her correct—the role is constituted by the specific relational contract, not by broader communal norms. Third, a teacher awards inflated marks to a student from her own hometown network, reasoning that the teacher-student relation is embedded within wider ties of loyalty (zhong); I judge this corrupt—the role of teacher requires impartial evaluation governed by li, and allowing external loyalty to distort assessment dissolves the role's integrity. Fourth, a daughter declines to visit her dying, estranged father who abused her throughout childhood, despite intense social pressure to perform filial piety (xiao); I think she is not failing her role—filiality is a reciprocal, affective bond rather than unconditional obedience, though minimal ritual duties might still be owed. Fifth, a civil servant enforces a uniform administrative rule that severely harms a vulnerable minority group, defending her action by appeal to the ruler-subject role's demand for consistent, impartial application of law; I judge her wrong—the official must exercise flexible, humane judgement (ren) rather than mechanical rule-following, even at the cost of procedural uniformity.

These verdicts pull in different directions: in the medical case I endorse strict adherence to the core relational boundary, whereas in the bureaucratic case I reject rigid adherence to formal rules in favour of contextual flexibility. Is there an account—perhaps one that distinguishes role-constitutive actions from role-expressive ones, or one that applies shu (reciprocal analogy) differently across asymmetrical relationships—that delivers all five judgements? If not, which verdict should be overturned, and what does the repaired principle predict for a case I have not listed—say, an AI system assigned the functional role of 'family mediator' but lacking any shared history or emotional bond with the parties? Please present your answer in British English, stating the principle precisely, indicating which clause addresses which case, and being explicit about which of my intuitions the principle cannot preserve.
