I've been trying to draft an essay for my poli sci seminar about the boundary problem—y'know, who actually belongs to the demos that gets to vote on stuff? Most people talk about the 'all affected interests' principle vs citizenship borders, but I keep coming back to this weird scenario: imagine a regional government votes to build a huge desalination plant that solves water shortages inside the city, but it drains the aquifer that three farming villages outside the border depend on. The villagers aren't citizens, but they're not exactly 'global strangers' either.

So in my essay I'm playing with this idea I made up called 'gradient membership'—basically your formal inclusion in the demos isn't binary yes/no, but scales with the intensity and directness of the material interdependance. The closer your livelihood is tied to the decision, the stronger your democratic claim, but it fades with distance and mediation. I sketched out some rough arguments: first, that pure citizenship boundaries fail here because they treat the border as morally arbitrary; second, that 'all affected' is too broad because then every climate decision includes everyone on earth; and third, that gradient membership captures the intuition that the farmers should count more than some random person in another continent, but less than actual residents. I also noted a problem—how do you measure 'material interdependence' without just smuggling geography back in?

Anyway, these are just notes, not a polished draft. Could you read them over and tell me if the 'gradient membership' idea is coherent or just a confused middle ground? I'm especially curious whether you think it actually solves anything, or if I'm just renaming the problem.
