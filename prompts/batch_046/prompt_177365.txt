I am preparing material for an advanced seminar on the foundations of value theory, and I find that the standard framing of the heterogeneity problem for hedonism tends to obscure a more interesting structural issue. The familiar objection asks what all pleasures—gustatory, intellectual, aesthetic, and even the peculiar satisfaction of masochistic or relief-type experiences—have in common, given that they share no obvious phenomenal quality and vary wildly in their intentional objects. Rather than rehearsing the usual philosophical moves—such as positing a uniform feeling-tone, appealing to a Wittgensteinian family resemblance, or retreating to a purely dispositional analysis—I want you to construct and defend a specific, somewhat unconventional position: that pleasures are unified not by any intrinsic qualitative content but by their functional relation to what I will call the agent's dynamic hedonic baseline. By this I mean the context-sensitive reference point, internal to the agent's current cognitive-evaluative architecture, against which an experience registers as a welfare-relevant positive deviation. Argue that an experience counts as a pleasure precisely when it operates as a local maximum relative to this baseline, regardless of its sensory modality, its intentional object, or even its surface-level affective tone. Show how this baseline-relative functionalism dissolves the heterogeneity problem without requiring phenomenal homogeneity, and explain in particular why it does not collapse masochistic pleasures or the pleasure of relief into pains despite their dependence on prior suffering.

Then address the circularity objection that naturally arises: if identifying the baseline already requires prior judgments about welfare or suffering, then defining pleasure through baseline-relative maximization appears to presuppose the very concept it aims to analyze. Defend the claim that this circularity is benign rather than vicious—perhaps on the grounds that the baseline is fixed by sub-personal predictive-processing mechanisms rather than by explicit evaluative judgments—so that the account remains genuinely explanatory. Throughout, avoid relying on bibliographic references or citation-heavy appeals to named scholars; the task is to work out the conceptual architecture from first principles, with particular attention to whether the baseline can be specified in non-circular, operational terms that apply equally to ordinary sensory pleasures and to the more anomalous cases that motivate the heterogeneity worry in the first place.
