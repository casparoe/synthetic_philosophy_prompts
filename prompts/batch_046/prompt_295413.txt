As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind, I have become fixated on disjunctivism and the argument from illusion, but I think the standard debate misses a structural point. Please write an essay examining whether disjunctivism can survive what I will call the "causal-parity objection": in both a veridical seeing of a red cube and a subjectively matching hallucination, the proximal causal chain—from stimulation (or its absence) through the retina and visual cortex—is physically identical except for the presence or absence of the distal cube itself. The essay should weigh whether this proximal identity forces us to posit a shared mental kind, or whether the disjunctivist can consistently claim that the object's actual presence transforms the mental state into something fundamentally different without violating causal closure of the physical brain. Reach and justify a specific conclusion. Keep the discussion conceptual and avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to particular authors.
