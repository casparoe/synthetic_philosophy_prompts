I am a doctoral student in philosophy of physics, and during a recent workshop on statistical mechanics a senior colleague brushed aside my concern about Loschmidt reversibility by saying that "entropy only ever increases, so time can only ever go forward". I am convinced that this line is doing several jobs at once, and I should like to have them separated with some care. Could you please set out the different precise theses that this slogan might be advancing — distinguishing, for example, a definitional claim that identifies temporal direction with the gradient of macroscopic disorder, an empirical claim about the behaviour of large-scale systems under typical Hamiltonian dynamics, an epistemic claim about why our records and memories align with one orientation rather than another, and a metaphysical claim about whether temporal relations possess an intrinsic asymmetry that is merely revealed rather than constituted by entropy change — and for each reading indicate whether it is trivial, whether it remains defensible once we allow for locally reversed subsystems within a globally entropy-increasing universe, whether it genuinely answers the reversibility objection or merely restates the boundary conditions embodied in the past hypothesis, and whether it smuggles in contingent assumptions about what counts as an appropriate system for entropy attribution? I should be grateful if you presented your reply as uninterrupted continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, in the manner of an extended letter.
