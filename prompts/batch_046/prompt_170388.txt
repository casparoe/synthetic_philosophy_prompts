I cover philosophy of technology and cognitive science for a publication aimed at intellectually curious general readers, and I have been invited to defend a working paper at an interdisciplinary symposium on algorithmic culture and moral psychology. The thesis I am presenting applies Sartre’s analysis of radical freedom and bad faith to the phenomenology of recommendation systems: I argue that when users treat an algorithm’s predictive profile as the authoritative origin of their own preferences—treating the curated feed as if it reveals a fixed nature of taste that precedes their choice—they are not merely outsourcing judgment but practicing a technologically mediated form of bad faith, disowning their transcendence by reifying statistical facticity. The audience will be divided between analytic philosophers of mind who work within compatibilist and causal frameworks, phenomenologists who resist extending existentialist categories to non-conscious infrastructures, and two engineers from a recommender-systems laboratory who are likely to challenge the empirical premises about user behavior. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight objections I will actually face in this room, ordered from the most damaging to my credibility with these specific interlocutors down to the merely irritating, each phrased in the idiom its speaker would naturally use—analytic, phenomenological, or engineering—rather than in existentialist jargon. For each objection, provide a concise reply I could deliver in approximately two minutes without notes, followed by a candid note on where that reply is weakest or concedes too much. Include the single objection I am most hoping no one raises, clearly marked. To help you calibrate the targets, my argument runs as follows: first, Sartrean freedom is not the liberty to select among pre-given options but the ontological structure of consciousness as being-for-itself, which necessarily transcends any given facticity; second, recommendation algorithms present users with an apparent nature of taste—a statistical profile—that can either be acknowledged as transcended through an active choice to accept or reject it, or treated as deterministic, which constitutes bad faith; third, the temporal design of infinite-scroll interfaces collapses the horizon of deliberation, encouraging users to experience the profile as an immediate given rather than as a mediated construction that they have freely endorsed. I do not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citation guides; I need strategic, audience-specific rhetoric that respects the commitments of each faction present.
