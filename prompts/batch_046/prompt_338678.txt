Compare the Quine-Duhem thesis of global underdetermination with its localized analogue in mechanistic anomaly detection: the case in which two distinct internal causal architectures produce identical distributions over observed anomalies yet diverge in their counterfactual responses to interventions outside the observed failure regime. First, explain why the standard Quinean remedy—appeal to pragmatic virtues such as simplicity and explanatory coherence—encounters a different obstacle when the competing explanations are rival mechanisms embedded within a single engineered system rather than globally incompatible theories. Second, introduce and evaluate a proposed resolution criterion, interventional distinguishability, which holds that mechanistic identity ought to be fixed not by observed anomaly patterns alone but by divergence under at least one physically realizable perturbation that lies within the system's design space yet outside its training distribution. Assess whether this criterion genuinely dissolves the local underdetermination or merely relocates it to the prior problem of delimiting admissible perturbations. Conclude by identifying which structural feature—global versus local scope, or the differential availability of pragmatic arbitration—performs the heavier philosophical labor in separating the two cases.
