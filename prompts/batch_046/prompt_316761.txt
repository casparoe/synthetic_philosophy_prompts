As a journalist covering decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice, I have been tracking my own intuitive verdicts across a family of predictive-choice puzzles that sit at the boundary of causal and evidential reasoning, and I want to know whether there is a single principle—perhaps one that does not collapse into standard expected-utility maximization—that captures the pattern, or whether I must abandon some of my judgments.

Here are my verdicts, case by case.

First, the baseline predictive donation: a philanthropic algorithm with near-perfect accuracy predicts whether I will contribute $200 to an environmental trust. If it predicts I will contribute, it donates $2 million to the same trust regardless of my actual behavior; if it predicts I will not, it donates nothing. I judge that I ought to contribute, even though my contribution does not causally influence the algorithm's prior decision.

Second, the penalized variant: the same algorithmic structure applies, but now I know that if I actually make the $200 contribution, an independent bureaucratic mechanism automatically levies a $180,000 fine on me. The $2 million donation is already fixed by the prediction alone. Here my judgment shifts: I think I should withhold the contribution and accept whatever the algorithm has already decided, because the actual act now carries a severe, causally independent penalty.

Third, a symmetric blackmail structure—call it the XOR contract: an adversary runs a simulation of my decision process and sends me a sealed message stating that if the simulation predicts I will pay them $1,000, then in reality they will not ask for payment but will publish damaging material about me; if the simulation predicts I will not pay, then in reality they will demand $1,000, and refusal will trigger publication. I judge that I should not pay the $1,000, even though paying might seem to align with the predicted branch.

Fourth, the transparent commitment: before any prediction is run, I can publicly bind myself to a rule—specifically, that I will never pay blackmail regardless of predictive evidence. The adversary knows this commitment with certainty, then runs the simulation and presents the XOR contract exactly as in the third case. Here I feel my judgment should not change: I should still refuse to pay. But this sits uneasily with my first verdict, where I allowed my actual choice to align with the prediction despite causal independence.

Fifth, the nested belief case—this is the one I keep returning to. A predictor forecasts not my action but whether I will form the belief that aligning with the prediction is rational. If it predicts I will form that belief, it deposits $1 million in an escrow account; if not, nothing is deposited. However, if I actually form and act on that belief, I suffer a reputational and professional cost of approximately $900,000. I genuinely do not know whether I should cultivate the belief.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps drawing on evidential decision theory, causal decision theory, or a disciplined hybrid—that accommodates all five verdicts? If not, which verdict must be overturned, and what does the corrected principle predict for the nested belief case? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, offer its verdict on at least one further case I have not listed, and be explicit about which of my judgments it cannot save.
