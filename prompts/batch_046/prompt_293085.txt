Exam question: John Stuart Mill's writings on socialism from the 1870s—published after his death and often grouped under the heading of his socialist essays—are frequently interpreted as an extension rather than an abandonment of his earlier liberal commitments. Focusing specifically on his distinction between voluntary cooperative association and state-imposed collective ownership, explain how Mill attempts to reconcile socialist economic organization with the principle of liberty defended in On Liberty. Student answer: Mill's late essays on socialism—composed in the 1870s and issued posthumously—recast socialism as voluntary association rather than expropriation. He distinguishes cooperative production, where laborers collectively manage capital, from state socialism, which imposes collective ownership by bureaucratic decree. This distinction parallels the argument in On Liberty that custom should not suppress individual choice; just as Mill defends personal eccentricity against social conformity, he defends economic experimentation against the customary dominance of wage labor.

Crucially, Mill's framework implies distributive neutrality: the state should embed no structural advantages for either capitalist or cooperative organization. From this principle, one can infer that inheritance taxation—which Mill elsewhere supports—would violate neutrality if applied specifically to finance cooperative transitions, since it selectively burdens one property form to advantage another. Rather, Mill requires that shifts toward cooperation occur through purely voluntary contracts, with government serving only as an impartial referee.

Moreover, Mill's concept of the stationary state—the prediction that material progress will eventually plateau—reinforces his socialist sympathies, because cooperative structures are naturally suited to a post-growth economy in which the drive for accumulation has subsided. Once growth ceases to be the primary social aim, distributive neutrality allows cooperation to emerge as the rational preference of free choosers.

Thus Mill reconciles socialism with liberty by treating economic organization as an extension of personal choice, protected from both state imposition and the inertia of custom, with distributive neutrality ensuring that no production mode receives artificial support.
