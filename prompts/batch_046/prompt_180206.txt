I cover the intersection of science, wellness, and public philosophy for a general-interest publication, and I am increasingly pitched studies, products, and institutional programs that claim to deliver, measure, or enhance the "meaning of life." I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, explicit stopping rules, and clearly flagged judgment points—that I can run in about fifteen minutes before writing about any such claim, grounded in Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life." The procedure should operationalize his core distinctions: the rejection of metaphysical or transcendent meanings; the contrast between activity as play (Spiel)—autotelic, intrinsically absorbing, carrying what Schlick associates with the attitude of youth—versus activity as work (Arbeit)—instrumental, directed at an external end, having crossed a "threshold of seriousness"; and his view that the very posing of the meaning-question is often a symptom of weariness rather than a legitimate philosophical inquiry.

Design the procedure so a journalist without philosophical training can apply it repeatedly. Specify, in order: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, where the procedure stops with a clear "no" or "category error," and where it must halt and hand the decision back to me because the issue is one of values rather than conceptual coherence. The procedure must survive hard modern cases: gamified interventions that overlay points, leaderboards, and biometric tracking onto formerly playful activities; mandatory "purpose" curricula that frame reflection as career productivity; and empirical studies that conflate survey-measured "purpose" with health biomarkers.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:
1. A longevity clinic marketing a "Purpose Protocol" in which clients track daily "meaning metrics," compete on dashboards, and earn discounts for consistency, citing studies that link purpose scores to telomere length.
2. A public museum's unpaid adult "Play Lab" in which participants build open-ended physics demonstrations with no prescribed curriculum, but must complete a two-hour safety orientation and sign liability waivers.
3. A university's required first-year seminar in which students write graded reflective essays identifying their "life purpose" and mapping it onto career pathways.

For each case, show what the procedure yields, highlight any conclusion that conflicts with the marketing or institutional framing, and state precisely what the procedure cannot settle—especially where empirical health claims and philosophical coherence claims are being illicitly merged.

Keep the deliverable practical and self-contained: no bibliographies, no requests for citations, no references to texts or theories outside Schlick's distinction, and an honest accounting of where the distinction breaks down under contemporary conditions.
