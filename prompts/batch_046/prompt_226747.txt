A recent essay on inter-subjective persistence conditions summarised Douglas Hofstadter's position as follows: the self is not a substance but a high-level pattern of symbolic loops; because these loops are defined by their functional relations rather than by their physical substrate, they can in principle be distributed across multiple brains that maintain the right causal connections. The essay claims that Hofstadter therefore holds a form of "mereological essentialism" for these loops—if a sufficient proportion of one's symbolic patterns are causally active in another person's brain, that other brain literally constitutes a part of one's extended self, not merely a copy. It cites split-brain patients as evidence that one body can host two distinct loop-structures, and concludes that this proves the converse: one loop-structure can span two bodies. From this, the essay derives that grief is conceptually confused, since the deceased's loops continue running in survivors, and that solipsism is impossible because every self is already partly constituted by others.

I want to know whether this is a fair reading. Which claims capture Hofstadter's actual arguments, which are subtle misrepresentations—particularly the move from substrate-independence to literal bodily extension, the use of split-brain evidence, and the "mereological essentialism" framing—and does the conclusion about grief actually follow from the corrected view?
