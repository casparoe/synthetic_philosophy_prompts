I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to understand historical materialism—specifically the claim that the economic base, understood as the organization of production and ownership of the means of production, determines or explains the superstructure of law, politics, culture, and ideology. The classic illustrations involve factories and agricultural land, which feel remote from my daily experience. In my field, labor produces immaterial goods that can be copied at near-zero cost, the physical infrastructure is usually rented rather than owned (cloud compute), and a significant portion of the tools we rely on are maintained through open-source collaboration that does not map cleanly onto wage labor or private ownership.

If one takes the base-superstructure distinction seriously and applies it rigorously to this kind of computational, post-industrial work, what are the most important implications for the stronger thesis that the economic base explains everything else? In particular, does the model require us to treat technical architecture—software licenses, network protocols, algorithmic governance mechanisms—as merely superstructural reflections of underlying economic relations such as platform capitalism and venture funding? Or does recognizing these technical structures as part of the material conditions of production actually split the 'base' in a way that weakens the determinism that makes historical materialism distinctive? I am asking not for a historical survey but for the conceptual implications: how should we understand the relationship between economic relations and technical infrastructure if we want to preserve, revise, or reject the claim that the base explains the rest?
