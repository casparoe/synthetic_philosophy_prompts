I sit on an advisory panel—convened by a university centre for comparative constitutional law—that reviews proposed state-religion arrangements for consistency with secular neutrality. We reason solely from our own track record and do not invoke bibliographies or external case citations. Our rulings to date are these. First, we approved a voluntary tax-designation scheme in which the state collects payments for registered religious communities and for secular philosophical organisations registered under an identical statute, with administratively simple exit and no state preference. Second, we permitted public funding for the structural repair of a fourteenth-century cathedral still used as a parish, but only on conditions guaranteeing free secular public access and excluding any expenditure on liturgical furnishings or clergy. Third, we struck down a statute requiring all state-school pupils to attend a non-confessional ethics course taught exclusively by clergy of the historically dominant church. Fourth, we rejected a parliamentary resolution to display a modified national emblem bearing a religious symbol, ruling that it constituted symbolic endorsement irrespective of historical tradition. Fifth, we approved a state-administered payroll-deduction facility for charitable donations open to registered religious bodies and secular humanist associations on precisely the same terms.

A new proposal has reached us. A regional government, facing a shortage of public buildings, wishes to mandate a civic-integration course for all naturalisation applicants. The curriculum—covering constitutional history, anti-discrimination norms, and administrative literacy—has been designed and approved by a secular education authority; it contains no prayer, worship, or doctrinal instruction, and examination is conducted by state officials. However, because of space shortages, every session will be held in premises owned by religious communities, selected by open tender with state rental payments at market rate. Moreover, although all instructors must hold state teaching certification, half the recruitment pool is drawn from networks maintained by the four largest religious communities and two secular humanist associations, on the ground that these organisations possess the linguistic and cultural reach to engage hard-to-reach applicants.

Predict our verdict, or recommend the decision that best coheres with our five precedents. Identify at least two plausible governing rules—such as cooperationist institutional neutrality, strict separation with a heritage exception, or asymmetric laïcité—that render our earlier rulings jointly intelligible, and show precisely how they diverge on this proposal. State which of our five rulings is the closest analog, and be explicit about the principle we would be adopting whichever way we decide. Finally, describe one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the rules you have identified.

Please answer in British English, with clear paragraphing, and without recourse to reading lists or citations.
