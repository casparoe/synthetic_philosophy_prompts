If heterophenomenology instructs us to treat a subject's first-person report as a text describing a heterophenomenological world whose ontology we bracket for scientific purposes, does this not entail that we must also bracket the distinction between a report that correctly tracks an experience and one that confabulates it? After all, both the report of a red afterimage and the report of a real red object are equally elements of the constructed world; if we refuse ontological commitment to that world, we seem to lack any internal criterion for saying that one is false about consciousness while the other is true, leaving us only with third-person behavioural or neural correlates to adjudicate the difference. But this suggests that heterophenomenology collapses into a form of behaviourism that denies the very richness of subjective report it promised to preserve. Is the error here not in assuming that ontological neutrality about the heterophenomenological world requires epistemic neutrality about its internal coherence — and might the method actually rely, perhaps through Dennett's notion of a narrative centre of gravity, on a hidden distinction between the mechanism that generates the report and the report itself, thereby smuggling back a kind of privileged access through interpretation rather than introspection?
