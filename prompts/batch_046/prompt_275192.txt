Here is an argument I reconstructed from Adam Smith's critique of empire and now worry proves too much. (1) For Smith, a state's political jurisdiction over a territory is justified only if that territory's economic contribution exceeds the combined fiscal and military cost of retaining it. (2) By Smith's own accounting, the American colonies consistently failed this test: imperial defense and administration consumed more value than mercantile exclusivity returned. (3) Therefore Britain ought to grant them independence. (4) The same cost-benefit ledger applies within a state: if a domestic region—say, the Scottish Highlands or a remote English county—costs more to govern, police, and defend than it contributes in revenue and trade, then retaining it fails the identical justification. (5) Hence Smith's principle, applied consistently, licenses internal secession whenever a region becomes a net fiscal burden, and perhaps requires dissolution into smaller units whenever division of labor makes them self-sufficient. (6) Yet Smith was a firm defender of the 1707 Union and of an integrated British market; he never endorsed domestic fragmentation on economic grounds. (7) So either his anti-colonial argument is unsound, or it smuggles in an unstated qualifier that saves domestic union but looks ad hoc. I would most like to preserve (1) and (3). Where does the reasoning slip—between (3) and (4), inside (1)'s hidden conditions, or elsewhere? What does giving up that step cost his broader account of empire and colonies?
