In our philosophy seminar last week, two classmates—Maya and Jordan—got into a sustained disagreement about vagueness in the concept of genuinely understanding a proof. They were examining a specific case from our study group: we had collectively worked through a lengthy argument in modal logic, and while every member could reproduce the formal steps, two members disagreed over whether a particular inference involving nested modal operators had been applied correctly. Maya defended an epistemicist position. She argued that there is an objective, sharp boundary between understanding and not understanding the proof, fixed by whether one has correctly grasped the logical form of that inference, but that our epistemic limitations often prevent us from knowing exactly where that boundary falls. She insisted that treating the cutoff as unknown but real respects the seriousness of logical knowledge; otherwise, she said, we collapse understanding into mere social agreement and lose the normative force of saying someone has made a mistake. Jordan defended supervaluationism. He maintained that “understanding this proof” lacks sharp boundaries altogether, and that different admissible ways of precisifying what counts as adequate grasp of the inference rule are equally legitimate. Consequently, he said, the claim that a particular student understands it can be true on some precisifications and false on others, without there being a further metaphysical fact that makes one precisification correct. He argued that this view avoids pretending to a precision that logic itself does not supply.

Maya then raised a pedagogical objection: if there is no fact of the matter about who understands the proof, then grading, correcting errors, or guiding study becomes arbitrary. Jordan replied that practical assessment can still proceed by stipulating a precisification for pedagogical purposes, without claiming that the stipulated boundary tracks a metaphysically privileged line. Next, Maya argued that epistemicism best explains our persistent intuition that “there must be a right answer” even in hard cases, whereas Jordan’s view must treat that intuition as a kind of confusion. Jordan countered that the intuition is better explained as a pragmatic pressure to coordinate on one precisification for collective action, not as evidence of an unknown sharp boundary hidden in reality. Finally, they considered whether their dispute changes if understanding is distributed across the study group rather than located in individual minds. Maya suggested that collective cognition might blur the boundary even further, but she maintained that epistemicism could still hold that there is a precise fact about whether the group, taken as a unit, has achieved understanding. Jordan suggested that distributed cognition actually strengthens the supervaluationist case, since different allocations of cognitive labor yield different admissible precisifications of what “the group understands” means.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not decide. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: who is right about the nature of vagueness in this specific case, about the pedagogical objection, about the best explanation of our intuition that there is a right answer, and about whether epistemicism or supervaluationism does better overall? In addition, please identify what both sides are missing, particularly with respect to how collective or distributed understanding might alter or complicate the standard individual-level framing of the dispute.
