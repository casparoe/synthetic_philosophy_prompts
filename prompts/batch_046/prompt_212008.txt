Since being appointed to a committee that exercises significant discretionary authority over allocations with minimal external audit, I have found myself returning repeatedly to Glaucon’s challenge in Plato’s Republic: the ring of Gyges, which renders its wearer invisible and thereby removes every conventional sanction. In discussions with colleagues—some ethicists, some not—the argument over whether modern moral philosophy has ever adequately answered this challenge has reached a frustrating impasse. On one side stand those who take the ring to expose a fundamental vulnerability in moral realism: if justice is only rational when observed, then invisibility collapses its normative force. On another side stand those who reply, broadly in Plato’s spirit, that justice remains intrinsically beneficial to the soul’s order regardless of external recognition, so the ring tests nothing but our confusion about where value resides. A third group insists that both sides miss the point, because the ring does not merely hide the agent but severs the feedback loop through which moral properties are socially constituted; for them, the challenge concerns not motivation but whether moral facts depend for their existence on being witnessed.

What strikes me is that all three positions agree on the thought experiment’s mechanics—complete invisibility, immunity to punishment, capacity to take what one wants—yet after months of discussion nobody has shifted. Is this disagreement substantive, or is it verbal, with the term justice doing different work in each camp—normative justification in one, psychological harmony in another, and social ontology in the third? Or is there a hidden empirical claim about human motivational architecture that neither side has made explicit, perhaps about whether intrinsic motivation can survive the absence of any external reinforcement? I am also struck by a distinction that rarely surfaces in these conversations: the ring removes the visibility of the agent, but it might also be interpreted as removing the visibility of the moral properties of the act itself, so that the wearer no longer perceives his own actions as falling under moral categories at all. If that interpretation is available, then the debate may be stalling because one side addresses agent-invisibility and the other addresses property-invisibility without realising they are answering different questions under one heading.

Given your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is—whether about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for—what would actually count as progress? Would it require a formal model of moral motivation under conditions of radical informational asymmetry? A conceptual distinction between the publicity conditions for moral agency and those for moral knowledge? Or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed because it asks us to evaluate justice from a standpoint that, by construction, removes the conditions under which evaluation operates?
