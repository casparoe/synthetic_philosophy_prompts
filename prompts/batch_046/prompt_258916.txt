I've been trying to work out my own view on how much citizens ought to conceal from the state, comparing three regimes I keep returning to: the German constitutional tradition of informationelle Selbstbestimmung, which treats personal data as an extension of personality rights; the American open-government tradition, which treats state secrecy as the greater danger and demands institutional transparency; and the Chinese social-credit model, which treats continuous citizen visibility as a mechanism for collective trust and state coordination. I have concrete verdicts that feel right, but I cannot tell whether they rest on one principle or on several that happen to point in similar directions.

Here are my judgments. First, I think a democratic state should be forbidden from collecting citizens' political opinions or religious affiliations for administrative convenience, even if the data is never misused—this is the protection I associate with strict data-protection doctrine. Second, I think the same state should be compelled to disclose its own decision-making—budget allocations, surveillance authorizations, police disciplinary records—through open-records mechanisms; transparency ought to flow upward, not downward. Third, I think the social-credit system is wrong not merely because it is authoritarian, but because it renders citizens continuously legible to the state while leaving the state's scoring algorithms and criteria opaque. Fourth, I also think citizens should sometimes be required to reveal information about themselves: campaign-finance disclosure, census responses for democratic apportionment, and tax reporting all seem legitimate obligations. Fifth, yet when a whistleblower leaks evidence of illegal state surveillance and the state demands the whistleblower's identity and sources, I think concealment is morally protected. Sixth, however, if a citizen in the same democratic society refuses to disclose income for tax assessment, invoking the same informational self-determination, I think that claim fails.

These verdicts seem to point toward some principle about directional transparency—who must be visible to whom, and for what purpose—but they also contain tensions. I protect political opinion from state collection but demand financial disclosure for democratic representation; I defend whistleblower anonymity but reject tax anonymity. Is there a single principle—perhaps about asymmetry of power, purpose limitation, or democratic reciprocity—that captures all six verdicts? Or do I need two principles, one governing citizen-to-state obligations and another governing state-to-citizen obligations? And if I had to overturn one verdict to preserve a simpler account, which one should go? Please state the principle or principles precisely, show which case motivates each clause, predict what they say about a case I have not mentioned—specifically, whether citizens should be permitted to use end-to-end encryption that state security agencies cannot technically penetrate—and be honest about which of my judgments they cannot accommodate.
