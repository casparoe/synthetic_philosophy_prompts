Write a deadpan parody of a standardized psychiatric intake form—the kind submitted to insurers—issued by a clinic that has officially adopted eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires. The form must collect a patient's presenting complaints, clinical history, risk assessment, and informed consent, but it may not use any mental-state vocabulary: no "believe," "want," "feel," "think," "intend," "remember," "hope," or "fear," and no attributions of propositional attitudes to either patient or clinician. Instead, descriptions should be confined to neurophysiological states, behavioral dispositions, environmental interactions, or bureaucratic placeholders. The satire should arise from the contortions required to capture ordinary clinical realities—for instance, a patient reporting that they "want to stop drinking" or that they "believe their spouse is angry"—translated into strictly eliminativist language, and from a clinician's notes section that struggles to record informed consent without attributing understanding or agreement. Keep the tone dry and institutional throughout, aim for about 400 words, and ensure the exaggeration tracks a genuine tension in the view: that folk-psychological concepts appear practically indispensable even for those who deny their reality. Do not name specific philosophers or texts; the target is the position and its practical consequences, not any individual proponent.
