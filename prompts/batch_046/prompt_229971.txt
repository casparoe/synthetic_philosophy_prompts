Consider the debate over presentism in historiography: the worry that historians inevitably import the conceptual, moral, and institutional standards of their own era into accounts of the past, undermining the aspiration to understand past societies on their own terms. Rather than treat presentism as a single vice, distinguish three analytically separable dimensions: epistemic presentism (the use of present categories—say, modern economic rationality or contemporary gender taxonomies—to organize and explain past behavior); normative presentism (the evaluation of past institutions, actors, or practices by current ethical or political standards); and archival presentism (the selective preservation, cataloguing, and digitization of sources according to present-day judgments about what counts as historically significant). Each dimension raises its own open questions, but they are not equally fundamental, tractable, or consequential for practice.

Your task is not to survey the literature, but to deliver a defended prioritization. Which of the open questions in this three-part landscape is most worth settling first, in the sense that answers to it would restructure the others? For example, is the upstream problem a theory of conceptual translation—how we can deploy present analytical tools without distorting past meanings—or is it a normative framework for when, if ever, historians should suspend moral judgment? Or is the real crux somewhere else, such as whether archival selection can ever be decoupled from presentist value judgments without rendering the archive useless?

Defend an ordering across these dimensions (or across specific questions within them) using explicit criteria: dependency (which answers are prerequisites for others), tractability (which could plausibly be advanced with current conceptual tools rather than requiring impossible neutral standpoints), and practical weight (which results would actually change how historians teach, curate, or advise policy, versus which debates are largely intra-disciplinary). Identify at least one line of work you would actively deprioritize—explain not merely that it is unresolved, but that resolving it would not substantially alter practice or downstream inquiry. If you see a subset of these desiderata—say, full conceptual neutrality plus robust normative evaluation plus unbiased archival practice—as jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly; that tension itself is part of the result I want.

Keep the focus on what deserves sustained effort—whether by an individual researcher, a museum board, or a curriculum committee—and why.
