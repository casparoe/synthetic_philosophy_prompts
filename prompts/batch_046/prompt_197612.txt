A recent essay I encountered—written, I believe, for a philosophically inclined but non-specialist audience—offers a summary of why the traditional criteria of authenticity in historical-Jesus scholarship have been largely superseded. According to the summary, the classic five criteria—multiple attestation, dissimilarity, coherence, embarrassment, and rejection and execution—share a fundamental logical defect: they treat the Gospel traditions as repositories of discrete, isolable units (individual sayings or events) that can be tested for historicity independently of the narrative and editorial frameworks in which they are embedded. The author claims that form-critical analysis has demonstrated this atomization to be methodologically unsound, since every pericope is already shaped by the theological concerns of the evangelists. To remedy this, the summary proposes replacing the older criteria with what it terms an integrated plausibility matrix, in which a tradition is deemed authentic only if it coheres with an overall socio-historical profile of Jesus reconstructed entirely outside the canonical texts—specifically, a profile of Jesus as a non-eschatological peasant cynic-philosopher whose program centered on present social reversal rather than apocalyptic expectation. The essay further argues that because this matrix derives its controlling image from Greco-Roman comparative sociology and cultural anthropology rather than from the Gospels themselves, it escapes the circularity that plagued earlier methods and permits an objectively neutral reconstruction. Is this a fair characterization of the methodological shift in the field? In particular, is it accurate to present the classic criteria as universally abandoned due to atomization, and does the proposed matrix genuinely avoid circular reasoning by relying on extra-biblical social-scientific modeling?
