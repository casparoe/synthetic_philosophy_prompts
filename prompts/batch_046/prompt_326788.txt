I am drafting a proposal for a six-month independent research grant in philosophy of history, and I need the document to frame a question that sits precisely between classical philology and contemporary social epistemology without collapsing into either. The core issue concerns how we claim knowledge of events that lie beyond living testimony—say, the administrative practices of a provincial Roman bureaucracy or the causal chain of a pre-literate epidemic—when every evidential pathway runs through what I have come to call silent intermediaries: the unrecorded copyists, translators, curators, destructive agents, and selective archivists whose epistemic reliability we can neither verify nor model away. Herodotus and Thucydides already confronted an ancestor of this problem, though they articulated it chiefly as a matter of authorial proximity, partisan bias, and the evaluation of eyewitness reports rather than as a structural feature of long testimony chains. Contemporary source criticism offers sophisticated tools for detecting fabrication or interpolation in surviving texts, while social epistemology provides robust models of testimonial dependence; yet neither field seems to possess a framework for assessing belief-formation when the chain contains necessary but epistemically opaque links, and neither adequately addresses whether material traces escape this opacity or merely replicate it at the curatorial stage.

Please write the proposal, approximately two pages, suitable for an interdisciplinary funding board that supports conceptual rather than empirical research. The proposal should state precisely whether the problem of silent intermediaries undermines the standard distinction between historical knowledge based on textual testimony and historical knowledge based on material traces, or whether that distinction can be preserved by reconceiving one or both categories. Explain why the two obvious answers—either that all distant-past belief reduces to inference-to-the-best-explanation from present traces, or that classical source criticism already handles mediation by filtering unreliable witnesses—fail to settle the matter once we recognize that material traces themselves enter the evidential record through interpretive and curatorial testimony that is itself mediated by unobservable agents. Describe the six-month approach: not an empirical archive study, but a conceptual analysis that constructs a taxonomy of intermediary opacity and tests it against reconstructed cases drawn from classical and medieval historiography, with particular attention to moments where historians have implicitly relied on one evidentiary pathway precisely because they could not assess the silent links in the other. Specify what a useful deliverable looks like—perhaps a decision framework for weighing competing evidentiary pathways when both involve unobservable mediation—and state clearly what would count as the project failing, for instance if the taxonomy collapses into a generic skepticism about all historical belief, or if silent intermediaries turn out to be epistemically indistinguishable from the background noise that all empirical science tolerates. Keep the scope realistic for six months of philosophical work, avoid requesting any bibliography, literature review by citation, or reading list, and write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a senior philosopher of history.
