Since I recently began field collaboration in a community where folk judgments systematically invert the WEIRD-patterned hierarchy—ranking in-group betrayal as graver than physical harm to outsiders—I have been forced to reconsider whether cross-cultural divergence in moral foundations undermines moral objectivity itself, or merely its universalist formulation. Please argue that such divergence does not represent plural applications of a shared normative framework, but instead shows that the distinction between thin and thick moral concepts is itself culturally calibrated: different communities do not merely weight loyalty and harm differently, they assign different intensions and extensions to terms like wrongness and betrayal, so that disagreement is not about shared properties but about the constitution of the properties themselves. From this, defend the stronger claim that moral objectivity, if it requires cross-cultural convergence on both the extension and the intension of core normative terms, is directly refuted by these patterns—not rescued by a pluralist amendment—while addressing the counter-argument that such divergence could be explained by differential weighting of objectively grounded, culturally invariant values. Keep the reasoning conceptual rather than empirical; I am not asking for ethnographic citation, but for the logical structure of the objection and where exactly the objectivist must concede or resist.
