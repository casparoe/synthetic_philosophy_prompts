The exam asked students to engage with Wittgenstein's claim—developed in his Lectures on Religious Belief and resonant with his remarks in On Certainty—that religious commitment functions not as an empirical hypothesis but as a passionate adherence to a system of reference or form of life. Specifically, students were to address whether religious disagreement (e.g., between believer and atheist) should be treated as a genuine contradiction or as a clash between incommensurable frameworks, and whether Wittgenstein's account permits any internal rational assessment of religious belief or treats all such frameworks as immune to revision.

Here is the student's answer (approximately 230 words):

Wittgenstein holds that religious belief is not a hypothesis but an emotional and expressive stance embedded in a form of life. When a believer says that God exists, this is not a factual claim comparable to saying that Neptune exists but expresses commitment to a particular lifestyle and value system. Because religious language operates within its own language-game governed by internal rules, there is no rational bridge to scientific discourse, and the believer and atheist do not genuinely contradict one another; they inhabit incommensurable frameworks, like players in different games. Wittgenstein describes religious pictures as mental representations that regulate behavior. Once a person adopts such a picture, it becomes immune to rational revision, since it serves as the ground for all other judgments rather than as one judgment among others. Therefore, internal criticism is impossible—the framework is self-justifying—and any external critique commits a category mistake. Religious disputes are best understood as cross-cultural expressions of different emotional needs, not as logical conflicts open to argument.
