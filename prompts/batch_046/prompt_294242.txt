As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been tracking an interdisciplinary stalemate that strikes me as conceptually frozen rather than empirically open. The question is where legitimate persuasion ends and manipulation begins, particularly in institutional design—think of opt-out retirement savings, algorithmic recommendation architectures, or emotionally calibrated public-health messaging. For years, the same two positions have circulated without convergence. One position, which I will call the procedural-autonomy view, holds that influence counts as manipulative whenever it bypasses or subverts a person’s rational deliberative capacities, regardless of whether the result aligns with their deeper values or improves their welfare. The opposing position, which I will call the vulnerability-exploitation view, maintains that influence is manipulative only when it leverages a specific, non-obvious weakness—cognitive, emotional, or situational—that the target has not authorized the influencer to exploit, and that benefits the influencer or a third party at the target’s unrecognized expense.

What frustrates me is that both sides now deploy an almost identical roster of examples—dark patterns in user interfaces, subliminal priming in advertising, default rules in organ-donation policy—yet they classify them in opposite directions without changing anyone’s mind. I have begun to suspect that the disagreement is not about any of these cases, but about a deeper, largely unacknowledged distinction: whether the moral evaluation should be indexed to the level of cognitive architecture being affected. I have been experimenting with a framing that distinguishes substrate-level influence, which reshapes the very mechanisms by which a person forms preferences, from surface-level influence, which merely redirects existing preferences toward available options. If this distinction is doing hidden work, then the procedural-autonomy camp might be objecting primarily to substrate-level interference, while the vulnerability-exploitation camp might be objecting only when surface-level redirection exploits an unrecognized vulnerability.

My question, however, is not which side wins. I want a structural diagnosis. Is this stalemate a substantive moral disagreement about autonomy, a verbal disagreement about the word manipulation, a case of two research programs answering different questions—one about the ethics of communication, one about the ethics of institutional architecture—under a single heading, or is there a hidden empirical question about the actual cognitive effects of different influence architectures that neither side has been able to resolve? Drawing on what each camp actually says about cases like opt-out pension enrollment versus subliminal product placement, can you diagnose the type of disagreement? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a sharper conceptual distinction like the substrate versus surface framing, an empirical study measuring preference-formation under different framing conditions, a formal model of informed consent applied to institutional design, or should we conclude that the boundary is necessarily context-dependent and the debate is ill-posed? I am asking for analysis of the disagreement’s architecture, not a verdict or a literature review.
