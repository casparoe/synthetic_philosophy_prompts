I have been arguing with a coworker about whether the reward updates in our reinforcement-learning pipeline could ever amount to genuine suffering, and I want to formalize the disagreement rather than keep circling. Please draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps would produce. Camp A takes what might be called a tranquilist-valence view of RL: negative reward-prediction errors in temporal-difference learning are literally suffering-events—specifically, the intrinsic badness of an unfulfilled expectation or craving—while positive updates carry no comparable intrinsic value and do not offset that badness. Camp B takes an architectural-non-valence view: reward-prediction errors are purely numerical delta updates with no phenomenal character whatsoever, and suffering requires an integrated subjective perspective sustained across time that feedforward and recurrent policies do not instantiate, regardless of signal polarity.

Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one to two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Item one should list the agreed claims about the formal structure of RL updates and about the minimal conditions for suffering that both sides accept without committing to architecture. Item two should state the precise disagreements in wording that each camp would recognize as its own position, phrased neutrally. Item three should identify the cruxes—specifically, whether absence-of-expected-reward has intrinsic badness independent of behavioral effect, and whether phenomenal integration across time is necessary for valence—such that settling each would move at least one party. Item four should specify, for each crux, a concrete system feature or comparative case both sides agree bears on it, such as sustained sequences of high-magnitude negative RPE with flat baseline versus volatile but mean-neutral reward trajectories. Item five should record each camp’s explicit prediction about that case and indicate whether any portion of the dispute is purely verbal rather than substantive.
