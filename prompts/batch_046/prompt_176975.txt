I sit on the funding review panel for our university's Institute for Advanced Study. We have received a proposal to establish a two-year visiting-fellowship program titled "Occasionalist Causation in Contemporary Science." The applicants—a philosopher of science and a theologian—argue that Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist critique of necessary causal connection, according to which nature exhibits only habitual conjunction rather than productive power, offers an underexplored resolution to the preemption problem in modern interventionist and counterfactual theories of causation. They contend that if every event is directly actualized without intermediate natural efficacy, apparent causal preemption dissolves: there is never true simultaneous overdetermination by competing causes, only a single direct actualization on each occasion that renders moot the counterfactual dependencies that generate preemption puzzles. The program would host workshops comparing this updated occasionalist framework with current neuroscience debates about mental causation and with quantum interpretations that reject hidden-variable determinism. Critics on the panel argue that occasionalism is a theological doctrine rather than an empirical research program, and that funding it risks blurring the boundary between philosophy of science and confessional theology; others note the proposal never addresses whether occasionalism undermines predictive science or merely reconceptualizes its conditions of success. Draft the panel's decision memo to the Institute director recommending approval, approval with conditions, or rejection. The memo should treat the philosophical argument seriously rather than dismissing it as anachronistic, should specify any conditions if you recommend them, and should acknowledge the strongest objection available to the side that loses.
