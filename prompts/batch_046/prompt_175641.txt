Design a decision-tree taxonomy for an upper-level political-philosophy seminar that sorts interpretive stances on the relation between exploitation and alienation in Marx's critique of capitalism. The tree must begin from one shared premise—capitalism systematically separates workers from the products, processes, and social meaning of their labor while simultaneously appropriating unpaid surplus labor—and then branch through concrete conceptual commitments rather than through author names or school labels. At each internal node, pose a direct yes/no or either/or question about a specific case or theoretical commitment: for example, whether alienation could obtain in a worker-owned cooperative that extracts no surplus value; whether exploitation can occur in the absence of any subjective estrangement; whether the four forms of alienation are hierarchically ordered with estrangement from species-being as the root; and whether the concept of species-being functions as a normative anthropology or merely as a rhetorical placeholder for social interdependence. Every leaf must be a named interpretive position—such as orthodox structuralist, early-humanist priority, analytical separationist, republican dominationist, dialectical co-constitutionist, or deflationary eliminativist—accompanied by a one-sentence profile of its core commitments. The tree should contain six to eight leaves total. You must include at least one apparently unoccupied branch—e.g., a view that treats alienation as purely subjective psychological distress while denying that exploitation has any objective structural reality—and briefly diagnose why that logical space is empty, whether through incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect. Build the tree from the logical space of answers first, then map the actual positions onto that space, noting any combinations that remain unclaimed. The diagnostic questions must be answerable by a student who understands the concepts but has not memorized secondary literature, so do not ask which theorist they prefer; ask what they believe about the cases. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot reliably separate because they yield identical answers at every node, explaining whether the divergence is merely rhetorical or genuinely substantive but hidden. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts by page; ground every node in conceptual distinctions navigable without access to particular sources. Write every node as a direct question the student answers about the concept, and conclude with a brief scoring guide that shows how a pattern of answers maps onto each leaf.
