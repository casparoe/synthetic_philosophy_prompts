Analyze the Luddite machine-breaking campaigns of 1811–1816 in the Lancashire and Yorkshire textile districts through Marx’s framework for machinery and the subsumption of labor under capital. The salient dynamics are these: skilled framework knitters and croppers saw stocking frames and power looms operated by lower-wage, less-skilled labor destroying customary earnings and craft control; they responded with organized breaking of machinery, but also with petitions for parliamentary protection and minimum wages, indicating that their grievance was not technology in itself but its deployment within capitalist social relations. In Capital, Volume I, Marx treats machinery as capital’s primary instrument for producing relative surplus value, and in his manuscripts on capital’s immediate process of production he distinguishes between formal subsumption—where capital takes over an existing labor process and deploys machinery as an external tool to lengthen labor or intensify its pace—and real subsumption, where capital revolutionizes the technical basis of production itself, so that machinery becomes the organizing principle and living labor is subordinated to its rhythm. For this analysis, treat formal subsumption as capital using tools within a craft logic that remains technically intact, and real subsumption as capital restructuring the labor process so that the worker serves the machine’s tempo and the factory system becomes autonomous.

Using that lens, say precisely what the Luddites confronted: was their situation an instance of formal subsumption (machinery inserted into an otherwise intact craft regime) or of real subsumption (the factory system and machine-paced production already restructuring labor)? Then apply the theory normatively: by Marx’s lights, what should the Luddites have done—broken machines, seized mills, organized across trades, or pursued legislative protection? Identify which features Marx’s framework explains well (for example, why capitalists adopted machinery regardless of worker welfare, or why destroying frames could not reverse the technical transformation) and which it misdescribes or leaves silent (for example, the Luddites’ own technical literacy, their simultaneous demands for legal regulation rather than pure anti-technology revolt, or the gendered division of labor in the mills that the framework largely ignores). Finally, turn the analysis back on the theory: does the fact that the Luddites combined direct action with parliamentary petitioning suggest that formal and real subsumption can coexist within a single industry, or does it expose a gap in Marx’s binary?

Please take a committed position: defend one specific reading—either that the Luddites were confronting an early but incomplete real subsumption, or that they were resisting formal subsumption before the factory system had fully matured—and argue for it with reasons drawn from both the historical dynamics and Marx’s conceptual architecture. Do not simply lay out both options and leave the choice to me; make the case, address the strongest objection, and state clearly where Marx’s account is silent or overstated rather than forcing it to speak.
