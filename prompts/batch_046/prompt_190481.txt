I'm delivering the closing remarks at the decommissioning ceremony for the persistent-world server Meridian-7, which has hosted continuous player-character lives for eighteen years. Several thousand players are attending in person or via stream; many formed marriages, grief rituals, and political alliances through characters now facing irreversible deletion. The server's founders are present and have asked that I not treat this as merely a technology retirement. I need to speak about whether the characters themselves—not just the players—have a kind of moral standing that makes this deletion something other than deleting files, without claiming they have consciousness or rights in any legal sense, and without invoking religious afterlives that this audience mostly rejects. The philosophical question should structure what I can and cannot say: if characters have a derivative but genuine moral status grounded in the sustained relational and narrative structures they hosted, then the deletion demands a particular kind of acknowledgment; if they are purely instrumental representations, then comfort must come entirely from player memory. Write a ten-minute address that navigates this for an audience split between game designers who understand virtual-world architecture, longtime players who mourn specific characters by name, and family members who think it is all make-believe.
