I spent three decades in molecular biology before retiring, and since then I have been trying to understand the procreation asymmetry not as a standalone ethical puzzle but as a conceptual problem that sits uncomfortably between evolutionary theory, normative population ethics, and the philosophy of risk. The familiar formulation—why is it impermissible to bring a suffering person into existence, yet not impermissible to refrain from bringing a flourishing one into existence?—seems to me to conceal several deeper disagreements: about whether evolutionary explanations of reproductive behaviour carry normative weight or merely explain away the intuition; about whether the asymmetry survives once we model procreative choices under deep uncertainty about welfare across distant generations; and about whether mechanistic accounts of suffering in evolutionary biology alter what we take the “badness” of created harm to consist in. Rather than a survey of positions, I want a defended research agenda. Among the open lines of work—first, whether the asymmetry collapses under non-person-affecting frameworks in population ethics; second, whether longtermist considerations spanning millennia require us to revise or abandon it; third, whether evolutionary drives undermine or merely contextualise the underlying moral intuition; and fourth, whether integrating mechanistic explanations of harm with normative theory yields a genuinely different assessment of procreative obligations—which two or three are upstream of the rest, and where should limited research effort be directed? Please defend your prioritisation with explicit criteria: tractability for someone with scientific training but limited formal philosophy, dependency relations among the questions, and the practical stakes for how we think about reproduction, risk, and obligation. I am not seeking bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—only the reasoning that determines what actually matters most and why.
