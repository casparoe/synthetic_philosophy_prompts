As a novelist who has spent the past several years writing about the spaces that institutions fail to map, I have been invited to deliver the opening address at the dedication of the Civic Archive of Unrecorded Voices—a center devoted to preserving what its curators term "interstitial privacy," the condition of being neither fully visible to administrative systems nor deliberately concealed from them, but rather suspended in the gaps between assigned roles. The audience will be split between information ethicists and philosophers of privacy, who will recognize the conceptual stakes, and the families of local activists and archivists whose lived experience is the archive's true subject. I need roughly eight minutes of remarks that treat interstitial privacy not as decorative ornament but as a structural constraint shaping everything I can and cannot say: the speech must argue that privacy is not primarily a right to concealment but a necessary feature of democratic life that depends on maintaining spaces no institution, including this archive itself, has full authority to render transparent. At the same time, it must face the central paradox—that an archive honoring privacy necessarily violates it by rendering traces legible—and treat that violation not as hypocrisy but as the very mechanism by which societies preserve non-accountability. The voice should remain that of a fiction writer: attentive to ambiguity, resistant to final answers, capable of speaking precisely to experts without losing the community members who built the place. Please compose this address so that I cannot praise the center without admitting its necessary failure, and cannot admit that failure without explaining why the attempt remains indispensable.
