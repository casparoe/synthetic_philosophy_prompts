I'm preparing a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of designers and architects — intelligent non-philosophers — on the view that consciousness operates as a kind of user-illusion: not that particular mental states are occasionally misreported, but that the unified, first-person feel of experience is a functional interface rather than an accurate mirror of underlying processes. I want a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself.

Specify the dependency order: which component must be established before the audience can understand what kind of illusion is being claimed, which distinction must come next, and what should only be introduced once the functional framing is secure. Choose one concrete carrying example drawn from design or built environments rather than the usual Cartesian theater or desktop-computer metaphors; say exactly what properties it must possess and what it must lack to do the work. Map the predictable misconceptions in the order the audience will form them — for instance, that illusion implies a hidden real perceiver behind the interface, or that the view denies the reality of subjective experience altogether — and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Provide one check-for-understanding question for the end that distinguishes grasping the functional role of the interface from merely adopting the vocabulary. Finally, state explicitly which part of the underlying theory should be left out at this level and how to acknowledge that omission without misleading the group.
