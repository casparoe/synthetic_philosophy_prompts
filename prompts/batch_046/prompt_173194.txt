I recently listened to an online philosophy lecture aimed at non-specialists that summarized the Hart-Devlin debate, and I want to verify whether I have grasped the positions correctly before writing an essay. According to the speaker, H.L.A. Hart defends a strict harm principle: the state may criminalize private conduct only when it causes direct, identifiable injury to specific individuals, and widespread moral disgust—no matter how intense—cannot by itself justify legal prohibition. Lord Devlin, writing in response to the Wolfenden Report's recommendation to decriminalize private homosexual acts, is said to argue that shared morality functions like an institutional immune system: a society can suffer a form of systemic disintegration when behaviors violating its core moral consensus become visible and tolerated, even if no particular victim can be named. The speaker introduced what she called a "threshold model" of Devlin's view—once private immorality crosses an invisible boundary of social intolerance, it produces collective harm analogous to ecological collapse, which is real but irreducible to individual damages. She further claimed that Hart's reply misses this point entirely because he demands proof of harm to discrete persons, which is impossible by definition when the damage is to social cohesion itself. I am inclined to think the speaker has captured Devlin's concern with collective harm but has made Hart appear more rigidly individualistic than he actually is. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what misrepresents either author—especially regarding whether Hart ever allows collective or indirect considerations into his harm principle, and whether Devlin's "threshold model" and "institutional immune system" framing are genuinely present in his writing or are later interpretations imposed on his argument.
