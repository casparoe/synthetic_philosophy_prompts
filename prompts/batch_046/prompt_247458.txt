I'm trying to defend a kind of extended Principal Principle — not just the classic physical-chance version, but one that applies to any objective probability fixed by a transparent, non-manipulable proceedure, like randomised trial assignment or a national-service lottery. The claim is that if you genuinely know the instutional chance of being assigned to condition X is 0.2, then your credence should match it at 0.2, even if you've got private hunches about your own suitability. I think this helps with expert deference in medicine and policy, but I'm worried it's overreaching. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward — maybe it collapses back into pure evidentialism, maybe it can't handle biased procedures you can correct for, maybe it forces you to ignore relevant base-rate info. Which of these would a committed defender concede is a real cost, and which would they just dismiss? Also, has it been qualified into unfalsifiability by saying only well-designed procedures count? Is that insulation actually a problem for a normative principle like this? I'd particularly like to know if there are formal results or specific thought experiments that expose the break points, not just generic scepticism.
