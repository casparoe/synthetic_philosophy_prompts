In discussions of personal identity and technological immortality, two analogies tend to dominate the conversation about mind uploading: the sudden digital scan is compared to photocopying a book, while gradual biological replacement of neurons with synthetic analogues is compared to rebuilding the ship of Theseus plank by plank. I am trying to clarify my own stance before a seminar on philosophy of mind and need to know whether these analogies are pulling me in opposite directions or whether one of them collapses under scrutiny.

Specifically, I want to defend the view that gradual substrate replacement—say, replacing biological neurons one by one with functionally identical neuromorphic silicon implants over several years—preserves personal identity and continuity of consciousness, whereas a destructive scan-and-upload procedure creates only a psychological duplicate. I also want to maintain that consciousness depends on organizational invariance rather than on specific biological material, but I reject both Cartesian dualism and the idea that there is an immaterial soul-file that gets transferred. At the same time, I find myself drawn to the intuition that if every neuron has been replaced, the resulting entity is genuinely me, yet I cannot see why a perfect digital copy made from my final synthetic brain-state should not also be me—unless I am smuggling in a causal-continuity condition that my functionalism was supposed to reject.

Can you lay out precisely what maps onto what in both the ship-of-Theseus and the photocopy analogies, where each mapping breaks down, and whether either analogy actually supports the asymmetry I want between gradual replacement and sudden upload? In particular, does the ship analogy covertly import a notion of spatiotemporal continuity that functionalism about consciousness should not need, and does the photocopy analogy unfairly assume that digital copies lack causal history rather than merely lacking spatiotemporal overlap? Finally, if both analogies fail in ways that undermine my preferred asymmetry, is there a better comparison—perhaps drawn from developmental biology or distributed engineering—that captures why continuity of process matters more than preservation of pattern, or should I abandon the asymmetry altogether?
