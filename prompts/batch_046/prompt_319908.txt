In my sociology seminar people keep reachng for Bauman's gardening picture of modernity: the modern state is a gardener, society is a garden, and the Holocaust was just rational weed-control scaled up with industrial tools. They lean on it to say the Holocaust wasn't some atavistic break with modernity but its logical endpoint, and that comparing it to other genocides misses the specifically modern bureaucratic-rational core. I want the mapping precise: what exactly plays the gardener, the soil, the weeds, the fence, and the pruning shears? Where does the analogy crack — for instance, does it sneek in a teleology or a unity of purpose that actual states don't have? And here's the twist: if we apply the same gardening logic to 19th-century colonial plantation management or to contemporary climate-engineering proposals, does the analogy still single out the Holocaust as uniquely modern, or does it just describe any large-scale rationalized domination? Does the 'uniquely modern' conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument?
