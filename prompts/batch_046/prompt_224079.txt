I am writing this from my study late in the evening, trying to order my thoughts before I speak with a small circle of friends who have agreed to read Kant’s essay on perpetual peace with me, and I find myself holding a cluster of views that I cannot tell whether they form a coherent picture or a concealed contradiction. First, I take the republican argument seriously: in a true republic, those who must bear the costs and risks of war are precisely the citizens who must authorize it, so the institutional design itself creates a rational brake on aggression. Second, I hold that the federation Kant describes is not a world state but a voluntary league of free republics, each retaining its sovereignty, bound together only by a pact of mutual non-aggression and shared juridical norms. Third, I accept the cosmopolitan limitation on hospitality: no stranger may be turned away if that refusal means destruction, yet no state may be compelled to absorb an influx that dissolves its own constitutional order. Fourth, I have come to think that this framework can be stretched—without breaking—to cover modern binding treaties on carbon budgets and transboundary resource use, provided these remain voluntary in adoption and do not establish a global legislative authority with coercive power over member states. Fifth, I regard democratic peace theory as essentially an empirical shadow of Kant’s first definitive article, but I deny that empirical correlation makes republican government sufficient; without the league and without the cosmopolitan brake, democracies can still slide into coalition warfare. Sixth, I believe that any republic entering a permanent defensive alliance with standing joint command structures violates the preliminary article against standing armies, not in letter but in structural spirit, because such alliances embed anticipatory violence into the constitutional architecture. Seventh, and finally, I treat the entire federation not as an achievable historical endpoint but as a regulative ideal that governs reform without ever being fully instantiated.

Do these seven positions hang together, or am I smuggling in contradictions that I have not noticed? I am particularly unsure whether my willingness to extend Kant to binding environmental treaties conflicts with my insistence that the league remain voluntary and non-coercive, and whether my treatment of defensive alliances as violations of the preliminary articles undermines the very mutual security that a league of republics might require. I also wonder if my regulative-ideal interpretation in the seventh position robs the fourth of its practical urgency—if the federation is only an orienting star, why should carbon treaties need binding institutional teeth at all? Please examine these commitments as an audit rather than a lecture: trace which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the tensions by severity. For each real tension, indicate what the least costly revision would be and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. I ask for this in continuous prose only, without headings or bullet points or any typographical emphasis, as one might write in a long letter; and please do not treat any one commitment as obviously the one I should abandon, since I genuinely do not know which is the weak link.
