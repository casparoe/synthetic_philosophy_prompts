I'm working through a conceptual tension in inverse reinforcement learning that I haven't seen addressed well in the alignment literature. Standard IRL assumes humans approximate optimal agents with respect to a stable reward function, and tries to recover that function from behavior. But there's a competing framework — call it the constructed-preference view — holding that choices don't reveal pre-existing preferences but generate them in response to framing, context, and cognitive limits. If that view is right, IRL's core ontology — a fixed reward vector producing behavior — looks like a category error. Yet if we drop stable preferences entirely, IRL reduces to behavior cloning with no normative leverage.

Can you explain where the conceptual boundary actually lies between 'preference revealed with noise' and 'preference constructed in the act'? I'm particularly interested in whether a coherent middle position exists — perhaps locally stable but globally plastic reward structures that are context-bound without being arbitrary — and whether that saves IRL's normative ambitions or merely renames the problem. I want an intuitive, gut-level account of why this identifiability challenge is philosophical rather than statistical.

Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or finding would convince you that the constructed-preference critique is either overblown or fatal to IRL's project, and identify the point in this reasoning most likely to fail.
