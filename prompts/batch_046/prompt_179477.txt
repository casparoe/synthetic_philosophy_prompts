Write a five-minute retirement-toast address for my doctoral advisor's banquet. She is a philosopher of language who spent her career on sortals and classification, and she always treated the white-horse argument from the School of Names not as a puzzle about color words but as a precise logical failure: distributive predication collapses when kind-terms are nested, so the inference that moves from white-horse to horse breaks down at the level of form rather than appearance. About half the room are philosophers of language and specialists in ancient Chinese thought who will recognize a sloppy rendering of the dialogue instantly; the other half are relatives who should feel included rather than lectured. Structure the toast around that logical form—use it to explain why she was stubborn about apparently obvious identity claims—without naming Gongsun Long, without citing texts or offering a bibliography, and without using quotation marks drawn from any source. Include one concrete, non-philosophical analogy (not a trolley problem) that lets the family grasp why nesting kinds changes what can truthfully be said. End with a single closing line that functions both as affectionate praise and as a quiet restatement of the paradox, intelligible to both halves of the room. The speech should be precise enough that the experts see you are tracking the actual separation of color and form predicates in the dialogue, not treating it as generic wordplay.
