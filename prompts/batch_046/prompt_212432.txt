I'm a junior serving on my school's historical-commemoration review board, and we have been asked to revise the rubric we use when deciding whether figures such as Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, or Christopher Columbus should continue to be honored in our campus displays despite serious moral objections—slaveholding, imperial governance, and authorization of colonial violence among them. Our debates repeatedly stall on the "man of his time" defense, but the board has no shared way to tell whether that defense is functioning as an explanation of an agent's moral psychology within a particular cultural framework or as an excuse that improperly undermines retrospective accountability. I want you to translate the conceptual distinction between explaining a historical agent's beliefs by reference to the norms available in their era and endorsing those beliefs from our present moral standpoint into an operational decision procedure that our five-person student board could actually run in about thirty minutes per case.

The procedure should take the form of ordered questions with explicit stopping conditions: indicate exactly where a negative answer removes the figure from consideration for removal, where a positive answer sends the case to a values-based judgment call rather than a factual determination, and where the process must flag that judgment cannot be eliminated. Then apply the procedure to three specific cases—Jefferson's ownership of enslaved people, Churchill's decision-making regarding the Bengal famine and broader imperial administration, and Columbus's governance of the Indies—making sure to include at least one case where the procedure produces a recommendation that our board will likely reject for emotional or symbolic reasons despite its logical consistency. Please avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or references to texts I should read; I need the conceptual architecture itself rendered as a practical checklist with worked examples.
