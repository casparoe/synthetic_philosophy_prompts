I spent much of last evening in a prolonged and somewhat heated discussion with a close friend about the ethics of belief, particularly the well-known disagreement between W.K. Clifford's evidentialist rigor and William James's defense of what he called the will to believe. I am trying to articulate my own position for an upcoming seminar and want a consistency check before I present it. I hold, first, that Clifford's principle—that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence—functions correctly as a regulative ideal for ordinary empirical claims, but that it overreaches if applied to the deep framework commitments that render empirical inquiry itself meaningful. Following James, I think that in genuinely live, forced, and momentous situations our passional nature may legitimately resolve the matter, yet I want to restrict this license not to direct first-order religious beliefs, such as that God exists, but to second-order framework stances, for instance the commitment that the universe is not morally indifferent, which make particular moral and religious beliefs intelligible rather than evidentially certified. At the same time, I accept the Alstonian critique that doxastic voluntarism is largely false: we cannot directly will ourselves into believing p at will. Still, I maintain that the ethics of belief should govern our indirect belief-formation practices—our habits of attention, the intellectual communities we inhabit, our willingness to entertain alternatives—rather than the belief-states themselves. Finally, I think that in rare cases of explicit practical identity-formation, such as a person deciding whether to adopt an entire religious way of life, direct doxastic voluntarism is not only psychologically possible but ethically significant, which seems to pull against my broader skepticism about belief-voluntariness.

Please audit whether this cluster of commitments actually hangs together.
