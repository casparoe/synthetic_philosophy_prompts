I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. At my company, engineering design reviews operate under an internal tolerance protocol. The protocol permits any engineer to argue that a feature or idea should be withdrawn from open discussion because debating it causes harm; however, if contributions arguing that open debate itself is harmful reach thirty percent of a review thread, the thread is automatically locked to protect the conditions of tolerance. Meanwhile, arguments for excluding specific people rather than ideas are banned immediately and are not counted toward that thirty percent threshold. What philosophical commitments about tolerance, harm, and neutrality does this practice presuppose? Does it assume that tolerance must limit itself in order to survive, or does it depend on a distinction between tolerating viewpoints and tolerating damage? Is it consistent with treating tolerance as pure procedural neutrality, or does it quietly commit to a substantive theory about which harms justify suppression? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say what would have to change in the review process if we replaced the underlying view with one that refuses to rank the value of open debate against the value of preventing harm.
