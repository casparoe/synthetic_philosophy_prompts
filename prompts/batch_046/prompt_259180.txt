Exam Question:
Bradley's regress arises from the demand that if a relation R connects two terms a and b, then there must be a further relation R1 connecting R to a and another R2 connecting R to b; but then R1 and R2 themselves require further connections, generating an infinite regress. First, reconstruct the regress in your own words and explain why Bradley took it to undermine the reality of relations and to support his doctrine of the Absolute as an indivisible, unified whole with no genuine parts.

Second, a contemporary neo-Bradleyan proposes to halt the regress by adopting an ontology of mutually constitutive nodes: a is not an independent term that stands in R to b, but is essentially defined as R-b, and b is essentially defined as R-a, so that there is no gap between relata and relation requiring an external bridge. Assess this defense. Does it genuinely stop the regress, or does it merely relocate the problem to the coordination of the two internal structures—specifically, to explaining how the R-structure embedded in a is matched to the R-structure embedded in b?

Third, apply these considerations to the problem of the unity of the proposition. In the proposition "Socrates is wise," what binds the subject and the predicate together into a single judgment rather than leaving them as a mere list? Russell argued that a proposition contains a relating relation—the verb actually connects its terms in a way that a bare name for a relation does not. Evaluate whether Russell's proposal avoids Bradley's regress, and identify one subtle weakness in Russell's account that a Bradleyan could exploit.

Student's Answer:
Bradley's regress shows that relations cannot be real. If R relates a and b, then you need R1 to relate R to a, and R2 to relate R to b, and this goes on forever with no end. So Bradley concludes that reality is not made of separate things in relations; instead it is one big unified thing, which he calls the Absolute.

The internal-relations fix works well. If a is essentially the father of b, then the relation is built right into what a is. There are no bare terms sitting around waiting to be linked, so there is nothing that needs an external bridge. The regress stops because the terms and the relation are the same thing.

For propositions, Russell says the verb in "Socrates is wise" is a relating relation that actually connects Socrates to wisdom. But this is mistaken, because the verb is just another term in the proposition, so you still need something to connect the verb to Socrates and something else to connect it to wisdom. So Russell falls right into Bradley's regress. In fact, Bradley himself applied the regress directly to propositions: he said that any attempt to combine terms in a judgment creates a new combined term that itself needs combining, which is impossible. Therefore Russell's solution fails, and we should accept Bradley's view that all judgment ultimately refers back to the unified Absolute.
