I work as a distributed-systems engineer and have no formal training in philosophy or history of science. Recently I have been reading Erwin Schrödinger's reflections on Vedanta, particularly his claim that individual consciousness is not ontologically separate but rather a local appearance of a single universal consciousness—an idea he illustrated with analogies to facets of one underlying substance. I have developed a structural parallel that seems plausible to me, but I suspect it commits a category error.

In distributed computing, the CAP theorem states that a networked system must choose among consistency, availability, and tolerance to network partitions; we treat partition tolerance as unavoidable and then build consistency models on top of it. Reading Schrödinger, I find myself interpreting his rejection of separate minds as analogous to rejecting mental partitions in favor of a global consistency of awareness—almost as if quantum entanglement, which he knew well, supplies the physical mechanism that prevents minds from being genuinely partitioned nodes. So my question is: is Schrödinger's Vedantic monism actually an extrapolation from quantum non-separability into metaphysics, using physics to dissolve the boundaries between subjects? Or have I illegitimately projected a computer-science framework onto a view that was drawn primarily from the Upanishads and Schopenhauer, with quantum mechanics playing at most an incidental role?

Please correct the confusion in my framing. In particular, separate what is well-established in the scholarship—regarding Schrödinger's actual textual sources and whether he ever explicitly linked his quantum results to his philosophy of consciousness—from interpretations that remain genuinely contested, such as whether he advocated a strict ontological identity of all minds or only a mystical-ethical unity that leaves individual experience intact. Do not present any contested reading, including the degree to which quantum mechanics informed his metaphysics, as if it were the consensus among specialists.
