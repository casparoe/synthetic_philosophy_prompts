Write a short story—conceptually dense rather than long—that dramatizes the old problem of whether revolutions necessarily consume their own architects. I am preparing it for a seminar discussion on revolutionary dynamics, and I want to avoid yet another fictionalized retelling of 1793; instead, set the action inside a closed institution that has just undergone an internal overthrow. Imagine a vast, self-governing archive of preserved biological specimens—an underground repository of formaldehyde tanks and glass slides—whose curators have overthrown the old taxonomic regime and installed a Committee of Radical Reordering. The revolution’s initial leaders are now the ones holding power. The crisis should arise not from an external enemy but from an internal dispute over preservation: one founding curator argues that certain obsolete classification categories still contain useful distinctions the new order needs, and for this she is accused not of corruption but of “ideological imprecision”—a failure to align fully with the revolutionary class of curators who reject all prior taxonomy.

The narrative should leave genuinely ambiguous whether her eventual purge was structurally inevitable—the logic of revolutionary purity demanding ever-narrower alignment within the curator class—or whether it was contingent on one leader’s paranoia and a specific missed chance for compromise. Include a scene in which one character invokes the maxim that revolutions devour their children, and have another challenge whether that saying names a historical law or only a self-fulfilling prophecy that licenses preemptive violence. The institutional language should shift gradually from administrative jargon about “reordering” and “alignment” toward something more coercive; the story should show, not tell, how the same vocabulary acquires lethal force. The tone should be formal and somewhat parabolic, with precise dialogue, and the ending should withhold judgment: the reader should not be able to tell whether the survivors are tragic figures caught in an iron structure or merely people who misread a contingent failure as destiny. No citations, no historical annotations—just the conceptual architecture rendered through plot, voice, and the slow transformation of bureaucratic speech into judgment.
