In a philosophy-of-science seminar I am taking, a discussion about scientific realism turned on a familiar one-liner: "with enough auxiliary assumptions, any theory can be made compatible with any observation." A fellow student invoked it specifically to suggest that current debates between dark-matter and modified-gravity explanations of galactic rotation curves are rationally incommensurable — each research program, she claimed, can always patch its auxiliaries to absorb anomalous data, so evidence alone can never adjudicate between them.

I am not convinced the slogan does the work she needs it to do, but I cannot precisely locate the ambiguity. Could you lay out the different precise theses this line might express — distinguishing, for instance, logical from epistemic underdetermination, global from local, permanent from temporary, and adjustments to observation reports from adjustments within the theoretical core — and for each variant say whether it is trivially true, substantively defensible, false, or irrelevant to her specific astrophysical example?

I want the answer structured as a menu rather than a single preferred reading. For each precisification, please state whether people who repeat the slogan usually mean that one, whether it is plausible on its own terms, and, crucially, whether it actually licenses the conclusion that rival research programs in astrophysics are on rational par. Where a variant fails to support her argument, explain exactly where the gap lies.
