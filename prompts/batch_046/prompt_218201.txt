I came across a summary of Sartre’s account of radical freedom and bad faith in an unnamed popular philosophy article. The author explains that for Sartre, consciousness is defined by a kind of double absence: it is never identical with its past, which it constantly negates, and never coincident with its present state, which it immediately transcends. From this, the author infers that bad faith is essentially a single mistake—treating one’s facticity, such as one’s social role or bodily needs, as an unchangeable destiny—and that authenticity therefore demands maintaining an absolute distance from every commitment, emotional attachment, and practical role. The summary also describes the café waiter as someone who is literally trying to turn himself into a thing, an attempt that must fail because consciousness can never achieve the self-coincidence of being-in-itself. Finally, the author concludes that since we are burdened with freedom whether we want it or not, any long-term project—a career, a relationship, or a moral principle—necessarily falls into bad faith, because it treats one’s future self as already fixed. The argument sounds coherent, but I worry it turns Sartre into an advocate of perpetual self-reinvention with no room for loyalty or sustained purpose. Which parts of this summary are accurate to Sartre’s position, which are subtle distortions or overstatements, and does the conclusion about commitment actually follow from the premises about freedom and bad faith?
