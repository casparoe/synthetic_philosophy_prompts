I'm working on a novel where an old economist explains to my protagonist why every long-term project is secretly a dollar auction in disguise. I built this argument for her dialogue but now it feels like it proves way too much—like nobody should ever quit anything, ever. Can you tell me where the leak is?

(1) In a dollar auction, both the winner and the runner-up pay their last bid, so quitting second-place costs you almost the full prize value with nothing to show.
(2) Once you've bid past $0.50 for a $1 bill, bidding $1.05 is locally rational even though the prize is only worth $1, because quitting locks in a $0.50 loss with zero return.
(3) So rational actors in this structure always escalate past the objective value of the prize.
(4) Now take any sequntial human commitment—writing a novel, running a campaign, maintaining a friendship—where you've sunk irrecoverable time and emotion, and there's no resale market for your investment.
(5) By direct analogy, quitting any of these projects is structurally identical to quitting the auction at $0.50: it guarantees a total loss of your sunk investment with zero return, while pushing forward keeps the possibility of payoff.
(6) Therefore a rational agent should never quit a sequntial project, no matter how bad it gets; the only rational exit is pre-commitment before entering.
(7) But people clearly do quit novels, campaigns, and relationships, and it's often the smart move. So (6) must be wrong.
(8) The question is whether the flaw is in the analogy at (4)—maybe life projects aren't auctions because the 'prize' is subjective and can be redefined—or whether (3) smuggles in an assumption about rationality that doesn't apply when there's no clear payoff matrix.

My character really wants to defend (6) as a kind of dark existential thesis—she's writing a manifesto about commitment traps—so I'd rather save (6) by attacking (4) or finding a hidden equivocation in (2). Where exactly should she strike, and what's the cost of that move? Also, is there a way to keep her thesis alive without making her sound like she doesn't understand sunk cost fallacy?
