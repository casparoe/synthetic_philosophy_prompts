Write a decision tree for a philosophy-of-mind and biology seminar that sorts positions on whether Darwinian evolutionary competence and Turing-test behavioral competence are the same kind of inversion—intelligent or functional performance in the absence of underlying comprehension.

Root question, answerable without knowing any philosophical label: Do you treat evolutionary design and machine linguistic behavior as structurally analogous instances of competence without comprehension?

Branch by these concrete distinctions:
1. In the Darwinian case, is the absence of comprehension purely ontological (no designer exists at all), or also functional (natural selection performs no computation that could count as understanding)?
2. In the Turing case, is the system's competence purely syntactic and behavioral, or does it require some minimal embodied, causal, or semantic grounding to count as genuine competence rather than mere simulation?
3. Where do intermediate biological cases—collective path-optimization in slime molds, quorum sensing in bacterial colonies—belong: as scaled Darwinian processes without minds, as proto-Turing systems with distributed functional roles, or as a categorically distinct form of non-comprehending competence?

Each leaf should carry either a named position or the label "unnamed," plus one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—e.g., being forced to deny that evolutionary explanations are genuinely explanatory, or being forced to attribute minimal comprehension to bacterial colonies.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: include all consistent combinations of answers, mark unoccupied branches, and briefly diagnose why they are empty (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Explicitly flag any pair of well-known views—say, a strict eliminativist about evolutionary understanding versus a pragmatic instrumentalist about selection—that the tree cannot cleanly separate, and say whether the ambiguity is logical or only verbal.

Keep the tree to six or seven leaves. The audience is graduate students who know evolutionary theory and the Turing test but are not specialists in either field's philosophy. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; frame everything so it can be answered from general conceptual knowledge alone.
