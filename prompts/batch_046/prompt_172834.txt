I run a philosophy-of-psychiatry reading group with 10 graduate students; we have one 90-minute session. Design a structured, in-person simulation that makes Hacking-style looping effects tangible before anyone names them. Give every participant a hidden role card: four are subjects who receive a provisional, unstable behavioral descriptor (e.g., prone to cognitive fragmentation under ambiguity); three are clinicians with checklists who observe and score; three are protocol architects who can rewrite the checklist criteria once, and only once, at the 30-minute mark based on aggregated scores. The rules must be arranged so that the clinicians' scoring behavior visibly alters how the subjects respond—subjects see scores in real time via a shared board—and the architects' revision then changes what the clinicians look for in round two, creating a live feedback loop. Use a non-monetary payoff tied to group resource access: subjects earn collective trust credits if they stay within the current definition, but the definition itself shifts. Include a precise timeline (0–20 min orientation, 20–45 min round one, 45–50 min revision, 50–80 min round two, 80–90 min debrief). The debrief should map each round onto the loop—the category, the behavioral adaptation, the criteria revision, the new category—state honestly what the simulation cannot reproduce (specifically the decades-long institutional inertia and power asymmetries of actual psychiatric taxonomy) and how the facilitator must supply that context verbally, and predict what will go wrong the first run (subjects reverse-engineering the loop, architects refusing to revise, or clinicians anchoring on initial scores) and how to correct it. Do not lecture beforehand; participants should not hear the phrase looping effect until the debrief.
