I've recently begun auditing activation spaces in a vision-language model for a transparency project, and I keep finding myself calling certain linear directions representations of color or shape without being able to say whether that is a literal claim or a predictive shorthand. To clarify my own commitments for a workshop I'm organizing on conceptual issues in interpretability, I want a decision tree—not a list of named camps—that takes a person from concrete judgments about mechanistic findings to an actual philosophical position on whether neural networks have representations.

The tree should contain five or six branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete scenario that someone could answer without knowing which philosophical label it leads to. One node might present the case of ablating a single direction in a middle layer: performance on one task collapses while others remain intact. The question should ask whether this establishes that the direction represented the task-relevant feature, or only that it was causally necessary for computation. Another node should address polysemantic directions—those that activate for seemingly unrelated inputs, such as both red objects and stop signs—asking whether this counts as one representation with disjunctive content, as multiple representations in superposition, or as evidence that representation-talk is being applied beyond its proper domain. A third node should ask whether representational content requires some form of aboutness directed at external states of affairs, or whether stable internal correlations are sufficient. A fourth might ask whether the possibility of misrepresentation—cases where the feature activates but the corresponding property is absent—is essential to treating the direction as genuinely representational, or merely an error-statistic.

The leaves must be named positions reached through these branches, not listed at the outset. I want at least the following to appear: a strong localized realist view that treats specific directions as genuine representations with determinate content and causal efficacy; a distributed or superpositional realist view that accepts representations but denies they are neatly localized; a mechanistic functionalist view that treats representational language as justified only by stable causal-functional roles without ontological commitment to content-bearing states; an instrumentalist view that treats interpretability labels as useful predictive heuristics with no mind-independent reality; and an eliminativist view that rejects representation-talk entirely in favor of purely geometric or dynamical descriptions of network behavior. Any logical combination of answers that does not map onto one of these should be flagged, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or an unexplored position—particularly the combination that accepts discrete localization as necessary for representation while also accepting that all meaningful directions are polysemantic.

Please design the tree for an audience familiar with basic interpretability concepts—activation spaces, feature directions, ablation studies—but not necessarily with philosophy of mind, so avoid relying on unexplained technical terms like derived intentionality or semantic normativity unless you define them within the node. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific papers; if you reference particular findings, such as the identification of curve detectors or polysemantic neurons, describe them in general terms rather than attributing them to authors or publication dates. Finally, please flag any pair of leaves that the tree fails to genuinely separate, and indicate what further distinction—perhaps about misrepresentation in recurrent versus feedforward architectures, or about whether representation requires consumer mechanisms—would be needed to split them.
