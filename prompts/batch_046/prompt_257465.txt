I'm a clinical neuropsychologist on an institutional ethics board for a neurosurgical unit, and I've followed the rationalist and analytic-philosophy debates about split-brain unity since the classic case discussions. We repeatedly have to decide, case by case, whether a post-callosotomy patient should be treated as one unified consciousness for consent, welfare advocacy, and legal representation, or whether genuinely separate streams require distinct representation. I want you to turn the philosophical distinction between phenomenal binding, global informational integration, and narrative coherence into a practical decision procedure I can run in about twenty minutes with clinical notes in front of me.

The deliverable should be an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation (treat as unified / treat as divided / refer to committee), and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is values rather than clinical fact. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, flag where the procedure is likely to misfire—especially when behavioural dissociation and first-person report conflict—and say what the procedure cannot do.

Then apply it to three hypothetical post-callosotomy profiles:

1. A patient with complete corpus callosum section but intact anterior and hippocampal commissures, showing integrated emotional responses and cross-modal priming, yet reporting no awareness of left-hand actions and denying any sense of an internal other agent.
2. A patient with partial anterior callosotomy who exhibits cross-cueing—the left hand solves puzzles using environmental cues visible only to the right hemisphere, with no direct interhemispheric transfer—while maintaining a coherent autobiographical narrative and insisting on singular identity.
3. A patient with near-total section who shows strong behavioural dissociation (independent goal pursuit by each hemisphere in competing tasks) but produces verbally fluent, unified self-descriptions and scores high on standard narrative-coherence measures.

Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a recommendation you think I will not like, and explain why. Write in British English. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
