I was discussing Toby Ord's concept of moral trade — the possibility that agents with genuinely different moral commitments can exchange goods or services such that each judges their own obligations better satisfied — with a colleague who works on AI governance, and we realized the idea raises a sharp institutional question that divides two camps. Consider a proposed AI mediator for a multinational philanthropic consortium: the members hold incompatible thick moral frameworks (some deontological, some consequentialist, some rooted in culturally specific virtue traditions), and the system is permitted to propose reallocations that function as trades — directing resources to elder-care obligations in one region in exchange for directing equivalent value to famine-relief obligations in another, where each party regards the exchange as improving satisfaction of its own moral reasons. The institutional-trade camp holds that blocking such mediated exchanges forces a false universalism, that liberal neutrality actually requires facilitating these Pareto-improving moral exchanges, and that an AI mediator can do so without imposing a meta-ethical framework if it operates purely on each party's self-reported obligation structure. The integrity-objection camp holds that treating obligations as fungible commodities corrupts the authority of moral reasons, that any mediator capable of recognizing a trade must already encode a preference-like structure onto moral reasons (thereby violating neutrality), and that the empirical risk of manufactured obligations and strategic manipulation makes institutionalization dangerous regardless of conceptual coherence.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration specifically about this mediator design. I want: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims — about the definition of moral trade, about the mediator's functional constraints (what it can observe, what it cannot adjudicate), about the conditions under which liberal neutrality applies to institutional design, and about existing human practices that approximate moral exchange; (2) a disagreements table in which each disagreement is phrased with wording its own side accepts — specifically on whether moral reasons have exchange-structures analogous to preferences, whether an AI system can represent obligations without reducing them to weights, and whether the corruption risk is conceptual or contingent; (3) identification of the cruxes — I take the central ones to be whether moral pluralism permits Pareto-improving exchanges across incommensurable frameworks and whether liberal neutrality permits an institution to facilitate exchanges that depend on substantive moral disagreement rather than on thin procedural agreement; and (4) for each crux, a concrete institutional test-case or deployment feature that both sides agree would be probative, with each camp's explicit prediction — for instance, both might agree that a pilot program with contractually binding prohibitions on strategic obligation-manufacturing would bear on the corruption crux, but they will predict opposite outcomes regarding whether moral satisfaction ratings converge or diverge. Format the output as the numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictive tests.
