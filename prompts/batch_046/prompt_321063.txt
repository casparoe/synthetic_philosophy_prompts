I'm moderating a podcast series on the intersection of quantum mechanics and Eastern philosophy, and next week's guest is a philosopher of science—let's call her Dr. Elena Voss—who has developed an interpretation she terms Schrödinger's mirror thesis. She argues that Schrödinger's Vedantic commitment to the absolute unity of consciousness should not be read as a mystical addendum to his physics but as the logical completion of his quantum-mechanical view of observation. On her reading, Schrödinger's claim that consciousness is singular and that there is fundamentally only one mind implies that quantum superposition is never physically resolved; rather, what we call decoherence is merely the apparent fragmentation of a single, non-local observing consciousness into phenomenologically distinct perspectives. She distinguishes this sharply from panpsychism, insisting that there are not many small consciousnesses unified into one, but literally one consciousness that only appears plural through what she calls perspectival refraction.

I'm taking a course on philosophy of physics this semester, and after discussing this with friends I'm convinced that a standard interview would let her retreat to vague holism. I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. Specifically, I need at least one question pressing on how her interpretation differs from Everettian many-worlds, since both reject physical collapse; one on whether perspectival refraction is an empirical claim or a definitional maneuver that protects the view from falsification; and one on how she handles the obvious phenomenological fact that different subjects report incompatible experiences—say, one observer measuring spin-up and another measuring spin-down—without invoking a hidden multiplicity of minds. For each of these, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: for the Everett comparison, anticipate her reply that branching worlds are redundant if consciousness never actually divides; for the empirical question, anticipate her appeal to the irreducibility of first-person access; and for the incompatible-experiences problem, anticipate her claim that contradiction only arises if we illegitimately project a third-person ontology onto first-person data. The questions should sound like they belong in a serious colloquium Q&A rather than a popular science chat, and should avoid generic biographical openers entirely.
