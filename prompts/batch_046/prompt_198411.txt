I recently found myself moderating a university-affiliated public forum where a contributor was permanently excluded after a sustained campaign of individual users blocking, reporting, and withdrawing cooperation, rather than through any centralized disciplinary decision. Watching the aftermath, I noticed that the usual arguments about “cancel culture” seemed to miss the structural feature that made the case puzzling: no single agent had imposed suffering as a response to wrongdoing, yet the aggregated result—loss of platform, income, and social standing—functioned exactly like a sentence. This has led me to think about deplatforming and social sanction not merely as controversial practices but as a conceptual problem for theories of punishment.

For several years now, debates about whether deplatforming counts as punishment have stalled in a familiar pattern. One side points to the intentional structure of blame, the expressive character of exclusion, and the aim of deterring future conduct, insisting that these are the hallmarks of punishment and therefore demand the usual justifications—proportionality, due process, and a legitimate authority. The other side emphasizes the disaggregated nature of the mechanism: each employer, venue, and private individual acts on independent preferences about association, with no collective intent to inflict harm, which makes the outcome closer to market sorting or social avoidance than to state punishment. Both sides can rehearse the same empirical cases—speakers losing invitations, professionals facing boycotts, figures removed from platforms—without ever addressing why the disagreement persists despite shared agreement on what actually happened.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily verbal, but that it conceals a deeper disagreement about whether moral desert can exist without a unitary agent of punishment—what I have started calling “disaggregated desert,” where harm is not administered but emergently accumulated through thousands of individually defensible choices. Is this diagnosis correct? Is the disagreement really about the metaphysics of agency in moral accountability, about hidden normative commitments to institutional authority, or about something else entirely—perhaps that the two sides are asking whether the practice is justified as punishment versus whether it is explicable without a punisher? And given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction, a formal model of collective action, or a shift in the framing of the question that could actually move the debate, or should we expect this particular stall to be permanent?
