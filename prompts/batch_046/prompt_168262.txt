So I was arguing with my friend about wheter you can blame someone when they literally couldn't have done anything different. Not in the big 'the universe is deterministic' way — I get that thats a whole other debate — but in this very specific, almost sneaky situation. Picture this: a person makes a choice that everyone agrees is wrong, and they make it freely, like, they think about it and go for it. But unknownst to them, there's this hidden safegaurd — maybe a psychological block, or in a sci-fi version a little neural override — that was standing by, ready to force them into the exact same choice if they had tried to pick the right option. So in reality, the safegaurd never fires, because they already wanted to do the wrong thing. The thing that bugs me is that people still blame them, and it feels right to blame them, even though technically there was never a moment where they could have actually taken the other path. The lack of an alternative was real, but it was completely invisible and never had to do any work.

Is there a specific philosophical name for this exact setup — where moral responsibility sticks around precisely because the missing alternatives were hidden and never triggered? I know there's broad talk about determinism and free will, but I'm not asking about that. I'm asking about the specific logical shape where the absence of choice is present but dormant, and that dormancy is what saves the blame. If there's an established concept for this, how is it different from just saying 'well, they meant to do it'? And if philosophers haven't named this particular wrinkle, what would you call it, and where does its boundary lie — like, would it still apply if the safegaurd was 90% likely to fire rather than 100%?
