A friend has led me into an argument about whether immoral artists can make good art, and I cannot find the flaw. The steps run as follows.

(1) A feature is artistically relevant only if it figures in the correct explanation of a work's aesthetic properties — its form, representational content, or the experience it produces.
(2) An artist's private moral failings — cruelty in personal life, offensive politics — are causally inert with respect to those internal properties; learning of them alters nothing about the work's structure or meaning.
(3) Hence for private failings, separating the work from the artist is correct: they are not artistically relevant.
(4) Apply this consistently. Even when a moral failing enters the work's causal history — say, a film's atmosphere of terror was achieved by genuinely abusing performers — this remains a fact about production, not a feature of the finished work's representational or formal structure.
(5) By (1), if a feature does not explain internal aesthetic properties, it is artistically irrelevant.
(6) So even works created through serious, avoidable harm should be judged purely on internal grounds; any negative reaction is moral disgust, not artistic criticism.
(7) But then a hypothetical masterpiece that required actually torturing its subjects to achieve its precise emotional effect has no artistic defect — only a moral one.
(8) That seems wrong. I want to say such a work is artistically compromised, because its achievement is inseparable from a real wrong.
(9) Thus the argument proves too much. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1), since without it we seem forced to investigate every creator's biography before appreciating a painting; yet I do not wish to collapse artistic evaluation into general moral appraisal. Please answer in British English.
