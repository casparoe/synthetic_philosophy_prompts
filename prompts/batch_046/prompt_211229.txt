I sit on my university’s Committee on Scientific Legacy and Technology Ethics, and this case has become personally urgent. My mentor, who directed our computing-history archive until his death last month, left a final internal memorandum arguing that institutions bear what he called a “fiduciary continuity obligation.” His claim was that when we preserve technologies capable of outlasting their human creators, we must embed in them the normative framework of the originating institution—not merely their functional specifications—so that the technology survives without escaping our values. He tied this directly to von Neumann’s well-known concern that technological systems can persist and evolve beyond the human contexts that produced them, carrying forward capabilities that may no longer align with future operators’ intentions.

The concrete proposal before us is the “Legacy Architecture Initiative.” The applicants—computer scientists and historians of science—seek institutional approval and pilot funding to build a self-modifying archival management system modeled on von Neumann-style automata principles. The system would continuously update its own preservation and access protocols for our scientific records. Its defining feature is what the team calls a “value-lock mechanism”: an embedded institutional charter that binds all future iterations of the system to our founding ethical commitments, preventing revision of core normative parameters even as technical operations adapt. The applicants argue this realizes my mentor’s vision and honors von Neumann by ensuring that surviving technology remains accountable to the values of its origin.

The committee is divided. Some members view the value-lock as responsible stewardship: we are allowing technology to survive us without letting it drift from our institutional purpose. Others see it as both technically naive and ethically overreaching—an attempt to freeze present authority inside autonomous machinery, and a misreading of von Neumann, who warned about technology escaping human control rather than advocating that it be permanently chained to human intentions.

Because my mentor’s memorandum was forwarded to me with a request that I guide the committee’s response, I have been asked to draft the official memo. I need a clear recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—with reasoning that the applicants could actually respond to, and with an honest, rigorous treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The memo should avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. It should engage seriously with the philosophical question: does von Neumann’s framework for technological survival support the idea of value-locked persistence, or does it instead suggest that any attempt to bind future technological evolution to present norms is unstable and presumptuous?
