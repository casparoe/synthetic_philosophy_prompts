I am a novelist preparing a philosophical fiction manuscript, and I am trying to render the conceivability argument regarding philosophical zombies into narrative form rather than analytic prose. I want to avoid the familiar classroom version of the thought experiment—where one simply imagines a molecule-for-molecule duplicate lacking inner experience—and instead embed it in a lived situation that raises the stakes of ontological identification. To that end, I have sketched a setting that should force the concept into unfamiliar territory: a near-future archival institute where researchers maintain a colony of "phenomenal nulls," biologically intact human analogues developed through precise neurodevelopmental suppression so that they possess full cognitive architecture, emotional expression, and linguistic competence, yet are stipulated to lack any subjective experience whatsoever. The narrative tension arises not from horror but from an epistemological asymmetry: an archivist assigned to document the nulls gradually realizes that if conceivability alone is sufficient to establish metaphysical possibility, then her own first-person conviction that she is not a null must be equally vulnerable to a symmetrical conceivability claim—namely, that she can conceive of herself as possessing experience she in fact lacks, or lacking it despite her certainty.

Please write a short story, roughly the length of a literary novella chapter, set within this institute. The protagonist should be the archivist, and the plot should turn on a single, concrete interaction—perhaps a shared meal, a collaborative translation of an ambiguous text, or a dispute over a damaged recording—through which she confronts the possibility that the gap between conceivability and actuality cannot be bridged by introspection alone. I am particularly interested in seeing the story dramatize what I have been calling "the mirror vulnerability": the idea that the very mental operation we use to conceive of zombies can be redirected upon ourselves, not to deny consciousness but to expose the fragility of the ontology we apply to others. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific philosophers by name; instead, let the philosophical pressure emerge entirely through the characters' perceptions, dialogue, and the institutional rituals that govern how experience is verified and denied. The tone should be restrained, precise, and slightly melancholy—closer to a meditation on identification than to science fiction spectacle.
