I am a retired physical chemist who has recently begun working in philosophy of technology and ethics. Next month I present to a philosophy of mind and applied ethics seminar: the audience is mostly analytic philosophers of mind skeptical of extended cognition, applied ethicists who study digital harm, and cognitive scientists who regard virtual environments as representational tools rather than ontologically loaded spaces.

My thesis is that virtual objects—persistent entities within shared virtual environments—lack ontological substance and should not be treated as entities in any robust metaphysical sense. Nevertheless, virtual harms are genuinely harmful, not in virtue of the virtual object's reality, but because sustained interaction with these environments operates as a cognitive-architectural exposure that durably restructures users' real-world affective, attentional, and social capacities—analogous, in its causal structure, to chronic environmental toxicity altering biological pathways. The restructuring occurs in the actual mind and brain; the virtual trigger need not be real for the harm to be real.
