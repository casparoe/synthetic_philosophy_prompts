I'm writing a paper for my philosophy-of-mind seminar on the temporal structure of consciousness, and I'd like to work out a reading of Michel Gondry's Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind through two frameworks: the Orwellian versus Stalinesque distinction in experiential revision, and the color phi phenomenon together with questions about the precise timing of conscious experience. In the film, Joel Barish undergoes a targeted memory-erasure procedure meant to eliminate every trace of Clementine, yet fragments persist—he hides her inside unrelated memories, and in the final beach scene the erased past seems to re-emerge after the procedure should have finished. Philosophers distinguish an Orwellian revision, in which an earlier experiential draft is overwritten so completely that no trace survives, from a Stalinesque revision, in which the original is suppressed or airbrushed but can resurface if suppression fails. Meanwhile, the color phi illusion shows that consciousness can construct apparent continuity across discrete frames, which raises the question whether the present of experience is an instantaneous slice or a temporally extended window within which revision and original content might overlap.

Could you help me think through three connected issues? First, does Joel's experience during erasure model an Orwellian or Stalinesque revision? The clinical intent looks Orwellian—total excision—but Joel's resistance and the surviving fragments suggest a Stalinesque mechanism in which suppression fails rather than overwriting succeeds. Second, the film's editing constantly jumps between disconnected spaces, reverses chronology, and forces the viewer to bridge gaps; does this formal structure mirror the color phi effect, in that the audience constructs experiential continuity across cuts that are not actually present? If so, might the film suggest that memory revision operates not by altering discrete, timestamped records but by inserting discontinuities that consciousness then smooths over? Third, if an experience of Clementine leaving can be revised after it occurs, when exactly does the revised experience take place? Is there a specious present in which original and revised content coexist, or does revision happen only retrospectively?

Please take both the philosophical distinctions and the film's own visual logic seriously—especially the scenes in which Joel buries Clementine inside childhood and unrelated adult memories, and the beach ending where the erased relationship seems to reassert itself independently of his will. I want to avoid reducing the film to a mere illustration of multiple-drafts-style theories; instead, I'm looking for where the framework genuinely illuminates a scene, where the scene complicates or resists the framework, and what the film might add to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not.
