I am a retired experimental physicist who spent much of my career designing feedback-control architectures for particle accelerators, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of mind in earnest. I keep returning to the classic discussion of sphexishness—the rigid, loop-like behavior of the digger wasp Sphex, in which interrupting its burrow-inspection routine causes the entire sequence to restart from the beginning rather than adapt to the perturbation. In my old discipline we would analyze such behavior not as a mental failure but as a dynamical feature: a system trapped in a deep attractor basin with high hysteresis, lacking a supervisory meta-controller that can re-plan when local feedback exceeds a set threshold. I want you to restate the philosophical debate about mechanical rigidity, behavioral flexibility, and free will—particularly the contrast between the reading that treats sphexishness as a deficiency of intentional architecture and the reading that treats human flexibility as merely a more complex version of the same loop structure—using only the vocabulary of dynamical systems and control theory: attractor basins, feedback gains, supervisory loops, bifurcation, and hysteresis. Be precise about which philosophical distinctions map onto which control-theoretic structures and which do not. Then provide a rigorous residue report: which claims about human freedom and normative responsibility survive this translation cleanly, which require distortion (for example, does intentionality become nothing more than a high-gain feedback loop with nested observers?), and which have no counterpart whatsoever in control architecture—especially anything involving first-person experience or moral accountability. I am trying to decide whether the residue reveals a genuine limitation of the dynamical-systems vocabulary, or whether it suggests that the philosophical distinction between mechanical and free action was never as sharp as the literature implies.
