I take the position that attributing genuine propositional attitudes—beliefs in particular—to large language models through Dennett's intentional stance is more than a predictive convenience: it tracks real patterns in the model's representational architecture, specifically the counterfactual robustness and systematic interrelation of its world-state representations, and the absence of phenomenal consciousness or biological embodiment does not undermine this attribution because neither feature was ever a necessary condition for belief in the human case. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the evidence and arguments that should actually move me, arranged by how decisive they are: empirical results about the lack of compositional structure or autonomous error-correction in these representations; conceptual arguments that belief attribution necessarily depends on participatory social practices that artificial systems cannot enter; demonstrations that the stance becomes unfalsifiable when applied to AI, since any output can be interpreted as belief-like behavior; and any formal or philosophical result showing that functional-computational similarity collapses without normative or affective dimensions that these systems lack. Please distinguish what would be merely awkward from what would be fatal, include what defenders of this position themselves would concede as counting against it, and say honestly whether the view has been insulated by qualifications into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind.
