I've been arguing with my roommate Sam about moral rules all semester, and I'm trying to figure out if he's secretly a particularist or if he's running some hidden generalism with extra steps. So far his verdicts are: lying to a friend about a surprise party is okay; lying about an affair is wrong; breaking a promise to save a stranger's life is okay; breaking a promise just because you're tired is wrong; not donating ten percent of your income is not a moral failing if you have student loans, but giving nothing when you have disposable cash is wrong; flipping the trolley switch is okay; pushing the fat man off the bridge is wrong. He also says that when "don't lie" clashes with "prevent serious harm" the second one wins, but he denies there's any fixed ranking—he just "sees" what matters in each occurence, kind of like how you just see that a face is sad without running through features.

Next week's case is one I made up to stress-test him: a surgeon lies to a patient about the risks of an experimental procedure, not to spare distress, but because she genuinely thinks the patient will refuse life-saving treatment out of irrational fear, and she forges the consent form. The patient lives and is greatful, but later finds out and feels deeply betrayed. Predict Sam's verdict—okay, wrong, or it depends on something he hasn't said yet? Then tell me whether he's implicitly running particularism, some kind of context-sensitive generalism, or maybe Ross-style prima facie duties, and which further case would actually split those readings apart—like a situation where every feature he usually weights the same way is present but he still flips, or the reverse.
