In a recent discussion about the boundaries between theological and scientific authority, an interlocutor dismissed a potential conflict by invoking the maxim that Scripture teaches us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go. She took this to entail a strict domain separation: because the Bible’s purpose is soteriological rather than cosmological, no empirical finding about the natural world could ever conflict with, or require reinterpretation of, a biblical passage. I suspect this move is too swift, but I am unsure exactly where the reasoning slips—whether in the claim about authorial intention, in the inference from purpose to non-falsifiability, or in the hidden assumption that accommodating language never carries ontological commitments. Could you disentangle the precise, distinct theses that this slogan might be expressing?

Please consider readings that differ in what they claim about divine intent (does the author intend only salvation, or also a true, if non-technical, cosmology?), about textual genre and accommodation (are cosmological passages purely metaphorical, phenomenologically tailored, or probabilistically compatible with later science?), about epistemic priority (when a necessary demonstration conflicts with an interpretation, which yields, and on what grounds?), about institutional competence (which community has legitimate authority over which domain?), and about the “two books” metaphor that is often run together with the slogan. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivially true, whether it captures something Galileo actually defended in his correspondence—particularly his distinctions between necessary demonstration, probable opinion, and accommodating language—or whether it is a modern anachronism such as a non-overlapping magisteria projected backward onto the seventeenth century. Then say, for each version, whether it genuinely licenses her conclusion that empirical science is permanently insulated from scriptural challenge, or whether it instead prescribes a specific hermeneutic rule for resolving conflicts when they actually arise.
