I belong to a small reading group that has met fortnightly in a member’s flat for several years. Over time we have developed an unwritten practice: if, during discussion of the next month’s proposed book, any member utters the phrase “I’m pulling the cord”, the proposal is immediately abandoned and we move to the next candidate. Last Tuesday, a member who was travelling and could not attend sent precisely this phrase in our group message thread at half past eleven at night. The following morning, half the group treated the proposal as withdrawn, while the other half insisted that because nobody was physically present to witness the utterance, the conditions for withdrawal had not been satisfied. The absent member maintains that the words themselves, once transmitted, carry the binding force regardless of the setting in which they appear.

I recently encountered some philosophy of language in an evening course, and it made me wonder whether this disagreement is genuinely about interpreting our existing practice, or about whether that practice can legitimately extend to digital messages sent outside the meeting room. Could you help me analyse the philosophical structure of the situation? Specifically, I want to understand whether an utterance can possess institutional force independently of the circumstances in which it is received, what distinguishes a failed attempt at such an act from a successful one, and whether resolving our dispute requires us to uncover an implicit convention or to establish a new one explicitly. I am not asking for a simple ruling on who is right, but for an account of what makes language capable of altering collective obligations in the first place, and whether our group now needs to formalise the scope of the phrase rather than relying on past usage alone.
