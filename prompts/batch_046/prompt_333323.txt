In my upper-division history of science seminar we have been examining the long-running dispute over whether grand narratives of human development—exemplified by Harari’s Sapiens and Diamond’s Guns, Germs, and Steel—have been genuinely undermined by Graeber and Wengrow’s The Dawn of Everything, or whether the apparent conflict masks a deeper conceptual divergence. The debate has persisted for years despite both sides appealing to the same archaeological and ethnographic literature, and my working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not primarily empirical but conceptual: specifically, that “hierarchy” and “inequality” function as umbrella terms rather than unified theoretical variables, so that critics and defenders are measuring different phenomena across societies separated by millennia and then treating the measurements as commensurable. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there instead a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about the permissible standards of causal inference in deep history, or about which cases count as genuine data versus anecdote—doing the substantive work? I want an honest structural diagnosis rather than a verdict or survey: do the sides disagree about facts, about evidentiary standards, about which starting intuitions regarding human agency are legitimate, or are they simply answering different questions under one vocabulary? Given whichever diagnosis you offer, what would actually count as progress—a new comparative framework for anthropology, a precise conceptual distinction that dissolves the opposition, an empirical finding that forces convergence, or is the impasse a sign that the framing question is ill-posed? I am trying to understand whether anything short of abandoning the comparative project could resolve it, and if not, why not.
