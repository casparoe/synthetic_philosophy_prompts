Consider the problem Pascal raises—whether justice lacking force remains powerless, and whether force lacking justice becomes tyrannical. I want to test whether a single principle can capture my judgments about when the two combine legitimately and when they corrupt each other. Here are five concrete cases and my verdicts.

Case 1 — The internalized caste. For three centuries, Society A has enforced a hereditary hierarchy through law and custom. The system is now widely internalized; even many disadvantaged members describe it as "the natural order" and resist reform. Verdict: It remains unjust, but it is not pure brute tyranny. It possesses a false, derivative legitimacy—what I am tempted to call pseudo-justice—because recognition has replaced fear as the primary mechanism of obedience, even though the original normative claim was never correct.

Case 2 — The unclaimed right. Society B recognizes an abstract principle of basic autonomy. A small minority is systematically denied it, but they lack any institutional power; their protests are ignored and they cannot enforce redress. Verdict: The denial is fully unjust. The absence of enforcement power on their side does not diminish the injustice, and the presence of coercive power on the side of the oppressors does not create any justice in what they do.

Case 3 — The engineered equality. By sudden decree and sustained coercion, Society C imposes a radical redistribution of property based on a philosophical theory of equality that had no prior popular support. After two generations, the arrangement is stable and widely endorsed. Verdict: The initial imposition was unjust, because force was not serving a pre-existing justice but manufacturing it ex nihilo. The present widespread endorsement does not retroactively justify the origin, though it may establish a separate, current justice.

Case 4 — The defended principle. Societies D and E share identical moral principles, but only D possesses the military and legal capacity to defend those principles against internal corruption and external aggression. Verdict: D is more fully just than E—not because its principles are superior, but because justice seems incomplete without the capacity for realization and protection. Yet E's principles are not less correct for lacking power.

Case 5 — The perpetual emergency. A revolutionary government overthrows a tyrant, establishes just laws, and then retains an emergency security apparatus for fifty years, claiming that justice is fragile and requires constant, forceful defense. Verdict: The revolution was just; the perpetual emergency is unjust. Once defensive force outlives its protective purpose and becomes self-sustaining, justice transforms into domination, regardless of the rulers' intentions.

These verdicts feel mostly coherent, but there is tension. Case 2 insists that justice exists independently of power, while Case 4 insists that justice is somehow deficient without it. Case 1 allows long-term recognition to generate derivative legitimacy, while Case 3 denies that recognition can justify an origin in pure coercion.

My tentative principle is this: justice has two dimensions—normative correctness, which is independent of power, and practical realization, which requires recognized, bounded force. Force is legitimate only when it protects or realizes normative correctness that is already valid; it is illegitimate when it creates correctness, perpetuates itself indefinitely, or operates without genuine recognition. Over time, sustained recognition can generate a secondary, derivative legitimacy, but only if the original normative claim was at least plausible, and never if it was purely fabricated.

Second, test it against further cases I have not mentioned: for example, what if outsiders with no stake in a claim use force to enforce it? What if a false claim is enforced and eventually sincerely believed? Third, state honestly which of my five verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without distortion, and whether saving that verdict requires complicating the principle or dropping the verdict. Fourth, if the principle needs repair, offer one or two revised candidates—each with its motivating case, its predictions for unconsidered cases, and the verdict it must sacrifice. I am not looking for a bibliography, reading list, or citation list; I want the conceptual work done directly from the cases and the logic of the principle itself.
