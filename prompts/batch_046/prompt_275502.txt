After an extended argument with a friend about whether the therapy/enhancement distinction can survive for psychological traits, I am second-guessing my grading of an exam in my philosophy of medicine seminar. Below is the short-answer prompt I assigned and one student's response. Please grade the answer with a specific score and justify that score point by point, identifying precisely where the reasoning is sound and where it slips—particularly any conflation between descriptive and normative baselines, any misstep in the argument about cognitive enhancement, or any unsupported claim about context-sensitive calibration. Do not advise me on my debate with my friend; evaluate only the student's work.

Question: Some philosophers argue that the therapy/enhancement distinction collapses for psychological traits—such as emotional responsiveness or working-memory capacity—because they lack a clear species-typical functional baseline. Evaluate this claim. In your answer: (a) distinguish descriptive (biostatistical) from normative ways of drawing the line; (b) explain why cognitive enhancement poses a special problem for at least one of these approaches; and (c) assess whether replacing fixed internal norms with a relational baseline—what we might call "affective calibration" to environmental demands—rescues the distinction or dissolves it further.

Student answer: The claim is half correct. On a descriptive biostatistical view, health combines statistical normality within a reference class with contribution to survival and reproduction. For emotional range or memory, reference classes are unstable—norms shift across cultures and occupations—so therapy and enhancement blur together descriptively. Normative accounts avoid this entirely, because they do not depend on statistical baselines; they ask only whether an intervention restores functioning required for fair equality of opportunity. Cognitive enhancement is specially problematic for normative accounts because it creates competitive advantages that shift the social baseline: once enhancement is available, the range needed for fair opportunity moves upward, turning prior therapy into inadequacy. This does not collapse the distinction, but it forces normative accounts to index their baseline dynamically. Meanwhile, affective calibration—tuning emotional response to context rather than to an internal norm—actually rescues the descriptive view by substituting a functional-environmental match for the missing species-typical baseline. Thus the distinction survives, though it must abandon fixed internal norms in favor of relational ones.
