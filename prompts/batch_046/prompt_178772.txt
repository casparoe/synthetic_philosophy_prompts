I am defending the following thesis in a forty-five-minute seminar at an interdisciplinary institute for memory studies and transitional ethics: that thick moral relations generate a genuine collective duty to remember the dead, but that institutional archives in post-conflict societies ought to incorporate structured forgetting protocols—time-bound preservation windows, negotiated redaction of certain survivor testimonies, and expiration clauses for state-mandated commemorative records—rather than pursue total digital retention. I argue from Margalit’s distinction between thick and thin moral relations, holding that remembrance obligations weaken when bureaucratized, together with Rieff’s claim that modern therapeutic culture transforms remembrance into emotional management for the living, thereby failing the dead. I apply this to contend that current transitional-justice archives reproduce exactly the therapeutic dynamic Rieff criticized: they preserve everything for present psychological closure, not for fidelity to past victims.

The audience is precisely composed: half are archival scientists and museum curators who regard preservation as technically neutral and chain-of-custody integrity as non-negotiable; a third are transitional-justice philosophers for whom remembrance is an absolute, non-derogable moral injunction summed up in the maxim never forget; and two senior theorists know Margalit and Rieff well but are skeptical of applied ethics. They will phrase objections in the vocabulary of archival science (provenance, data integrity, irreversibility of deletion), transitional justice (the right to truth, survivor autonomy, state accountability), and critical theory (the risk that structured forgetting becomes neoliberal erasure).

Prepare me for the Q&A. Deliver a prep sheet, not a full defense: order the objections by likelihood and by damage if mishandled. For each, state the objection as that audience segment would actually phrase it; indicate what I should concede up front and what I must hold; provide a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes; and flag the weakest point in that reply. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely from the transitional-justice philosophers—phrased exactly as they would put it, with the reply I must have ready but prefer not to deploy. The procedure should be usable by a speaker reasoning only from the thesis, the well-known frameworks of Margalit and Rieff, and common knowledge in memory studies and archival ethics. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations to recent obscure papers, or references to case studies and theories that lie outside standard training exposure; do not quote any real text verbatim. Make the reply honest about where judgment replaces argument and about the one point in my position that is genuinely soft.
