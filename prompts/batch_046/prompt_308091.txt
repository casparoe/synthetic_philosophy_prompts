As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I have been trying to understand why the absent-minded driver problem has stalled for so long without resolution. The scenario is familiar enough: a driver needs to reach a destination by taking the second highway exit, which yields the highest payoff; taking the first exit yields a slightly lower payoff; failing to exit yields nothing. The driver is absent-minded in the precise sense that upon reaching the first exit he cannot tell whether he is at exit one or exit two, and he knows this limitation about himself in advance. The puzzle is that ex ante he prefers a strategy of continuing past the first exit, yet ex post, upon reaching either exit, he reasons that continuing is worse than exiting now—so he never takes an exit and receives the worst outcome.

What I see in the literature is a recurring exchange between two camps that never seem to converge. One camp treats rationality as a property of the global plan or specification: the agent should select, at the outset, the strategy that maximizes expected utility over the entire decision tree, which in this case means committing to continue past the first exit. The other camp treats rationality as a property of local execution: at each node the agent should do what is best given his beliefs and information at that node, which means exiting immediately upon arrival. Each side has standard replies—the planners invoke commitment devices, conditional intentions, or the authority of the prior self; the executors invoke the principle that rationality requires maximizing conditional on one's actual location—but the debate feels circular rather than progressive.

My own hypothesis, shaped by working with distributed systems, is that the disagreement may not be about rationality at all, but about what kind of object the agent is assumed to be. The planning camp seems to treat the agent as a single process with shared state across nodes, capable of enforcing a global invariant over the whole tree. The execution camp treats the agent as a stateless local node with no shared memory between instances, forced to act only on immediate inputs. If that is correct, then asking "what should the driver do?" is ill-posed without first specifying the system architecture, and the two sides may be answering different questions—one about global consistency, one about local optimality—under a single heading.

Can you diagnose the stall in these terms? Is the disagreement verbal, turning on different senses of "rational" or "should"; is it substantive, about whether commitment is genuinely possible or normatively required; or is it a hidden architectural disagreement disguised as a normative one? Are there empirical claims about human cognition or about the feasibility of self-binding mechanisms doing unstated work? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a formal result showing that local and global optimality are either equivalent or irreducibly distinct in this structure; a conceptual distinction that dissolves the paradox by clarifying the agent model; or an argument that the scenario is itself incoherent? If you think the impasse is bedrock—rooted in incompatible starting intuitions about agency—explain why further argument should not be expected to move it.
