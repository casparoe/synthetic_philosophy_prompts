I'm drafting a one-semester dissertation prospectus—roughly eight thousand words—on a tension I see in Douglas Hofstadter's claim that an "I" can be distributed across brains. The standard literature on distributed cognition asks whether cognitive processes extend beyond the skull, but Hofstadter's strange-loop model goes further: it treats the self as a symbolic pattern that can be partially instantiated in other minds, as in his well-known discussion of how the patterns constituting his late wife's self continued to exist within his own brain after her death. What remains unclear is whether this distribution creates a serious individuation problem. If two brains gradually share more of the loop-structure—say, through deep neural interface or prolonged shared experience—there should be intermediate cases in which neither clearly hosts a distinct self, yet Hofstadter's framework offers no explicit criterion for where one loop ends and another begins. Existing functionalist and narrative-identity accounts either reject distribution outright or accommodate it without addressing this boundary vagueness, so the question is genuinely open and not settled by standard metaphysics of mind.

I want the prospectus to investigate whether Hofstadter's thesis entails a sorites-style problem for personal identity, and whether that vagueness is a feature of his view or a defect that can be repaired without abandoning the core idea. To do this, I plan to inject a specific conceptual distinction—between structural isomorphism, which permits distribution across substrates, and functional closure, which individuates loops—asking whether both are necessary for selfhood or whether one can do the work alone. The method should combine conceptual analysis of loop-closure conditions with a constructed thought-experiment involving gradual inter-brain linking, rather than relying on empirical neuroscience or standard intuition pumps about brain transplants. The proposal should state the precise question, explain why current answers leave it unresolved, justify this conceptual approach over purely empirical or metaphysical alternatives, specify what a positive result looks like (either a principled individuation threshold or a rigorous demonstration that none is required without the view collapsing back into functionalism), state honest failure conditions (if the isomorphism/closure distinction collapses or if the argument becomes empirically untestable), and map the work onto a realistic one-semester timeline with monthly milestones.
