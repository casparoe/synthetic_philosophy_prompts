I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun hosting an interview series for an audience of scientifically trained readers with no specialist background in philosophy of religion. For the next episode, I will be speaking with an unnamed philosopher—let us call the position she defends “interval-sensitive karma”—who argues that karma should be reconstructed not as a metaphysical law of rebirth governed by cosmic accounting, but as a secular, normative theory of moral desert in which the temporal gap between a wrongful action and its deserved consequence is itself morally loaded, rather than merely a causal delivery mechanism. I want a structured sequence of interview questions that avoids biographical preamble and presses directly on the conceptual architecture of this view, treating it as a live philosophical option rather than an anthropological curiosity.

Please include questions covering the following tensions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. First, ask how she grounds the identity of the moral subject who receives karmic consequence when that recipient is psychologically discontinuous with the original agent—whether through ordinary aging, trauma, or the traditional rebirth framework—given her insistence that mere psychological continuity is insufficient. The follow-up should anticipate her appeal to narrative or practical identity and ask whether that appeal collapses the distinction between karmic desert and ordinary social punishment distributed across generations. Second, ask whether her view is vulnerable to a form of moral luck: if the length and qualitative character of the interval are partly determined by contingent external factors—social structures, random events, biological fortune—does that not infect the severity or form of deserved suffering in precisely the way retributivists try to avoid? The follow-up should target her probable distinction between “interval-contingency,” which she accepts, and “base-contingency,” which she rejects, and ask whether that distinction can be maintained without circularity. Third, include a question comparing her framework to Western retributivism: does the interval-sensitive feature make karma more demanding, or does it allow for forms of desert that retributivism cannot accommodate? A brief follow-up should ask what practical institutional design—say, in legal sentencing or restorative justice—would actually implement such a view without collapsing into mere deterrence. Finally, I need a closing question asking what kind of evidence—phenomenological, neuroscientific, or logical—could in principle undermine the claim that moral desert is genuinely sensitive to temporal interval, now that she has abandoned the metaphysical machinery of rebirth and cosmic accounting. The questions should be suitable for listeners without training in Asian philosophy, should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should be polished enough for a formal colloquium setting.
