Question: Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument is standardly taken to threaten mental causation for non-reductive physicalists. First, reconstruct the argument from its two premises—causal closure of the physical domain and the exclusion principle—through to the dilemma it poses for anyone who accepts token-physicalism but denies type-identity. Second, consider a reply that proposes "diachronic closure": rather than requiring a sufficient physical cause for every physical effect at the same instantaneous slice, closure is satisfied only over extended temporal intervals, which allegedly leaves intermediate moments open for mental causation without producing overdetermination. Assess whether this reply actually blocks Kim's conclusion or whether it misplaces the pressure point.

Student answer:
Kim's argument starts with causal closure: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause occurring at the same time. He pairs this with the exclusion principle, which says that if one cause is already sufficient for an effect, a second sufficient cause is redundant and produces implausible systematic overdetermination unless the two are identical. Non-reductive physicalists hold that mental properties supervene on physical properties without being reducible to them. So when a belief—say, that coffee is desirable—causes an arm to reach for a cup, both the belief and its neural realizer are present and each appears sufficient. That yields overdetermination, which is unacceptable.

The diachronic reply weakens closure by making it interval-relative rather than instantaneous. On this view, physics guarantees a sufficient cause for an effect at t3 only across the span from t1 to t3; at the intermediate point t2 there is no fully sufficient physical cause. Consequently, a mental event at t2 can cause the movement at t3 without competing with a simultaneous physical sufficient cause. This blocks Kim's dilemma because the exclusion principle applies only when two sufficient causes coincide, and here they do not coincide in time.

This reply succeeds. Kim's formulation assumes synchronic closure, but that is merely one interpretation of physical completeness. Once we allow diachronic closure, there is no overdetermination at any slice, since the physical story is distributed. Furthermore, Kim's exclusion principle is essentially Davidson's anomalous monism recast in causal terms—both deny that mental events can be strictly law-governed by physics unless mental and physical are identical—so weakening instantaneous closure undermines the premise that generates the conflict. Therefore, non-reductive physicalism can preserve mental causation without accepting type-identity.
