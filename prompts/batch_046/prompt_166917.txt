Here is the exam question that was given:

Raphael Lemkin coined the term genocide in his 1944 study, proposing a broad concept of group destruction that included cultural and social dimensions alongside physical killing. Compare Lemkin’s original conception with the definition adopted in the 1948 UN Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, paying particular attention to what was narrowed or lost—especially regarding protected categories and the place of cultural destruction. Then discuss how the politics of naming an atrocity genocide rather than ethnic cleansing or war crimes shapes both moral response and legal obligation. Use either the Armenian case or the Rwandan case as your example, and be precise about whether the central obstacle to recognition is evidentiary, conceptual, or geopolitical.

Here is the student answer to be graded:

Lemkin introduced genocide in 1944 to describe coordinated destruction of a group’s essential foundations, and he initially emphasized physical and biological annihilation—killing members, preventing births, and destroying livelihoods—rather than cultural destruction. The 1948 UN Convention adopted a narrower legal framework that protects only national, ethnical, racial, and religious groups, but interestingly it preserved and even expanded Lemkin’s cultural concerns through Article II(e) on the forcible transfer of children. So the Convention is broader culturally but narrower in group coverage, since political and social groups were dropped despite being in Lemkin’s original draft.

On the politics of naming, take the Armenian genocide. The debate over whether to apply the term is largely an evidentiary dispute about dolus specialis—the specific intent to destroy Armenians as a group—because restricted archives make direct proof of intent difficult to establish beyond reasonable doubt. By contrast, ethnic cleansing is essentially genocide minus the specific intent requirement, so labeling the Armenian case genocide is mainly a moral upgrade rather than a distinct legal category. Therefore, resistance to the name reflects legitimate epistemic caution rather than pure denialism.
