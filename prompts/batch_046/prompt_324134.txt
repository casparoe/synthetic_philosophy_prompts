I've just moved back to my hometown and there's this wierd thing the council does with dodgy historical figures. They renamed a road near my flat within months of a petition — new signs, maps updated, the lot — but the big bronze statue of the exact same bloke in the central park just got a small plaque with a QR code saying he was 'a man of his time'. Meanwhile, they moved a different statue into storage immediately, but that one was of a trader who'd never actually lived round here; they kept the local mill owner's statue even though he ran the same kind of operation. So local connection seems to matter, and putting information on a plinth counts as enough, but putting the name on a signpost doesn't.

What philosophical commitments does that pattern presuppose? Does it rely on a distinction between vertical monuments and horizontal signage — maybe an implicit theory of 'moral zoning' where height and location change what kind of public endorsement you're making, like a seperate ethical register for plinths versus postcodes? Or is there a hidden view about collective memory, that remembering in place is different from naming after? And why does being local make the harm easier to contextualise for a statue but not for a street?

I'm trying to work out if the practice is even consistent. If the goal is not to celebrate cruelty, why does living nearby change anything? If the goal is just not to forget, why rename the road at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory of commemoration here — is it about spatial loyalty, about who belongs to the city's narrative, or about something else? Tell me whether the council's rules actually need one specific philosophy, or whether several rival views could license the same mess. And if we replaced whatever hidden view is driving this with a simpler one — say, that any public marker which highlights a figure is an endorsement regardless of postcode or plinth height — what exactly would have to change in how they run these consultations?
