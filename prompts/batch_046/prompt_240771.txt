You are a senior moral philosopher specialising in value pluralism, hard choices, and institutional ethics, with particular fluency in the literature on incommensurability and practical reasoning. Before you answer, take a moment to brainstorm the main theoretical positions that could consistently explain a track record of verdicts on conflicts between incommensurable values — for instance, strong non-comparability, lexical ordering with a dignity threshold, context-sensitive practical reasoning, or a more specific hybrid such as institutional-logic pluralism. Think carefully about where these rules diverge, especially when quantitative trade-offs or procedural resolutions are proposed.

Below is the published record of verdicts given by Dr. A. Lindqvist, chair of a municipal ethics review board, across five case assessments. In each, she ruled whether the proposed resolution was ethically acceptable and whether aggregate cost-benefit or preference-ranking analysis was appropriate.

1. A city council must choose between demolishing a seventeenth-century market hall to build climate-adaptive housing for two hundred families, or preserving the hall and building elsewhere at triple cost. Verdict: unacceptable to demolish; quantitative cost-benefit analysis is inappropriate here because heritage preservation and housing provision belong to incommensurable institutional logics.

2. A hospital has one ICU bed; the choice is between a renowned composer aged 68 and an unknown teenager aged 16, both with equal medical prognosis. Verdict: acceptable to resolve by lottery; individual dignity does not license quantitative comparison of lives, but procedural equality is required when no further value-relevant distinction applies.

3. A university must decide whether to maintain a merit-only scholarship that excludes disadvantaged groups, or to introduce targeted support that explicitly considers socio-economic background but lowers the formal merit threshold. Verdict: acceptable to introduce targeted support; equality of opportunity can be structurally corrected even when merit and distributive justice operate in different value domains.

4. A prison reform pilot, modelled partly on Halden’s normalisation principle and partly on American rehabilitative programmes, allows inmates to wear civilian clothes and cook their own meals, but requires them to participate in profitable private-sector labour contracts with no wage protection. Verdict: unacceptable; normalisation and economic exploitation belong to incompatible institutional frameworks, and combining them produces a category mistake rather than a genuine compromise.

5. A national arts fund must choose between funding a single monumental public artwork of lasting cultural significance, or distributing the same sum as small grants to one hundred local community projects with no lasting monument. Verdict: acceptable either way, provided the decision is made through participatory deliberation rather than aggregate preference ranking; the values are genuinely incomparable, but democratic procedure can legitimate either outcome without reducing one value to the other.

Next week, the board must assess a new proposal: a city introduces a universal basic income pilot that treats every resident identically, ignoring historical patterns of dispossession, but simultaneously creates a parallel, race-conscious reparative housing scheme that uses explicit demographic categories in law. The two programmes are mutually exclusive due to budget constraints; the city can adopt only one.

Predict Dr. Lindqvist’s verdict on which programme, if either, should be adopted. Say which theoretical framework best explains her full track record — strong incommensurability, lexical ordering, practical reasoning, or something more specific such as institutional-logic pluralism — and justify your choice by showing how it accounts for both case 4 and case 5. Then specify the single further case that would definitively distinguish your preferred framework from its closest rival. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason entirely from the conceptual structure of the cases.
