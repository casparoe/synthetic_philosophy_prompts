Compose a villanelle — nineteen lines arranged in five tercets followed by a quatrain, observing the ABA rhyme scheme and employing two refrains that appear in alternating lines and finally together in the closing couplet — on the categorical problem of what makes every lowercase 'a' an 'a' across typefaces, handwriting styles, and partial distortions. The philosophical tension should be structurally embodied: let one refrain articulate the essentialist claim that all instances share a single defining feature, and let the other refrain articulate the family-resemblance claim that 'a'-ness is only a web of overlapping similarities with no common core. Proceed through concrete visual particulars — the enclosed counter, the terminal stroke, the presence or absence of an ascender, the curvature that distinguishes it from neighboring letters — but design the poem's turn so that in the final quatrain the two refrains sit adjacent and must be read as describing one and the same perceptual act from opposite directions: the essence of the letter is not a positive stroke but a precise constellation of absences, the specific ways it refuses to become an 'o', a 'd', or an 'e'. To move the idea away from familiar textbook formulations, build in the concept of 'ghost strokes' — the imaginary lines that the visual system completes when actual ink is missing, broken, or obscured — and let these ghost strokes govern the hidden internal rhythm and rhyme of the poem, so that the formal structure itself performs the cognitive completion that categorical recognition requires. The result should be intelligible as poetry to someone who has never encountered the Letter Spirit problem, yet someone familiar with that problem should recognize the rendering of style, category, and letterform essence without finding it merely named. Do not request citations, bibliographies, or further reading; carry all conceptual weight through imagery, refrain, and form alone.
