As a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy in earnest, I have become struck by an asymmetry in how institutions manage commemorative naming. In public spaces—parks, university quadrangles, and municipal streets—many bodies now practice what might be called contextual retention: a statue or street name honoring a historical figure is preserved, but paired with an interpretive plaque that records the subject's moral failures, so that the monument no longer functions as unqualified praise. By contrast, in the physical sciences, eponymous laws, constants, and instruments are embedded in textbooks, lecture protocols, and technical vocabulary as bare designations, almost never subjected to analogous contextualization—even when the biographical record of the namesake includes conduct that the institution's own commemorative ethics would condemn. What philosophical commitments about the separation of epistemic achievement from moral biography does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Must it invoke a hierarchical separation—something like an insulated domain of scientific knowledge that should not be tangled with ethical evaluation—or can it be sustained without any such stratification? And is the asymmetry philosophically consistent with the framework the same institutions apply in negligence and attribution, where harm and responsibility are not so cleanly compartmentalized?
