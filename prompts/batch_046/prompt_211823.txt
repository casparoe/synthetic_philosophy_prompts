In our philosophy of science seminar we have been discussing Milton Friedman’s F-twist—the claim that unrealistic assumptions in a scientific model are harmless provided the model yields accurate predictions. I want to understand a conceptual tension that was only briefly mentioned: the distinction between predictive adequacy and counterfactual robustness. Take the standard idealization of frictionless air flow in climate dynamics. The assumption is manifestly false, yet the model predicts large-scale temperature trends well, which seems to vindicate Friedman. But now suppose we use that same model to evaluate a counterfactual intervention—say, introducing localized turbulence at a sub-grid scale. The unrealistic assumption about frictionlessness may not corrupt the predictive output for global averages, yet it might systematically mislead about how the system would respond to that perturbation, because the causal structure governing turbulence is precisely what was idealized away. Can you explain, conceptually, why the F-twist might survive in the predictive register but fail in the counterfactual register? I am not asking for a bibliography or formal proof; I want a clear articulation of what feature of unrealistic idealization makes it benign for forecasting but dangerous for interventionist reasoning, and whether this means Friedman’s defense needs to be restricted to a specific class of scientific aims.
