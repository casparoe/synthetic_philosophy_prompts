Here's an argument I cookd up after skimming Leibniz and now I can't find the exit. It feels like it proves we should never cure anyone, which is obviously wrong.

(1) God picks the world with the greatest total of compossible goods — call that W.
(2) If W is best, then for any specific evil e inside it, removing e either breaks compossibility with the remaining goods or yields a world that's worse overall.
(3) So every actual evil is stuck into the optimal package; you can't pull one thread without tearing the fabric.
(4) Human action happens inside W, not outside it. When a doctor cures a disease, she isn't jumping to another world; she's altering how W unfolds.
(5) But if W was acutally the best unfolding, then her altering it pushes reality away from the optimal trajectory.
(6) By the same reasoning, every successful intervention — diplomacy, fire rescue, climate policy — makes the world deviate from its best path.
(7) So if Leibniz is right, moral success literally worsens the world.
(8) That's absurd, so one step must be wrong.

I want to keep (1) and (3) if possible — they feel like the core of the view. Is the mistake in (4), treating agency as internal? In (5), assuming local changes affect global optimality? Or is (2) wrong that local removals are always blocked? What's the cost of dropping the weakest one?
