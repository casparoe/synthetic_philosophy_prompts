hey, i run a philosophy workshop in the library basement, about 12 people mostly grad students plus a couple undergrads, 90 minutes every tuesday. im retired from microbiology so i still think in false positives and replication failure. i want an activity that makes them feel the actual tension between inside view and outside view, and especially why epistemic modesty is painful - you have to discount evidence you personally gathered.

design a runnable game with rules and payoffs. split them into pairs and give each pair a sealed envelope called a 'lab notebook' describing one study they supposedly ran. the card lists inside details: 'my grad student was unusually carefull,' 'the effect was 3x larger than prior work,' 'i preregistered,' or 'p was 0.047 and i peeked at the data once.' they get 5 minutes to rate 0-100 how likely their result is real and write a one-sentence justification - but they dont name the philosophical concept yet.

then reveal the outside view: in this fictional field, 65 percent of published results fail replication, and in the aggregate data none of those inside features actually predict success. now they bet 'credibility chips' - each pair starts with 100. they choose PUBLISH (win 300 chips if it replicates, lose everything if it fails) or HOLD (keep chips but lose 20 prestige points). the hidden twist: half the envelopes secretly contain one genuinely predictive feature (actual double-blinding with large N) but it looks identical to the noise features on the other cards. after bets are locked, roll dice for replication and reveal which envelopes had the real feature.

the debrief should map the initial rating onto inside-view reasoning, the aggregate stats onto outside view, and the betting pain onto epistemic modesty. say what predictably goes wrong - people overvalue their card details, some pairs will be angry that 'good science' lost chips because of base rates. be frank about what the game cant reproduce: in real research you never open the envelope to check if you had the hidden feature, so the facilitator must supply that permanent uncertainty verbally.

no lectures up front, dont ask for citations or reading lists, and please dont tell them the names 'inside view' or 'outside view' until after the betting round. they should walk in cold.
