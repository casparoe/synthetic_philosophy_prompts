Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains, A1 and A2, and only two rhyme sounds—on Karl Jaspers’s distinction between political guilt and moral guilt as it bears on collective responsibility across generations. Do not use the words “guilt,” “nation,” “crime,” “Jaspers,” “generation,” “inherit,” or “collective” anywhere in the poem; render the two categories solely through concrete imagery and the formal logic of the refrains. Make A1 the voice of assignable, institutional liability—something like “We kept the ledgers, we sealed the borders”—and make A2 the voice of non-transferable personal accountability—something like “My hands never signed, my name is not there.” The five tercets should alternate these refrains so that each stanza forces them into proximity with different intervening lines: the first three building images of structural continuity (archives, taxes, monuments, citizenship papers), the last two showing the collapse of that continuity into individual conscience. The volta must occur in the final quatrain, where A1 and A2 appear together for the first and only time; they should sit side by side in the closing lines without resolving into either pure collective blame or pure individual innocence, and the rhyme scheme should make them sound like fragments of a single sentence even as their meanings remain irreconcilable. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Jaspers’s Schuldfrage can identify which refrain tracks political guilt—assignable to the collective, remediable through institutional action, not requiring personal wrongdoing—and which tracks moral guilt—individual, non-delegable, not transmitted by membership alone—but a reader without that background should still feel the structural tension between inherited institution and personal innocence. Keep the diction plain, the images historical rather than mythic, and the form strict: no deviation from the villanelle’s repetition pattern.
