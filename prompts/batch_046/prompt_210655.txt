I'm working through my intuitions about when parts make up a single thing, and I'd like to see whether there's a principle behind them or whether some have to be revised.

Case 1: An ordinary oak table in a kitchen. Verdict: one object.

Case 2: Two identical tables pushed flush together, back-to-back, with no fastening, glue, or shared parts. Verdict: two objects, not one.

Case 3: A mature oak tree that had a limb from a different oak grafted onto it twenty years ago; the graft is now fully integrated, with shared vascular tissue and continuous growth. Verdict: still one tree.

Case 4: A human being whose every biological cell has been gradually replaced by functionally identical synthetic analogues over a decade; consciousness, memory, and behaviour are continuous throughout. Verdict: one persisting person.

Case 5: A cloud of dust particles suspended in a vacuum chamber, arranged by lasers into the exact shape and density distribution of a table, but with no chemical bonds, causal interactions, or forces between the particles beyond being held in position. Verdict: no table; only arranged dust.

Case 6: A murmuration of starlings that, for about forty minutes, maintains a stable toroidal shape in the sky; individual birds constantly enter and leave, but the overall form and flight dynamics persist. Verdict: there is no toroidal object; there are only birds.

Case 7: A distributed computing cluster whose nodes are physically arranged in a cubic lattice in a server room; they only run the unified programme when arranged in that geometry. If the nodes are scattered across cities but networked wirelessly, the programme still runs, yet the cubic physical arrangement is lost. Verdict: when arranged in the cube, there is one composite system (the cluster-in-configuration); virtual connectivity alone is not enough for the physical composite.

Case 8: A statue carved from glacier ice that is continuously melting at the surface and being replaced by fresh ice carved by an automated system, preserving the form precisely. Verdict: one statue over time, despite complete material turnover.

My difficulty is reconciling Case 3 with Case 6: both involve biological parts integrating into a functional whole with constant turnover, yet I call one a single thing and deny it for the other. Likewise, Case 4 and Case 5 both involve arranged parts lacking traditional bonding, but I accept one and reject the other.

Is there a precise principle — or a small family of principles — that captures all eight verdicts? If so, state it clearly, indicate which case motivates each clause, and apply it to two further situations I have not judged: (a) a coral reef where living polyps cover dead calcium skeletons built by prior generations, functioning as one ecological unit; and (b) a "table" made of two separate wooden slabs that share a single central leg screwed into both. Then be honest about which of my eight verdicts your principle cannot accommodate without contortion, and whether I should abandon that verdict or complicate the principle.

Please write in a reflective, equilibrium-seeking style, not as a literature survey.
