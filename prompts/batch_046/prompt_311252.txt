"my country, right or wrong" keeps getting tossed around in my poli phil seminar whenever someone tries to defend critical patriotism. last week the prof used it to shut down a q about whether loving your country requires being able to call it wrong. i think the slogan is doing at least four seperate jobs and nobody's saying which.

can you lay out the precise claims it might express? i'm thinking: (1) a descriptive claim about affective attachment, (2) a normative claim that state membership generates special obligations regardless of merit, (3) a particularist claim that 'wrongness' doesn't apply inside the patriotic relation the way it does outside, (4) a performative claim about solidarity under attack, and maybe (5) a conceptual claim that you can't really identify with a polity and simultaneously reject its actions?

for each, say whether it's trivial, defensible, false, or actually vicious. and tell me which readings actually support dismissing critical patriotism vs which ones just sound like they do. i'm writing this up for a thing on whether loyalty can be rationally demanded at all, so if there's an equivocation between "loyalty" and "uncritical deference" hiding in the phrase, point it out - especially whether reading (3) collapses patriotism into tribal preference or if there's space to keep the particularism without losing the virtue part.
