I'm preparing a graduate seminar on causal explanation in macro-history, and I find the standard "great man versus structures" framing unsatisfactorily vague. I want to press it into a technically sharper comparison. Compare the possible-worlds counterfactual framework common in analytic philosophy of history—where historical counterfactuals are evaluated through similarity relations between worlds and tracked for necessity or sufficiency—with the structural-overdetermination model drawn from historical sociology, in which outcomes are treated as multiply realized by converging institutional, economic, and cultural conditions that render individual agents replaceable.

Apply both specifically to the counterfactual removal of Adolf Hitler from interwar Germany. Under the counterfactual framework, what counts as the relevant similarity metric for comparison worlds, how should we weigh Hitler's distinctive oratorical and organizational capacities against the persistence of the NSDAP apparatus and Weimar's institutional fragility, and does the framework yield a binary answer or allow graded counterfactual dependence? Under the overdetermination model, how do we assess whether interwar crisis dynamics—reparations trauma, paramilitary culture, presidential emergency powers—already formed a sufficient structural vector for an authoritarian nationalist regime, and in what precise sense can Hitler be deemed "replaceable" if no other figure possessed exactly his coalition-building abilities?

Finally, evaluate which apparatus is more adequate: does the counterfactual framework illegitimately individualize a collective process, or does the overdetermination model obscure real causal differences by treating structural conditions as fully fungible?
