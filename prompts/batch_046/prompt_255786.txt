Within the historiographical framework I'm calling 'distributed protagonism,' a factor counts as a historical actor not by possessing intentional states, but by introducing irreducible contingency that restructures the strategic field for other entities—so that the spread of plague pathogens through Eurasian trade networks, or the global climatic cascade triggered by a massive volcanic eruption, operate as protagonists rather than as static background conditions. Critics of environmental determinism typically reject treating climate or disease as agents because they fear this collapses human history into a mechanistic causal chain; yet if these forces genuinely open and close possibility spaces in ways not predetermined by prior human choices, haven't we already conceded that human agency is always circumscribed by constraints that are themselves agential? Specifically, does recognizing non-human protagonism necessarily entail a soft determinism in which human decisions are always already bounded by ecological or epidemiological 'choices,' or can one coherently maintain that these forces act upon history—reshaping the landscape within which human action occurs—without thereby determining its course?
