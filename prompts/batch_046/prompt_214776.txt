I'm a phd student in comparitive ethics working on confucian and liberal theory, and i've noticed this recurring slide in east/west debates about the self. First example: a scholar argues that in confucian thought there's literally no self prior to family relations — the person IS the roles. Then a liberal critic says that kills moral agency, and the scholar immediately says "oh i just mean people should care more about relationships, individuals still exist." Second: in guanxi studies someone describes networks as ontologically prior to the nodes, but when asked about exploitation they switch to saying it's just a cultural norm about reciprocity. Third: relational autonomy theorists cite east asian sources — sometimes watsuji tetsuro — to claim the self is "socially constituted", but under pressure they clarify they only mean developmentally shaped, not metaphysically dissolved. It's not quite equivocation because the same texts and slogans do work for both claims. Is there a name for this specific pattern — the slide between saying relations make up the self and saying relations are just very important to the self? If not, I want you to commit: is it one phenomenon with two faces, or two separate theories that share vocabulary? Don't just list options and leave me hanging — take a definite side on the right carving, define its edges clearly (e.g., does it include buddhist anatta arguments or only modern comparative philosophy?), and tell me what you'd call it.
