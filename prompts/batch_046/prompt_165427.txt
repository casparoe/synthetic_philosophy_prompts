I'm writing a feature on how social scientists teach causal reasoning about culture, and I need the pedagogical architecture—not the lecture script—for a single sixty-minute session with philosophy graduate students who are fluent in causal inference but have no background in sociology or intellectual history. The concept is Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis together with the philosophical problem of explaining behavior through values rather than material conditions; the audience will likely arrive treating culture either as a deterministic law, a post-hoc rationalization, or a variant of Marxist base-superstructure determinism. Design the dependency order: what idea about Weber's notion of value-rational action must be in place before modern capitalism can even be introduced as an explanatory target, and why the carrying example must be a specific occupational practice rather than a broad statistical correlation, including what properties that example needs and what it must avoid. Map the misconceptions—values as deterministic mechanisms, values as epiphenomenal narratives, and the collapse of Weber into Marx—indicating exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Provide one end-of-session diagnostic question that reveals whether participants understand the explanatory problem (subjective meaning functioning as a cause) rather than merely recalling the historical claim. Finally, specify what should be explicitly omitted at this level—perhaps the comparative religious dimensions or Weber's later revisions in the sociology of religion essays—and justify why that omission is defensible. The plan should be formal, self-contained, and require no bibliography or further reading.
