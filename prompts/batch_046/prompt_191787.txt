I am a retired molecular biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of biology. In attempting to refine the concept of life, I have proposed a condition I call autonomous dissipative closure: a system counts as living only when its physical boundary is both self-produced and causally directs the internal chemical dynamics, rather than merely containing them. Applying this, I observe that recent synthetic minimal cells clearly qualify, since their membranes actively regulate metabolism. Yet it also seems to me that self-propagating prion aggregates satisfy the same condition: they form structurally stable boundaries that protect internal conformation and direct the misfolding of neighbouring proteins. Given that biologists classify minimal cells as alive but prions as inert, I am led to ask whether this discrepancy shows that autonomous dissipative closure is an inadequate criterion, or whether the distinction between living and non-living is ultimately a pragmatic convention without a natural kind boundary. Could you identify the error in my application of this closure idea to prions, and advise whether life admits of a non-arbitrary cutoff? Please reply in British English.
