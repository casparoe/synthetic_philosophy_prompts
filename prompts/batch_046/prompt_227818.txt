Please explain the conceptual issue that arises when Epicurus's argument that death is not harmful to the one who dies — on the grounds that there is no experiencing subject present to suffer it — is applied consistently to other posthumous events, such as the destruction of one's legacy or the betrayal of one's life's work after death. Your task is to clarify whether this argument relies on a presence requirement for harm, according to which harm must be contemporaneous with the subject's existence and experienced by them. If so, determine whether applying that requirement consistently forces the Epicurean to deny that any post-death event can harm the deceased, and whether there is a coherent distinction between being in a state that is bad for one and actually experiencing that state as bad, one that allows the Epicurean to accept posthumous harm to reputation or legacy while still maintaining that death itself is not bad for the one who dies. Focus on the logical structure and intuitive pressure points rather than historical exegesis; in particular, address the mirror-image problem that if death is not harmful because it lacks an experiencing subject, then many apparently harmful post-death events seem to lose their status as harms as well, and explain whether this undermines the original argument or can be resolved without collapsing the distinction between harm and experienced harm.
