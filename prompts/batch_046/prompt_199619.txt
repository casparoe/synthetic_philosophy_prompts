Take Edward Lorenz's 1961 discovery at MIT, when a rounded decimal in his weather-simulation code produced a radically different long-range forecast and led to the idea of sensitive dependence, later popularised under the name of the butterfly effect. Analyse the episode through the philosophical tension between ontological determinism and epistemic predictability, rather than through the usual popular-science narrative. In particular: does the divergence show that the atmosphere is non-deterministic, or only that deterministic dynamics can defeat practical forecasting? Map the relevant agents and choices — Lorenz, his computational model, the meteorological institutions treating forecasts as authoritative — onto what determinism actually requires for predictability. Which aspects of the case does determinism explain cleanly (the governing equations never changed, only the initial conditions were truncated), and which does it fail to capture (the institutional decision to round values for speed, the authority given to a single model run)? Finally, ask whether chaos in this case is best understood as a feature of the weather itself or as a feature of modelling practice — especially the engineering trade-off between precision and computational efficiency that a software engineer recognises immediately.
