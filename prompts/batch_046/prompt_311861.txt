As a PhD candidate working at the intersection of historical epistemology and Earth-system science, I am trying to settle my own reflective equilibrium regarding proposed temporal boundaries for the Anthropocene. I have formed a set of concrete verdicts about specific start-date proposals, but I am unsure whether they cohere around a single underlying principle or whether I need to abandon some judgments.

My verdicts are as follows. First, I regard the 1610 Orbis spike—characterized by the CO2 nadir and large-scale biotic homogenization linked to the Columbian Exchange—as a serious boundary candidate, even though the underlying depopulation was mediated largely by unintended epidemiological dynamics rather than by intentional industrial transformation. Second, I reject the 1784 milestone associated with Watt’s improved steam engine and the subsequent mobilization of fossil capital, not because the transformation was insignificant, but because it remained geographically concentrated for generations and lacks the globally synchronous stratigraphic legibility that I take to be necessary for a formal period boundary. Third, I accept the 1945 Trinity nuclear detonation and its associated global fallout layer as a strong candidate: the radionuclide signature is unambiguous, globally distributed, and marks a conscious technological rupture with planetary reach, though I concede it privileges one militarized domain over broader socio-metabolic change. Fourth, I take the 1952 convergence of plutonium, plastic, and transformed microfossil signals recorded at Crawford Lake—the so-called Great Acceleration—to be the best available boundary, because multiple independent proxies align with a post-war exponential surge in energy, material, and information throughput. Fifth, I reject the 1988 establishment of the IPCC; institutional recognition is epistemically important but does not constitute a stratigraphic event. Sixth, I reject the 1972 launch of Landsat-1 and the accompanying global shift in environmental perception; representational ruptures may be civilizational milestones, yet they leave no durable geological trace.

The tension in my judgments is this: I accept 1610 despite its dependence on unintended disease-driven depopulation, while I reject 1784 despite its being driven by intentional technological innovation. Likewise, I accept 1945 despite its narrow militaristic origin, yet I reject 1972 despite its broad significance in restructuring humanity’s self-image in relation to Earth. Consequently, the principle behind my verdicts cannot be reduced to intentional agency, institutional framing, or the mere presence of a global signal.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that requires the conjunction of globally synchronous stratigraphic legibility, multi-proxy convergence, and dominance in socio-metabolic throughput—that accommodates all six verdicts? If no single principle works, which of my judgments should I overturn, and on what grounds? Please also apply your proposed principle to a case I have not listed above: the argument that synthetic nitrogen fixation, scaled globally through the Haber-Bosch process around 1913, should mark the Anthropocene boundary because it altered the planetary nitrogen cycle at a magnitude comparable to all natural fixation combined.

Please conclude with a line beginning Verdict: followed by the one-phrase label of the principle you endorse, or "No unitary principle" if you think my judgments are irreconcilable. Then provide a single-sentence summary of your answer as the very last line.
