I'm drafting an essay for a philosophy-of-mind working group that extends Douglas Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self—specifically his claim that the pronoun I functions as a self-referential symbol pointing downward into the neural substrate that generates it—by introducing a formal analogy drawn from combinatory logic. Rather than treating the loop as purely metaphorical, I propose that this self-referential symbol operates analogously to a fixed-point combinator: an operator that takes a representational function describing its own activity and yields a stable dynamical attractor, which I term the subjective fixed point. On this framing, consciousness is not the strange loop itself but the attractor state that emerges when the combinator is applied to distributed neural representations. The essay is divided into four parts: (A) a reconstruction of Hofstadter's downward-causation thesis without relying on empirical neuroscience; (B) the analogy to the Y-combinator and an argument that syntactic fixed-point formation can illuminate semantic self-reference without reducing one to the other; (C) the distinction between loop-as-process and attractor-as-state, with the claim that only the latter accounts for the phenomenological unity of the subject; and (D) a preliminary reply to the objection that this approach smuggles in a representationalist framework that Hofstadter's pre-formal, phenomenological view should reject.

Before I finalize the draft, I would appreciate critical feedback focused on conceptual coherence rather than empirical validation. First, does my reconstruction in section A fairly capture Hofstadter's idea that the symbol I is both generated by and generative of the patterns beneath it, or have I flattened his recursive ontology into a unidirectional causal story? Second, is the Y-combinator analogy in B genuinely illuminating, or does it commit a category error by treating the logical self-application of terms as equivalent to the biological self-modeling of brains? Third, in section C, does relocating consciousness to an attractor solve the binding problem for self-reference, or does it merely rename the loop without explaining why that attractor should possess a first-person character? Finally, I am concerned that my representationalist commitments in D undermine the very strange-loop structure I am trying to formalize; if so, I would like to know exactly where those commitments enter and whether they can be excised without collapsing the analogy. Please treat this as an exercise in conceptual philosophy of mind, and feel free to identify the single objection that, if unanswered, would most damage the essay's central claim.
