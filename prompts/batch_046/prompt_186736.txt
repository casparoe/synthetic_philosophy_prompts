My friends Maya and Leo got into it over coffee about the Principal Principle — the idea that if you actually know the objective chance of something is, say, 40%, your rational credence should match that 40% unless you've got other relevant evidence. Maya said its basically just rational deference: you learn the physics gives a decay rate of 30%, you set your belief to 30%, done. Leo said thats way too quick. He thinks there's a hidden second-order problem: what if you know there's a competing physical model that gives 60%, and you're like 70% sure the first one is right? Then what's "the" objective chance? Maya said you just apply the principle hierarchically — weight the chances by your confidence in each model. Leo said thats not deference anymore, thats just smuggling in your epistemic uncertainty about theories and diluting the direct link between chance and belief. Maya then said Leo's demanding a precision that doesn't exist — in quantum mechanics with branching worlds there might not even be a unique chance value, so the principle has to be flexible. Leo replied that if it needs that much flexibility it stops being a constraint and just becomes "believe whatever you want."

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? I want to know where Maya's correct, where Leo's correct, and what both are missing about how deference to objective chance actually works.
