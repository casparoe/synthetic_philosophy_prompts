I am a novelist drafting a philosophical interlude for a work-in-progress in which one of my characters—an aging physicist who has gradually lost faith in the ideology of progress—defends a deliberately modified version of Moritz Schlick's thesis that the meaning of life resides in play rather than in purpose-driven achievement or moral culmination. I have, however, extended Schlick's framework into territory he did not explicitly chart: my character maintains that play confers meaning only under the condition of structural incompleteness, meaning that an activity must resist narrative closure, retrospective justification, and subsumption under any final goal if it is to remain genuinely ludic. From this she derives a stronger, more polemical claim: that modern culture's insistent demand for coherent "life projects," career arcs, and morally integrated self-narratives functions not as a benign framework but as a form of narrative colonization, converting intrinsic absorption into instrumental labor and thereby annihilating the very condition that makes existence meaningful. I wish to subject this composite position to rigorous scrutiny rather than shield it.

What specific evidence, conceptual objections, or empirical findings would genuinely count against it? I am particularly concerned with whether psychological research on human flourishing suggests that coherent long-term goals are actually indispensable to well-being rather than destructive of it; whether Schlick's original distinction between work and play collapses when applied to complex, sustained creative activities that are simultaneously goal-directed and intrinsically rewarding; and whether my supplementary condition of "structural incompleteness" inadvertently renders the view unfalsifiable—if every reported experience of satisfaction can be retrospectively redescribed as concealed play, has the thesis been qualified into vacuity? Please order the objections by how decisive they would be, distinguish those that merely embarrass the position from those that ought to force its abandonment, indicate what a defender of this view should concede without hesitation, and pinpoint the weakest joint in the argument—the point most likely to buckle under pressure—that I should prepare to defend openly rather than hope an interlocutor misses.
