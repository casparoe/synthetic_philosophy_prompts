I'm working through my institutional-design commitments for a seminar on democratic reform and want a consistency audit before I present. Here is the package I have arrived at:

(1) Legislative legitimacy derives from sortition rather than election: selecting representatives by lot eliminates the structural advantages of wealth, charisma, and organizational capacity that distort representativeness, and any elected element introduces a democratic deficit that pure random selection avoids.

(2) At the same time, governing complex modern societies requires accumulated expertise in macroeconomic forecasting, international treaty law, and regulatory design; a chamber composed entirely of amateurs serving brief, non-renewable terms would suffer severe epistemic deficits that election alone does not remedy.

(3) To reconcile these, I support a unicameral primary legislature selected by lot for single, non-renewable ten-year terms, paired with a smaller "deliberative council" of career specialists appointed through competitive examination and serving renewable fifteen-year terms; the council may propose amendments and request reconsideration but may not initiate legislation or vote on final passage.

(4) I insist on absolute anti-career provisions for the lot-selected chamber: no seniority privileges, no post-service advisory roles, and no pathway for members to transition into the deliberative council, because I believe that long tenure in any voting body is the dominant vector for regulatory capture and that expertise accumulated in a careerist context is likely to be privatized rather than deployed for the public good.

(5) The lot-selected chamber should hold full constitutional sovereignty, including the power to amend the fundamental charter by simple majority without judicial review or supermajority requirements, because a body that mirrors the demographic composition of the citizenry should not be constrained by unelected institutions.

(6) To address the epistemic deficit without creating careerists, I propose that every lot-selected member complete a mandatory two-year preparatory apprenticeship in legislative drafting and statistical reasoning before assuming office, paid at standard civil-service scale and open only to those who pass a minimal civic-literacy screening; the apprenticeship carries no voting rights and offers no preferential access to future office.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each conflict? For every genuine tension, specify what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them equally. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most problems at once. Be specific about which commitments pull against each other and do not simply lecture me on democratic theory in general.
