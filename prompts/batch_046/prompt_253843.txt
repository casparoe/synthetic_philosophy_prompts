I write about logic, epistemology, and the conceptual architecture of science for a quarterly magazine read by researchers across disciplines. For an upcoming feature on how classical paradoxes survive in modern non-philosophy seminars, I have agreed to lead a forty-minute whiteboard session for a cohort of computer-science doctoral students—rigorous thinkers, but with no prior exposure to epistemic logic—who want to understand the surprise exam paradox not as a recreational puzzle but as a structural failure in backward induction under common knowledge. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture transcript itself. Please set out the exact dependency order in which the components must be introduced: where the logical constraint of the announcement must be established before any belief-revision step is permitted, and why reversing that order collapses the paradox into a simple contradiction rather than a live tension; identify the single carrying scenario—distinct from the canonical classroom announcement—that preserves all the formal bones while stripping away pedagogical cliché, specifying precisely what it must contain (a hard temporal boundary, an authoritative announcement with full credibility, and an agent capable of reasoning about others' reasoning) and what it must lack (probabilistic ambiguity, hidden information, or any escape clause); map the misconceptions these technically trained minds are likely to import—treating the authority's promise as a statistical forecast, conflating surprise with Bayesian updating, or assuming that reasoning away the final day eliminates the premise rather than the belief—and state the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted; propose one concise check-for-understanding that reveals whether they have grasped the paradox as a feature of common-knowledge structures rather than a linguistic trick; and finally, list explicitly what I should refuse to unfold at this level—formal modal-logic representations, the full taxonomy of proposed resolutions, or analogies to strategic games like Newcomb's problem—together with an honest justification for each omission. The audience is sharp, skeptical of hand-waving, and will not tolerate being told to simply accept the intuition; I want the conceptual architecture exposed, not smoothed over.
