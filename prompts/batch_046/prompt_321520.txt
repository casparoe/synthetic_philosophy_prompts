I've been reading about the so-called Year Without a Summer that followed the massive volcanic eruption in Indonesia in 1815, and how it seems to have rippled through grain prices in Europe, migration patterns, and even the writing of Frankenstein. In conversation with a friend, I tried to defend a distinction that I haven't seen clearly spelled out in popular writing, and I want to see if it holds up. I want to separate three roles a non-human event might play: a background condition (like a generally cool climate); a trigger that releases forces already present (like a spark in dry tinder); and what I am calling a "structural actor" — an event that does not merely initiate change but actually reconfigures the menu of options available to human agents for a durable period, in ways that cannot be fully translated back into social or economic causes that were already in motion. The 1815 eruption and its 1816–1818 aftermath sit ambiguously between the second and third roles, and that ambiguity is exactly what I want examined.

Could you analyze the episode — specifically the global temperature drop, the famine and policy responses, the migration shifts, and the cultural productions associated with that winter — through the lens of whether the volcano functioned as a structural actor in this precise sense? I am not asking for a standard ecological or economic chronology. Rather, I want to know: in which domains did the eruption reshape the field of human choice in a durable, non-reducible way, and in which domains did it merely accelerate dynamics already underway? More importantly, if we grant the volcano this kind of historical agency, how do we avoid sliding into environmental determinism, where human societies become passive recipients of geological force? What conceptual labor does the "structural actor" category perform to keep institutional response and human decision genuinely in the picture, rather than treating them as epiphenomena?

Finally, I am trying to test whether this middle category is genuinely stable. What would have to be true about the 1816 crisis — or about analogous episodes — for the "structural actor" framing to collapse back into either a pure trigger story or a deterministic account? I want an honest assessment of whether the distinction does real work, or whether it is mainly an illusion born of our reluctance to choose between full human autonomy and full natural causation.
