I have drafted notes for an essay arguing that the global anti-corruption regime imposes a specifically Western, post-Weberian distinction between public office and private obligation, thereby misclassifying many forms of clientelism and ritual gift-giving as corruption. The essay distinguishes three phenomena: first, transactional bribery, in which a quid pro quo is explicit and immediate; second, relational clientelism, in which a patron distributes resources through a network of enduring reciprocal obligations that may never involve direct exchange; and third, ceremonial gift-giving, in which material transfers occur within a moral economy of honor and social reproduction rather than market logic. I claim that Western legal standards tend to conflate the second and third categories with the first because they presuppose an ideal-type bureaucrat whose authority derives solely from formal rules rather than embedded social relations.

Please comment on these notes, with particular attention to the following points. First, does the tripartite distinction hold up conceptually—especially, can relational clientelism be clearly separated from ceremonial gift-giving in practice, or do they typically collapse into one another in empirical settings? Second, is the critique of Western universalism overstated, given that many non-Western legal systems themselves criminalize certain forms of patronage and that international anti-corruption frameworks are often adopted by domestic reformers rather than imposed externally? Third, does the framing risk romanticizing clientelism by underemphasizing the coercive, extractive, and exclusionary dimensions that frequently accompany these networks? I would also welcome suggestions for sharpening the central argument without relying on highly specialized regional case studies that require expertise a general reader might lack.
