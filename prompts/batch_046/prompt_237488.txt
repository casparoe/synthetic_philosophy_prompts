I've been trying to wrap my head around the South African TRC's amnesty-for-truth deal, specifically the part where individual perpetrators could apply for amnesty if they made full disclosure about politically motivated crimes. The commission wouldn't just rubber-stamp it; there was a legal test about whether the act was proportional to the political objective, and victims or thier families could object. But there was no requirement that the applicant show remorse, and the state essentially traded away criminal punishment for information.

What philosophical commitments about justice and moral responsibility does that institutional design actually presuppose? It seems to treat truth as a kind of currency that can compensate for the absence of retribution, but does that only make sense under a restorative-justice framework, or could a retributivist who cares about desert accept it as a second-best? And there's this weird asymmetry: the law distinguishes politically motivated violence from ordinary crime for amnesty eligibility, which seems to presuppose some view about how motivation affects culpability or about collective vs individual responsibility. Does the practice require a specific theory of moral repair—maybe one where recognition of the victim's dignity happens through disclosure rather than through punishment? Or is it philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several theories as long as they value epistemic transparency?

I'm also wondering whether the fact that victims didn't have a veto but only a right to be heard reveals something implicit about who the primary wronged party is—the state, the community, or the individual victim. Could you reconstruct the implicit theory, say which parts are strictly necessary and which are just convenient fits, and point out where the practice is internally inconsistent if we push on it? Like, if we really think disclosure restores dignity, why allow amnesty for people who disclose but clearly don't care about the harm they caused?
