I've been trying to read Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism through the lens of contemporary modal metaphysics, specifically the debate between dispositional essentialism and categoricalism about properties. On this framing, Al-Ghazālī denies that fire possesses the causal power to burn as part of its intrinsic modal profile; instead, God creates the burning directly on each occasion, with the presence of fire serving only as an occasion (sabab) rather than an efficient cause. But this raises a conceptual tension I'm struggling to resolve. If fire lacks any essential causal disposition, then its membership in the kind fire seems to depend entirely on which effects God habitually conjoins with it—namely, heating and burning—rather than on any internal nature. This looks like it collapses occasionalism into a stronger thesis: not just that causation is extrinsic, but that natural-kind membership itself is extrinsically imposed. If that is right, then Al-Ghazālī's distinction between natural regularities (the ʿāda) and miracles seems unstable, because there is no nature of fire that defines what its habitual behavior should be; any deviation from heating would simply be God assigning fire a different extrinsic profile. So my question is: does occasionalism necessarily commit one to this kind of global extrinsicism about properties and kinds, or is there a reading on which fire retains an essential categorical nature—perhaps its qualitative or sensory properties—while lacking only causal efficacy? And if the latter, how does one prevent that categorical essence from playing an explanatory role that looks indistinguishable from a dormant causal power?
