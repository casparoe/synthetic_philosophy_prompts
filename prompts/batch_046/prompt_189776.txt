Hey, I'm in this philosophy study group and we keep running into this same weird argument pattern and I don't know if philosphers have already named it. So there's this move where someone blames another person for a moral failing—like being unreliable or breaking promises—but the blamer is actually guilty of the same general failing, just in a different "flavor." Take Jordan, who constantly calls people out for flaking on commitments, yet Jordan is also flakey; Jordan just does it in an obvious, upfront way (like warning "I'll probably be late") whereas the others do it sneakily (canceling last minute). When Jordan gets called a hypocrite, Jordan doesn't deny it—Jordan says "yeah I'm unreliable too, but my unreliability is transparent, not deceptive, so I'm not in the same category, which means I still get to blame the deceptive kind."

We started joking about calling it "transparent hypocrisy" but I'm not satisfied with that label. It's not excatly the standard hypocrisy that undermines standing, because Jordan admits the shared fault. It's not moral licensing either, since Jordan isn't cashing in past good deeds. And it's not just tu quoque, because Jordan isn't deflecting blame away—Jordan is insisting they can keep blaming others by re-slicing the moral category so that their own instance falls outside the boundry they just enforced. Is there an established concept for this specific maneuver—preserving standing to blame by partitioning a fault into transparent vs deceptive variants? And if not, can you name it and map its edges: does the partition have to be moral, or can it be aesthetic, practical, whatever? At what point does it collapse back into regular hypocrisy, and does the answer change if Jordan's "transparent" version is actually more harmful?
