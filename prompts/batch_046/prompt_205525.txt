Please write the opening remarks for a workshop titled "Forecasting and Policy Under Deep Uncertainty," to be delivered to a mixed audience of climate scientists, risk analysts, decision theorists, and government policymakers. The remarks should run about eight minutes when spoken, in a tone that is formal and precise but does not require philosophical training to follow.

The philosophical idea that should do real structural work is David Lewis's Principal Principle—roughly, that a rational agent's subjective credence in an outcome ought to align with her knowledge of the corresponding objective chance. Do not treat this as decoration or a passing reference; it should shape what the speaker can and cannot say. Specifically, inject the following tension: in the domains this workshop addresses—climate projection, epidemiological forecasting, financial systemic risk—what gets presented as an "objective chance" (for example, a model-ensemble statement that there is a 65 percent probability of exceeding a 1.5-degree threshold by 2040) is not a raw feature of the world but an output mediated by structural choices, parameterizations, and prior distributions that are themselves rationally contestable. Because of this, the speaker cannot straightforwardly urge the audience to "defer to the objective probabilities" as a direct application of the Principal Principle, since there is no unmediated access to chance independent of the very models being evaluated. At the same time, the speaker must avoid collapsing into a skeptical posture that treats every forecast as equally arbitrary or that abandons the normative idea that beliefs should track genuinely probabilistic structure in the world.

Your task is to navigate this reflexive problem. Propose a practical posture—perhaps something like "structured deference" or "calibrated alignment"—that respects both the normative pull of aligning credence with chance and the epistemic reality that our access to chance is always model-mediated. The speech should be specific enough that philosophers of science and decision theorists recognize the conceptual architecture, but concrete enough that working scientists and policymakers understand what is at stake for their practice. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write in American English.
