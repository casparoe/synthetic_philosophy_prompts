So I retired from bird population genetics about five years ago—spent thirty years tracking allele frequencies in finches, mostly—and lately I've been poking around philosophy of social science on my own, especially Weber and whether culture can actually be a cause. I think I've settled on a cluster of views for my little reading group of ex-scientists, but I'm worried they tear eachother apart and I'd like an audit before I open my mouth.

Here is roughly where I stand: (1) Weber was right that the Protestant work ethic causally contributed to capitalism's rise—it's not just correlation or a story we tell afterwards. (2) But I don't think values or culture are causes in the mechanistic sense I'm used to from biology; they don't have physical properties that push things around. (3) Still, I think you have to grasp subjective meanings to explain human action—Weber's verstehende approach feels right. (4) At the same time, I hold that the best explanations anywhere are the ones with the strongest predictive power and empirical testability, even if they ignore meanings. (5) I reject methodological individualism; societies have emergent properties that can't be reduced to summed-up individuals. (6) I also think evolutionary psychology gives a cleaner account of economic behaviors like risk calculation and delayed gratification than Weber's cultural values do, so I worry Weber's thesis is redundant.

Do these hang together? I can see (1) and (6) look contradictory on the surface, but maybe that's only an apparent conflict if cultural and evolutionary explanations operate at different levels. The one that really bugs me is (2) with (3): if values aren't physical causes, why do I need subjective meanings at all? Is that a real tension or am I just confusing causation with explanation? There's probably also trouble holding (4) alongside (3) and (5). Can you rank the actual tensions by severity, say what has to give in each case, point out which conflict dissolves on inspection, and tell me the least costly revision? I don't want to ditch Weber entirely—there's something beautiful about beliefs shaping economies—but I also don't want to be a sloppy naturalist who sneaks physicalist causation in when it suits him.
