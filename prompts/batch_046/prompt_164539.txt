I'm preparing a dissertation prospectus in philosophy of psychiatry, and I want to turn a half-formed idea into a rigorous one-year proposal. In a recent philosophy-of-science seminar we discussed Ian Hacking's looping effects—the idea that psychiatric classifications can change the people they classify, who in turn alter the classification itself. Talking it over afterward with friends, I kept wondering whether the framework still holds when classification is no longer clinician-administered and categorical, but instead decentralized, voluntary, and continuous, as in consumer mental-health applications that ask users to rate their own symptoms on dimensional scales rather than receiving a binary diagnosis.

My proposed research question is this: does the shift from categorical, institutional diagnosis to continuous, self-administered dimensional tracking change the structure of looping effects so radically that Hacking's model needs revision, and if so, what form should that revision take—perhaps identifying a mechanism we might call reverse looping, in which the user's repeated self-interpretation reshapes the rating scale itself? Please write a two-page proposal that states the precise question and explains why existing philosophical work on looping effects leaves it open; lays out the method for the year month by month, emphasizing conceptual analysis rather than empirical data collection, and justifies this approach over obvious alternatives such as running a user study or writing a straight intellectual history of psychiatric taxonomy; describes what a successful result would look like—whether a refined taxonomy of looping mechanisms, a demonstration that self-tracking produces a novel feedback loop, or a normative conclusion about epistemic authority in decentralized classification—and specifies what would count as failure, such as finding that the distinction between clinical and self-tracking contexts collapses conceptually; and gives an honest assessment of whether the project is realistic within a single year.
