I have noticed a recurring pattern in discussions of large language models that I cannot map onto any familiar concept in philosophy of mind. When a model produces an output that a commentator finds erroneous or objectionable—say, echoing a widespread misconception—the commentator frequently explains the failure by attributing a specific belief or representational state to the system, describing it as having acquired that view from its training data. Yet when the identical model generates a nuanced, accurate, or ideologically inconvenient response, the same commentator typically abandons mentalistic language entirely and describes the process in purely mechanistic terms: statistical token prediction, correlation computation, absence of understanding. The shift tracks the commentator's own epistemic commitments with striking regularity: belief-attribution language is deployed mainly to criticize outputs, and withheld mainly to defend or praise them. This is not full anthropomorphism, since mental states are denied in favorable cases; it is not pure mechanism, since they are granted in unfavorable ones; and it is not a standard equivocation, because the speaker does not oscillate between two meanings of a single term but rather activates and deactivates an entire explanatory framework. Is there an established term—perhaps in the literature on the intentional stance, artificial agency, or cognitive bias—that precisely captures this selective, commitment-calibrated deployment of mental-state attribution toward AI? If not, I would like a precise definition that distinguishes it from ordinary confirmation bias, from Dennett's uniform predictive heuristic, and from strategic rhetoric, together with boundary conditions: for instance, whether the phenomenon requires that the attributed belief be false, whether it extends to true but unwelcome outputs, and whether it applies symmetrically when a speaker praises an error that happens to align with her views.
