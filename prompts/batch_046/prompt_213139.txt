A colleague summarised a recent defence of lottocracy for me in these terms: elections are inherently aristocratic in the classical sense, since they favour candidates with superior wealth, charisma, or education and thereby violate strict political equality; random selection remedies this by yielding a descriptively representative body, and because descriptive representation just is democratic representation, such an assembly automatically embodies the popular will without requiring an electoral mandate. Being free from campaigning and re-election, it is also immune to party discipline and lobbying, which renders it both more democratic and more deliberatively rational. Consequently, worries about the incompetence of ordinary citizens are said to miss the point, as statistical representation guarantees that the group’s collective wisdom matches that of experts. I suspect this summary may conflate distinct claims or overstate what proponents actually hold. Which parts accurately reflect arguments made by thinkers such as Bernard Manin or advocates of citizens’ assemblies, which are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements, what objections would a careful defender raise, and does the conclusion—that sortition chambers are self-legitimating and that competence-based objections are thereby neutralised—survive once the errors are corrected?
