As a retired natural scientist who has only recently turned to philosophy, I have found the species problem far more perplexing than my earlier, practical engagement with taxonomy ever suggested. In particular, I am struck by a development that lies entirely outside the conceptual world of the eighteenth century: the deliberate engineering of synthetic organisms whose genomes are mosaic assemblies of archaeal, bacterial, and eukaryotic modules, and which are designed to disseminate these chimeric gene clusters horizontally through wild microbial populations. This phenomenon appears to dissolve the classical notion of a reproductively isolated, morphologically stable natural kind.

With this in mind, I ask you to predict how Carl Linnaeus—who held that species are fixed, divinely ordained essences—would have responded philosophically to this specific challenge. Would he have retained an essentialist framework by redefining species in terms of functional or ecological roles rather than immutable form? Or would he have concluded that the very concept of species must be abandoned in favor of a purely relational or process-based ontology? In constructing your answer, reason directly from Linnaeus's broader metaphysical commitments regarding natural order, stability, and design, rather than merely summarizing his historical texts. Please reach a definite conclusion about whether a modified essentialism could survive this particular form of reticulate, synthetic biology, and justify that verdict clearly.
