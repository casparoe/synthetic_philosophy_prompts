I've come round to thinking that split-brain patients still have one consciousness, not two. The idea is that cutting the corpus callosum just breaks the main highway for cross-hemisphere report and explicit integration, but both sides are still modules inside a single system — more like your visual cortex and motor cortex being partly isolated, not like two people in one skull. The classic experiments (pointing with the left hand, saying something different) only happen in forced-choice lab setups with lateralized stimuli; in ordinary behaviour the body moves as one unit and there's no sign of competing agents. So unity survives at the phenomenal and agential level, and what splits is just high-level informational access.

What would actually count against this? I want defeaters that bite, not the usual reheated stuff. For instance: are there split-brain behaviours outside the lab that genuinely look like two competing intentions? Would it matter if each hemisphere turned out to maintain its own indepedant long-term narrative memory? What about cases where one hemisphere is anaesthetized and the remaining one gives rich subjective reports that never make referance to the "missing" side — is that fatal, or just awkward?

Also, has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If every counterexample gets explained away as "access split, not consciousness split," is that a problem? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones I'd have to concede as a defender, and be honest about whether the position risks anything real or is just well-insulated. I particularly care whether naturalistic behaviour really is as unified as I claim, since that seems like the weakest link.
