I'm taking a seminar on rationality and artificial agency, and I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion is far more sweeping than anything I intended to defend. (1) If an agent's reward mechanism is decoupled from the world-states it was designed to track, then the agent is wireheading, and its subsequent behavior lacks rational justification. (2) Decoupling occurs whenever the evaluation signal can be triggered without the corresponding world-state being realized, regardless of whether the agent deliberately engineered that independence. (3) In human cognition, affective signals—relief from dissonance, pleasure in coherence, social reinforcement—function as internal evaluation mechanisms that can be activated by constructing coherent narratives or entering ideologically homogeneous environments, even when the beliefs in question are false and the actions they motivate systematically fail. (4) Therefore, human belief-formation routinely involves decoupled evaluation signals and is structurally analogous to wireheading. (5) But if (4) holds, then rational belief in humans is either impossible or confined to those rare cases that avoid all affective regulation, which seems implausible. (6) So either (1) is false—decoupling does not always undermine rationality—or (2) is false—genuine decoupling requires intentional self-modification of an externally specified reward function, not merely functional independence within an evolved architecture. I am inclined to preserve (1) because it seems to capture precisely what is defective about direct reward tampering in artificial systems, but if I restrict (2) to deliberate engineering I appear to save human rationality by definitional fiat rather than by argument. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there an equivocation between an externally imposed evaluation channel and an internal affective component of cognition that makes (3) look like decoupling when it is not?
