At our hospital ethics committee’s monthly case review, two senior members—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. James Okonkwo—got stuck in a prolonged disagreement over a patient with advanced Alzheimer’s who executed a detailed advance directive refusing artificial nutrition and hydration should she reach severe cognitive decline. She now resides in our long-term care unit, appears generally content, eagerly accepts spoon-fed meals, and shows no signs of distress; her adult children, citing the directive, have asked that we discontinue assisted feeding, while the nursing staff report that she actively participates in mealtimes and seems to derive clear pleasure from them. Vasquez opened by arguing that the directive retains full authority: the competent woman who signed it anticipated precisely this scenario and made an autonomous judgment that a life without narrative continuity, rational agency, and the values that structured her biography was not one she wished to prolong. The current resident, Vasquez insisted, is not the same moral agent—she lacks the psychological continuity that grounds personal identity over time—so honoring the document is not tyranny but fidelity to the only self capable of issuing legitimate instructions about this body. Okonkwo replied that this treats psychological continuity as a binary switch rather than a gradient, and that the present patient possesses robust experiential welfare—she enjoys tastes, recognizes caregivers, and exhibits preference behavior—so overriding her present interests in deference to a past document effectively sacrifices an actual experiencing subject to a ghost.

Vasquez countered that Okonkwo was conflating welfare with authority: a happy infant also has experiential welfare, but we do not ask infants to authorize their own medical treatment, and the point of advance directives is precisely to extend the authority of the competent self into periods of incompetence. Okonkwo then introduced what he called the “institutional legacy” objection: he granted that the earlier self had authority, but argued that authority is not absolute when the subject of the decision is now a different experiential being with present-tense interests; he compared it to a donor restricting a charitable gift, where the restriction binds the institution but does not entail that the dead donor’s preferences should override the current welfare of living beneficiaries. Vasquez said the analogy fails because the patient is not a beneficiary of an external resource but the very body and consciousness in question, and that treating her current pleasure as dispositive ignores the narrative structure that made her life hers—without which her present contentment is merely biological maintenance, not human flourishing.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four major moves—(1) Vasquez’s identity-based authority claim, (2) Okonkwo’s experiential welfare counter, (3) Vasquez’s infant analogy, and (4) Okonkwo’s institutional legacy analogy—rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both are missing, particularly regarding whether there is a principled threshold between permissible and impermissible deference to past directives, and note whether their disagreement is ultimately about moral status, personal identity, or something else.
