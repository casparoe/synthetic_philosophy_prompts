I am a PhD student in philosophy of science preparing for a workshop defense of a short paper on decision-theoretic metaphysics. My thesis is that accepting the simulation argument—that technologically mature civilizations are likely to run large numbers of ancestor simulations—should dampen, not amplify, longtermist fanaticism about the far future. I argue that nested simulation hierarchies introduce two failures in the standard longtermist causal chain: first, causal truncation, in which the capacity of present interventions to propagate across time decays across simulation levels because the base-level physics governing our simulated environment may not preserve the causal pathways that longtermist planning assumes; second, epistemic attenuation, in which uncertainty about our position in a hierarchical stack undermines the expected-value calculus that treats distant outcomes as direct linear extensions of current choices. I do not deny that the far future has moral weight in base reality; I claim that under plausible simulation assumptions, the longtermist expectation of reliable, long-range causal efficacy collapses.

The audience will be philosophers and decision theorists working in effective altruism and longtermist research. Their commitments include: that the moral weight of the far future is astronomically large; that simulation reasoning, if anything, increases the stakes because of potential observer multiplicity; a methodological preference for formal expected-value reasoning over metaphysical or phenomenological objections; and deep suspicion of arguments that appear to grant our present epistemic position special privilege.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face. Order them by likelihood and damage. For each, phrase it in the audience’s vocabulary—expected value, observer multiplicity, base-rate reasoning, causal closure, special pleading. Note which objections have real force and which are mostly rhetorical. Tell me what I should concede up front, likely something about the formal structure of expected value under radical uncertainty, to avoid sounding defensive. Identify the single reply that must land for the proposal to survive: the argument that causal truncation is not an external ad hoc restriction but an internal consequence of the simulation hypothesis when combined with the longtermist commitment to physicalist causal closure. Finally, tell me the objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one about whether my attenuation dynamics implicitly rely on an unexplained ontological exemption for base reality, treating it as immune to the very truncation I apply everywhere else—and sketch how to respond if it is raised.
