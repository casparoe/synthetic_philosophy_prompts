so lately i've been kinda obsessed with the ring of Gyges thing, probably because i just realized i could've taken some cash from the shared office pool and literally nobody would've known. i mentioned it to my friends ravi and jenna over coffee and they ended up in alot of back-and-forth about whether being moral is ever actually required, not just smart.

ravi said glaucon's challenge is basically undefeated. if you have the ring — perfect invisibility, zero social cost — then justice is just a coordination scheme for suckers. he said even the "internal" stuff people cite, like not wanting to be the kind of person who steals, is just another preference, no more rational than liking vanilla. he brought up this idea he read about, indirect normativity: you shouldn't aim at being moral directly, you should aim at good outcomes, and being moral is usually the best instrument. but with the ring, the instrument breaks, so there's no reason left.

jenna said thats missing something huge. she thinks even with the ring there are direct reasons not to wrong people — not just because you'll feel bad, but because the wrongness is in the act itself regardless of who's watching. she said ravi's treating morality like dieting, where you only care about the scale, but actually being moral is partly about recognizing that other people aren't just obstacles to your goals. she also said that if you really could become invisible to all social tracking, you'd still have to deal with your future self, and breaking your own commitments fragments your agency over time.

ravi fired back that future-self talk is just smuggling in social norms under a different name — "agency" is just what society trains us to value. he said if you genuinely don't care about coherence, there's no argument that forces you to care. he also said jenna's "direct reasons" sound like mysticism: if there's no observer, human or divine, and no consequence, then calling it wrong is just labeling your own discomfort.

jenna answered that ravi's own view collapses into a weird kind of solipsism where only your current preferences exist, which makes long-term planning impossible too — if you can change preferences tomorrow, why save money today? she said the ring doesn't erase the structure of reasons, it just removes one kind of enforcement. and she brought up that even in invisible scenarios, people usually still don't murder, which suggests there's more going on than fear of punishment.

ravi said people don't murder mostly because of conditioning and empathy, not rational obligation, and that with the ring you'd probably still avoid it out of squeamishness, not because you should. he asked jenna what she thinks actually changes normatively if the ring exists — like, is there a hidden property of wrongness that only activates when observed?

they asked me to referee since i was just sitting there, and honestly i couldn't tell who was right. can you go through there exchange point by point and say who's right about what? i'm especially stuck on whether indirect normativity actually helps ravi's case or undermines it, and whether jenna's future-self argument is just a different version of the sunk-cost thing. also, is there something both of them are missing about what the ring scenario actually tests?
