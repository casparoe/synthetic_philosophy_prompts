After a long, exhausting argument with a close friend last night about whether personal identity is anything more than a center of narrative gravity in Daniel Dennett's sense—an explanatory center like the center of mass of a physical object, useful for predicting behavior but not a metaphysical substance hidden inside—I kept thinking about a concrete institutional case that forces the abstraction into practice. I serve on my university's Committee on Research Continuity and Intellectual Identity. We must decide whether to grant formal institutional recognition to a synthetic philosophical agent constructed from the complete digital archive—drafts, correspondence, lecture recordings, and marginalia—of Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of mind who died unexpectedly last year. The system, maintained by her estate and a group of collaborators, produces new philosophical papers, responds to peer review, and maintains the distinctive argumentative trajectory of Voss's work. Her estate argues that, on a Dennett-style view, this system literally continues Voss's self: it preserves and extends the narrative gravity that made her an identifiable agent, and denying it recognition implicitly demands a hidden soul behind the explanatory center. Our integrity office and several senior faculty members counter that narrative gravity requires an embodied, living first-person perspective; without biological continuity and conscious experience, the system is a sophisticated artifact, not a continuing self, and granting it emeritus status, co-authorship rights, and grant eligibility would confuse simulation with substance and undermine the moral boundaries of academic personhood.

Please draft the Committee's formal written opinion recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, including any concrete conditions and a clear implementation time frame. The memo should treat the narrative-gravity argument seriously rather than dismissing it as mere poetry, and should include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side—not a strawman.

Structure your response as follows:

1. Executive Summary (approximately 150 words): state the bottom-line recommendation and the single most important reason for it.
2. The Case for Institutional Recognition (approximately 300 words): present the argument that the Voss system continues Voss's self as a center of narrative gravity, engaging Dennett's conceptual framework without treating it as settled truth, and explain why institutional recognition follows on this view.
3. The Case Against Recognition (approximately 300 words): present the opposing argument that narrative continuity without embodied consciousness and first-person perspective is insufficient for institutional personhood, addressing specifically why the center-of-gravity analogy breaks down at the boundary of rights, responsibilities, and authorship.
4. The Committee's Recommendation and Implementation Timeline (approximately 250 words): state the concrete decision, specify any conditions with deadlines, and explain exactly what the university will and will not do regarding emeritus status, publication attribution, and grant eligibility.
5. Engagement with the Strongest Counter-Argument (approximately 200 words): identify the most powerful objection to your recommendation, present it in its best form, and explain precisely why it does not overturn the conclusion—without simply restating the recommendation.

Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, citation requests, or invented references to specific texts or page numbers. The memo should be entirely self-contained and answerable without web access. Paraphrase philosophical concepts in your own words rather than using verbatim quotations.
