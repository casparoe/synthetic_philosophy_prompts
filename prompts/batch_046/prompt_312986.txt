I'm working on my diss about distributive justice and I'm trying to pin down my actual intuitions about luck egalitarianism, not just parrot Dworkin/Cohen/Arneson. Here are some cases where I have a gut verdict and I'm not sure they cohear.

Case 1: Two people with identical talents and ambitions, but one is born with a genetic condition that makes sustained labor painful. I think society owes them compensation. Case 2: Same talents, but one just hates hard work due to temperament - no pathology, just lazy. I think they don't deserve compensation for being poor. Case 3: Two people work equally hard, but one takes a risky startup path and fails due to market randomness, while the other takes a safe government job. The risk-taker is now poor. I feel like they should get some help, but not full compensation - they chose the risk. Case 4: Someone is born into a culture that glorifies risk-taking and stigmatizes safe paths, so they take the startup path not really as a free choice but as social conditioning. They fail. I think they deserve more compensation than case 3, maybe as much as case 1? That feels inconsistent with case 2. Case 5: Someone has a genetic predispostion to be risk-averse, so they take the safe job, but then feel miserable. Should they be compensated for the 'brute luck' of their risk aversion? I go back and forth.

Is there a principle that captures these? Maybe something about 'choice within a reasonable range of options'? Or does my case 4 verdict force me to drop either case 2 or case 3? Also, what does the principle say about this weird mechanism I'm imagining: a society introduces a 'luck tax' that only applies to people who win genetic lotteries but opt out of contributing? Does that change the moral calculus or is it just a policy mechanism? And what's the verdict on case 5 - is risk-aversion a brute luck feature or a choice feature?
