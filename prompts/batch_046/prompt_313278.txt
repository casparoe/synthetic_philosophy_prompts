Write an essay examining whether David Hume's argument against the credibility of miracle testimony, conventionally associated with the section commonly titled Of Miracles, can be sustained consistently with his broader sceptical commitments regarding induction, custom, and the psychology of belief developed elsewhere in his philosophy. Your essay should weigh the orthodox reading, according to which Hume deploys a straightforward probabilistic calculus in which uniform human experience always outweighs testimony, against the more revisionist interpretation that he is primarily describing how belief is formed and suspended rather than offering a normative rational justification for rejecting miracles. Focus particularly on whether Hume's appeal to the uniform experience of humankind as a standard of evaluation tacitly presupposes an epistemic authority for inductive generalisation that his own arguments about the lack of rational foundation for causal inference appear to deny. Additionally, consider whether the argument can be reconstructed on purely descriptive, psychological grounds without collapsing into a performative contradiction. Reach a clear, defended conclusion — for instance, that the argument survives only if reinterpreted psychologically, that it is indeed self-undermining, or that the tension can be dissolved by distinguishing normative from descriptive claims — and justify it with precise reasoning. Write your response in British English.
