In studying the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, I have come to understand it as a constraint on the identity of reasons: for an agent to perform a genuinely moral act of kind K—such as a genuinely just distribution or a genuinely generous gift—the agent's motivating reason must match in content the normative ground that makes K correct. Thus, if justice is grounded in giving each person their due, then an agent who distributes goods correctly but is motivated by aggregate welfare maximization has not performed a genuinely just act, only a behaviorally identical one. This seems to generate a structural problem for consequentialism, since consequentialism identifies the normative ground of every right action with the maximization of welfare, whereas particular actions like saving a child or returning property appear to have their own specific grounds (the child's peril, the owner's claim). Consequently, a consequentialist agent who saves the child because that maximizes welfare always supplies, for that particular act, a reason of the wrong kind. Does this argument show that consequentialism is incapable of accounting for moral worth, or does it rest on a confusion between the normative reason that justifies an action and the psychological reason that moves the agent? And does virtue ethics avoid the same difficulty because the virtues are defined as stable dispositions to be moved by precisely those reasons that ground the corresponding actions?
