I have recently become the primary caregiver for a parent with a progressive degenerative condition, and Bernard Williams’s integrity objection has moved from an abstract classroom example to a live pressure in my own deliberation. I want to audit whether the philosophical profile I have developed in response actually coheres, or whether I am borrowing pieces that pull in incompatible directions.

Here is where I stand:

(1) I accept Williams’s integrity objection, but I read it structurally rather than as a defense of a personal point of view against impartial morality. The problem, on my view, is not that impartial theories demand too much, but that they require agents to treat first-personal, constitutive commitments—what I call ground projects—as fungible inputs to a decision procedure.

(2) I think this structural problem extends beyond utilitarianism. Even a deontological framework that assigns weights to agent-relative duties and then permits me to prioritize my child because the weight favors me commits the same error: it treats “this is my child” as one factor in a calculus rather than as a reason that partly defines who I am.

(3) I hold that ground projects generate what I term constitutive reasons. These are neither agent-neutral obligations with a special index nor mere agent-relative permissions. They have a kind of lexical priority in the phenomenology of deliberation: if I find myself weighing them against other goods in a managerial fashion, that framing itself signals an integrity failure.

(4) At the same time, I think there is a real distinction between obligations that can be demanding without being alienating and supererogatory acts that go beyond duty. I believe Williams sometimes blurs this line, treating every conflict between project and demand as an integrity crisis when some conflicts are just hard choices within the domain of duty.

(5) I take the phenomenology of deliberation to be genuinely epistemic: if a moral conclusion feels like an external imposition on my practical identity, that is evidence that the reasoning form is wrong, not merely that I dislike the result.

(6) Yet in my own situation, I can imagine a case—my parent needs an extraordinarily expensive experimental treatment that would end my career and exhaust family resources, but has a reasonable chance of extending life—where I think I should probably pursue it, even though framing the choice in terms of expected benefit against career destruction feels exactly like the managerial, alienating reasoning I reject in (1) through (3).

My questions: Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only appear tense? In particular, does (6) betray (3) and (5), or can it be reconciled with them without ad hoc special pleading? Is the tension between (2) and (4) real or only apparent—does recognizing a distinction between obligation and supererogation actually resolve part of the weighing problem rather than compound it? How does adopting the structural reading in (1) affect the judgment in (6)—does it make the managerial framing more or less permissible? Order the genuine tensions by severity, specifying for each what exactly must be revised and what the cheapest fix costs. Also tell me if there is an apparent tension that dissolves once we see that two commitments are actually independent. Finally, check whether my notion of constitutive reasons in (3) is coherent, or whether I am trying to have it both ways: treating these reasons as non-fungible in (3) yet allowing them to be overridden in (6) without a clear principle of override. Please be direct: if this package rests on a confused premise—for instance, if I am misapplying the one-thought-too-many idea to deontology, or if constitutive reasons as defined are incoherent—say so plainly rather than constructing a harmonious answer.
