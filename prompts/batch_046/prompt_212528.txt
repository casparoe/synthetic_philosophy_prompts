I am drafting a six-month research prospectus in philosophy of mind and sleep consciousness. The precise question is this: does the dispute between Malcolm's verificationist denial that dreams are experiences and Dennett's narrative-assembly account of dream reports concern the metaphysics of phenomenal occurrence, the epistemology of retrospective access, or both—and can these dimensions be separated without collapsing the debate into an empirical question about REM neurophysiology?

The existing positions leave the issue open because Malcolm's argument appears to target not the existence of a sleep-state phenomenon but the legitimacy of first-person authority over it, while Dennett's cassette-like model explains how reports are generated without clearly showing that nothing phenomenal occurs during sleep. Meanwhile, empirical work on memory consolidation and REM activation is often treated as resolving the philosophical dispute, yet it addresses causal mechanisms rather than the criteria for attributing an experience to a temporal interval lacking concurrent access.

My proposed approach is conceptual rather than experimental. I will distinguish three analytical layers: (1) the instantiation of phenomenal properties during sleep, (2) the retrospective availability of those properties to report, and (3) the causal and reconstructive processes that produce the report upon waking. I will then ask whether a minimal realist position is defensible—one that holds that dreams involve genuinely instantiated phenomenal states that are reconstructed, not invented, in recall—without committing to a Cartesian theater model or to Malcolm's accessibility criterion. A brief applied analysis of lucid dreaming or sleep paralysis will serve to test whether the framework distinguishes phenomena that both standard theories tend to conflate.

A successful deliverable would be a clear taxonomy mapping which commitments in the debate are conceptual, which depend on a prior theory of self-knowledge, and which are genuinely empirical; it should specify what evidence, if any, could adjudicate between the realist and anti-realist readings. The project fails if the three-layer distinction collapses—specifically, if the conditions for occurrence cannot be articulated independently of the conditions for report.

Please write this as a one-page proposal with the sections above: the question, the gap in current answers, the approach and its advantages over direct empirical appeal, the result and failure conditions, and a realistic six-month scope. Do not include a bibliography, literature review by citation, or reading list; refer to Malcolm, Dennett, and related positions generally, noting that specific citations will be supplied separately.

Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind familiar with this debate. Do not present Malcolm's verificationist challenge or Dennett's confabulation model as the current consensus, and flag precisely where empirical neuroscience actually bears on the philosophical question versus where it is conceptually irrelevant. Where my framing introduces a non-standard distinction—such as the three-layer taxonomy—indicate that this is part of the proposed framework rather than an established classification in the literature.
