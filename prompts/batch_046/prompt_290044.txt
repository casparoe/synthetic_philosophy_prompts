I am preparing a graduate seminar module on vagueness that asks students to test whether semantic theories survive translation into institutional practice. Please restate the core commitments of epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness—treating them not as rival descriptions of language but as rival accounts of how a precise boundary can exist despite widespread ignorance and apparent borderline cases—using only the vocabulary and conceptual machinery of municipal zoning and building-code enforcement. Work with precise statutory thresholds (for instance, a “tall building” classification triggered at exactly seventy-five feet), the administrative mechanism of variances granted near boundary lines, the distinction between a structure that is clearly non-compliant and one that sits in an unadjudicated zone where no variance or ruling applies, and the epistemic situation of a code inspector who applies the rule without access to the legislative intent behind the boundary choice. For each theory, map its account of sharp boundaries, borderline cases, and epistemic ignorance onto this regulatory setting, and be explicit about what must be distorted to make the fit—particularly whether supervaluationist “admissible precisifications” correspond to different inspectors’ discretionary judgments, to ambiguities in the statute itself, or to something that has no institutional analog. Then provide a residue report: identify which philosophical claims survive the translation intact, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in zoning practice at all—especially any claim about vagueness being a feature of linguistic representation rather than of the world, or about the ontological status of the boundary itself. Conclude by assessing whether the residue reveals a limitation of the zoning metaphor (it is merely institutional, not metaphysical) or whether it exposes that one of the philosophical positions depends on distinctions that collapse once we treat the boundary as a practical construct rather than a semantic or ontological one. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation; work entirely from the conceptual content of the two positions.
