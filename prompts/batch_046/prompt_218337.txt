My roommate has been arguing with her lab partner about the unilateralist's curse and whether it actually pushes us toward centralised risk governence or away from it. J thinks the curse is basically a prisoner's dilemma with an exit option: if any single lab can unilaterally deploy a risky tech and capture private benefits, they'll do it even if everyone'd be safer with a joint agreement. So she wants hard multilateral caps, especially for open-source bio-design tools where one rogue release hurts everyone.

Her partner R says J is smuggling in an assumption that harm is immediate and symmetric. In the classic framing — like one country geoengineering the climate — the actor bears little cost and everyone else gets the fallout. But with AI or biotech, the releaser often internalises a lot of risk too (reputational blowback, legal liability, technical failure), so it's not a pure negative-externality game. He says that makes it closer to a coordination problem with private costs, not a curse.

J fired back that even if costs are partly internalised, they're not fully internalised. The global tail risk — say, an engineered pathogen escaping — is borne mostly by people who never consented. So the asymmetry remains, and the curse still bites.

R then brought up something neither had named: a "reverse unilateralist curse" where requiring multilateral consent for beneficial risk reduction can lock you in paralysis. If every country has veto power over a climate intervention that saves lives, the dangerous status quo persists because no one can act alone. So demanding multilateralism everywhere solves one curse but creates another.

J said that's false equivalence because the reverse case involves omission versus commission, and moral philosophy usually weights commission more heavily. R replied that under decision-theoretic uncertainty the line blurs if you're maintaining a dangerous status quo by inaction.

Then J asked: if both curses are real, how do we know which governance structure applies? R said you have to check whether the tech has a "threshold profile" — harmless below a scale, catastrophic above — versus a "linear profile" where every increment adds risk.

They got stuck tehre. I don't know who's right about what. Can you adjudicate point by point? Specifically: is R right that internalised costs break the curse logic? Is J right that global tail risk restores it? Does R's reverse curse actually parallel the original, or is J correct about omission/commission? And what's this threshold vs linear profile distinction — is it a real resolver, or are they both missing something deeper about how governence should actually work?
