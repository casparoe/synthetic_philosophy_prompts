I was discussing philosophy of medicine with colleagues last week, and we kept returning to the analogy that naturalists in the health-and-disease debate rely on: identifying disease is like diagnosing a malfunction in a complex machine—say, a misfiring turbine or corrupted circuit—where an engineer can point to a broken part relative to a design specification without invoking anyone's values about how life ought to go. The comparison is used to license the claim that disease is an objective, value-free category defined by subnormal biological function relative to a species-typical design, and that normativists are merely smuggling ethical preferences into clinical description under the cover of "harm" or "flourishing." I want you to audit this analogy with real precision.

Please begin with an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the machine part, the design specification, the statistical baseline, the repair manual, and the engineer—then show exactly where the mapping fractures: the absence of an intentional designer in evolutionary history, the statistical rather than prescriptive nature of species-typical function, cases of adaptive dysfunction such as sickle-cell trait or protective allostatic responses that are statistically abnormal yet biologically advantageous, and the value-laden choices involved in selecting a reference class or setting the threshold for "subnormal." Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the value-free conclusion from those that are only cosmetic. Then take a committed position: does the machine analogy survive in any form strong enough to ground naturalism, or does it collapse into something quite different—perhaps disease as a deviation from a negotiated or context-sensitive baseline rather than a broken gear? If the analogy was carrying the entire argument, say so plainly and explain what naturalism must do instead to defend objectivity; if a better analogy captures what naturalists actually need, propose it and defend the substitution. Do not merely list options—argue for the verdict you think holds.
