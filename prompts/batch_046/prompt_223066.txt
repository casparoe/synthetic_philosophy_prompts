I've been leading a small discussion group through the moral-perfection section of Benjamin Franklin's autobiography, and we've been circling a tension that the usual self-help summaries flatten out. Franklin frames virtue as an engineering project — thirteen items arranged in a weekly rotation, failures recorded as black marks in a pocket ledger — but he also treats perfect attainment as asymptotic, admitting he never mastered Order and eventually inserting Humility only after a Quaker friend pointed out the pride of displaying the list at all. What's less often noticed is the structural conflict between Order and Industry: the labor of cataloging and arranging becomes its own drain on productive work, so the metric of improvement undermines the work being improved. For our closing session, I'd like a poem that inhabits this specific bind rather than praising Franklin's industriousness in generic terms.

Please write an Italian sonnet — fourteen lines, iambic pentameter, with an octave and a sestet — spoken in Franklin's voice, not a modern commentator's. The octave should present the rotational system with its mechanical regularity: the thirteen virtues, the weekly cycle, the little book with its accumulating black marks. At the volta, line nine, the speaker should recognize that the rotation never terminates, and that this non-completion is not a defect of the method but its hidden logic — the marks remain precisely because the attempt continues. The rhyme scheme and meter should feel methodical, almost calendrical, but the diction should stay lyrical rather than didactic. The poem should hold open, rather than resolve, the tension between systematic self-correction and the humility of perpetual failure, especially the irony that Humility enters the system only after pride in the system itself has been exposed. Render the conceptual architecture accurately enough that someone familiar with Franklin's scheme would recognize the specific interaction between Order, Industry, and asymptotic virtue, while someone unfamiliar would still find the poem coherent on its own. No citations or bibliographic references are needed.
