Consider the institutional practice of archaeological conservation and museum accreditation for classical marble sculpture. When a statue arrives fragmented—say, with a replaced arm carved in the nineteenth century and original surfaces eroded—conservation boards apply norms of "integrity of original fabric": they may refuse to integrate a modern limb invisibly, or deny the object its highest heritage designation if too much original stone is missing, even though the form remains intact. At the same time, the same institutions treat the object as numerically identical to the statue created in antiquity, despite continuous material loss through weathering, cleaning, and repair. Meanwhile, a perfect modern replica of that form, carved from fresh marble, is classified as a copy or forgery rather than the statue itself. Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics of material constitution here: does the practice presuppose that the statue is identical to a particular aggregate of original matter (so that replacement undermines identity), or that it is identical to a form or structure that survives material turnover (so that replication should yield the same object), or some hybrid view? Is the practice internally consistent, or does it silently shift between a matter-focused criterion in heritage law and a form-focused criterion in curatorial identification? And if we replaced the underlying metaphysics with a strict four-dimensionalist account—where the statue is a temporal part of a continuous material worm—what concrete changes would have to occur in restoration policy, forgery law, and museum labeling?
