I'm preparing a presentation for a seminar on analogical reasoning and have been working through Hofstadter's Copycat framework and the idea that concepts in small, well-defined domains are fluid rather than rigid. Rather than revisit familiar alphabetic string examples, I want to examine an invented microdomain—let me call it Triad-Shift—to see whether conceptual slippage can be localized precisely when the domain is extended.

In Triad-Shift, every valid object is a horizontal arrangement of three geometric figures drawn from the set {circle, square, triangle}, each being either filled or hollow. There are exactly two licensed transformations. The first, Mirror, reverses left-to-right order. The second, Inversion, flips the fill-state of exactly the two outer figures, but only if the center figure is hollow; the center figure itself never changes. So the sequence filled-circle, hollow-square, filled-triangle becomes, via Inversion, hollow-circle, hollow-square, hollow-triangle, and a subsequent Mirror yields hollow-triangle, hollow-square, hollow-circle. Now suppose a reasoner is asked to extend Triad-Shift to four-figure arrangements—say, circle, square, triangle, circle—while preserving the same spirit of transformation. The reasoner must decide whether Mirror operates symmetrically around a central pair, whether Inversion targets only the two extreme ends or also the figures adjacent to them, and whether the hollow-center condition generalizes to a hollow pair in positions two and three.

Please argue for the following position: in microdomains like Triad-Shift, conceptual slippage is not primarily a failure of rule-application at the object level, but rather an unavoidable ambiguity in the meta-criterion that determines which structural features count as salient enough to be preserved across the extension. Your argument should treat the shift from three to four figures not as simple scaling, but as a case that forces the reasoner to invent—rather than discover—the boundary conditions of Mirror and Inversion. Specifically, defend the claim that once the domain is extended, the fluidity of the concept resides in the observer's changing judgment about what counts as the center or the ends, rather than in any hidden vagueness within the original rules themselves.

Structure your response with the following sections and approximate lengths: (1) "The microdomain defined" (about 150 words), restating Triad-Shift and the four-figure extension problem for clarity; (2) "The argument for meta-level slippage" (about 350 words), developing the position that judgments of salience, not rule ambiguity, drive the fluidity; (3) "A counterargument and reply" (about 250 words), addressing the objection that the rules are merely underspecified and that fluidity is therefore just concealed vagueness; and (4) "Conclusion" (about 100 words), summarizing why this distinction matters for understanding analogical reasoning in constrained domains.
