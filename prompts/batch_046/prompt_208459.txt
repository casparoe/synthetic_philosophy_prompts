I have been trying to apply the Buddhist anatta doctrine to the problem of diachronic personal identity, and I think I have reached an unavoidable contradiction. The standard view denies any permanent, unitary self and reduces the person to five impermanent skandhas in constant flux. Some recent interpreters attempt to preserve moral and practical continuity by introducing what they call a "narrative-aggregator" model: the conventional self is not a substance but a predictive binding loop that integrates successive mental states into a single accountable trajectory. Yet if this aggregator is itself merely one more impermanent formation—say, a complex configuration within sankhara—then it possesses no special authority to link moments across time; it is just another fleeting element in the stream. Consequently, anatta seems to force a choice between eliminativism about moral agents and the tacit reintroduction of a non-impermanent substrate functionally equivalent to an atman. Is there a coherent Buddhist response that avoids both horns without merely redefining "self" in a way that evades the original denial?
