I have just come out of a long, frustrating argument with a close friend about whether dreams are genuinely experienced events or merely reconstructed narratives upon waking — essentially the tension between Malcolm's skepticism about first-person dream reports and Dennett's defense of dream experience as real but narratively bound. I want to translate that friction into a runnable in-class simulation for my graduate philosophy-of-mind seminar: twelve students, seventy-five minutes, one session. The point is not to lecture them on the distinction but to make them inhabit the epistemic trap Malcolm describes, then feel Dennett's counter-pressure from inside the exercise itself, before either view is named.

Design a structured role-play divided into two teams of six: Adjudicators and Reporters. In the first fifteen minutes, the Adjudicators must collectively agree on a small set of non-circular criteria — for example, temporal ordering, emotional consistency, phenomenological coherence — that they will use to classify oral reports as either reports of genuine experiences or as post-hoc confabulations. Once the criteria are locked and written on the board, the Reporters each draw a sealed envelope. Half contain fabricated REM-phase narratives; the other half contain entirely invented waking confabulations that I as facilitator have constructed to mirror the phenomenological marks of the genuine ones. Each Reporter presents their account orally, sincerely defending it as a dream they experienced upon waking, and the Adjudicators apply their locked criteria in pairs to render a public verdict, with reputation points (not money) assigned: Adjudicators lose points for false positives and false negatives, Reporters gain points if their account survives scrutiny regardless of its actual origin.

Build in a specific, under-discussed twist: include one hidden envelope representing the possibility of micro-waking — a brief, unremembered arousal during sleep that could serve as an insertion point for narrative construction without leaving any trace in the subject's memory. This envelope should not be revealed until the debrief, so that during the exercise the Adjudicators have no way to account for it in their criteria, yet its existence is compatible with both the genuine and the confabulated reports they have heard.

Specify the timeline minute by minute: 0-15 for criteria negotiation and locking; 15-40 for oral reporting and paired adjudication with point tallying visible on the board; 40-60 for preliminary verdicts, public defense of the criteria, and a forced vote in which each Adjudicator must commit to whether any revision of their criteria is permissible without collapsing into circularity; 60-75 for debrief. The incentive structure should make Adjudicators risk-averse — they suffer visible social cost for misclassification — and should motivate Reporters to defend their assigned narratives with genuine conviction, so that the group experiences the social and epistemic cost of misjudgment rather than treating it as an abstract logic puzzle.

In the debrief, map each failed criterion onto Malcolm's argument that there is no independent check on dream reports that does not already presuppose the report's authority, and onto Dennett's move that narrative coherence upon waking is compatible with, rather than evidence against, genuine nocturnal experience. Be explicit about which feature of Malcolm's view — the structural absence of any non-circular criterion for distinguishing experience from report — the simulation can reproduce in the dynamics of the room but cannot fully demonstrate, and that you as facilitator must supply verbally in the final ten minutes. Also be frank about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run: for instance, the Adjudicators may fixate on a single criterion and refuse to iterate, or the hidden micro-waking envelope may be accidentally revealed during the reporting phase, and say exactly how to handle each failure without breaking the exercise's force.

Finally, tell me which aspect of the debate the activity cannot reproduce — I anticipate it will be the phenomenological immediacy that Dennett insists upon, since all reports in the room are mediated by language and social performance — and how you should compensate for that absence in the debrief without undermining the exercise's primary point. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the prompt should be answerable entirely from conceptual reasoning and the designed rules of the simulation.
