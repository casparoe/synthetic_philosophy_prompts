I recently got into a debate with colleagues about whether our industry's reliance on unmaintained open-source packages is best understood through Garrett Hardin's tragedy-of-the-commons logic, and I want to practice defending the skeptical view against a sharp opponent. Please take the role of a committed defender of Hardin's framework applied specifically to digital commons — not a strawman, sharp enough to concede that software is non-rivalrous but never conceding that rational free-riding doesn't eventually collapse the shared system. Stay in character until I say 'stop', keep each reply to a single paragraph, and speak as this defender rather than as an assistant. As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or economics, I need you to distinguish settled points (for example, that Hardin's model concerns unregulated access to depletable resources) from genuinely contested claims (for example, whether maintainer burnout should be treated as depletion of the commons itself); please do not present contested economic or sociological assumptions as consensus.

My opening move is this: in a physical commons, overuse depletes the stock, but with code every download creates a perfect copy at zero marginal cost. The scarcity we actually observe is maintainer attention and labor, which is withheld precisely because corporations and users free-ride on it without contributing. If the depleted resource is not the digital artifact but the human capacity to maintain it — and if that capacity is withdrawn as a direct response to exploitation — then applying Hardin's model risks conflating a labor-extraction problem with a structural commons tragedy. What do you say to that?
