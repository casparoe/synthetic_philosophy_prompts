okay so there's this thing where geologists have an official body that decides when geological epochs start, using these golden spikes in rock layers as proof. For other epochs they vote and move on, but with the Anthropocene there's a working group that proposed tying the start to nuclear fallout in the 1950s, while another camp wants 1610 linked to the columbian exchange and population collapse (some call it the orbis idea?). Then recently the main commission voted it down even though physical markers exist, yet climate scientists and museums just call our current era Anthropocene like it's settled.

What philosophical commitments does thier whole institutional dance presuppose? Does demanding a rock-layer marker for a human epoch assume impact only counts as geological when it leaves a specific chemical trace, rather than when societies restructure the biosphere? Or does it presuppose that stratigraphers should hold central authority even when other fields already treat the concept as live? There's also this weird tension: they reject Anthropocene for lacking formal boundary rules, but apply those rules inconsistently to other proposed subdivisions. Is the practice grounded in a view that historical periods are discovered in nature rather than negotiated, or is it mostly bureaucratic inertia? And if we swapped thier implicit theory for one where human historical agency itself defines the boundary regardless of rock chemistry, what parts of the voting practice would actually have to collapse?
