I've been reading about this historian Braudel and his idea that history moves at different speeds — geography and climate are like glaciers, people and battles are like sparks. I've tried to apply it to stuff I care about but my gut verdicts don't line up neat, so I want to check if there's actually a principle here or if I'm just making excuses.

The fall of the Western Roman Empire: definately the slow layer. Climate getting drier, soil tired out, trade shifting east for centuries. Alaric sacking Rome was just the visible bit, like a spark on dry wood.

Britain being first to industrialise in the 1800s: wierdly, I think this is the opposite. Yes coal was sitting there, but coal was sitting in China and Germany too. It was specific tinkerers — Watt, Arkwright — and specific patent laws. Swap out five people and Britain stays farming.

The Berlin Wall falling in '89: pure quick layer. Gorbachev not sending tanks, those specific protests in Leipzig. The Soviet economy had been rotten since the 70s but rotten economies don't just dissolve in one autumn.

The French Revolution in 1789: slow and middle together. Decades of fiscal crisis, bad harvests piling up, merchant class growing for generations. The Bastille was theatre, not cause.

The Maya lowland cities being abandoned around 900: I'm torn. Tree-ring data shows brutal drought — that's glacier-speed. But specific kings were overbuilding monuments and fighting stupid wars. Better leadership might have managed the drought, or maybe not.

My draft rule: if you can stop it by removing one person or cancelling one meeting, it's the quick layer; if you have to change the climate or soil, it's the glacier layer; in between is what I call "sticky habits" (laws, institutions) that take generations to shift.

2. Mapping my cases: for each clause, which case motivates it and whether it saves the verdict. About 150 words.
3. Two fresh cases: pick ones I haven't listed — maybe the Dutch Republic staying Protestant after 1648, or Japan modernising fast after 1868 — and say where your principle puts them. About 100 words.
4. The verdict I have to drop: which of my gut feelings doesn't survive, and what's the cost? About 50 words.
