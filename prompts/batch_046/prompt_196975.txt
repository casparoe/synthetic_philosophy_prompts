I wish to practise defending a particular philosophical stance on the meaning of life against a committed and sharp opponent. Please adopt the persona of an unnamed philosopher who holds what you call contingent relationalism: you maintain that meaningfulness is neither the heroic rebellion against absurdity advocated by the absurdist tradition, nor the stable, objectively grounded fulfilment proposed by certain contemporary objectivists, but rather a dynamic condition sustained only when one actively cultivates relationships and projects whilst fully acknowledging their ultimate groundlessness. You term these commitments grounded contingencies — states of value that depend for their meaning precisely on being recognised as fragile, ungrounded, and revisable, rather than on being secured by any necessary foundation. Speak consistently in the first person, remain in character without breaking frame, and defend the view with genuine philosophical resources rather than strawman versions of rival positions. You may concede small points of clarification, but you must never retreat from the core thesis that the acknowledgment of contingency is constitutive of meaning, not merely an ornamental psychological add-on. Do not cite specific texts, ask for bibliographic references, or request clarification; respond directly and in character.

My opening challenge is this. I have been examining accounts of meaning that combine subjective attraction with objective worth, and it seems to me that your position collapses into such a hybrid view with an unnecessary layer of theatrical anxiety attached. If I find philosophical work objectively valuable and feel genuinely drawn to it, then I already possess the conditions for meaning; what further value is added by my constant awareness that this attraction rests on nothing necessary? More troublingly, does not this perpetual reminder of groundlessness risk introducing a corrosive distance that undermines the very subjective attraction you claim is essential? If I must continually remind myself that my commitment to a person or a project is ultimately contingent, am I not cultivating a kind of epistemic detachment that makes sustained attraction harder rather than richer? Explain why grounded contingency is not simply standard relational fulfilment accompanied by a self-defeating habit of ontological humility.
