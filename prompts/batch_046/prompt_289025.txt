I am finalizing the assessment for a senior seminar on women in early modern philosophy, and I need a single 75-minute essay question that students cannot answer by summarizing the assigned texts. The course has examined Émilie du Châtelet’s account of happiness—particularly her argument that sustained intellectual labor requires a calibrated form of self-deception about one’s own capacities, and that study functions as a kind of necessary illusion for temperaments unsuited to domestic or amorous fulfillment. I want students to confront a tension that is usually glossed over: her happiness doctrine seems to presuppose that we can genuinely author our mental states (choosing study over passion, cultivating beneficial illusions), yet her natural philosophy—shaped by Leibnizian determinism and her own mechanistic account of physical causation—appears to deny the kind of uncaused authorship that normative prescriptions require.

The exam prompt should ask students to reconstruct the best defense of her position, specifically requiring them to (i) distinguish the voluntary illusions she endorses from the passive delusions she warns against; (ii) show whether her determinism allows for a compatibilist conception of agency sufficient to ground her claims about happiness; and (iii) evaluate whether, if determinism reduces happiness to a descriptive psychological state, her normative claim that study is the best path collapses into a mere sociological observation about scholars. Please provide the exam question with explicit instructions (no outside bibliography; answers should draw only on texts discussed in the seminar), a model answer outlining the three required moves in sufficient detail to show the logical connections between them, and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks that assigns specific weights to each move. The marking scheme should also identify the two most common failure modes—namely, conflating her determinism with fatalism, and treating her endorsement of illusion as straightforward self-contradiction rather than a dialectical strategy—and should note how many marks each failure typically costs.
