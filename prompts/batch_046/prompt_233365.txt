I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether “corruption” names a coherent cross-cultural wrong or is mainly a Weberian bureaucratic norm projected onto societies where clientelism and ceremonial gift-exchange function as primary social-insurance mechanisms; we kept talking past each other because neither of us had specified what an adequate theory of corruption is actually required to deliver. Before I try to settle the dispute, I want the full specification written out as a list of desiderata.

Write the requirements that any adequate theory of corruption—political, institutional, or interpersonal—must satisfy, with the concrete case or conceptual distinction behind each and a flag marking which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps. The list must handle at least: (i) the classic quid-pro-quo bribery case; (ii) clientelism in which resources flow through personal networks that simultaneously provide welfare, security, and political representation; (iii) obligatory or ceremonial gift-giving that generates diffuse, long-term reciprocity rather than a discrete exchange; (iv) practices that are locally legitimate yet globally corrosive to democratic accountability or rule-of-law; and (v) the distinction between corruption and mere inefficiency, unfairness, or favoritism, without presupposing that formal legal-rational procedures are the universal baseline. You should also require the theory to preserve a distinction between a corrupt act by an individual and the corruption of an institution’s purpose—illustrated by a charity that gradually shifts from serving beneficiaries to serving donor prestige, or a regulatory body that slowly replaces public-interest reasoning with industry-capture reasoning—so that the theory does not collapse institutional decay into a series of discrete bribes.

In addition, inject one niche conceptual requirement that standard lists usually omit: the theory must account for “reverse-corruption” scenarios, in which refusing an expected exchange—declining a ceremonial gift or breaking a patron-client obligation—causes more social harm, including the collapse of mutual-insurance networks, than accepting the exchange would. The theory should therefore explain whether corruption can be defined solely as deviation from formal rules, or whether it must also reference the functional role of the exchange within a larger system of obligations, and it must be able to say why reverse-corruption is not itself a form of corruption. Then identify which single desideratum, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest share of existing accounts, including both pure legal-formalist theories and strong cultural-relativist ones.

Briefly score three approaches against your list: a Weberian formal-rule-deviation account, an anthropological functionalist account that treats clientelism as adaptive reciprocity, and a hybrid “institutional purpose betrayal” account. Keep the scoring brief—the criteria are the deliverable.

Please lead with your bottom line—the core claim about what corruption fundamentally is—in the first sentence, then present the reasoning and the desiderata list. No preamble, no restating of the question.
