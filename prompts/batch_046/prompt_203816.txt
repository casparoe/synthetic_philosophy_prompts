As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind, I have become preoccupied by a structural parallel that the literature seems to treat as belonging to separate domains. In physics we routinely grant causal efficacy to macroscopic properties—temperature driving thermal expansion, superconductivity explaining zero resistance—despite the fact that every such macroscopic event is fully realized by microscopic interactions. We do not regard these macro-level explanations as causally redundant or excluded by their microphysical bases; instead we treat them as legitimate interlevel causes. By contrast, in philosophy of mind the causal exclusion argument contends that if mental properties supervene on physical properties and the physical domain is causally closed, then mental properties are rendered causally redundant. The asymmetry is puzzling: why should downward or interlevel causation be unproblematic in physics yet fatal for mental causation?

I seek a proposal for a one-semester term paper of approximately 6,000 words that treats this comparison as its central question. The proposal should formulate the problem with precision: do the exclusion conditions apply symmetrically to emergent physical properties and mental properties, or is there a principled metaphysical distinction that protects the former while undermining the latter? It should explain why existing positions leave this open—philosophers of mind rarely examine the metaphysics of emergence in physics, while discussions in physics rarely address the causal-closure premise that drives exclusion. The proposed method should be conceptual analysis rather than empirical research: selecting a standard physical case (for example, the causal role of temperature in phase transitions or of rigidity in structural failure) and mapping its explanatory structure onto a mental-causal claim (for example, a belief causing an action), then comparing how each handles the relation between macro-level cause and micro-level realization.

The proposal should specify what success would entail—perhaps a taxonomy of conditions under which interlevel causation avoids redundancy, with mental and physical properties falling on different sides of the boundary—and what failure would look like, such as finding that the physical cases collapse upon analysis into pure micro-causal descriptions with no genuine downward efficacy. Finally, it should include a realistic month-by-month timeline for a single semester and an honest assessment of scope risks, particularly the danger that resolving the physics side requires more metaphysics of emergence than can be adequately treated in 6,000 words.
