I'm working on my diss in historiography of science and I've been getting pretty sold on this anti-periodization line: that "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution" aren't just rough heuristics but actually do damage as retroactive ontological sorts. The core idea I'm playing with is what I'd call chronocentric clustering—forcing continuous practices of commentary, translation, and instrument-building into discontinous boxes that serve modern disciplinary identity more than historical accuracy. So my position is that treating these as real epochs is conceptually misleading, kind of like organizing combustion chemistry around phlogiston.

But I want to actually stress-test this instead of just preaching it. What would genuinely count against it? Not just "historians use these terms," but what kind of evidence or arguement would show there really are non-arbitrary discontinuities in, say, the social organization of knowledge production or the self-understanding of practicioners? And what would defenders of the standard periodization themselves concede is a live problem—maybe the blurring at the margins, the Eurocentrism, the anachronistic projection of "science"—without that concession collapsing their core claim? I want an honest map of where this view is actually exposed, what's decisive versus merely awkward, and whether it's well-insulated in a way that's intellectually healthy or just defensive. What risks does it take that its rivals don't?
