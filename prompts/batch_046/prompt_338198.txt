I'm preparing a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and I keep encountering a particular analogy in classroom discussion and in the secondary literature: prioritarianism is said to operate like a progressive tax system applied to well-being. On this picture, benefits to the worse-off are taxed at a low rate (or even subsidized), while benefits to the better-off face a steep marginal rate, with the "revenue" being the total weighted moral value. The leveling-down objection is then often rebutted by comparing it to a tax bracket that confiscates income from the rich and literally burns it—harmful, wasteful, and not part of any rational fiscal design. Recently, a colleague pushed the framing further by proposing that we model prioritarian weights using something like a negative-income-tax schedule for moral status: below a certain well-being floor, weights are amplified; above it, they are reduced but never become negative, so that destroying well-being for the rich can never be justified as a "clawback." I want my students to see whether this fiscal framing genuinely helps prioritarianism or whether it smuggles in assumptions that break under philosophical pressure.

Please write a formal audit of this analogy for my seminar handout. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the tax authority, the taxpayer, the tax base, the revenue, the progressive schedule, the negative-income-tax floor, and the destructive "burning" that corresponds to leveling down. Second, identify the disanalogies—particularly where well-being is not fungible like revenue, where moral evaluation is not coercive like taxation, and where interpersonal comparison differs from income measurement—and separate those that genuinely undermine the rebuttal to leveling down from those that are merely decorative. Third, assess whether the negative-income-tax variant genuinely blocks the leveling-down objection or whether it simply builds the rejection into the schedule's architecture. Fourth, tell me whether the analogy, once audited, still licenses the conclusion that leveling down is irrational, or whether the objection survives in a form the fiscal picture cannot capture. Finally, if you think there is a more accurate analogy for prioritarian weighting—perhaps drawn from resource allocation, medical triage, or another domain—propose it briefly and explain why its mapping is tighter. Write in a formal register suitable for a philosophy professor distributing it to advanced students.
