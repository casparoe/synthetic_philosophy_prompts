Analyze the Apollo 13 carbon-dioxide scrubber crisis of April 1970 as a case study through Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian minds. Focus on the decision sequence: the lunar module crew's recognition of rising CO2 levels, mission control's design of the adapter using only items available aboard the spacecraft, and the execution under severe time and oxygen constraints. For each stage, specify which kind of cognitive agent—individual or distributed collective—is performing the work: whether the astronauts under stress operate as Darwinian reflex-responders or Gregorian language-users, whether the ground team acts as Popperian internal-modelers testing configurations mentally before radioing instructions, or as Skinnerian trial-and-error agents relying on physical mock-ups in Houston. Then turn the analysis back on the taxonomy: does the resolution demonstrate that Gregorian cultural tools—checklists, shared engineering notation, written procedures—are indispensable, or could an isolated Popperian agent have reached the same solution? Identify where the taxonomy is silent on the division of cognitive labor between the crew and mission control, and whether extreme environmental pressure collapses Gregorian capacities into lower-level modes. Conclude with what the episode confirms, challenges, or fails to illuminate about Dennett's claim that Gregorian minds constitute a qualitatively distinct kind of problem-solver.
