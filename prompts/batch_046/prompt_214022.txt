In a graduate seminar on metaphilosophy, a recurring debate concerns whether philosophy makes progress in anything like the way the sciences do. One colleague presses the analogy that philosophy is essentially science operating at a higher level of abstraction: both construct cumulative theoretical frameworks, both employ something analogous to experiments via thought experiments and counterexample-driven refinement, both discard failed paradigms—Aristotelian hylomorphism, logical positivism, naive correspondence theories of truth—and both aim at descriptive accuracy about reality. She takes this correspondence to license the conclusion that philosophy does progress, merely more slowly and with disagreement more visible because the domain is harder. Another interlocutor offers a different mapping, comparing philosophy to mathematics: progress occurs not through empirical accumulation but through the establishment of necessary results—Gödel's incompleteness theorems settling Hilbert's program, modal logic formalizing previously vague modal intuitions, or the precise articulation of paradoxes—where convergence is local but definitive.

I want you to audit both analogies systematically: provide the explicit correspondence table for each (what plays the role of data, experiment, theoretical framework, falsification mechanism, and proof or verification), pinpoint exactly where the mapping breaks—particularly regarding persistent disagreement without convergence, the absence of a shared empirical baseline, and the normative or conceptual-engineering aims that distinguish philosophy from both empirical science and formal mathematics—then assess whether either analogy genuinely supports the progress thesis or whether it merely imports the conclusion by selecting favorable correspondences. Finally, if neither holds up well, propose a more apt analogy—perhaps to legal reasoning, cartography, or conceptual engineering—and explain what dynamics it captures that the science and mathematics comparisons obscure.
