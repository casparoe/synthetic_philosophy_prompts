I am drafting an essay for a seminar on pragmatism and antirepresentationalism, and I would welcome critical comments on its central framing. The essay takes Rorty’s rejection of the “mirror of nature” as its point of departure, but instead of treating antirepresentationalism as a purely negative thesis, it argues that Rorty’s account of “final vocabularies” contains an underappreciated constructive dimension. Specifically, I propose that any working vocabulary sustains what I call representational debt: a network of pragmatic obligations among its constituent beliefs that pressures individual claims toward internal coherence. On this reading, coherence is not a proxy for correspondence to an independent reality, but it functions analogously—beliefs that violate the vocabulary’s established commitments incur a kind of pragmatic liability that must be discharged through revision, reinterpretation, or abandonment of the offending term. I then apply this idea to scientific change, suggesting that what feels like “discovering we were wrong” during a paradigm shift is not nature correcting our representations, but rather the accumulated representational debt of the old vocabulary becoming too costly to service, thereby forcing abnormal discourse.

My concern is that this economic metaphor may smuggle back in exactly the representationalist criterion Rorty wants to eliminate: if coherence-pressure operates like accuracy-pressure, have I merely renamed correspondence rather than eliminated it? I am also unsure whether linking this mechanism to Kuhnian paradigm shifts respects Rorty’s sharp distinction between normal discourse—working within a shared vocabulary—and abnormal discourse—attempting to change vocabularies for edifying rather than truth-seeking reasons—or whether it collapses his pragmatic pluralism into a philosophy of science he would reject. Finally, I wonder whether the argument offers anything beyond a relabeling of familiar pragmatist moves. Could you assess whether the “representational debt” framing genuinely captures Rorty’s distinction between normal and abnormal discourse, comment on the aptness of the Kuhnian analogy, and say whether the essay risks reintroducing representationalism through its coherence conditions? Any observations on clarity, interest, or conceptual coherence would also be valuable.
