Design a 90-minute workshop activity for twelve philosophy graduate students in a collective-action seminar that makes them experience the dynamics of a commitment race—not as a standard prisoner's dilemma, but as an escalation driven specifically by the competitive logic of credible self-binding, in which each agent's irreversible pledge becomes a strategic constraint on others. The participants should not hear the term "commitment race" until the debrief.

Structure the session as a 50-minute structured simulation followed by a 40-minute debrief. For the simulation, create a rule system with tangible, small-stakes payoffs—such as points that determine seating priority or exemption from a shared administrative task—centered on pairs managing a common resource pool. On each turn, a student may place part of their personal endowment into an irreversible "pledge box" visible to their partner; once placed, it cannot be withdrawn. The joint payoff for the pair rises with the total pledged amount up to a precise threshold, then collapses sharply if both exceed it. Crucially, include a "credibility penalty": if one student pledges less than their partner, they suffer a loss larger than the resources they conserved. After observing a partner's pledge, a student may also execute a "counter-pledge," locking in an additional amount that alters the payoff structure for both parties in subsequent rounds.

Arrange the payoff matrix and turn order so that rational, cooperative pairs tend to overshoot the optimal threshold within three to four rounds, and so that unilateral withdrawal is visibly punished by the rules rather than by social pressure. Do not explain the strategic logic beforehand; participants should discover through play that backing down feels individually irrational even when both prefer the lower-pledge outcome.

In the debrief, map specific moments of play onto the conceptual anatomy of a commitment race: (1) the initial pledge as a signal that binds one's own future options; (2) the partner's counter-pledge as a response not to hostility but to the need to match credibility; (3) the collapse point as a collectively undesired outcome; and (4) the failed unilateral withdrawal as evidence that de-escalation requires coordinated release mechanisms, not individual choice. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—particularly the temporal and institutional extension of real-world commitments that unfold over months or years rather than minutes—and specify exactly how the facilitator should supply that missing dimension through a brief framing narrative after the game concludes. Predict two predictable failures in execution: one pair refusing to engage competitively and thereby flattening the experiential tension, and participants misinterpreting the game as a pure prisoner's dilemma and missing the irreversibility feature; for each, state how the facilitator should intervene without breaking the experiential frame.

The deliverable should be a fully runnable script: the exact rules and payoff table, the physical materials required, the turn sequence, the ordered debrief questions, and a frank assessment of which conceptual feature must be supplied by the facilitator rather than emerging organically from play. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific theorists; the conceptual framing should stand on its own.
