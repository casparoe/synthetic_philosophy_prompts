As a novelist mapping characters' motivations, I keep encountering the same two-step argumentative pattern. First, the pleasures I actually depict—a character savoring wine, another savoring revenge, another the cold click of a solved proof—share no discernible inner texture whatsoever. A defender might reply that they are united not by feeling but by being pursued for their own sake; yet that same directional mark applies cleanly to non-pleasures, such as keeping an onerous promise out of pure duty or maintaining a reputation one secretly despises. So the proposed boundary dissolves not because the pleasures feel alike, but because the substitute criterion overreaches into adjacent categories. Does this specific configuration—phenomenal scatter followed by normative overextension—have an established philosophical name of its own? I want less a bare label than a precise carving: how it differs from the familiar heterogeneity worry about hedonism, whether it threatens other mental-state theories of value, and what distinguishes cases where the substitute criterion succeeds from those where it collapses as it does here.
