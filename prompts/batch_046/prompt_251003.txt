I am drafting a short philosophical essay that tries to complicate the standard cyclical-versus-linear taxonomy of historical time. Rather than treating Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, and Toynbee as representatives of a single cyclical camp opposed to progressive linearity, I propose distinguishing two internal varieties of recurrence: directional cyclicality and recursive cyclicality.

In the first section, I argue that Polybius's anacyclosis—the constitutional sequence running from monarchy through kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, ochlocracy, and back to monarchy—is best understood as directionally cyclical. Although the formal sequence closes upon itself, each iteration unfolds within an expanded territorial and administrative context that inherits legal and institutional residues from prior phases. The loop is topologically circular, but the substrate is not identical; earlier structures constrain and reshape the returning constitutional form, producing something closer to a constrained spiral than to pure repetition.

In the second section, I contrast this with Ibn Khaldun's model in the Muqaddimah. There, the dynastic cycle driven by asabiyyah (group solidarity) exhibits recursive cyclicality. When urban luxury erodes solidarity and a dynasty collapses, a fresh nomadic group with unspoiled cohesion conquers the center and founds a new dynasty. The institutional memory of the fallen state is largely erased rather than transformed, and the new ruling group begins with a tribal structure substantially analogous to its predecessor. The recurrence genuinely resets the social substrate.

The third section applies this taxonomy to Spengler and Toynbee. I suggest Spengler's morphology of culture entering its civilizational phase fits recursive cyclicality: once creative vitality is exhausted and urban intellectualism dominates, decline is terminal within that culture, and renewal requires an external, fresh cultural seed rather than internal institutional adaptation. Toynbee's challenge-and-response framework, by contrast, permits directional cyclicality: a civilization that fails to meet one challenge retains spiritual and institutional resources that shape its subsequent responses, so breakdown represents path-dependent divergence rather than a pure reset.

I present these as essay notes rather than a finished draft. I would welcome focused comments on four points. First, is the distinction between directional and recursive cyclicality conceptually coherent, or does it collapse under closer scrutiny of Polybius's actual account of constitutional transformation? Second, does this framing misrepresent Ibn Khaldun by overstating the erasure of institutional memory between dynasties? Third, does it distort Spengler or Toynbee—for instance, by attributing too much openness to institutional continuity in Toynbee's model of breakdown? Fourth, is there a significant third mode of recurrence visible in other cyclical theorists that this binary omits? Finally, does the taxonomy genuinely illuminate something that the conventional cyclical-linear dichotomy obscures, or is it primarily a relabeling?

Please evaluate the argument for conceptual accuracy, interest, and specific weak points in the reasoning, without providing bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
