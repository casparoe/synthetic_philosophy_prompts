I am composing a conceptual profile for a private salon I convene—an exercise in intellectual self-portraiture, conducted with the same severity I apply to tracing a character's hidden motives across a novel's arc. The subject is the so-called alignment problem, and I have arrived at a cluster of commitments that I fear may be more theatrical than coherent. I want you to audit them as you might audit a plot for hidden inconsistencies in causality.

My first conviction is that the alignment problem should be defined with severe narrowness: it is the problem of intent alignment alone. The task is to ensure that an artificial system pursues precisely the objectives its designers have specified, and nothing more. Whether those objectives are morally admirable is irrelevant to whether the system is aligned; a machine perfectly calibrated to a malevolent purpose is, technically speaking, well aligned, and the moral failure belongs to the designers, not to the mechanism. From this I draw a second commitment: value alignment is not a sub-discipline of AI safety but a matter for normative political philosophy and social choice theory. It should not be treated as an engineering prerequisite for deployment.

Yet the profile darkens. I also hold that designers cannot specify intent in a vacuum of values, because any formalization of a goal requires an interpretive framework—conditions of success, metrics of failure, boundaries of acceptable side effects—that inevitably embeds normative judgments. I reject the argument that development should be frozen until universal moral agreement is achieved, since postponement is itself a decision with distributive and ethical consequences that cannot be evaded by framing it as neutrality. At the same time, I am suspicious that discourse around value alignment often serves as rhetorical cover, allowing particular ideological commitments to enter technical debate disguised as neutral safety requirements. And despite all this, I maintain that designers bear direct moral responsibility for the outcomes of the systems they build, and that intent-aligned artifacts must still be subjected to rigorous external ethical evaluation.

The tension, I suspect, lies between my insistence on a pure, value-free conception of intent alignment and my admission that intent cannot be formalized without normative residue. There may also be a quieter contradiction between my cynicism about value-alignment rhetoric and my demand for moral accountability. Please examine these commitments in pairs. Identify which conflicts are genuine and severe, which are only apparent, and for each genuine tension specify what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Rank them by severity. Then tell me whether there is a single change—perhaps to my definition of intent, or to my view of designer responsibility—that would resolve the deepest conflict without forcing me to abandon either my skepticism toward value-alignment discourse or my refusal to treat moral consensus as a deployment gate. I ask this not for a lecture, but for an audit.
