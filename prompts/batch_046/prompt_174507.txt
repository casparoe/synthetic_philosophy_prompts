I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or archaeology, but I have been trying to understand why the scholarly debate about societal 'collapse' — particularly the Bronze Age crisis near 1177 BCE, the Classic Maya abandonment of the southern lowlands, and the ecological and demographic transformation of Easter Island — has remained deadlocked for decades. One group of researchers treats these episodes as genuine failures: centralized administration dissolves, interregional trade collapses, monumental construction stops, writing systems disappear, and populations fall sharply. Another group insists that societies never truly fail; they simply reorganize into lower-complexity, more localized forms — pastoral or village economies, different agricultural regimes — and that labeling this process 'collapse' smuggles in a false teleology of progress and decline.

Both camps cite the same archaeological strata, the same pollen evidence for deforestation on Rapa Nui, the same disappearance of Linear B administration in the Aegean, and the same demographic reconstructions showing rapid decline. Yet neither side has convinced the other. Is this stalemate a substantive disagreement about historical facts, a verbal dispute over whether the term 'collapse' applies whenever sociopolitical complexity drops rapidly, or a case of two disciplines answering different questions — one about lived experience and institutional loss, the other about long-term evolutionary trajectories — under a single heading?

To complicate the diagnosis, I have begun to wonder whether the hidden disagreement is about the identity conditions of a society itself. In software engineering we think about distributed systems: individual nodes can fail, protocols can degrade, and the overall network can lose functionality without being annihilated, depending on which connections we treat as essential to the system's identity. Applied here, the Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean might be a cascading node failure in which Phoenician trade protocols and local agricultural nodes survive, making the system a degraded but continuous network; the Maya lowlands might represent graceful degradation rather than a crash. If the 'failure' camp implicitly requires preservation of specific institutional nodes (palace economies, writing) for societal identity, while the 'reconfiguration' camp treats identity as preserved so long as local agricultural and kinship protocols continue, then they may be arguing past each other about which parts of a social graph are essential. Is that diagnosis plausible, or is the stall better explained by a masked normative disagreement — perhaps about whether rapid simplification is inherently harmful — disguised as an empirical debate? And if the impasse is indeed conceptual rather than factual, what specific distinction — empirical, formal, or definitional — would actually allow the field to move forward?
