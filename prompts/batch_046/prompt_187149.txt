A friend tried to explain to me Hofstadter's idea in Gödel, Escher, Bach about where meaning lives in a message. She said every message has three layers: the frame, which is the background rules or format that make interpretation possible; the outer message, which is the explicit literal content; and the inner message, which is the implicit meaning that only emerges once the frame is properly understood. She illustrated this with Gödel's incompleteness proof, saying the frame is formal arithmetic, the outer message is a particular formula about numerical relations, and the inner message is the hidden self-referential claim about that formula's own unprovability within the system. Then she added her own extension, which she calls the echo frame: she claims there is a fourth layer in which the listener's own mental model becomes part of the framing structure, so that two people with the same language and culture can understand the same joke differently because their echo frames resonate differently with the message. She said Hofstadter missed this layer, and that without accounting for it one cannot explain ordinary interpretive disagreement; she also concluded that artificial intelligence can never truly understand language because it lacks an echo frame entirely. I am not sure which parts are faithful to the book's three-layer distinction, which are her invention or misreading, whether the echo-frame idea is a reasonable extension or a confusion of the original framework, and whether the conclusion about AI actually follows from anything in Hofstadter's view. Could you audit the summary: separate what accurately represents the frame/outer-message/inner-message distinction, identify the distortions or additions, evaluate whether the echo frame coherently extends the idea or breaks it, and say whether the argument about artificial intelligence is supported by the corrected version of the framework?
