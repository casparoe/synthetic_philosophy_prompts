I am preparing a formal proposal for my school's semester-long independent study in moral philosophy. My half-formed thesis: Peter Singer's drowning-child argument is standardly treated as a problem about physical distance and the cost of rescue, but I believe it conceals an unexamined assumption about epistemic visibility. In the original pond case, the victim is present to the agent's perception; in the global-poverty analog, the victim is absent and rescue is mediated by institutions. I want to investigate whether this difference in visibility is merely a psychological obstacle or a conceptually relevant feature of the duty itself. More precisely: does Singer's principle of rescue depend on direct perceptual access to the victim, and if so, does institutional mediation weaken or transform the obligation? Existing responses tend either to defend Singer by denying that distance matters, or to reject him on grounds of over-demandingness and psychological limitation. Neither camp examines whether mediated rescue introduces a distinct moral structure—specifically, whether causal-chain length and emotional salience alter the normative force of the duty rather than just its feasibility. For my project, I propose to construct two parallel cases—one direct, one institutionally mediated—matched for outcome severity and agent cost, then analyze them across three conceptual variables: epistemic access, length of causal chain, and emotional salience. Over twelve weeks, I will: (Weeks 1–3) reconstruct Singer's analogy and map the standard objections; (Weeks 4–6) develop the direct-versus-mediated case framework and defend the three variables; (Weeks 7–9) argue whether visibility is a conceptual condition or a contingent feature, testing both conclusions; (Weeks 10–12) assess failure conditions—specifically, whether the argument collapses into empirical psychology or the familiar over-demandingness debate. A successful result will either show that mediated rescue is normatively equivalent, thereby strengthening Singer's universalism, or demonstrate that it belongs to a different moral category, thereby limiting the analogy's reach. The project will fail if it requires empirical data on moral motivation or if the distinction between direct and mediated rescue dissolves under analysis. The scope is strictly conceptual: no fieldwork, no citation-dependent literature review, and no reliance on empirical studies. I will supply all references afterward. Please turn this into a polished two-page proposal with a precise research question, an explanation of the open gap, the phased method, the success and failure criteria, and a clear justification for the conceptual approach over empirical or historical alternatives.
