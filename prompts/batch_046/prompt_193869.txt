I have been covering international atrocity investigations for a long-form feature, and I keep encountering a pattern I cannot name precisely. Investigators publish exhaustive factual findings—targeted ethnic or religious groups, documented intent to destroy, systematic methods of killing—that align with the established legal criteria for genocide, yet the official report deliberately withholds the single-word designation, using instead formulations such as acts that may constitute genocide or references to a genocidal context. In the public debate that follows, advocacy organizations cite the factual chapters as confirmation that genocide has occurred, while state actors and opposing parties point to the absence of the formal legal label to reject the charge entirely. The verified substance and the precise nomenclature thus become decoupled and are deployed as independent currencies: one side trades in documented intent and scale, the other in the absence of classification, and the gap itself is exploited rather than resolved. I am aware that this relates to the politics of naming atrocities and to Lemkin's conceptual legacy, but I am not asking about genocide denial in the usual sense, nor about ordinary strategic ambiguity. Rather, I want to know whether there is an established concept for this specific configuration—the decoupling of substantiated factual patterns from their legal classification, followed by the strategic use of that decoupling by opposing parties. If there is no standard term, how should it be distinguished from definitional evasion, semantic hedging, or denialism, and what would be the most precise way to define its boundaries?
