I'm trying to map which open problems around the separateness-of-persons objection actually deserve effort from anyone building decision frameworks—whether for longtermist resource allocation, artificial-agent governance, or large-scale institutional planning—rather than treating it as a settled seminar topic. The classic interpersonal case is well-trodden, but I'm focused on four less-examined lines: (1) whether separateness operates as a constraint on the aggregation function itself or as an action-level side-constraint that holds regardless of how values are summed; (2) whether it survives a functionalist or relational account of personhood, or whether it is parasitic on biological continuity and thus inapplicable to non-biological agents or future generations instantiated differently; (3) whether it imposes different obligations toward actual versus potential persons when the creation of persons is part of the optimization target; and (4) whether accommodating it requires abandoning cardinal interpersonal comparison altogether, or only restructuring it, and what that restructuring implies for algorithmic planning rules.

Of these four, which two or three are genuinely upstream—meaning that resolving them would change the architecture of a real allocation or governance system—versus which are conceptual refinements that leave practical outputs unchanged? Rank them with explicit criteria: dependency on other questions, tractability with current conceptual tools, and the magnitude of practical change if resolved in either direction. Identify which line is most overrated in philosophical discourse relative to its institutional payoff, and flag whether there's a fifth line—perhaps involving information-theoretic boundaries of the self or multi-agent coordination failures—that should displace one of the four. I want a defended agenda, not a survey, and I care particularly about cases where the answer wouldn't change what a planner should do, only how they justify it.
