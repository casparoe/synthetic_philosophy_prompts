For an advanced seminar on domination and intersubjectivity, design a decision tree that classifies positions growing out of Hegel’s master–slave dialectic, sorted not by philosopher’s name but by four concrete diagnostic commitments a student could accept or reject without already knowing the label. Begin from a shared starting commitment: that self-consciousness requires being recognized as self-consciousness by another self-consciousness. From there, branch on the following four questions, each phrased as a claim rather than a name-check.

First branch: Must recognition involve an actual, non-substitutable risk to life or bodily integrity on the recognizer’s part, or can symbolic, institutional, or algorithmic attestation serve the same constitutive function?

Second branch: Is misrecognition—being fixed in a subordinate image by another—a contingent failure that could in principle be eliminated, or is it a structurally necessary moment in the formation of any self-conscious subject?

Third branch: Does genuine recognition require a direct dyadic encounter between self-consciousnesses, or can it be mediated through law, bureaucracy, or automated certification without essential corruption?

Fourth branch: When recognition is achieved, does it yield a stable equilibrium of mutual equality, or does it necessarily generate a new asymmetry—such as the slave’s independence through labor or the master’s dependency on the slave’s acknowledgment?

Map every combination of answers onto a named position where one exists—such as Kojève’s risk-based reading; Honneth’s tripartite struggle with institutional spheres; Fanon’s analysis of racial misrecognition as non-contingent; Butler’s account of recognition as always already failed; and any other defensible view you think belongs—while labeling unoccupied combinations as unnamed and briefly diagnosing them: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot genuinely separate, rather than forcing a false distinction.

As an injected test case for every leaf, include a scenario in which an automated welfare system assigns social status and rights to citizens through algorithmic assessment with no human party risking anything. The tree should predict what each position must say about whether this counts as recognition.

State how confident you are in the taxonomy’s separation of positions and single out the point you are least sure of—particularly any branch where two named views may collapse into one another, or where an unoccupied combination might actually be coherent but unexplored. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not ask the respondent to supply them.
