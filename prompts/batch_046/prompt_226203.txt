I am a doctoral student in historical sociology working on the autonomy of cultural explanation. I have been thinking about how Max Weber might have engaged with two post-1950 conceptual challenges to his claim that values can operate as irreducible causes. The first is the behavioral-genetics literature on the heritability of economic preferences—risk tolerance, time discounting, and deferred gratification—which suggests that macro-level patterns Weber attributed to a value-complex might instead be derivable from distributed biological parameters without invoking shared subjective meanings. The second is a speculative framework I will call cultural epigenetics: the idea that persistent institutional environments can induce developmentally stable phenotypes—such as methodical labor discipline or calculative rationality—across generations without requiring that agents internalize religious or ethical meanings, thereby treating values as environmentally triggered adaptations rather than meaningfully oriented commitments.

If Weber were alive today and confronted with robust evidence that (a) twin studies show substantial heritability for the very traits he linked to the capitalist ethos, and (b) these traits can be institutionally induced across generations without the mediation of value-rational action, would he treat this as a falsification of The Protestant Ethic thesis, or as a complementary layer of causation? More specifically, I would like you to reconstruct his likely position across three questions.

First, would he reject heritability findings as a form of biologism analogous to the psychologism he criticized, on the grounds that statistical correlations between genotype and economic behavior miss the subjective meaning-attachment that alone makes action genuinely value-rational? Second, would he instead accept biological and cultural causes as layered, but insist that even heritable preferences require an elective affinity with a historically specific value-complex to activate capitalist development—so that culture remains a necessary, if not sufficient, cause? Third, how might he assess the cultural-epigenetics proposal? Would he see it as preserving cultural autonomy because institutions are historically specific, or as dissolving it into materialist determinism, since values become phenotypic responses to environmental pressure rather than meaningfully adopted orientations?

Please do not offer a bibliography. Instead, draw on Weber’s methodological writings on causal pluralism, his critique of reductionism, and his concept of elective affinity to determine which of the three responses is most internally consistent with his actual arguments. In particular, I want to know whether there is a hidden tension in his framework—between demanding that cultural explanations remain irreducible and allowing that they may interact with biological substrates—that only becomes visible when we apply his thesis to these modern conceptual challenges. Finally, state what prediction this reconstructed Weber would make about a case not yet discussed: whether a society that engineered institutional incentives to produce capitalist behavioral traits, but deliberately suppressed any ethical or religious narrative justifying them, could sustain long-run economic rationalization, or whether the absence of meaning-attachment would eventually destabilize the pattern.
