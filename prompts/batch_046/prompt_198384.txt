For a feature I am writing on how philosophers stage abstract ethical limits in live classroom settings, I need a fully specified in-class simulation for a postgraduate seminar in practical ethics. The group comprises fourteen participants; the session runs for ninety minutes; all are conversant with elementary utilitarian reasoning but have not been taught formal population axiology. Rather than asking for a lecture or textual analysis, I want a structured activity — with rules, assigned roles, and non-monetary payoffs — that makes the participants feel the pull of the repugnant conclusion, but through a framing that departs from the familiar “vastly larger population of barely worth living lives” shorthand that circulates in popular philosophy. Instead, the exercise should centre on a hypothetical Intergenerational Welfare Board charged with distributing a fixed quantum of “flourishing potential” across successive cohorts. Each cohort must be assigned welfare profiles built from three explicitly incommensurable goods — autonomous agency, deep relational attachment, and sustained aesthetic engagement — each realisable at varying intensities. The board’s charter requires every life to meet minimal thresholds in all three dimensions, yet permits trade-offs between the intensity of these goods and the total number of lives sustained, governed by a simple additive scoring rule that the participants apply themselves.

Please design the activity so that its mechanics — not the facilitator’s commentary — drive the group, round by round, through a gradual “axiological drift”: they begin by endorsing small, intensely flourishing populations, then find that locally rational expansions of cohort size, each preserving minimal thresholds, dilute all three goods until an immense population remains in which each dimension is present only at the barest margin. The participants should discover this endpoint for themselves.

Specifically, include: a precise ninety-minute timeline; the physical or digital materials required; clearly defined roles (for instance, board members, independent auditors, and dissenting advocates); a payoff or incentive scheme that uses symbolic status, graded access to a shared resource, or structured deliberation rights rather than real money; and a debrief plan that maps each round onto the corresponding logical step in the aggregation argument, anticipates what will predictably fail on the first run (such as participants collapsing the three dimensions into a single metric, or refusing to authorise further expansions), and states honestly which feature of the classic repugnant conclusion — particularly the person-affecting intuition or the non-identity problem — this multi-dimensional framing is structurally unable to reproduce and therefore must be introduced by the facilitator after the exercise concludes.

Please write your response in British English.
