I am drafting notes for an essay on whether habitat destruction reduces net wild animal suffering, and I want to move beyond the familiar replacement argument. I propose that destroying habitat does not merely scale down welfare-bearing lives but transforms their quality: in intact ecosystems, animals endure "structural suffering"—acute predation, starvation, and disease embedded in complex food webs—whereas survivors in degraded fragments face "depletion suffering," a chronic deficit of positive experiences and ecological complexity I call "ecological anhedonia." This anhedonia arises from trophic relaxation and biodiversity loss: survivors experience less acute harm but also lose foraging variety, social complexity, and environmental stimulation that may constitute positive welfare. Consequently, the net welfare effect is ambiguous, because destruction eliminates future lives without clearly reducing suffering among those that remain, and standard arguments fail to weigh these transformed survivor states properly.
