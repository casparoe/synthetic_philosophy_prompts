I am a journalist who covers philosophy and ideas, and I have agreed to lead a single ninety-minute session for a non-specialist reading group on whether immoral art can be genuinely good art. Rather than explaining the debate to me, lay out the teaching plan: specify the order in which the three distinctions—authorial corruption, representational harm, and structural complicity, where formal excellence is inseparable from immoral content—must be introduced and why each depends on its predecessor; identify the one concrete case that should carry the discussion and why familiar figures such as Wagner or Pound confuse rather than clarify; map the misconceptions participants will bring, especially that aesthetic value is merely moral endorsement, and state precisely where in the sequence each must be intercepted; propose one closing question that diagnoses whether they have grasped the framework; and say which advanced theoretical positions should be omitted entirely at this level. Present this as a numbered list of exactly five points, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
