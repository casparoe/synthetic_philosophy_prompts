Having left experimental physics after a long career and only recently begun studying philosophy of science, I am struck by a disciplinary double standard I never noticed as a practitioner. In scientific publishing, peer-review norms treat a quantitative argument presented solely through standardized pictograms—arranged, say, in the manner of Otto Neurath’s ISOTYPE syntax—as categorically inadequate; reviewers require every visual result to be translated into a textual figure caption that states explicit propositional claims, and major journals reject submissions that allow diagrammatic syntax alone to carry evidential weight. By contrast, international safety regulations such as ISO 7010 mandate that chemical and physical hazards be communicated through purely pictographic symbols that are legally required to be intelligible without any linguistic gloss, in any language, and these symbols are trusted to guide immediate protective behavior. What philosophical commitments about representation, knowledge, and the mediating role of language does that institutional bifurcation presuppose? Does scientific practice implicitly hold that genuine evidential reasoning must be propositional and linguistically mediated, whereas practical warning need only trigger a causal response? Or can the split be explained without committing to any particular theory of picture-language? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology and philosophy of representation that licenses treating ISOTYPE-style visual syntax as sufficient for hazard communication but insufficient for scientific argument; evaluate whether Neurath’s project of a universal Bildsprache for empirical knowledge was undermined precisely by this commitment; and specify what would have to change in peer-review conventions if one accepted Neurath’s view that standardized pictorial syntax can carry full scientific content without textual translation. Also indicate where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—whether several rival theories of representation and knowledge could license the same split—and whether the two institutional domains actually presuppose consistent views or quietly depend on incompatible ones.
