As a retired physicist who has only recently begun working through philosophy of action with any seriousness, I find myself less interested in which decision-theoretic formula “wins” Kavka’s toxin puzzle than in knowing, beforehand, what an adequate theory of rational practical commitment is actually required to deliver. The canonical case remains the one Gregory Kavka introduced: an agent is offered a substantial reward if, at midnight, she genuinely intends to drink a mildly toxic substance the next morning, even though she will not be compelled to drink it once the reward is secured, and even though carrying out the intention is irrational by her own lights. Before I evaluate particular theories, I want the full specification of desiderata.

Please lay out the criteria: which phenomena any framework for rational commitment must explain, which cases it must get right—starting with the toxin scenario itself, but also extending to variants where the temporal gap between intention and execution widens or where external enforcement is partial—and which distinctions it must preserve, especially the distinction between genuinely intending an action and merely predicting that one will perform it. Crucially, I want the list constructed so that it does not silently presuppose a substantial, enduring self; instead, it should accommodate a reductionist view of personal identity, according to which the deliberating agent at midnight and the acting agent the next morning are best understood as distinct temporal parts linked only by psychological continuity rather than by a persisting substance. Your desiderata should therefore include what cross-temporal binding requires if there is no single subject to “bear” the cost across the gap; whether backward-looking evaluation of the intention remains coherent under such reductionism; and whether the theory can avoid collapsing executive commitment into representational belief about future behavior. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it, flag any pair you take to be jointly unsatisfiable—particularly where accommodating reductionism conflicts with the intuitive demand that a rational commitment binds the very same subject across time—and indicate which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are merely prejudices of one camp. Finally, briefly score how the main existing approaches—causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and self-binding or precommitment accounts—fare against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scoring, since my purpose is to fix the standard of adequacy before judging the contenders.
