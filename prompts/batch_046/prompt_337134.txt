I have recently been appointed to a university ethics committee, and I find myself returning to a case from my undergraduate days that now feels uncomfortably close to home: in Franz Kafka's The Trial, Josef K. is executed for an offence that is never specified, yet the narrative treats his culpability as somehow objective rather than merely bureaucratic error. I would like to read The Trial through the lens of free-floating rationales — specifically, the view that normative reasons can persist as structural pressures within institutional practices even after every agent who could have authored or recognised them has vanished into anonymity. Rather than treating K.'s guilt as an absurdist fiction about opaque authority, I want to examine whether the novel dramatises reasons that have become detached from any reasoning agent: obligations or culpabilities that inhabit the interstices of procedural forms rather than minds. Does Kafka's court represent a genuine metaphysical possibility — scattered normative forces that exert pressure without a reasoner — or does the novel's irony precisely collapse that idea, showing that once reasons are severed from agents they become indistinguishable from arbitrary violence? I am preparing a discussion for a philosophy of law reading group and want a reading that takes both the narrative logic and the metaethical debate seriously, without reducing the novel to an illustration or distorting the concept to fit the plot. Where does the text resist this interpretation, and what might it contribute to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot capture?
