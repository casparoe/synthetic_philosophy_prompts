I'm working through material for a course in moral philosophy, and a fellow student shared an informal summary of the supererogation literature that strikes me as plausible but possibly distorted in ways I can't fully diagnose. According to the summary, supererogation designates actions—such as donating a kidney to a stranger or enduring significant personal risk to rescue someone in danger—that are morally excellent yet fall outside the scope of moral requirement. The summary insists that the concept depends on a rigid demarcation: if failing to perform an act carries even a minimal moral cost or traces of blameworthiness, then the act was never truly supererogatory but rather an unrecognized duty; conversely, true supererogation demands that omission be entirely free of negative moral evaluation, leaving only the absence of praise.

The summary then applies this framework to consequentialism, arguing that consequentialists treat extreme self-sacrifice as supererogatory in order to deflect demandingness objections: by reclassifying obligations to save distant strangers or surrender most of one's income as optional excellences rather than failures, the theory allegedly preserves its calculus while avoiding the charge that it requires saintly lives. It also invokes Susan Wolf's familiar contrast between moral saints, who sustain supererogatory behavior over a lifetime, and moral heroes, who perform isolated acts of extreme sacrifice, but it claims that Wolf introduced this distinction to show that sustained supererogation is actually a form of moral misjudgment—because it renders a life unattractive or fanatical—and therefore that the category collapses into ordinary duty once we recognize that we have no reason to cultivate such lives. Finally, the summary advances what it terms the continuity thesis: there is no qualitative break between meeting one's obligations and exceeding them, only quantitative differences in difficulty or cost, so labeling some acts supererogatory is merely a rhetorical way of praising choices that happen to lie far along the same scale.

Please audit this characterization. Which parts accurately reflect standard accounts of supererogation, which parts are common but oversimplified glosses, and which are genuine misrepresentations—particularly regarding the sharp-boundary condition, Wolf's actual purpose in distinguishing saints from heroes, and the claim that continuity undermines rather than merely complicates the category? Also, assess whether the summary's conclusion—that supererogation is essentially an ad hoc pressure-release mechanism for demanding moral theories—follows from any of the accurate elements, or whether it depends on the errors you've identified.
