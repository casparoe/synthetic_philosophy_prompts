You are giving the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop called "Founding Violence and Its Inheritors," held in a university auditorium. The audience is split three ways: professional historians of the French Revolution who will notice if you flatten 1793 into a generic morality play; political philosophers interested in legitimacy, contingency, and constituent power; and local civic leaders and graduate students from unrelated fields who need to understand why the workshop matters without being lectured at. Your remarks should run about eight minutes and be suitable to read aloud.

The workshop’s framing question is whether revolutions necessarily devour their children because of structural class antagonism that cannot be mediated once sovereignty is claimed in the name of the people, or because of contingent ideological choices made in 1793–94—specifically, the collapse of representative mechanisms into a politics of direct virtue. You are personally agnostic between these explanations, but you hold one somewhat idiosyncratic view that must shape the architecture of your remarks: you think the standard devouring narrative is not merely historically contingent but conceptually confused in a precise way. The confusion, as you see it, is that it treats revolutionary violence against former allies as a failure of revolutionary consistency, when in fact it is often the logical completion of a particular kind of constituent power that defines itself by the perpetual exclusion of the corrupt element. This means that asking how Robespierre could turn on Danton already imports a model of political friendship and consistency that the revolutionary logic of purity had explicitly rejected. You should not defend the Terror, but you should show why the moral shock we feel is partly an anachronism.

Your speech must do three things structurally. First, welcome all three groups without jargon that requires specialist decoding, but without erasing the historical specificity that the historians care about. Second, present the workshop’s central tension—class-struggle explanation versus contingency versus ideological logic—in a way that makes clear these are disagreements not only about what happened in 1793 but about what kind of explanation counts as adequate for political violence. Third, close with a concrete, non-triumphalist framing question for the day that emerges directly from your idiosyncratic point: if revolutionary legitimacy is defined by the perpetual purification of the body politic, then is the problem that revolutions fail to live up to their ideals, or that they succeed too well at an ideal that is structurally hostile to pluralism? Pose this without resolving it.

Constraints: Do not cite books, articles, or scholars by name; do not offer a bibliography or reading list. Do not put any historical figure or modern commentator in quotation marks. You may paraphrase well-known positions, but avoid attributing common phrases about revolutions consuming their own to any specific author. The philosophical idea must do real structural work: your remarks should be impossible to deliver if you dropped the point about constituent power and purity, because that point is what allows you to frame the workshop’s question in the particular way you do. Keep the tone formal but spoken.
