Here's an argument I cooked up for a historiography thread and now it feels like it eats itself. (1) If a historian should avoid presentism, she must evaluate past actors only by norms availble to those actors at the time. (2) But "availible norms" can't mean just explicit beliefs; it has to include implicit background conditions—like what counts as evidence in their archival practices—otherwise we're just smuggling our own evidentiary standards in and calling it neutrality. (3) So to truly escape presentism, the historian must adopt the past's full normative framewrok, epistemic and moral together. (4) Once she's adopted that framework, she can no longer criticize past practices like suppressing dissent or ignoring testimony, because inside that framework those moves might be perfectly valid. (5) But then she also can't explain why those practices led to false beliefs—since "false" gets evaluated by her adopted framework, not by ours. (6) So escaping presentism makes historical explanation impossible. (7) Therefore historians should just embrace presentism, because the alternative is intellectual paralisis.

The conclusion is wild—basically that we either judge everything by today's standards or we give up explaining the past altogether. I want to keep (1) and (4) if I can, but I smell a hidden slide between adopting norms to understand and being bound by them to judge, maybe around step (3) or (5). Where does it actually break, and what's the least painful fix? If it's valid and I just have to pay, tell me which premise costs the least.
