I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading seriously in philosophy of history and archaeology, and I find myself caught in a recurring argument about whether societies truly “collapse” or merely reorganize. After studying the Bronze Age crisis, the Classic Maya transition, and the Rapa Nui sequence, I keep encountering two camps that seem to talk past one another: one treats collapse as a genuine analytical category describing rapid, coupled failures across administrative, economic, and demographic systems; the other rejects the term as teleological, insisting that we observe adaptive transformation, demographic redistribution, and institutional restructuring rather than societal death. I want you to draft the joint statement that a committed collapse-theorist and a committed transformation-theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, addressing these three cases without appealing to bibliographies, citation lists, or named authorities that a reader without search access could not verify.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the available archaeological and paleoclimatic evidence for each case, the conceptual distinction between demographic continuity and institutional discontinuity, and the epistemic limits of inferring absence of activity from absence of monumental or written remains—followed by a disagreements table in which each camp’s position is stated in wording its own proponents would accept. You must isolate at least three cruxes. First, whether the loss of administrative literacy and inter-regional exchange networks—call it “semiotic collapse”—can occur without a corresponding breakdown in local subsistence practices, and what that pairing implies for the very concept of societal failure. Second, whether the Easter Island trajectory demonstrates successful long-term adaptation to isolation despite severe environmental degradation, or rather a delayed systemic failure masked by short-term demographic and technological resilience. Third, whether the Bronze Age crisis represents a genuinely synchronized, trans-regional event or merely a cluster of locally independent transitions that appear unified only from a distance. For each crux, specify a concrete comparative archaeological pattern or piece of cross-case evidence that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that pattern will reveal upon closer analysis—particularly noting where they disagree about whether archaeological silence indicates a true absence of organized activity or only a shift toward non-monumental, non-written practices that leave a lighter material signature. Finally, include a brief section identifying any portion of their dispute that is purely verbal rather than substantive, explaining exactly which definitional choice about “society,” “failure,” or “continuity” generates the appearance of disagreement where none may exist. Keep the tone formal, precise, and suitable for actual signature.
