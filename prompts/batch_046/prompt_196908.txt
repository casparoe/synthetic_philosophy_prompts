I have been working through my own view on whether normative reasons can genuinely float free of any reasoning being, and after a long discussion with a colleague who thinks I am smuggling in agents through the back door, I want to test the coherence of the package. Here is where I have landed. First, I maintain that there are free-floating rationales proper: normative considerations that obtain in worlds entirely lacking agents, not merely counterfactual prescriptions for hypothetical ones. Second, I hold that to be a reason rather than a brute causal push, a consideration must be such that it could in principle be registered by a reasoning faculty; this is an ontological condition, not a claim about actual motivation or about internal reasons. Third, I reject both Humean derivations of reasons from contingent desires and constructivist derivations from practices of mutual appraisal, which means I take normative force to be neither psychological nor institutional in origin. Fourth, I also want to say that in a world containing only non-sentient causal processes, there remain true normative statements about what would be rational for a system with the relevant cognitive architecture, and these statements are not reducible to descriptive predictions about behavior. Fifth, and here is the tension I feel most acutely, I think that if a rationale applies to no being that could ever entertain or be moved by it, then describing it as a reason is at best a metaphor, like calling a shadow a kind of light rather than an absence of it. I can see that the second commitment seems to require at least possible reasoners, which threatens the first; that the fourth seems to reintroduce hypothetical agents that the first was meant to avoid; and that the fifth seems to undercut both the first and the fourth by denying that unattached rationales merit the name. But I am unsure whether some of these tensions are only apparent, and I do not know which single revision would do the least damage to the core idea. Please go through these five commitments in pairs, identify which conflicts are genuine and which dissolve upon closer inspection, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each suggest the minimal adjustment that restores consistency. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or any bibliographic guidance; I want only an internal audit of whether my view holds together.
