I am a high school student enrolled in an introductory moral philosophy course, and during a recent visit to a university research-ethics office I became puzzled by the institution’s tiered animal-review framework. The board applies sharply discontinuous oversight rules: protocols involving mammals—especially non-human primates—require exhaustive justification with mandatory pain-minimization plans; studies on vertebrates such as zebrafish and amphibians undergo only a lighter administrative review; research using insects, including bees and fruit flies, falls almost entirely outside the mandate; and proposals that employ purely computational architectures—specifically, machine-learning models trained on nociceptive neural datasets to simulate aversive signaling in silico—are routed through a data-ethics committee concerned with bias and privacy, but never with whether the simulated system possesses any gradated form of sentience or moral status, regardless of how precisely its functional dynamics mirror those of the biologically exempt organisms. The practice thus treats phylogenetic rank as tracking a discrete moral boundary, even though the same researchers study pain by comparing functional neural and behavioral markers across these very categories.

What I find philosophically uneasy is that the institution’s scientific methodology implicitly accepts a kind of cross-species functionalism about nociception, yet its ethical architecture refuses to apply that comparison consistently across substrates. Rather than distributing moral consideration along a continuum of cognitive or affective capacity—say, from simple reflex arcs through complex suffering—the framework seems to presuppose that sentience is either fully present or entirely absent according to taxonomic classification, while simultaneously holding that an accurate silicon-based replication of the relevant functional patterns raises no moral question whatsoever. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of sentience and moral status that this practice actually requires: does it depend on a biological essentialism about consciousness, on a substrate-dependent threshold view, or on some other conception? Please also identify which rival views—such as a consistently gradational account assigning degrees of moral status across biological and artificial systems, or a functionalist position that would extend minimal consideration to high-fidelity nociceptive simulations—are quietly ruled out, and distinguish what the framework strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates. Finally, tell me whether that implicit theory is internally coherent, and specify exactly what would have to change in the review procedures if the institution replaced its underlying view with a substrate-independent, gradated theory of moral considerability running from thermostats to primates.
