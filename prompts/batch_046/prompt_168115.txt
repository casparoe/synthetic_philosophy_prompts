As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I recently sat in a design review for an autonomous emergency-response module. One of my colleagues argued that if the system must choose between rescuing a single person and rescuing five strangers—where there are no prior relationships or special obligations to anyone involved—it should not automatically prioritize the larger group. To defend this, he kept repeating the phrase, the numbers don't count; every life is just one life, no matter how many are at stake. He treated this as if it settled the algorithmic ethics question outright, using it to reject any maximization logic that would weight outcomes by headcount.

I recognize that this sounds related to the well-known philosophical debate about whether numbers matter in rescue cases, often associated with the thinker John Taurek and his scenario involving saving one versus saving five. But I want to understand the slogan itself rather than simply study one author's view. Could you lay out, as a precise menu of distinct readings, what the claim that the numbers don't count could actually be asserting? For each interpretation—from the trivial observation that every individual has irreplaceable dignity, to a substantive non-aggregative principle, to a radical denial that group size carries any moral weight at all—please say whether it is plausible, whether it genuinely supports my colleague's conclusion that the system should treat one and five as morally on a par (perhaps by randomizing), and whether it is compatible with still saving the five after all. In particular, identify which hidden premises—about fairness, epistemic uncertainty, or the idea that persons are not interchangeable parts of a collective—would need to be added for the slogan to do real argumentative work, and indicate which version someone in an engineering ethics discussion probably means when they invoke it.
