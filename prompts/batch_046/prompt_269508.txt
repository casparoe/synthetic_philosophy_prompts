I am preparing a seminar discussion that reads Jorge Luis Borges’s short story “The Library of Babel” through Skolem’s paradox—the downward Löwenheim-Skolem result that if ZFC has any model, it has a countable one, so that “uncountability” is not an absolute property of sets but is relative to the model in which it is evaluated. In the story, the Library is asserted to be total, spatially infinite, and to contain every possible 410-page volume; the librarians inhabit it from within, experience its collections as unsearchably vast, and never confirm whether the perfect catalog exists, even though the narrator insists the Library is complete and therefore must contain it. I want you to treat the Library as an informal set-theoretic universe: from an internal perspective, certain subsets (all volumes sharing a property, all possible catalogs) appear uncountably large and hermeneutically inexhaustible, while from an external meta-model perspective the entire Library might be countable, with “uncountable” internal collections that are in fact externally enumerable. Where does this analogy genuinely illuminate Borges’s central tension—between internal epistemic limitation (the librarians can never verify completeness) and ontological totality? Where does it break down, for instance because fiction supplies no formal language or satisfaction relation, or because Borges is ultimately concerned with combinatorics and interpretation rather than with model-theoretic relativity? I also want you to consider a specific, somewhat niche extension: in certain countable models of set theory there are “non-standard” elements—objects that formally satisfy the defining conditions (here, being a 410-page volume of specific characters) yet are not finitely specifiable from within the model. Could Borges’s indecipherable books or volumes of pure gibberish be read as analogues to these non-standard elements, and does that reading enrich the story’s meditation on meaning or merely distort the logic to fit the narrative? Please be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the story to an illustration of Skolem, and do not warp the mathematics to serve the fiction; if the work genuinely resists this framing, say where and why.
