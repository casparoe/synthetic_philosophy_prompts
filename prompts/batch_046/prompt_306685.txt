I have been working out my own position on suffering and biotechnology without any formal training, mostly from reading popular essays and thinking through the logic myself. I want a consistency check before I share it with others. Here is where I have landed, in five commitments:

(i) The only thing that is intrinsically bad is suffering; pleasure, knowledge, and achievement matter only as tools that prevent or distract from suffering. If suffering were fully abolished, nothing else of moral importance would be missing.

(ii) Biotechnology—germline editing of pain and affective circuits, synthetic neurochemistry, and eventually managed intervention in wild ecosystems—makes the total abolition of suffering achievable within generations. We should pursue this aggressively, without letting appeals to "naturalness" block research or deployment.

(iii) Many capacities people now cherish—deep compassion shaped by grief, artistic drive born from struggle, moral courage tested by adversity—seem causally tied to the very neural mechanisms that produce suffering. Removing suffering entirely would likely weaken or erase these traits.

(iv) Once a being genuinely lacks the capacity for negative valence, it has no moral claims on anyone. A world of flat but suffering-free beings is therefore incomparably better than ours, even if it lacks the richness described in (iii).

(v) Despite (ii), any competent adult and any parent should have an unconditional right to refuse these modifications for themselves and their children, with no legal penalty or social stigma; the state should fund and encourage the research, but never mandate it.

My questions: do these five actually hang together, or am I smuggling in contradictions? I can already see that (ii) sounds like it demands universal adoption while (v) protects refusal, but I think that tension might dissolve if "pursuing" the project is about collective funding and availability rather than coercion—though I want you to tell me whether I am right about that or just comforting myself. There may also be a hidden tension between (iii) and (iv): if abolishing suffering erodes the compassion and resolve that motivate people to carry out the abolition in the first place, then the project might undermine its own drivers. And I wonder whether (i) makes (iii) irrelevant, or whether (iii) still creates a problem for (iv) that I have not noticed.

Please audit the package rather than lecture me on the hedonistic imperative generally. Rank the tensions by severity, distinguish the ones that are only apparent from the ones that are real, and for each real tension say which commitment is the least costly to revise and what giving it up actually costs me. If there is an objection I am hoping nobody raises—the one that would force me to abandon the core idea rather than just adjust a detail—name it and explain why it is the dangerous one. Be specific about which pairs of commitments pull against each other and why.
