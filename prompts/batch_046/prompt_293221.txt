Here's teh student's answer (around 200 words):

Logical fatalism argues that either there will be a sea-battle tomorrow or there won't be, so one of these statements is true right now. Since the causes that lead to the battle are already fixed, this means tomorrow's outcome is unavoidable. Aristotle avoids this by denying bivalence: future contingent statements don't have a truth-value yet, so there's no present fact making the future necessary.

But retrograde determinism brings the problem back. Once the battle happens, we can truly say yesterday that it was going to happen, and since past truths are necessary, the battle was unavoidable all along. Some branching-time models solve this by treating every branch as equally actual, so there's no single privileged line that makes one past-truth necessary. This means talk of inevitability is just about where we sit in the tree.

Indexical contingency supports this: if contingency depends on the speaker's branch-position, then on our actual branch the battle was always going to happen, but from other positions it wasn't. So bivalence holds locally on each branch, and Aristotle's global denial of bivalence is unnecessary. The branching structure does the real work, and retrograde determinism is dissolved because "necessary" only applies relative to the speaker's index.
