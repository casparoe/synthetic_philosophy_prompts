Arendt distinguishes traditional deception, which conceals a specific fact from a particular enemy, from modern defactualizing lies that target the entire public and destroy the shared fabric of factual reality. Traditional lies preserve the general distinction between truth and falsehood; modern lies, like those exposed in the Pentagon Papers, replace factual records with coherent fictional narratives so thoroughly that citizens lose the capacity to distinguish reality from fabrication. For Arendt, factual truth is politically fragile because it is contingent on witness and testimony rather than rational necessity, which makes it vulnerable but also essential to the common world—though she ultimately derives this vulnerability from her analysis of labor in The Human Condition, where modern technology separates human beings from the world of shared appearance.

Turning to the AI-curated archive: because no individual document is technically falsified, this is not a traditional lie directed at an adversary. Instead, by systematically replacing primary sources with reordered narratives, it destroys citizens' shared access to the factual basis of politics, which makes it a modern defactualizing lie. That said, Arendt would likely argue that such a mechanism can only fully succeed under totalitarian conditions, where independent testimony has already been eliminated; in a democracy, residual access to witnesses prevents complete defactualization. Therefore the scenario describes a dangerous precursor but not yet a realized modern lie.
