I'm a moderator and occasional facilitator for a long-running online rationalist forum, and we've been wrestling with a recurring governance problem: members frequently invoke hypocrisy when leveling blame — "you did X last year," "you benefit from the same system," "your in-group does it constantly" — and we have no consistent framework for deciding whether that callout actually defeats the blamer's standing or is just a rhetorical deflection. I've been thinking through the philosophy of blame, particularly the idea that hypocrisy can undermine one's standing to blame, but only when specific conditions about one's own moral record, the similarity of the fault, and whether one has acknowledged or corrected it are met. I want to turn this into something my moderation team can actually run in real time, but I also want to inject a distinction that feels under-discussed in the literature I've encountered: the difference between private hypocrisy, where the blamer secretly violates the same norm without public acknowledgment, and public hypocrisy, where the blamer has openly endorsed or practiced the very behavior they now condemn. I suspect these should affect standing differently, especially when the blamer has since changed their view, and I want the procedure to force that distinction rather than collapse them.

Please write a reusable decision procedure — ordered questions, what counts as sufficient evidence or documentation for each, explicit stop points where standing is intact, explicit stop points where standing is defeated, clear hand-off points where the procedure must yield to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than verifiable fact, and explicit flags for where the procedure is likely to misfire — that a non-philosopher moderator can apply in roughly ten minutes during an active dispute. The procedure must distinguish between first-party blame, where the wronged party is speaking, and third-party blame, where an observer or community member steps in, since I think hypocrisy operates differently in those two registers. Then run the procedure on three worked cases: a member blaming a startup founder for using user data in ways the blamer once criticized in old posts that have since been deleted; a victim blaming someone for breaking a commitment despite the victim having broken similar commitments in private, never acknowledged; and a prominent community figure publicly condemning another member for committing a logical fallacy that the figure themselves prominently used in a celebrated past debate, without ever having acknowledged the prior use. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer that will feel wrong to parts of the community — either allowing blame that seems unfair, or blocking blame that seems legitimate — and say exactly what the procedure cannot settle and where an editor or senior moderator must make a values call. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; build purely from the conceptual structure. Keep in mind this will be used by people without philosophy training, under time pressure, and they may need to defend the outcome to the community.
