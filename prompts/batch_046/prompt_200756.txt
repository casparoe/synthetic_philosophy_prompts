I'm taking a graduate seminar on Arendt's political thought and have been struggling with what appears to be a deep tension between her ontology of politics and her normative stance on deception. In The Human Condition, Arendt argues that action and speech primarily disclose the agent's unique who rather than communicating information about what something is, and she characterizes politics as the realm of doxa—opinion—rather than philosophical or scientific truth. Meanwhile, in her essay on the Pentagon Papers, she condemns systematic political lying not merely as ineffective but as destructive, insisting that factual truth, despite being contingent and vulnerable to power, furnishes the indispensable ground without which opinions cannot form. A fellow student suggested that these positions are compatible: Arendt is not demanding that political speech correspond to a pre-given reality, but only that deception not destroy the common world that makes authentic disclosure possible. I remain unconvinced. If political speech is essentially performative and world-building, and if factual truths are merely contingent historical occurrences lacking rational necessity, then requiring factual accuracy in politics seems to impose an epistemic norm alien to a realm whose essence is freedom, plurality, and appearance. Does this not imply that Arendt's ontology of politics actually undermines her condemnation of lying, reducing deception to just another mode of world-building action? I would like a careful analysis of whether this objection succeeds, or whether there is a principled distinction in Arendt—perhaps between destructive defactualization and legitimate political action—that resolves the tension without reducing politics to mere self-expression.

Please conclude with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by a concise judgment of whether the alleged tension is genuine or resolvable within Arendt's framework.
