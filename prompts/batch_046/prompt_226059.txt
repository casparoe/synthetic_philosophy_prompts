I keep coming back to this idea of promises binding people, and I think I'm importing my old scientific intuition wrong. In chemistry a bond is literally an energy well—break it and you need input. So when philosophers say a promise "binds" me, I keep wondering: where is the energy well? If there's no causal mechanism enforcing the obligation—no external sanction, no neurological lock—then is "binding" just a metaphor left over from contract law, or is their some hidden normative field I haven't read about?

But here's the thing that's really stuck in my head. I was sketching out this little model, call it a social Hamiltonian, where every promise creates a potential spike in the social field that makes defection costly. I realize that's probably just game theory dressed up in physics jargon, but it made me ask: if promises actually do bind in some non-causal, normative way, shouldn't we be able to distinguish that binding from mere preference stability? Like, if I break a promise and feel guilty, is that guilt the "force" holding the bond together, or is it just feedback after the bond already snapped?

So my actual question is: do promises bind because there's some hidden normative mechanism we haven't mapped—analogous to an unseen field—or is the whole notion of "binding" in ethics a category mistake that borrows its authority from physical science? And if it's the latter, why does breaking a promise feel like violating a law rather than just changing my mind? I feel like I'm missing a distinction that philosophers take for granted, probably because I'm still thinking like a bench scientist and trying to recieve obligations as if they were measurable forces.
