In my graduate seminar on rational agency, we have been working through the absent-minded driver problem—the planning puzzle in which a motorist must take the second of two consecutive highway exits to reach a highly valued destination, receives only a modest payoff for exiting at the first, and knows that should he reach the second intersection he will have forgotten whether he already passed the first. The class has largely converged on the orthodox interpretation of how rational planning and local decision-making ought to relate in this case, but a provocative extension proposed by one of the doctoral students has split the room. She asks us to consider a double-absent-minded variant: the driver not only risks forgetting his position on the route, but also suffers second-order opacity—he cannot determine with confidence whether he is genuinely forgetful, since he might be fully attentive yet misread the signage, or he might be forgetful yet falsely believe himself attentive.

Write an essay that examines whether this second-order ignorance destabilizes the standard prescriptions for rational planning in the original first-order case—particularly the idea that the driver should adopt an ex ante plan and then, upon reaching an intersection, update in light of his present beliefs about where he is—or whether it instead strengthens the view that evidential reasoning must govern every decision node, including the meta-level choice about how to model one’s own cognition. Your essay should articulate the strongest version of both positions, consider whether rationality requires transparency about one’s own cognitive architecture, and reach a clearly justified conclusion about what the double-absent-minded driver ought to do, together with an explanation of whether that answer generalizes back to the standard puzzle or marks a genuine divergence.
