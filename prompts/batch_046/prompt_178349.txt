As a novelist trying to understand the moral architecture of armed intervention, I hold the following judgments and want to know whether they cohere into a single principle or force me to abandon one.

In Kosovo in 1999, NATO acted without Security Council authorization, yet I consider the intervention permissible because it halted an imminent ethnic cleansing, even though it entrenched ethnic partition and left sovereignty unresolved.

In Libya in 2011, the Council authorized force solely to protect civilians in Benghazi, but the coalition quickly pursued regime change, producing a collapsed state and years of civil war. I judge the protective mandate legitimate, but the transformation into regime change made the overall enterprise wrong—not merely botched, but morally corrupted by that overreach.

In Iraq in 2003, there was no imminent mass atrocity, the weapons rationale was fabricated, and the occupation unleashed prolonged sectarian violence; this was plainly impermissible.

Now consider a case I am inventing for a novel: in 2008, a regional coalition entered the small republic of Marovia without UN approval, halted an active genocide in its northern provinces within three weeks, installed a brief transitional council, and withdrew within six months, leaving a fragile but functioning democracy that has endured. By my lights this was right—perhaps more clearly right than Kosovo—precisely because it was limited, swift, and refused to restructure the state.

What principle, if any, reconciles these four verdicts? It cannot be UN authorization alone, since I excuse Kosovo and Marovia but condemn Libya's excess; it cannot be outcome alone, since Kosovo's messy aftermath does not undo its permissibility, while Libya's chaos does condemn it. Is there a coherent account—perhaps centered on the proportionality of means to the protective end, or on the distinction between halting atrocity and engineering political transformation—that saves all four judgments? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the surviving principle predict for an unconsidered case: an authorized, narrowly executed intervention that nevertheless fails to stop a genocide because the aggressor is too well-armed?
