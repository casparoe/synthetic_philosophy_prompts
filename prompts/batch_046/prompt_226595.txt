The question concerns what I have called a negative-choice election: both available candidates will, upon election, implement policies that cause foreseeable and serious harm to vulnerable populations, though one candidate's policies are substantially more destructive than the other's. The student was instructed to analyze whether a citizen has a moral duty to vote for the less harmful candidate, drawing on two conceptual resources: first, the distinction between expressive and instrumental reasons for voting; and second, the notion of negative responsibility in collective action—the claim that moral accountability for harm is distributed in a non-linear fashion, such that an individual can bear responsibility for contributing to a harmful collective outcome even when that contribution is not individually decisive.

Please provide your assessment below. In doing so, please indicate how confident you are in your judgments overall, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least certain.

Question: In a negative-choice election of the kind described above, does a citizen have a duty to vote for the less harmful candidate? Evaluate this question by carefully applying the expressive/instrumental distinction and the concept of negative responsibility. Be precise about whether negative responsibility generates an instrumental duty despite the non-decisiveness of a single vote, and about what the expressive account actually permits or requires.

Student Answer:
Voting in a negative-choice election creates a conflict between expressive and instrumental reasoning. On the expressive view, the ballot is mainly a vehicle for registering one's values rather than for causing outcomes, so one should vote for whichever candidate least violates one's moral commitments, or abstain entirely if neither is acceptable. This suggests that voting for the less harmful candidate can be permitted as an expression of partial moral alignment, but it is not required.

The instrumental side is where negative responsibility becomes relevant. Even though a single vote almost never decides an election, collective harm depends on aggregation. Negative responsibility holds that we are accountable for harms we help bring about even when our contribution is not sufficient. Therefore, voting for the more destructive candidate makes one complicit in greater harm. By parity of reasoning, one should vote for the lesser evil to minimize one's contribution to collective harm.

However, I think the instrumental argument ultimately collapses. Because negative responsibility is distributed non-linearly, the marginal harm attributable to my individual vote is effectively zero in a large electorate. So negative responsibility actually undermines the case for an instrumental duty: if my contribution is negligible, I bear no meaningful responsibility either way. Consequently, any duty must come from the expressive side. Here, Brennan and Lomasky are right that voters have no duty to be informed because voting is fundamentally expressive. So in a negative-choice election, I may vote for the least harmful candidate if that expresses my values, or I may abstain; but there is no moral duty to vote for the lesser evil—only a permission.
