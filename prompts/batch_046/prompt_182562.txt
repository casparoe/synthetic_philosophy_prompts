Here is an argument about the intrinsic badness of pain, built around pain asymbolia—the condition in which patients retain precise sensory discrimination of noxious stimuli (location, intensity, quality) but report that the experience is not unpleasant and does not motivate avoidance. (1) If pain is bad in virtue of how it feels, its badness must be intrinsic to a specific phenomenal quality: the hurtful, aversive character that distinguishes pain from mere tactile pressure. (2) In asymbolia, the full sensory-discriminative profile is present, yet the negative affect is absent; subjects recognize the stimulus as noxious but deny that it bothers them. (3) So the hurtful phenomenal quality, if present at all, can occur without being experienced as bad; hence badness is not intrinsic to that quality. (4) If the badness is not intrinsic, then in ordinary people pain is bad only derivatively—because of a contingent coupling between the sensory state and an aversive reaction. (5) But then there is no direct, non-instrumental reason to relieve another's pain; we have reason only to disrupt the coupling, which could in principle be achieved by an affective decoupling that leaves the sensory component intact rather than removing the sensation itself. (6) Yet we clearly take there to be a direct obligation not to cause or allow suffering, not merely an instrumental reason to adjust others' reactions. (7) Therefore (1) is false, and pain's badness is not grounded in an intrinsic phenomenal hurtfulness.

The argument strikes me as suspicious because the conclusion undermines the directness of our moral response to suffering, but I am unsure where the slide occurs—perhaps between (2) and (3), where it is assumed that the hurtful quality is present rather than absent, or between (4) and (5), where contingency is mistaken for mere instrumentality. I would prefer to preserve (1) and (6). Which step should be rejected, what exactly is wrong with it, and what does giving it up cost?
