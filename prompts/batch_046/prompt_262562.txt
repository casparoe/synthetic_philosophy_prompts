As a journalist who covers philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I am developing an interactive workshop module for an upcoming feature on personal identity, and I need a rigorously designed classroom exercise rather than a lecture outline. The session will run ninety minutes with about eighteen graduate students in a university seminar room; the group has read broadly in metaphysics but should not hear the standard framing until after they have experienced the conceptual tension themselves. Rather than walking them through the familiar brain-transplant narrative associated with Daniel Dennett, I want them to enact a deliberately engineered variant: each participant receives one hidden "continuity token" representing a distinct psychological relation—autobiographical memory, embodied procedural skill, third-person social recognition, or first-person phenomenal access—and must collectively negotiate how to distribute these tokens between two post-accident candidates, one retaining the original biological brain in a life-support body and one running on a high-fidelity neural emulation hosted remotely. The rules should begin by requiring that tokens be assigned as indivisible units, then introduce a deadlock-breaking mechanism allowing only fractional splits after a unanimous vote fails, so that students discover through bargaining that their intuitive criteria pull in incompatible directions—producing, for example, a scenario in which both candidates seem to hold roughly sixty percent continuity—before the facilitator ever names this as the brain-transplant puzzle.

Please design the full activity: the initial hidden-card distribution, the symbolic-point payoff scheme that rewards consensus but imposes a steep cost on arbitrary division, the specific negotiation phases, and a debrief that maps each bargaining failure onto the tension between locating oneself with one's brain versus with one's anchored narrative. Specify what will predictably go wrong on the first run—perhaps students will conflate emotional attachment to a role with logical allocation of tokens—and how to correct for it in the moment. Also identify precisely which feature of the first-person experience of being duplicated the mechanics of tokens and points cannot reproduce, and explain how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally at the close. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the fracture should emerge entirely from the interaction, and the students should come away seeing that any single criterion for personal continuity, pushed consistently, produces results that feel intuitively wrong.
