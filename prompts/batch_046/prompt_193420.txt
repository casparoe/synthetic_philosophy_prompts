After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend last night about whether the Anthropocene should be dated to the mid-twentieth-century plutonium spike, to the 1610 Orbis rupture, or to the long industrial arc, I realized we were not merely disagreeing about sediment cores. My friend insisted that the real deadlock is jurisdictional: geologists treat epoch-definition as a matter of global synchronous stratigraphic correlation and a Golden Spike, while historians and earth-system scientists treat it as a normative-historical judgment about when human activity became a dominant planetary force, and that these two groups are answering entirely different questions under the single heading "when did the Anthropocene start." She also suggested that the recent failure of the Crawford Lake proposal to secure formal ratification reveals not an empirical deficiency but a clash over who holds chronostratigraphic sovereignty — whether the authority to declare a new epoch belongs to bodies that read rock layers or to disciplines that read socio-ecological transformation.

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. Is this stall substantive, verbal, or institutional? Do the sides differ about facts (the global distribution of plutonium, microplastics, or nitrogen isotopes in lake sediments versus the distributed ecological impact of colonial exchange), about standards of evidence (global correlation versus diachronous, regionally asynchronous rupture), about the definition of "epoch" itself (a formal chronostratigraphic unit versus a historical-ontological condition), or about hidden normative commitments (whether declaring an epoch is a scientific description or a political intervention)? Please support the diagnosis with what each side actually says — for instance, how the Anthropocene Working Group frames its 1952 marker and the Crawford Lake reference site, versus how proponents of the 1610 or long-Anthropocene hypotheses frame their criteria — and tell me whether my friend's jurisdictional hypothesis is right, or whether there is a hidden empirical or formal question that could actually resolve the disagreement.

Specifically, address whether a precise conceptual distinction — say, between stratigraphic correlation authority and historical periodization authority — would dissolve the debate, or whether the disagreement runs deeper, perhaps into incompatible ontologies of causation (a global synchronous signal versus cumulative, regionally heterogeneous socio-ecological change). And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would it be a formal decision procedure for cross-disciplinary epoch-definition, a new sedimentary dataset, a theorem about correlation across heterogeneous archives, or should we conclude that no single start date is possible and that the question is ill-posed? Give me an honest answer, not a diplomatic both-sides summary.
