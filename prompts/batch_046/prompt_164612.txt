I am drafting the outline for a short essay I intend to present at next month’s workshop on philosophy of mind and agency. The central question is whether self-deception is genuinely possible, but I want to move away from the familiar framing in which a single agent is said to hold contradictory beliefs about one and the same proposition. Instead, I propose that we understand self-deception as a form of doxastic compartmentalization: the agent maintains two incompatible evaluative frameworks—let us call them the reflective framework and the operative framework—that are activated by different contextual cues and governed by distinct evidential standards. The transition between them is not a matter of explicit reasoning but is mediated by what I am calling attentional gating mechanisms, which suppress the salience of countervailing evidence without requiring the agent to represent that suppression as such. On this view, the agent never simultaneously endorses p and not-p within a single framework; rather, she systematically prevents the frameworks from being jointly activated, which produces behavior that is functionally indistinguishable from deception without collapsing into logical contradiction.

I have sketched three sections: first, a diagnosis of why the traditional simultaneous-belief model fails (it conflates dispositional belief states with occurrent judgments); second, an argument that attentional gating is not merely a psychological description but carries normative weight, since the agent remains responsible for the conditions under which each framework is deployed; and third, a comparison with cases of collective self-deception in institutional settings, where no single individual holds the contradictory belief but the organizational structure replicates the gating function. Before I develop the full draft, I would value your critical comments on a few specific points. Does the compartmentalization model genuinely avoid the paradox, or does it merely relocate the contradiction into the meta-level conditions of framework activation? Is the appeal to attentional gating an illicit borrowing from empirical cognitive science that weakens the philosophical rigor of the argument? And does the analogy to institutional self-deception illuminate the individual case, or does it obscure the first-person asymmetry that makes self-deception conceptually distinctive?

This is a somewhat speculative framing, and I am particularly concerned that I may be reinventing a distinction that already exists in the literature under a different name—perhaps something like epistemic partitioning or contextual belief dynamics—without recognizing it. That said, I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; rather, I want to know whether the conceptual architecture holds together and where the weakest joints are likely to be found. Any suggestions for sharpening the argument, identifying hidden assumptions, or testing the model against borderline cases—such as self-deception about one’s own emotional states versus self-deception about external states of affairs—would be especially welcome.
