I recently mediated a multi-party negotiation over a community land-use agreement that collapsed in successive rounds of compromise, each party withdrawing precisely because they anticipated others would defect once final permits became irrevocable; watching rational cooperation unravel in real time made me want to recreate that experience—not as an abstract lecture—for a workshop of about fourteen graduate students in decision theory and philosophy of economics. The session is seventy-five minutes, and I want participants to feel the backward induction paradox in a form that captures what the standard classroom centipede game usually omits: an opaque or only partially observable termination horizon, and the fragility of common knowledge about rationality when the payoff structure is not fully transparent to every agent.

Design an in-class simulation in which pairs or small groups face a sequential offer-exchange protocol across a finite but initially undisclosed number of rounds. Mutual continuation should yield escalating point rewards, but include a hidden defection premium that activates only if the sequence reaches a specific, unannounced threshold. Assign roles so that different participants have asymmetric visibility into how many rounds remain and into whether their counterparts share the same information about payoffs. Use a point-based system with no real money, but attach a small tangible consequence at the end—such as a public ranking or a symbolic token—so stakes feel real yet remain low. Arrange the rules so that participants begin cooperating and then experience anticipatory withdrawal on their own, before anyone names backward induction or the centipede paradox. Incorporate one structured perturbation mechanism—perhaps a single-use visibility token that one party can expend to reveal or obscure a round-count parameter—so the group can observe directly how a slight shift in shared epistemic conditions either stabilizes or accelerates the unraveling.

Then provide a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the formal logic of backward induction: where mutual knowledge held but common knowledge failed, how the hidden defection premium operated like the rational exit at the final node, and where the opacity of the termination horizon disrupted the induction chain. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—namely, the formal proof that induction requires fully transparent parameters and common knowledge of rationality at every node—and supply that missing bridge in the debrief rather than expecting it to emerge from play. Also note what will predictably go wrong the first time you run it: for example, participants trying to reverse-engineer the hidden round count, treating the visibility token as a bargaining chip rather than an epistemic probe, or one group accidentally achieving full transparency through side conversation, and specify how to handle each without breaking the flow. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the activity must stand alone for a facilitator who will not consult sources during the session.
