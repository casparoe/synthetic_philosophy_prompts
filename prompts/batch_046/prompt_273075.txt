As a longtime reader of rationalist forums who has followed debates on decision theory, autonomy, and cognitive influence, I keep noticing a pattern in how we classify persuasion that I do not have a precise name for. The pattern is this: an influence technique—framing a medical risk in relative rather than absolute terms, setting a default opt-in, or using a particular emotional framing—shifts someone’s preference or behavior. If, upon later reflection, the person endorses the result (they kept the organ-donor registration, they stayed with the beneficial treatment, they saved more for retirement), we classify the technique as legitimate persuasion, education, or a benign nudge. If they later reject the result (they feel duped by a dark-pattern subscription flow, they regret the treatment choice, they resent the framing), we retroactively classify the exact same mechanism, with the same informational structure and the same bypassing of explicit deliberation, as manipulation. The classification appears to hinge on retrospective outcome endorsement rather than on transparency, reversibility, or the specific cognitive pathway exploited. I have seen this asymmetry in discussions of public-health messaging, therapeutic reframing, and choice architecture. I am not asking for a taxonomy of influence techniques; I want to know whether there is an established concept—perhaps in philosophy of action, in the autonomy literature, or in the study of nudging—that specifically captures this retrospective, outcome-contingent reclassification of a mechanism. How is it different from applying ordinary moral luck to persuasion, and how is it different from simple hindsight bias? If there is no standard term, what boundary conditions would distinguish it from either? Please say what observation or argument would change your identification—where is the carving of this phenomenon most likely to fail, and which nearest concept should win if yours is wrong?
