I cover philosophy of language and digital culture for a science-and-ideas desk, and I am preparing a long-form piece on whether generative AI has rendered authorial intention obsolete. Before I file, I want to stress-test the interpretive framework I have adopted, because I worry that my commitments pull in incompatible directions. Here is my current package. First, for traditional single-author literary texts, I hold a strong intentionalist view: the meaning is fixed by the author's actual psychological intentions at the time of writing, and rival interpretations should be settled by historical and biographical evidence rather than by reader response alone. Second, for collaboratively maintained texts—living documentation, open-source repositories, institutional policy pages—I hold that the relevant meaning-fixing intention is distributed across the contributing community at the moment of the last substantive revision, not reducible to any single editor. Third, I take Barthes's death-of-the-author thesis to be normative—about how critics ought to proceed—rather than descriptive of how meaning is constituted, which is why I treat it as compatible with intentionalism rather than as its rival. Fourth, I accept a principle of interpretive charity: when a text genuinely supports two mutually exclusive readings with equal textual support, we should prefer the reading that renders the author's overall belief system maximally coherent, even if that requires attributing an unstated or unconsciously formed intention. Fifth, I maintain that purely AI-generated outputs lack authorial intention in the philosophically relevant sense, so asking what the model meant is a category mistake; however, when a human curator selectively edits and approves the output, the curator's intentions become the authoritative source of meaning.

I need an audit of whether these five commitments form a coherent package. Specifically, does my distributed-intention view for collaborative texts undermine the psychological individualism I defend for novels? Does my charity principle violate my first commitment by licensing intentions that were never actually present in the author's mind? Is my normative reading of Barthes genuinely defensible, or does it depend on a reinterpretation that specialists in hermeneutics and literary theory would reject as non-standard? Please identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts dissolve upon closer inspection, and rank the remaining tensions by severity. For each, specify the least costly revision and what that revision costs me—whether it forces me to abandon intentionalism for literary works, to reject charity, or to concede that distributed intention collapses into individualism. Throughout, separate what is relatively settled in philosophy of language from what is genuinely contested, and do not present any contested view—particularly my charitable-attribution principle or my normative reading of Barthes—as though it represents the consensus among people who work on these questions.
