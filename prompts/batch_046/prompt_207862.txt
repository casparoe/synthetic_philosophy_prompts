I'm putting together a seminar module on epistemic dominance and that alignment idea called ascription universality — the one where an overseer's explicit beliefs are supposed to pack just as much information as any other belief-ascription we'd make about the computation it's watching. My study group tried to push it reflexively and now we've walked ourselves into an argument that seems to blow up the whole framework, and I'm trying to find the exit without throwing out the baby with the bathwater.

Here is the chain we built. (1) A family of overseers A[·] is ascription universal relative to our epistemic state E if, for every computation C, A[C] epistemically dominates C — that is, A[C]'s explicit beliefs contain just as much information as any other belief-ascription we could make about C. (2) Now apply this reflexively: let C be one of the overseers, call it A0. Then A[A0] dominates A0, so whatever beliefs we might ascribe to A0 are already contained in A[A0]'s explicit beliefs. (3) But A0 is also part of the family, so A0 dominates every other computation, including the human evaluators who are trying to check whether A0 is correct. That means our beliefs about whether A0 is mistaken are already present inside A0's explicit belief set. (4) So if we form the belief 'A0 is wrong about X,' that belief is already sitting in A0's beliefs. (5) Yet A0, being correct (since it dominates us), explicitly believes 'A0 is not wrong about X.' (6) So A0 both believes and doesn't believe it's wrong about X, contradiction. (7) Therefore reflexive ascription universality is impossible, and any framework requiring it — like using a universal overseer in iterative distillation — collapses into incoherance.

I feel like there's a scope slip or an equivocation between 'contains information about C' and 'literally contains the belief that C is wrong,' but I can't quite name it. Which premise should I reject? I'd really prefer to keep (1) and (3) since they're basically definitional, and I'd hate to abandon the idea that an overseer can dominate its subjects. But I want to know exactly what I'm paying if I save the framework. Where does the argument go wrong, and what's the price tag?
