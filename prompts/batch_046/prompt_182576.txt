I have been trying to work out my actual commitments about suffering-focused ethics by reflecting on concrete cases, and I want to know whether there is a single principle behind my verdicts, or whether I am holding incompatible views.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Preventing an active genocide is not merely good but obligatory; allowing it to continue when one could stop it is gravely wrong.

2. A voluntary global moratorium on reproduction that prevents the existence of billions of people who would have lived happy lives is not wrong; the absence of those happy lives is not a harm that needs justification.

3. A technological project that creates a civilisation of ten billion blissful beings, but only by maintaining one hundred sentient beings in perpetual severe suffering as an essential energy source, is impermissible. The suffering of the one hundred is not cancelled out by the bliss of the billions.

4. A long-term wildlife management policy that gradually eliminates all wild populations through non-violent means, in order to end all predation and starvation suffering in nature, is not merely permissible but morally required, despite the total loss of wild happiness, biodiversity, and ecological complexity.

5. A climate adaptation programme that requires present generations to endure moderate, widespread hardship in order to prevent catastrophic suffering for future generations is required.

6. A medical research protocol that deliberately inflicts severe suffering on twenty conscious subjects in order to develop a universal pain-elimination therapy that would spare billions from suffering is impermissible, regardless of the net reduction in suffering achieved.

There is also one case on which I keep oscillating: telling a dying patient that their estranged child called, when in fact the child did not, solely in order to spare the patient the suffering of knowing they died alone. I sometimes think this is permissible comfort; at other times I think it is wrong because it treats the patient’s emotional state instrumentally rather than addressing real suffering.

Please structure your response exactly as follows:

1. Reconstructed principle (approximately 300 words). State the precise principle, or competing principles, that best capture verdicts 1–6. For each clause, identify the specific case that motivates it. Distinguish what the verdicts strictly require from what merely fits them well, and say whether any clause depends on an asymmetry between suffering and happiness, on a direct-versus-indirect distinction, or on something else.

2. Testing against unconsidered cases (approximately 200 words). Apply the reconstructed principle to two cases I have not listed: (a) an industrial farming system that produces immense animal suffering but prevents starvation for billions of humans; (b) an artificial-intelligence alignment protocol that requires running thousands of brief, intense suffering-simulations in order to prevent a future in which trillions of beings suffer. State the verdict that each principle delivers, and note any conflicts between them.

3. Verdicts that must be dropped (approximately 200 words). Identify which of my six verdicts is hardest to reconcile with the others. Say whether I should drop it to preserve coherence, what the cost of dropping it is for the overall view, and whether dropping it pushes me toward pure negative utilitarianism, toward a deontological constraint, or toward some hybrid.

4. The oscillating case (approximately 150 words). State what your reconstructed principle says about the dying-patient falsehood. Say whether my oscillation indicates a genuine ambiguity in the principle or a failure of intuition, and identify the clause of the principle that resolves it.

5. Overall assessment (approximately 150 words). Is there one coherent suffering-focused ethic here, or am I straddling incompatible theories? Give your bottom-line judgement on whether the view is salvageable.
