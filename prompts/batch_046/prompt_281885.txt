Consider the institutional architecture now common in employer-sponsored retirement savings: workers are automatically enrolled at a minimal contribution rate, are offered pre-commitment mechanisms that escalate savings automatically whenever future salary increases occur, and face substantial penalties for ordinary withdrawals; yet the same architecture permits rapid, minimally documented hardship withdrawals that allow a present self to dissolve those accumulated commitments with little friction. The design thus treats the preferences of the worker’s future self—who will live with the accumulated savings—as authoritative enough to bind her present, reluctant self, while simultaneously treating the present self’s urgent preferences as sufficient to override the very bindings made on behalf of that future self.

What philosophical commitments about weakness of will, intertemporal rationality, and personal identity does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it conceive hyperbolic discounting as a correctable failure of unified agency, as a legitimate welfare claim of the present stage, or as evidence that an agent over time is not one continuous subject but a sequence of partly independent decision-makers? Say whether the practice strictly requires any one of these views or is compatible with several, distinguish what it genuinely presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and describe how the architecture’s binding and release mechanisms would have to change if we replaced its underlying view with one that treats all temporal preferences—including steep present-bias—as equally authoritative expressions of a single, continuous agent.
