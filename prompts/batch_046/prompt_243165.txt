I write as a journalist who covers science and philosophy of science, and I serve on the editorial advisory board of a quarterly review that publishes long-form arguments at the intersection of biology, normativity, and public reasoning. We are currently deciding the fate of an appealed manuscript that advances what its author calls “vector rationales.” The core claim is that evolutionary selection environments embed normative directions—gradients that prescribe adaptive change—without those prescriptions being reasons for any agent, subject, or reasoner. In other words, the manuscript defends genuinely free-floating rationales: normative structures that are instantiated in biological dynamics rather than in minds, allowing one to say that a trait is “better supported” by selection without implying that any entity has evaluated it. Our board is divided. One faction sees this as a coherent, if audacious, extension of evolutionary normativity that lets us speak of adaptive “oughts” in a strictly non-anthropocentric register. The opposing faction regards it as a category error that collapses causal explanation into spurious rationalization, arguing that without a reasoner there is no rationality, only mechanism, and that the author’s “vector” language is therefore either metaphorical or mystifying. I need a formal memo recommending one concrete outcome—accept, reject, or accept with conditions—addressed to the board for circulation before Thursday’s meeting. The memo should provide actionable evaluation criteria we could apply to this and similar submissions, treat the strongest version of the opposing view with intellectual honesty rather than dismissal, and, if you recommend conditional acceptance, specify the revisions or accompanying material (for instance, a critical response or an operationalization requirement) that would make publication responsible. Please include a clear bottom line and the reasoning that supports it, recognizing that whatever we decide will set precedent for how we handle philosophical arguments that attribute normative force to non-mental processes.
