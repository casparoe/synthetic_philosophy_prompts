I am a high school student currently enrolled in an independent study course on the philosophy of science, and last week my study group had a long debate about whether modern chaos theory actually undermines classical determinism or merely complicates it. We kept returning to Laplace and his demon—the hypothetical intellect that, knowing every force and position at a single instant, could in principle calculate the entire future and past of the universe. But then we discussed deterministic chaos, particularly dynamical systems with positive Lyapunov exponents, in which infinitesimal differences in initial conditions grow exponentially, rendering long-range forecasting impossible for any observer with finite measurement precision.

If Laplace were alive today and fully grasped these mathematical results, how do you think he would reconcile them with his determinism? Would he insist that ontological determinism remains entirely intact and simply revise the demon's requirements—arguing that it needs continuous, infinitely precise updates rather than a single snapshot? Or might he recognize that, for any embedded and finite agent, deterministic chaos produces statistical patterns structurally indistinguishable from true randomness, thereby weakening the philosophical significance of his claim that the future is as fixed as the past? I am particularly interested in whether he would draw a sharp line between metaphysical determinism and epistemic predictability, and whether he would treat exponential divergence as a mere practical obstacle or as a fundamental limit that changes what it means for the present to 'contain' the future. Please articulate his likely defense of the demon against the charge that such divergence makes initial-state knowledge useless for long-range prediction, and address whether he might alter his view that nature proceeds like a clock if he accepted that deterministic laws can generate behavior that is, in principle, uncomputable by any agent within the system.
