Consider the historiographical practice—prominent in debates over the British Raj—of constructing a “moral balance sheet” for empire: listing railways, legal codes, and aggregate economic growth on one side, and extraction, famine mortality, and political subjugation on the other, then rendering a net verdict on whether the empire was “worth it” or left a positive legacy. This ledger method treats imperial rule as an evaluable project with commensurable outputs, and it routinely relies on counterfactual baselines—what development or state-formation would have occurred absent colonial intervention—to fill in the debit column. What philosophical commitments about moral aggregation, historical counterfactuals, and the identity of the beneficiary does this practice presuppose? Does it require a form of rule-utilitarianism that licenses inter-personal and inter-temporal netting of goods and evils, or can it be reconstructed on non-aggregative grounds—say, a deontological prohibition on domination, with institutional benefits treated as non-canceling side effects? Moreover, the framework depends on speculative economic and political models to construct its “without-empire” baseline, yet those models are themselves contested and not adjudicated by the ledger’s own norms. Is the balance-sheet approach philosophically coherent if its entries rest on historical conjectures that the method cannot validate? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say what an imperial historiography that rejected ledger-thinking would have to change—particularly in how it relates institutional development to coercive extraction without weighing them on a single scale.
