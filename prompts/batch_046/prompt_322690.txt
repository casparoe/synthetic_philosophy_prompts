I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop tomorrow — the audience is split between formal epistemologists who actually care about veritism and scoring rules, and ML/policy people who think about calibrated forecasting but haven't read any philosophy of belief. The theme is acuracy-first epistemology versus epistemic utility theory, and I need the philosophical idea to do real structural work, not just be decoration.

The specific angle I want is this: if accuracy is the only intrinsic epistemic good, then every agent should maximize thier expected global calibration score. But that creates what I'd call an "epistemic free-riding" problem — beliefs that are high-cost, low-base-rate, or require dissent become locally irrational to hold, even when the group desperately needs someone to hold them. So the speech needs to argue that adding things like explanatory coherence or understanding to the utility function isn't just a luxury upgrade; it's structurally necessary to prevent belief-collapse in distributed systems. The talk should land for the philosophers without losing the ML people, and it should avoid both the "truth is good actually" sermon and the math dump. Keep it casual, like bad-coffee conversation. No citations, no reading lists, no "as Joyce argues" — build it from the ground up, and let both halves see why they should care about the same failure mode.
