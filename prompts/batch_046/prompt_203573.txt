I've been reading about moral revolutions — why dueling died out, why slavery was abolished — and there's a pattern I keep seeing that I don't have a name for.

A practice gets condemned and disappears, not because anyone proved it was wrong in some deep sense, but because it starts looking vulgar or low-status. But then if you look at what fills the gap, a lot of the same social or economic funciton is still happening, just under a new label with cleaner moral branding. Duelling fades because it's seen as tacky, but then formal litigation and corporate reputation management take over some of that same honor-protection work. Slavery ends, but convict leasing and later prison labor preserve parts of the economic structure, now framed as justice instead of property. The old defenders don't migrate to defending the replacement; they just disappear. Meanwhile the replacement gets defended on totally different grounds, and nobody connects the two because the symbols are different.

Is there an established concept for this — the survival of a practice's underlying funciton through symbolic rebranding after its moral label gets stripped? I know about Williams's idea that rationalization follows material change, and Appiah's honor-world thesis, but this feels more specific: it's about the gap between moral condemnation and functional replacement. If there's no name, can you coin one and say exactly what it is and isn't — especially how it's different from plain hypocrisy or from saying that interests just won out?
