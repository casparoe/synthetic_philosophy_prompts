I serve on the Institutional Neuroscience Ethics and Protocol Review Board at our university medical center, and we must circulate a decision memo before Thursday’s vote on a Phase-II brain-computer interface study in patients with disorders of consciousness. The protocol asks participants to answer binary questions about subjective experience—whether they feel pain, whether they perceive light, whether there is “something it is like” to be in their current state—so that the BCI can be calibrated to neural signatures of awareness. The research team has built their design around an in-house theoretical framework they call “parallel-track phenomenal reporting,” which holds that first-order phenomenal states and second-order judgments about them run on causally independent neural streams. This raises what one board member has called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if the subjective experience itself is causally inert with respect to the verbal report—if the “yes” is generated entirely by the judgment-track without being caused by the phenomenal state—then the patient’s response is not evidence that the experience occurred, and using it to calibrate the interface risks circular reasoning. The principal investigator counters that this is metaphysical speculation with no clinical relevance; in practice, consistent correlations between neural patterns and reports are sufficient for calibration regardless of which causal story is true.

Half the board wants to approve the study outright, arguing that ethical review should not adjudicate philosophy of mind. The other half wants to reject it unless the researchers can demonstrate that their reports are causally connected to the experiences they claim to measure, which they say is impossible without first solving the hard problem of consciousness. Please draft the board’s formal memo—no more than three pages—recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, with a concrete procedural safeguard or time-bound review clause if we approve. The memo must treat both sides honestly, and it must directly address the strongest argument on the losing side: specifically, whether requiring causal proof of phenomenal efficacy sets an impossible standard that would invalidate all subjective-report research, and whether ignoring that standard collapses the distinction between measuring awareness and measuring the mere appearance of awareness. Write it as a document we could actually circulate and act on.
