I'm in a philosophy seminar on Strawson and we just read his essay on reactive attitutes and moral responsability, and I tried to build an argument that I think proves way more than I want it to. Here is the chain — tell me which link actually breaks and what I pay if I reject it.

(1) For Strawson, reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude are the real ground of moral responsibility, not some metaphysical fact about libertarian freedom.
(2) These attitudes are appropriate only when directed at beings who are participants in interpersonal relationships, which means they can understand and respond to normative demands.
(3) But understanding a demand requires recognizing the authority behind it, and that authority is just the collective capacity of the community to hold people to account through reactive attitudes.
(4) So being a participant is partly defined by being embedded in a reciprocal network: you can't be a proper target of blame unless you're also a potential source of it.
(5) Now apply this to self-blame. Guilt looks like a reactive attitude turned inward, so for guilt to be appropriate I must see myself as inside this same reciprocal network.
(6) Here is the suspicious move: if determinism is true, my actions are fixed by prior causes, but that doesn't change my social role, so determinism doesn't by itself push me outside the network. Strawson is right that determinism doesn't undermine reactive attitudes.
(7) But suppose I'm severely depressed and lose all capacity to feel resentment or gratitude toward others. By (4), I then cease to be a participant, which means I'm no longer a proper target of blame or praise from anyone else.
(8) That seems wrong — if I harm someone while depressed, I'm still blameworthy, even though I can't reciprocate. So either (4) or (7) must go.
(9) Yet if we drop (4) and allow one-way reactive attitudes — others can blame me even if I can't blame them — then reactive attitudes apply to infants, animals, and maybe even well-trained AI systems that display quality of will but can't reciprocate. That collapses the boundary Strawson wants between the objective attitude and the reactive stance.
(10) So the framework either makes moral responsibility vanish for anyone who temporarily loses emotional reciprocity, or it dissolves the distinction between responsible agents and sophisticated non-participants.

I feel like there's an equivocation in (4) between being able to hold others accountable and being able to feel resentment, or maybe a modal slide in (7) from losing a capacity to losing a status. I really want to keep (1) and (9) if possible, but I'm not sure what the standard compatibilist reply looks like here. Which premise should I actually reject, and what's the least costly way to do it?
