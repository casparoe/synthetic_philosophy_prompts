I've recently begun supervising clinical trainees and have been struck by how often they describe patients—and themselves—in situations that seem to involve self-deception, yet I remain unsure whether the concept is even coherent. Here are my intuitive verdicts on five concrete cases, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture them, what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, and which verdict I should drop if they conflict.

Case A: A researcher genuinely believes her preliminary data support her model, but she has repeatedly postponed a straightforward control experiment for months, each time finding a plausible reason it is unnecessary. She does not consciously think she is afraid of the result. Verdict: This is genuine self-deception—she has engineered her own ignorance without knowing she did so.

Case B: A man sincerely denies he wants children, yet he consistently selects partners who do and then ends the relationships citing other conflicts. When friends point out the pattern, he feels genuinely attacked and denies it. Verdict: This is not self-deception; there is no false belief, only a refusal to integrate knowledge about his own behavioral patterns.

Case C: A politician privately called a policy "necessary theater," then after a party shift delivered speeches defending it with apparent conviction. Close aides say he now genuinely believes it is right. Verdict: If self-deception is possible, this is it—one mental part persuaded another—but I am unsure that description makes sense rather than describing strategic insincerity that became habit.

Case D: A professional genuinely believes she is not jealous of a colleague's promotion, yet she consistently raises "objective concerns" about the colleague's work that she does not apply to others, rationalizing each instance separately. Verdict: Paradigmatic self-deception: she holds a false belief about her own emotional state, sustained by motivated reasoning.

Case E: A student who barely studied insists his failing grade reflects professor bias. He hides the exam and avoids discussing his preparation, yet when alone he admits he didn't work. Verdict: Not self-deception—he partly knows the truth and is constructing a face-saving narrative; it is closer to self-flattery than to deception of oneself.

The sharpest tensions are between A and E (both involve avoiding an unpleasant truth, yet I call only A deception) and between B and D (both involve hidden emotional motives, yet I deny deception in B and affirm it in D). Is there a principled account—perhaps turning on whether there is a simultaneous contradictory belief, a divided mental architecture, or a specific failure of epistemic responsibility—that delivers my pattern? If so, what does it predict about a therapist who sincerely believes she is objective about a patient but unconsciously shapes sessions to confirm her diagnosis, and who, if asked directly, would deny any bias? And if no principle saves all five verdicts, which one—particularly B or E—must be overturned, and what does the resulting view say about whether self-deception requires literal belief division?
