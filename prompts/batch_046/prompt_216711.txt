I'm trying to map my own judgments about when rational agents should reason as if their choices are correlated with identical copies, and I want to know whether they fit a single principle or whether some verdicts must be sacrificed for simplicity.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

Case 1 — The two-agent prisoner's dilemma: You and one other agent are perfect duplicates in reasoning and information. Mutual cooperation yields 3 each, mutual defection yields 1 each, unilateral defection yields 5 for the defector and 0 for the cooperator. Verdict: Both should cooperate. If I defect, my copy defects; correlation makes defection self-defeating.

Case 2 — Newcomb's problem at 99 percent accuracy: A predictor filled an opaque box with $1,000,000 if she predicted you would take only that box, or left it empty if she predicted you would take both boxes. A transparent box holds $1,000 regardless. Verdict: Take only the opaque box. The predictor's past decision is already fixed, but my current reasoning process is correlated with hers, so one-boxing maximizes expectation.

Case 3 — Platonia with two agents: Each of two agents in sealed rooms can press a button to receive $5,000. If either presses, both receive nothing; if neither presses, both receive $3,000. Verdict: Neither should press. The reasoning is structurally identical to Case 1: correlation makes pressing self-defeating.

Case 4 — The Luring Lottery at scale: One thousand rational agents each receive an invitation. If exactly one sends an entry message, that agent wins $10,000,000 and the other 999 each receive $1,000,000. If zero or more than one send, everyone receives $200,000. Verdict: Send the message. With 999 others, perfect correlation is implausible; I should treat them as largely independent and maximize my own expectation.

Case 5 — The Luring Lottery at intimacy: The same payoff structure, but now there are only two agents, both philosophers who have jointly studied superrationality and know they share this exact reasoning. Verdict: Do not send. Here correlation is strong; mutual abstaining secures $200,000 each, while sending risks mutual destruction of that fallback.

Case 6 — The transparent predictor: Same setup as Case 2, except the predictor's accuracy is exactly 50 percent—no better than chance. The boxes contain the same amounts. Verdict: Take both boxes. Without reliable correlation, causal reasoning dominates.

The pair that keeps reversing for me is Case 3 and Case 4: in both, correlation is imperfect—there are only two agents in Case 3, yet I still invoke correlation, whereas with 999 I dismiss it. Is the difference really just the number of copies, or is there a hidden structural feature?

I'm also unsure about Case 2 versus Case 6: I treat 99 percent correlation as decisive but 50 percent as negligible, which feels right, but I want to know whether there is a sharp threshold or a continuous function behind that judgment.

So my questions are these. First, is there one precise principle—stated in terms of correlation strength, number of copies, causal independence, or some other factor—that delivers Cases 1 through 6 without ad hoc adjustments?
