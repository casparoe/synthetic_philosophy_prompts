You are delivering the opening address at the dedication of the Meridian Center for Civic Repair, a new interdisciplinary institute jointly housed by a university philosophy department and a school of social work. The audience is split: roughly half are scholars familiar with the sociology of moral cultures—specifically the tripartite distinction between honor cultures (moral status is competitive, externally conferred, and restored through public reciprocity), dignity cultures (moral status is intrinsic, legally protected, and restored through rights-based recognition), and victimhood cultures (moral standing is partly indexed to documented suffering and structural offense)—and half are local civic leaders, students, and funders with no background in that literature.

The Center’s charter commits it to “restoring moral standing after public conflict,” but its board is deadlocked over whether that mission should be operationalized in the vocabulary of dignity (universal rights and procedural neutrality), honor (reputation, reciprocity, and face-to-face reconciliation), or victimhood (acknowledgment of harm, structural accountability, and asymmetrical redress). Write a fifteen-minute address that takes a definite normative position on whether any one of these three frameworks is genuinely superior—not as an empirical claim about prevalence, but as a claim about which framework best supports the Center’s practical work of mediating conflicts between groups that speak incompatible moral languages.

Your judgment must do real structural work in the speech: it needs to determine which categories of grievance the Center can legitimately take up, which forms of redress it must refuse to institutionalize, and what it can say to a party whose injury is conceptually real within one framework but invisible within another. Do not resolve this by praising pluralism or calling for dialogue; the address must show that incoherence is unavoidable if the Center refuses to rank them.

Carry the argument through two concrete, invented scenarios rather than through academic citation or historical summary: first, a case in which a student is expelled for speech that honors one community’s norms of reciprocal respect while violating another community’s dignity-based rules of protected status; second, a reparative claim that makes normative sense only if suffering itself confers special moral authority. The speech must land for both halves of the room—the philosophers should recognize that you are engaging the actual conceptual tension between competitive status, intrinsic worth, and victimhood-based standing, while the non-specialists should understand exactly what institutional choice is at stake.

Close with one specific, actionable institutional commitment—something about intake protocols, mediation procedures, or funding priorities—that follows directly from your normative ranking, and frame it as a safeguard against conceptual incoherence rather than as a victory for one cultural faction. The tone should be that of someone trying to keep the Center honest, not someone winning a culture-war skirmish.
