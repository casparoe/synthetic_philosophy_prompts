I'm writing a feature on the historiography of catastrophe—how germs, climate shifts, and volcanic winters are increasingly framed as "historical actors" with causal agency, and where that framing risks sliding into environmental determinism. For the philosophical spine of the piece, I want to read Albert Camus's The Plague not as the familiar allegory of occupation and resistance, but literally: as a narrative about a microbial pathogen that kills indiscriminately and reshapes a city's moral and political life. Read the novel through the lens of pathogenic agency and the critique of determinism: does Camus present the plague bacillus as something like an actor with its own historical trajectory, or does he systematically show every outcome—quarantine failure, religious crisis, Rieux's medical resistance, Cottard's profiteering—as mediated by human institutions and choices, so that attributing agency to the germ itself would be a conceptual mistake? I'm especially interested in scenes that tempt a deterministic reading—the rapid collapse of Oran's administration, the mass graves, the apparent inevitability of infection—and whether the text actually undermines them by emphasizing contingent human failure or solidarity. Does the novel anticipate the modern philosophical worry that treating non-human forces as "actors" can obscure moral responsibility for suffering? Where does the metaphor illuminate, and where does it break? And please: if this framing imposes a conceptual distinction on a novel that never meant to engage it—if asking fiction to adjudicate between agency and determinism is itself confused—say so plainly rather than accommodating the premise. I need the pushback for the piece.
