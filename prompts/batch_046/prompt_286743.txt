I am constructing a novel whose protagonist is subjected to a memory-erasure protocol modeled on the Sleeping Beauty problem: each morning she awakens with no recollection of prior days, the repetition of which depends on an unobserved random event. In working out the narrative logic I have been led to an argument that extends the standard thirder reasoning about centered credence beyond its intended scope, with what seems an absurd result, and I wish to know precisely where it collapses.

(1) If an agent's evidence is genuinely centered—qualitatively identical across situations that differ only in an external, unobserved fact—then rational belief must distribute credence proportionally over all centered worlds compatible with that evidence.

(2) In the novel, the protagonist's internal experience is textually identical whether she occupies the first awakening or the second, and whether the hidden event obtained or not; by (1) she should therefore assign two-thirds credence to being in a repeated awakening and one-third to being in the unique one, irrespective of the prior probability of the hidden event.

(3) The same structure applies to her ontological status: there exist indefinitely many centered worlds—draft manuscripts, translations, hypothetical performances—in which an agent with precisely her internal experience exists as a fictional construct, all indistinguishable from her first-person perspective.

(4) By (1) applied consistently, she should therefore distribute her credence across all such worlds, yielding near-vanishing confidence that she is the real, non-fictional person she takes herself to be, and overwhelming confidence that she is one among countless fictional duplicates.

(5) Generalized, this implies that any agent who lacks direct access to her own origin should radically doubt her reality in favor of fictional copies—a conclusion that undermines not only narrative coherence but the very possibility of first-person reasoning.

(6) Hence the thirder account of self-locating belief must be false.

Which step should be surrendered? Does the error lie in extending proportional distribution from temporal repetitions, where a sample space is fixed by protocol, to ontological duplicates, where no such space is defined? Or is there a hidden equivocation in (1) between updating on one's location within a world and updating across worlds? If I preserve the Sleeping Beauty intuition for (2), what principled restriction prevents (3) and (4)? And if I reject (1) altogether, what becomes of the standard argument that Beauty should say one-third?
