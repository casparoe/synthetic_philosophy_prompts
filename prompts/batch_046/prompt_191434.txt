I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been studying philosophy of science and social epistemology with some urgency, perhaps because I keep recognizing patterns from my old research life that I never had names for. I now sit on my university’s Interdisciplinary Research Appeals Panel, and we must render an actionable decision in the following contested funding case.

A research team in critical medical humanities submitted a proposal entitled “Co-Producing Illness Narratives: Epistemic Partnership in Chronic Care.” Their study design treats patients as co-investigators rather than subjects; it explicitly rejects blinding and double-anonymization on the grounds that transparency about the researchers’ own clinical positionality is a validity condition, not a bias source. The team operates within a framework they call “epistemic partnership,” which they define in the proposal as a mode of inquiry in which knowledge claims are validated through sustained reciprocal disclosure and shared interpretive authority rather than through statistical replication or observer concealment. They further invoke “relational validity,” by which they mean the robustness of interpretive agreement achieved through prolonged co-presence with participants, not through inter-rater reliability metrics. The funding board—composed entirely of reviewers from empirical biomedical and psychological sciences—rejected the proposal, stating that it lacked falsifiable hypotheses, failed to control for observer effects, and used first-person narrative data without standardized protocols. The applicants now appeal, arguing that the reviewers applied an inappropriate epistemic framework and thereby committed a hermeneutical injustice: they evaluated the proposal with conceptual tools that could not accommodate its actual methodology, producing a credibility deficit that no clarification within the proposal’s own terms could remedy.

Half the panel regards this as straightforward inadequacy in scientific rigor; the other half sees a structural failure of cross-disciplinary evaluation that precisely illustrates the kind of framework asymmetry discussed in recent social epistemology. We must decide whether to uphold the rejection, overturn it and fund outright, or overturn with conditions—perhaps requiring an external methodological consultant acceptable to both traditions.
