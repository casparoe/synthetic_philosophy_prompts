I am running a 90-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of mathematics with about twelve students who have already worked through ZF set theory and know the statement of the axiom of choice, but who still treat it as a harmless bookkeeping device. I want them to feel, through their own actions rather than through lecture, why selecting representatives can be genuinely destructive to structure—specifically, I want them to run into the wall between “picking one shoe from each pair” and “picking one point from each orbit of rotation” without being told the Banach-Tarski result in advance. Design a structured in-class game or simulation with explicit rules, roles, and small physical stakes (cards, tokens, or spatial positioning) in which the group must partition and reassemble a shared “resource space” according to rules they themselves write down, and in which they discover that any selection procedure they can actually articulate either leaves gaps, creates duplicates, or violates an invariant they wanted to preserve. Include a timeline for the 90 minutes, the materials needed, the payoff or incentive scheme (no real money required), and a debrief that maps each phase onto the corresponding feature of AC—choice functions, equivalence classes, non-measurable sets, and the loss of rotational invariance. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run (for instance, students who try to “cheat” by using geometric intuition, or groups that converge too quickly on a naive well-ordering) and how the facilitator should handle it. Also include an honest note on which aspect of the philosophical controversy around AC—its independence from ZF, its role in analysis versus its paradoxical consequences, or the constructivist rejection of it—the activity is structurally incapable of reproducing and must therefore be supplied in discussion. In your design and debrief, please separate what is mathematically settled (the consistency and independence results, the actual construction of non-measurable sets given AC) from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and mathematicians (whether AC should be accepted despite the paradoxes, whether there is a principled reason to reject it, or whether the paradoxes reveal a flaw in our geometric intuition rather than in the axiom itself), and do not present any one of these contested philosophical stances as if it were the consensus view.
