Write the joint statement that two camps in institutional moral philosophy should have produced after a sustained disagreement about the normative status of the distinction between killing and letting die in the design of automated systems and public protocols. The first camp defends the Doctrine of Doing and Allowing (DDA): they maintain that actively causing a death carries a moral weight that failing to prevent an equivalent death does not, that this asymmetry is not merely a cognitive bias but a genuine deontological boundary, and that policy architectures—including algorithmic decision rules—should encode it rather than suppress it. The second camp defends Causal Uniformitarianism (CU): they maintain that moral assessment of an institution or agent must depend solely on the counterfactual profile of outcomes for which it is responsible, that the causal path—direct action versus omission—is morally inert, and that building the active/passive distinction into institutional design produces arbitrary, harmful, and poorly justified results.

Produce a document with the following parts, in this order.

1. Agreed claims. List four to six specific propositions—about moral responsibility, institutional design, and the psychological reality of aversion to active harm—that both camps can sign without reservation. These should be substantive, not vacuous.

2. Precise disagreements. For each camp, state its position in neutral wording that the camp itself would accept. Identify exactly where they diverge on (a) whether the active/passive distinction tracks a real moral boundary or only a difference in causal mechanism, and (b) whether institutional rules ought to reflect that distinction even when doing so produces worse aggregate outcomes.

3. Cruxes. Identify three sub-questions such that resolving one in either direction would move at least one camp substantially. For each crux, specify a concrete scenario or structural feature that both sides agree is relevant. One crux must concern "programmed omission"—a system explicitly designed to withhold intervention in a harm pathway whose occurrence rate is known with high confidence. For that crux, record in advance what each camp predicts about the moral evaluation of such a system, assuming its designers know the harm rate to be 99 percent and have deliberately chosen the omission architecture.

Keep the tone formal, avoid straw-manning either position, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or appeals to authority. The deliverable should be a text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made as exact as possible.
