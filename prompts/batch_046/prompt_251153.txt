I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about whether modal realism of the concrete-worlds kind can survive once ontological parsimony and the epistemic isolation of worlds are taken seriously. I want to defend the position that possible worlds are not abstract representations but concrete, spatiotemporally isolated entities with precisely the same ontological status as our own, and—this is the crux my friend kept attacking—that this commitment is genuinely required to ground the objectivity of counterfactual judgments about institutional and technological trajectories, rather than allowing them to collapse into mere redescriptions of the actual world. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list. Instead, tell me what would count against this view: which arguments, observations, formal results, or conceptual shifts should move me, sorted by how decisive they are. Please include what defenders of the position themselves concede as damaging, not only the standard criticisms from rivals. Has the view already been made awkward by developments in the philosophy of science, in the semantics of modal language, or in accounts of theoretical virtue? Has it been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by retreating to a claim about 'ways things could be' that no longer entails the existence of concrete worlds—and if so, is that insulation a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind? I want an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything at all, or whether it has become so well defended that it is immune to refutation, and whether that immunity is a virtue or a vice. For what it is worth, I find arguments from ontological parsimony and from the apparent redundancy of worlds in explaining modal discourse especially persuasive, so please weigh those specifically.
