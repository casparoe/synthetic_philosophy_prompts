Design a 90-minute final-exam essay question for an upper-level seminar in metaphysics and philosophy of physics, centered on Gödel’s argument that rotating universes permitting closed timelike curves undermine the objectivity of temporal passage. The question must be unanswerable by mere summary of standard accounts; it should require students to reconstruct the logical architecture of the argument—specifically, the connection between global rotation, the absence of a global time coordinate, and the physical possibility of returning to one’s own past—and then apply that architecture to a modified scenario. In this variant, there is no global rotation axis; instead, space contains only local, bounded vortices that generate isolated closed timelike curves within circumscribed regions, leaving the global spacetime structure otherwise intact. Ask students to determine whether Gödel’s anti-realist conclusion about objective time still follows in this case, and to identify precisely which premise of his original reasoning the local-vorticity scenario undermines.

Together with the question, provide:
- A model answer of approximately 800–1000 words demonstrating the required reasoning: accurate reconstruction of Gödel’s argument; precise identification of the global-rotation premise; analysis of how local, bounded CTCs affect (or fail to affect) global temporal ordering; a reasoned evaluation of whether the anti-realist conclusion survives; and clear distinction between local loopability and global non-integrability of time slices.
- A marking scheme out of 25 marks, with explicit allocations and a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks for each component. Weight the scheme as follows: (i) correct reconstruction of Gödel’s logical structure (6 marks); (ii) precise identification of the global-premise challenged by the variant (5 marks); (iii) rigorous analysis of the local-vorticity modification (6 marks); (iv) evaluated judgment on the survival of the anti-realist conclusion (5 marks); and (v) logical coherence, philosophical precision, and clarity (3 marks).

Specify that student answers should run approximately 1500–2000 words and that the exam allows 90 minutes. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation instructions. Introduce all conceptual novelty—such as the local-bounded-vorticity variant—within the prompt itself, so that a well-prepared student can address it without recourse to outside research.
