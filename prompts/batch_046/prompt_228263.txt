I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm trying to think through a career decision that feels structurally strange rather than merely difficult. I have two offers: one as a lead engineer at a small climate-modeling nonprofit, where I'd work directly on open-source tools for emissions forecasting, at a salary that covers my needs but little more; the other as a senior engineer at a profitable fintech firm, where the technical work is engaging but morally neutral, at roughly three times the compensation. The extra income from the second role would allow me to support family members who currently depend on me, and if I donated a portion strategically, I could plausibly fund more climate-research capacity than my direct labor in the first role would provide.

What makes this hard isn't just weighing pros and cons. The goods involved—direct participation in meaningful work, financial security for dependents, intellectual engagement, and the indirect impact of donated capital—seem to belong to different categories. I find myself asking whether taking the higher-paying job and donating the surplus is actually a more rational way to serve the values I care about, or whether there's something incoherent about treating direct engagement and financial substitution as interchangeable currencies. A colleague argued that if I genuinely can't rank these goods on a single scale, then rational choice requires me to recognize that neither option is better, which should make me indifferent; I replied that I don't feel indifferent at all—I feel pulled in two directions by forces that don't cancel out.

I'm not looking for a recommendation about which offer to accept. I'm asking whether there's a coherent philosophical account of what happens when practical reasoning involves goods that don't share a common measure—whether my confusion indicates that one framework for decision-making is failing, or whether the failure itself tells us something about how rational choice works when values are genuinely of different kinds. Specifically, does the possibility of substituting one form of contribution for another (direct labor versus funded labor) resolve the tension, or does it misunderstand what makes these values valuable in the first place?
