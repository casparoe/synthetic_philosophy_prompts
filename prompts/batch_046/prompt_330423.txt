I'm putting together a workshop handout for a history grad class — we're looking at what happens when you try to write about people who never wrote anything down, and the only records are tax rolls, court minutes, and bishops' letters. I want a decision tree, or maybe a taxonomy, that sorts the actual methodological stances people take, not just the labels they stick on themselves.

The root question should be whether the absence of direct non-elite testimony is mainly an epistemic gap we can bridge with clever reading, or an ontological feature of the archive that makes true history from below impossible without elite mediation. From there, branch on whether shrinking the scale — microhistory — actually fixes this or just reproduces it in miniture. Then branch on whether state-produced documents contain readable noise that lets us recover subaltern agency, or whether every trace is already formatted by elite categories.

The leaves need to map onto real positions: Thompson-style social history from below, Ginzburg-style microhistory with its clue-paradigm, Spivak-style subaltern studies, Braudel-style structural long-durée history, and old-school elite and state narrative history. But build it from the logical space first — the combinations of yes/no answers — then drop the named positions onto the branches. Note any empty combos, like someone who thinks microhistory solves the epistemic problem but refuses to read state archieves against the grain; say if they're incoherent, just unattractive, or unexplored.

Every leaf gets a one-sentence awkward commitment — the thing defenders have to swallow. Flag any pair your tree can't really seperate, say microhistorians who reject the exceptional-normal idea versus ordinary local historians. And please, no bibliographies or citation lists — this is for people who haven't done the reading yet. Write it casually in British English; don't worry if there's a typo or two.
