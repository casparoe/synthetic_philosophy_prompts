I'm attempting to formalize the implicit principle behind Karl Marx's assessments of technological change under capitalism, reconstructed from his treatment of machinery, infrastructure, and scientific application in Capital and related manuscripts. His verdicts across four cases seem jointly consistent, yet I believe they underdetermine the rule he is actually running. First, on the mechanized factory system with power looms, he holds that capital transforms living labor into an appendage of dead machinery, increasing relative surplus value while simultaneously developing productive forces that will eventually outgrow capitalist relations. Second, on continental railway networks, he treats them as requiring such extensive social coordination that they anticipate socialist organization, yet in practice they serve primarily to accelerate capital circulation and integrate markets for private gain. Third, on the direct application of chemical science to industrial dye production—where scientific knowledge replaces traditional craft skill—he emphasizes that capital now subordinates science itself, extracting surplus not only from manual labor but from the social intellect, though he also notes that this demonstrates how drastically necessary labor time could be reduced under different social arrangements. Fourth, on the mechanization of American grain harvesting, he argues that it violently separates producers from the land and swells the reserve army of labor, yet simultaneously concentrates agricultural production in a form that makes collective, planned management technically straightforward.

Consider now a new case: a large-scale logistics network in which human workers continue to perform manual picking and packing, but every movement is governed by a proprietary algorithm that predicts individual fatigue, dynamically reroutes tasks, and adjusts compensation in real time according to productivity metrics. The workers have formed a cooperative that collectively owns the warehouse buildings, conveyor belts, and physical robots; however, the predictive algorithm, its training data, and its optimization protocols are licensed exclusively from a private technology platform, so that surplus extraction occurs primarily through rent on the embedded cognitive infrastructure rather than through direct wage suppression. Would Marx classify this configuration as a deepening of real subsumption—since labor is now subordinated to capital's intellectual apparatus—or rather as a transitional contradiction in which the socialization of physical means conflicts with private appropriation of collective scientific knowledge? Which of the four earlier cases is the closest analog, and what implicit rule—development of productive forces, degree of labor subordination, or the social form of ownership—best predicts his verdict? Identify also the competing rule that fits the earlier four verdicts equally well but diverges here, state precisely what that alternative rule predicts for this logistics arrangement, and propose the single further hypothetical case—specifying both the technology involved and the ownership structure—that would decisively distinguish the two rules.
