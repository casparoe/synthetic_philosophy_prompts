I am a doctoral student in moral and political philosophy working on autonomy, state power, and behavioral governance. For a graduate seminar of roughly sixteen students in a single seventy-five-minute session, I want a runnable in-class simulation that makes participants feel the destabilizing tension inside libertarian paternalism—specifically, the transparency paradox combined with preference endogeneity—before either concept is named. The core idea is this: a nudge claims to preserve autonomy only because the chooser retains an opt-out, but the opt-out is effective precisely when it is hidden in the architecture; meanwhile, if preferences are endogenous to framing, there is no neutral baseline from which an architect can identify a “true” preference to correct. I want the rules, hidden instructions, and role asymmetries arranged so that students enact both the seductive logic of “asymmetric paternalism” and its collapse, experiencing the absence of a welfare baseline rather than merely hearing about it.

Please design the activity with the following elements. First, specify the group division—perhaps “choosers,” “choice architects,” and “assessors”—and the materials (cards, sealed envelopes with hidden framing, a point-based reputation scheme that requires no real money). Second, lay out a multi-round sequence in which the architects receive aggregate “choice data” that has itself been framed differently for different subgroups, then design a default for the choosers; the choosers, in turn, must “reflect” on their preferences using a sheet whose wording is covertly gain-framed for half and loss-framed for half, and finally vote on adoption of the architects’ plan through a mechanism that contains a hidden default opt-in unless they actively check an obscure box. Third, include a debrief that maps each round onto the conceptual parts: revealed versus constructed preference, the efficacy cost of transparency, and the missing baseline problem. The debrief should also predict what will go wrong on the first run—likely defensive rationalization by architects, resentment and feelings of betrayal among choosers when the hidden framing is revealed, and the assessors’ rubric collapsing because autonomy and welfare improvement are now incommensurable—and advise how to handle these dynamics without undermining the pedagogical point. Finally, be frank about which feature the simulation cannot reproduce—almost certainly the long-run empirical claim about sustained welfare improvement across repeated choices—and explain exactly how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally.

Keep the conceptual framework within well-established philosophical discussions of nudging, default effects, and autonomy, but configure them in a novel interaction; do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific empirical papers I would need to look up. Avoid verbatim quotations from any text.
