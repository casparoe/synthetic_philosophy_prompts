Consider a gradual mind-uploading procedure in which a living person's biological neurons are replaced, cluster by cluster over several years, with synthetic analogues that preserve synaptic weights and global connectivity but rely on a different internal signaling chemistry and have a functional lifespan an order of magnitude longer than biological neurons. Replacement happens in discrete monthly batches, so at every intermediate stage the subject is fully conscious and socially embedded, yet no single substrate ever houses more than a fraction of the original neural tissue; continuity is maintained only at the level of integrated functional patterns, not in any continuous physical or phenomenal stream. What are the most important implications of this specific architecture—especially the discrete batching, the substrate discontinuity, and the divergent failure modes of the synthetic clusters—for (1) whether legal personhood and accumulated rights survive intact across the full transition, (2) how moral responsibility should be assigned for actions taken during the intermediate hybrid stages, and (3) whether this case ultimately supports psychological-continuity theories, undermines them, or forces a third criterion for personal identity? In your answer, treat the synthetic clusters as genuinely different in kind from neurons rather than as improved biological substitutes, and reason from first principles rather than invoking named frameworks without explanation.
