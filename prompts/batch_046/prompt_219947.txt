Here is the exam question that was assigned: Explain Russell’s theory of definite descriptions, including the quantifier analysis and the distinction between primary and secondary occurrence. Apply the analysis to determine whether the claim that the present King of France is wise is false or meaningless, and explain why. Then apply the same framework to the statement that Pegasus does not exist, showing how Russell avoids committing to a realm of non-existent objects. The student submitted the following response: Russell holds that definite descriptions are not logical proper names but hidden quantifiers. So the present King of France unpacks into there exists exactly one x such that x is King of France and x is wise. Because France currently has no king, the existence clause fails, which makes the entire sentence false rather than meaningless. This solves the problem of negative existentials without requiring us to name something absent.

Russell also distinguishes primary and secondary occurrence. In the present King of France is wise, the description occurs primarily, so the uniqueness and existence conditions are asserted about the actual world. But in George believes the present King of France is wise, the description necessarily occurs secondarily: George’s belief concerns only the descriptive content, and Russell is not committed to there actually being a king. This allows Russell to analyze belief about fiction without positing fictional objects.

When we turn to Pegasus, Russell treats Pegasus as the definite description the winged horse captured by Bellerophon. Applying the quantifier analysis shows that there is no unique winged horse captured by Bellerophon, so Pegasus does not exist comes out false. This avoids Meinong’s realm of being. Russell also borrows Frege’s distinction between sense and reference to explain how we understand descriptions that lack reference—the sense remains intact even when the reference is missing. In addition, descriptions are incomplete symbols that only acquire full meaning inside a complete proposition, which is why they cannot stand alone as names.
