I'm currently enrolled in a course on epistemology and rational belief, and I've been trying to apply the structure of the lottery and preface paradoxes to normative reasoning rather than empirical prediction. Here is the argument I constructed, which seems to me to prove far more than it should. (1) Rational belief is governed by a high-credence threshold: if an agent assigns a credence above t to each member of a set of independent propositions, she is rationally entitled to believe each one individually. (2) In a standard 100-ticket lottery, this yields belief that ticket 1 loses, ticket 2 loses, and so on, together with the statistical knowledge that exactly one wins—hence belief in a contradiction unless we reject closure under conjunction or the aggregation rule. (3) The standard resolutions either deny that belief is closed under conjunction for large sets, or they treat the lottery case as a special failure of the threshold rule. (4) Now consider a systematic normative theory—say, a form of rule-utilitarianism—that generates specific judgments: action A is wrong, action B is wrong, through action Z is wrong, each with credence 0.98 based on the theory's criteria. (5) At the same time, the agent recognizes her theory is fallible and subject to revision; she therefore assigns high credence to the claim that at least one of these specific normative judgments is mistaken. (6) By parity with the lottery case, she should therefore hold each specific normative belief while also believing that at least one is false, producing what we might call a normative preface paradox. (7) If this undermines rational confidence in particular moral judgments, it threatens moral realism; but if it undermines all systematic normative theorizing—including the anti-realist's own critical framework—it seems to prove too much. (8) So either there is a principled asymmetry between empirical and normative belief-aggregation, or the argument collapses into a global skepticism about all structured reasoning.

I suspect the error lies in step (4) or (5)—perhaps normative beliefs are not independent in the way lottery tickets are, or fallibilism about a theory doesn't distribute over its outputs like statistical knowledge distributes over a fair lottery—but I cannot identify the precise misstep or its cost. Which step should be rejected? And if the answer requires treating normative beliefs as structurally different from empirical predictions, does that amount to an ad hoc exemption designed to save moral realism? Please say in your response how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the specific point—whether the independence assumption, the closure principle, or the analogy to fallibilism—about which you are least certain.
