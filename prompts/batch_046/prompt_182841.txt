Consider an institutional funding protocol that ranks philanthropic proposals by multiplying three scores—importance, tractability, and neglectedness—to yield a single priority number. The protocol applies two asymmetrical rules. For proposals whose main benefits lie more than fifty years in the future, tractability is assessed over century-long timelines and may be very low; for proposals addressing present suffering, tractability must be high within a five-year grant window, or the proposal is rejected regardless of its importance score. Meanwhile, "neglectedness" is measured not by actual resources committed, but by the cause's absence from mainstream media coverage, so a technically well-funded but journalistically invisible problem scores as highly neglected.

What philosophical commitments about value, intergenerational ethics, and practical reason does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in the scoring rules: does it treat future and present welfare as governed by different decision procedures, and if so, does that require a lexical priority, a form of satisficing, or a non-consequentialist constraint? Does the media-based neglect metric presuppose that moral urgency tracks epistemic salience rather than resource saturation? Distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether it is philosophically coherent or whether its parts pull in incompatible directions, and describe what would have to change in the rules if they were rebuilt on a uniform, time-neutral expected-value framework.

If the premise that these design rules embody one unified implicit theory is confused—perhaps because they are pragmatically disjoint rather than philosophically integrated—say so plainly rather than forcing a reconstruction.
