After a long argument with a friend last night over whether labeling an atrocity "genocide" is first a legal determination that then authorizes moral outrage, or whether the moral recognition must come first and the legal label is merely its bureaucratic echo, I need to design a teaching plan for a seminar of advanced undergraduates who have read the UN Convention but still treat genocide as an unproblematic quantitative extreme—"the worst crime"—rather than a qualitatively distinct category. I want the session to turn on the conceptual dependency between Raphael Lemkin's original formulation, which encompassed cultural destruction and the targeted dissolution of group life-worlds, and the narrower physical-biological framing that was codified in international law, and then to show how that narrowing created the conditions for a politics of naming in which states resist the label not simply to avoid treaty obligations but because accepting it would restructure the moral ontology of the events they have perpetrated or permitted. For the carrying example, I am weighing a comparison between the systematic eradication of a minority language, religious institutions, and intergenerational transmission mechanisms without widespread killing, and a campaign of mass killing driven by individual hatred and resource extraction rather than by an intent to destroy the group as such; the example must make visible why the intent-to-destroy-the-group clause is the hinge that separates genocide from other atrocities, and why the legal definition's exclusion of purely cultural destruction is a contingent political settlement rather than a conceptual refinement. Please lay out the explanatory arc: which idea must be secured before the legal-moral tension can even be introduced; the precise order in which Lemkin's broader conception, the codified narrowing, the intent clause, and the politics of naming should appear; the misconceptions students will predictably form—such as "without bodies there is no genocide," "genocide requires state machinery," or "the legal definition captures the full moral meaning"—and the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be interrupted, with a targeted follow-up question for each that forces the student to articulate the dependency rather than repeat the definition. Include a final check for understanding that reveals whether students see the politics of naming as operating upstream of moral judgment—shaping what can be recognized as an atrocity—rather than downstream as a mere administrative tag. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—perhaps ICC jurisdictional thresholds, analogies to ecocide, or the debate over group-protection versus individual-protection frameworks—and acknowledge that omission directly so the session does not overreach.
