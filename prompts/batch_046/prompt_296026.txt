This has become personally urgent for me. Two months ago, I approved an identical maintenance protocol to one approved by a colleague in a parallel unit; through no difference in diligence, information, or intention—only an unpredicted thermal fluctuation—my unit’s equipment failed catastrophically (no injuries, but severe resource loss), while my colleague’s functioned perfectly. Since then, I have been treated as having committed a serious lapse, while my colleague has been praised. I want to defend the view that this disparity is unjust because our moral standing should depend solely on the quality of our will, not on outcomes outside our control. Here is my opening challenge: if you insist that outcomes matter constitutively, how do you avoid the conclusion that I am now, in some meaningful sense, a worse agent than my colleague? And how do you account for the asymmetry I actually feel—a lingering sense of moral taint or residue that attaches to me but not to them—without simply saying that feelings track reality? I am not asking for comfort; I am asking whether your view can respect the intuition that I have not become less worthy, even if the world has made my choice far more costly. Go.
