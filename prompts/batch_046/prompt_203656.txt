For my senior seminar on moral status and interspecies justice, I want to assign a philosophical reading of Disney's The Lion King that does not reduce the film to a fable about hierarchy. The film's explicit metaphysic, articulated in its central song, is the circle of life: predation is natural, death is regenerative, and Mufasa's death is tragic only because Scar disrupts the balance, not because lions consume wildebeest. I want the class to examine whether this metaphysic functions as a form of ecological functionalism—the idea that individual prey suffering is morally weightless when it serves systemic equilibrium—and whether the film's narrative undercuts that view through its anthropomorphism. The critical task is to track the asymmetry in grief: we mourn Mufasa and later the hyenas' exclusion, yet the stampede kills anonymous prey and Simba's adult hunting is framed as rightful kingship. Does the film's emotional architecture depend on an unacknowledged speciesist distinction between noble predators and disposable herbivores? Where does the song's philosophy do its work, and where does the visual rhetoric of the savanna—sunlit grass for lions, shadow and chaos for prey—betray a moral framework that collapses if we extend the argument from marginal cases consistently? I want an interpretation that is faithful in both directions: it does not distort the film's aesthetic achievement to make it illustrate animal ethics, and it does not distort the ethical debate to fit the plot. Specifically, I need the scenes that do the philosophical labor identified precisely—the stampede sequence, Simba's exile and return, the contrast between Scar's scavenging and Mufasa's hunting—along with an honest account of where the film resists the reading. What does this particular work of popular art reveal about wild animal suffering and speciesism that a standard philosophical argument, unaided by narrative, typically fails to capture?
