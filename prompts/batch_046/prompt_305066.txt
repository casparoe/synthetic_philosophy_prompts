For a philosophy reading group on classical Chinese thought (10 participants, 90 minutes, no opening lecture), design a structured role-play with physical props that makes the group experience the force of Zhuangzi's claim that death is not a loss but a transformation within an undifferentiated process—specifically, the idea that conventional grief presupposes a fixed self that persists across change, which Zhuangzi denies. In the first phase, each participant receives an identity token (a card with a name, role, and commitment) and must enact a brief ritual of attachment and anticipated loss. In the second phase, the facilitator physically transforms or removes every token without replacement, and participants must continue interacting under new, randomly assigned forms with no mechanism to recover their prior identities; they are told that consistency with pre-transformation commitments earns one kind of collective reward, while successful coordination in the new form earns another. Provide explicit rules, role assignments, a 90-minute timeline, and a payoff scheme that does not use money. After the activity, include a debrief that maps the ritual, the removal, and the coordination problem onto Zhuangzi's rejection of the life/death boundary and the idea of wu hua (the transformation of things). Be explicit about which metaphysical feature—likely the denial that discrete selves exist to be harmed—the activity can only approximate through performance rather than prove, and warn about what will predictably fail when running this for the first time.
