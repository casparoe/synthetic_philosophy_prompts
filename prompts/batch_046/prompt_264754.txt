I defend the view—developed in the Chase-Sanborn line—that qualia are not merely epistemically elusive but conceptually incoherent: the posit requires phenomenal properties to be simultaneously absolutely intrinsic and determinately comparable, yet any condition that fixes what a given quale is like already imports relational or functional structure, leaving no consistent property-space in which they could reside. I want a committed assessment of what would genuinely count against this dissolution claim. Please distinguish decisive defeaters—such as a formal demonstration that intrinsic determinacy can be secured without functional mediation, or a phenomenological result showing that first-person comparison operates without implicit relational framing—from observations that would merely be awkward, like the persistence of widespread introspective reports. Say what defenders of the incoherence thesis should themselves concede as damaging; assess whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability through ad hoc qualifications of “intrinsicness”; and take a definite position on whether such insulation, if present, undermines the view or is appropriate for a conceptual dissolution argument. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; evaluate the arguments and predictions on their own merits.
