I am organizing a graduate seminar on the epistemology of art and want to test whether a classic literary case can illuminate the debate about aesthetic testimony without being turned into a mere allegory. I have in mind Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray, which I think raises a sharper problem than the usual examples used in the literature.

Read the novel through the lens of aesthetic testimony—specifically the dispute over whether justified aesthetic belief requires direct perceptual acquaintance or can be transmitted through the reports of others. Focus on the triangular relationship between Basil Hallward's testimony about the portrait's corruption, Lord Henry Wotton's epigrammatic judgments about beauty and morality, and Dorian's private, repeated examination of the hidden painting. Ask whether the novel supports a pessimistic position that Basil's verbal reports are necessarily inadequate because they lack the first-personal visual acquaintance that the painting demands; or whether, conversely, the portrait itself operates as a form of non-verbal, persistent testimony that should have convinced the other characters had they not been motivated to reject it. Pay attention to the scene in which Basil first reveals the finished portrait, to his later horror upon seeing it altered, and to Dorian's solitary inspections behind the locked curtain—what does each scene suggest about whether aesthetic knowledge of corruption can be shared?

Your reading should be faithful in both directions: do not reduce Wilde's irony or his critique of aestheticism to a philosophical thesis, and do not distort the epistemology of testimony to fit the plot. Take a committed stance on whether the novel's ultimate verdict is epistemic—the failure lies in the nature of aesthetic testimony itself—or psychological—the failure lies in the audience's willful refusal to hear it.
