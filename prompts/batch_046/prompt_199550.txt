I am drafting a six-month research proposal for a small philosophy fellowship. The project concerns Rousseau’s views on theatre, particularly the argument of his Letter to d’Alembert. The standard interpretation holds that Rousseau condemns theatrical spectacle because it replaces active civic participation with passive emotional identification with fictional characters, and that his occasional praise for popular festivals is either rhetorical or belongs to a separate moral category. I want to test a less familiar framing: that Rousseau’s core objection is not representational but attentional. Specifically, I suspect he thinks theatrical spectacle trains citizens in a structurally divided mode of attention—simultaneously judging the performance and being emotionally captured by it—which undermines the undivided, co-present attention required for genuine democratic deliberation, and that his favoured festivals are distinguishable precisely because they sustain this undivided presence rather than because they lack representation. Write the proposal. It should contain: a precise statement of the research question; an explanation of why the standard moral-political and representational readings fail to settle whether this attentional distinction is defensible within Rousseau’s own framework; the philosophical method (close conceptual analysis of attention and presence in the Letter, paired with a structural comparison to the festivals Rousseau praises); what a positive result, a negative result, and a failed project would each look like; and an honest assessment of scope for six months of full-time work. Do not request a bibliography, literature review, or citation list—I will supply those separately. The argument should be constructible from general philosophical competence and familiarity with Rousseau’s text, without reliance on obscure secondary sources.
