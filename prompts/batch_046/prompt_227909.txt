As a novelist preoccupied with whether a story's conclusion is fixed by its premises or shaped by the particular consciousness that writes it, I find myself returning to the paired discoveries of calculus by Newton and Leibniz and of natural selection by Darwin and Wallace. If two independent minds can arrive at what we now call the same theory within months, does this not show that these ideas were structurally inevitable—like a plot point any competent author would reach—rather than acts of singular vision? If the discovery was bound to occur regardless of who was present, then asking which individual made it seems as empty as asking which wave carried the ship to shore; the discoverer is interchangeable, and priority disputes are merely administrative tags. By the same logic, differences in conceptual vocabulary—Newton's geometric fluxions against Leibniz's analytic notation, or Darwin's emphasis on Malthusian pressure against Wallace's biogeographic framing—must be superficial ornament on an identical underlying idea, not constitutive parts of the theory itself. Why, then, do historians of science treat these disputes as intellectually consequential rather than arbitrary? Please answer in American English, and feel free to correct any false premise embedded in my comparison between narrative inevitability and scientific discovery.
