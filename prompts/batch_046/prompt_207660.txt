I've come to accept the one-third answer in the Sleeping Beauty problem, but I arrived at it through an analogy I actually use at work: distributed state replication. My reasoning is that Beauty's awakening is like a compute node coming back online without knowing whether it's the first or the second replica spawned by a coin-flip protocol. If heads, there's one awakening (Monday); if tails, there are two identical ones (Monday and Tuesday). Because she can't distinguish which replica she is, her prior should be partitioned equally across all three centered worlds—Monday-heads, Monday-tails, Tuesday-tails—giving heads a weight of one-third rather than one-half. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I don't want the usual half-er versus third-er stalemate restated; I want the specific points of failure. Does the replication analogy collapse at the level of self-locating evidence, or at the prior-partitioning step? Would a result showing that self-locating beliefs don't update via standard Bayesian conditioning undermine the view itself, or only show that the distributed-systems framing is the wrong tool? And is there an observation or modified case—perhaps involving continuous memory, a single unbroken consciousness, or an external observer's report—that should force the credence back toward one-half? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish which ones a committed one-third defender should concede as genuinely damaging versus which they should dismiss as category errors, and say whether insulating the position against all of them would be a strength or a weakness for a view of this kind.
