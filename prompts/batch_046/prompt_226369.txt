In our philosophy of mind and biology unit we have been examining the claim that both Darwinian natural selection and Turing computation exhibit a strange inversion: they reliably produce competent outcomes—well-adapted organisms, correct calculations—without any internal comprehension or semantic understanding on the part of the mechanism itself. Write an essay assessing whether this parallel genuinely undermines the thesis that intelligent or adaptive behavior requires internal representations with genuine semantic content. Consider the objection that evolutionary history merely encodes environmental information in genetic and phenotypic structure, which might count as an embodied form of representation, and the objection that computation is purely syntactic manipulation that acquires meaning only through an external interpreter. Weigh these responses against the view that the inversion is philosophically radical and reveals that competence can be entirely mechanistic and representation-free. Reach and defend a clear verdict: does the Darwin-Turing analogy provide strong support for eliminativism about mental content, or does the analogy collapse once we distinguish different senses of understanding and representation?
