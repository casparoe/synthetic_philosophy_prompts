I'm working on a story about when a community's shared memory clashes with the archival record — you know, the classic oral-history-versus-document problem but pushed further. A researcher I interviewed threw this argument at me, and now I can't find the exit. She starts with the idea that collective memory isn't just a sloppy version of history; it runs on what she calls "mnemonic indexicals" — shared experiential frames that anchor references to people, places, and harms in ways that don't survive translation into document-language.

(1) If that's right, then collective memory and the historical record aren't competing accounts of the same events; they're incommensurable representational schemes, like trying to map a dream onto a census form, and should be treated as seperate kinds of knowledge altogether. (2) So when they conflict, there is no neutral metric — no "view from nowhere" — that could adjudicate between them without already favoring one frame. (3) Therefore, in any conflict, the community's memory should retain epistemic sovereignty, because only its mnemonic frames preserve the original referential content of what actually occured. (4) Applied consistently, this means a community that remembers being victimized by a group the archives say never existed is expressing an untranslatable truth, not a false belief, and historians shouldn't "correct" them. (5) But that seems to license denying well-documented atrocities just because a local narrative conflicts with the record — which is obviously wrong.

I want to keep (1) and (3) if I can, since they capture something real about how testimony works, but I feel like something slips between (3) and (4), maybe a modal slide or an equivocation on "incommensurable." Which step should I reject, and what's the cost of giving it up? Does dropping (3) force me into some naive objectivism about archives, or can I keep the idea that memory and history are different kinds of knowledge without letting either be immune to correction?
