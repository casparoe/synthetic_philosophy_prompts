Write the opening remarks—approximately six minutes when delivered—for a three-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "Institutional Commitment and Verbal Obligation." The audience is split evenly: analytic philosophers working on normative ethics and philosophy of language sit on one side; contract lawyers and institutional designers, who think in terms of enforceability, organizational credibility, and structural incentives, sit on the other. You are the host and must welcome both groups without condescension.

Your remarks must do real philosophical work rather than serve as decoration. Frame the workshop around the question of why promises bind, but structure the speech around a specific conceptual tension that you should inject directly into the remarks: in institutional contexts, promises frequently exhibit what can be called a "displacement of the obligee." The individual speaker exercises a normative power—she creates a directed moral obligation—yet because she speaks in an institutional role, the resulting claim is structurally absorbed by the organization rather than held by the original human promisee. This produces a puzzle for any dyadic theory of promissory obligation: if the obligation is genuinely directed, there must be a specific obligee with standing, but the institution seems the wrong kind of entity to hold a moral claim, while the human promisee is left with only derivative or indirect standing.

Use this tension to govern what you can and cannot say. Do not reduce promises to contracts or to strategic commitment devices. Do not claim that institutional promises are unproblematic, and do not resolve the displacement puzzle for the room. Instead, show the legal and design audience why the philosophical framing carries practical weight: if displacement undermines the directedness of institutional promises, then organizational commitments—warranties, mission pledges, public guarantees—may invoke moral language without generating the corresponding moral obligations, with direct consequences for institutional legitimacy. At the same time, present the philosophers with a precise problem to carry into the sessions: can a normative-power account of promising be reconstructed for collective or role-based speech acts, or does it require an individual obligee that institutions cannot supply?

Keep the tone formal, polished, and appropriate to a workshop opening. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts that would require verification. The speech should stand alone and be fully intelligible to a listener with no access to outside sources.
