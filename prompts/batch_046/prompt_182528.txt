I serve on my university's Committee for General Education Reform. We must decide whether to approve a pilot program that restructures the core curriculum according to Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education, specifically his account of the rhythm of education—romance, precision, and generalization—interpreted not as stages within a single discipline but as institutional phases that every student cycles through repeatedly across all four years, with no fixed departmental distribution requirements and with courses organized by epistemic mode rather than by subject matter. Proponents argue that this avoids Whitehead's concern about inert knowledge—ideas that are not utilized or tested in fresh experience—by ensuring that disciplinary content is always encountered in the context of imaginative exploration, rigorous technique, and synthetic application. Opponents, including several department chairs, contend that this dissolves the disciplinary boundaries necessary for precision, risks reducing science and humanities to interchangeable modes, and misreads Whitehead as endorsing institutional chaos rather than pedagogical rhythm within stable fields. The proposal introduces a further element not found in standard readings: a synthesis commons in which students must complete an annual integrative project drawing on material from all three phases, assessed not by disciplinary experts but by interdisciplinary panels—an extension of the idea that generalization should be active and lived rather than abstract.

Please draft the committee's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—recommending a concrete course of action with a timeline for implementation or review. The memo should treat Whitehead's arguments seriously rather than using his name as ornament, engage the strongest objection from the disciplinary side (especially the concern that precision requires institutional stability), and honestly distinguish what is settled in Whitehead scholarship from what is genuinely contested—such as whether his rhythm describes cognitive development or prescribes institutional design, and whether his critique of inert ideas targets classroom pedagogy or disciplinary structure—without presenting a contested interpretation as the consensus among philosophers of education. Separate settled claims from contested ones clearly.
