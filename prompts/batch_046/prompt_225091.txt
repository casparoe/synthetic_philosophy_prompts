I am currently taking an introductory course on twentieth-century European history, and I find myself repeatedly returning to the same argument I had with a friend over dinner regarding Adolf Hitler's personal necessity to the rise of Nazi Germany. Not long ago I listened to a podcast about organizational systems that introduced the term equifinality—the notion that complex structures can arrive at nearly identical outcomes through entirely different initiating paths, much as separate rivers might converge on the same delta. Applying this idea to the interwar period, it seems to me that structural accounts of fascism imply something like a historical funnel: had Hitler perished during the 1923 putsch, the economic devastation of the Great Depression, the lingering resentment over the Treaty of Versailles, and the institutional fragility of the Weimar Republic would still have produced an essentially identical authoritarian state—complete with comparable antisemitic legislation and expansionist foreign policy—simply led by some other figure who occupied the structural opening. My friend objects that if the structural conditions alone guarantee that specific endpoint, then holding Hitler individually responsible becomes conceptually incoherent, since he collapses into a merely interchangeable placeholder for any ambitious nationalist who might have stepped forward.

I am trying to determine whether the Great Man thesis genuinely requires that, absent Hitler, there is no Nazi Germany at all, or whether one can consistently maintain that he altered the specific trajectory—perhaps accelerating rearmament or shaping the particular administrative form of the Holocaust—while still conceding that some variant of fascist dictatorship was structurally overdetermined. Moreover, if equifinality truly applies here, does the counterfactual question "would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?" rest on a false presupposition, namely that removing one individual can divert a system that is already locked into a predetermined equilibrium? I would value a response that disentangles the causal question from the moral one, and that clarifies whether structural explanation and individual accountability are genuinely incompatible rather than addressing different dimensions of the same events.
