I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on the ethics of post-conflict archiving, and I have been trying to translate the debate between Margalit’s ethics of remembering victims and Rieff’s argument that collective forgetting can be a moral precondition for social repair into something my colleagues in archival practice can actually use. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, explicit stopping conditions, and clear flags for where judgment is unavoidable—that a curator or digital archivist could run without philosophical training when deciding whether to actively preserve, actively suppress, or allow the degradation of traumatic documentary records that concern events more than two generations past but still shape present communal identity. The procedure must distinguish factual determinations, empirical claims that require verification, and irreducibly normative choices that must be escalated to a community ethics board rather than resolved by the archivist alone. To keep it from being a generic summary of the literature, I want it built around two non-standard framing concepts: mnemonic fatigue, meaning the non-linear degradation of a community’s capacity to integrate traumatic testimony as the density of archived material increases; and preservation asymmetry, the structural fact that digital storage eliminates natural decay, so that allowing forgetting now requires an active technical intervention rather than passive neglect. Please construct the procedure, then apply it to three cases: first, a digitized survivor-testimony database from a conflict that ended seventy years ago, where descendants report that mandatory educational use produces generational numbness rather than ethical insight; second, a proposal to actively suppress administrative records of a past regime’s bureaucratic violence, requested by survivors who argue the records perpetuate harm by preserving the perpetrators’ categories; and third, a case where the procedure yields an answer—likely active preservation—that the affected community finds morally intolerable, explaining exactly what the procedure is designed to do when its output conflicts with the community’s values. At each step of the procedure and in each application, mark whether the move is factual, empirical, or normative, and identify the precise point where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a person.
