Read Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day through the lens of whether white lies are wrong, framed specifically by the distinction between propositional transparency (the duty not to misrepresent states of affairs) and interactional fidelity (the duty to preserve the social conditions that make truthful exchange sustainable). Stevens never lies about facts—he reports Lord Darlington’s activities with precision, describes Miss Kenton’s departure accurately, and hides nothing that a direct question would expose—yet his indirectness, strategic omissions, and emotional suppression systematically reshape the moral reality of every encounter. Is he practicing a defensible form of interactional fidelity, protecting the dignity of service and the emotional space of others in a way that Dutch directness would destroy? Or is he committing a sustained white lie about his own commitments and about the moral character of the house he serves, using politeness as cover for complicity? Take a definite position—either that Stevens’s reserve is ethically superior to bluntness in this context, or that it is a moral failure masked as courtesy—and defend it by identifying which scenes are decisive: his concealment of the romance novel from Miss Kenton, his refusal to correct Lord Darlington’s political errors, and his final meeting with Miss Kenton (now Mrs. Benn) on the pier. Be explicit about where the novel resists your reading: does Ishiguro conflate Japanese indirectness with Western professional reserve, or does he keep them distinct? And does the text ultimately suggest that some forms of indirectness collapse into self-deception rather than courtesy, thereby adding something to the philosophical debate about white lies that a purely conceptual argument cannot capture? I want a committed interpretation, not a survey of options, with an honest account of where the work pushes back.
