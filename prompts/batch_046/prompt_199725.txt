My instructor emphasized that the principle sounds like a simple calibration rule but actually generates several unresolved tensions that pull in different directions. The four I've been able to identify are: first, how it interacts with Newcomb-style decision problems, where knowing the objective chance of an outcome seems to affect whether you should take the action that produces it; second, a worry associated with Humphreys' paradox about whether objective chances can genuinely be probabilities if they depend on background conditions in the wrong way; third, whether quantum mechanical probabilities should count as the kind of "objective chance" the principle requires, given that quantum mechanics does not always supply single definite values for measurements; and fourth, a newer applied tension about whether predictive algorithms and forecasting tournaments ought to calibrate their outputs to these objective base rates or instead weight them against their own learned epistemic states.

I'm writing a short research proposal that must select two or three of these lines of inquiry as genuinely upstream—meaning that resolving them would reshape how philosophers handle the remaining ones—and defend that ordering with explicit criteria. Could you rank these four tensions by philosophical importance for someone with about one semester to investigate, explain which are tractable through conceptual analysis alone versus which are stuck because they depend on prior answers in physics or metaphysics, and say what kind of argument or empirical result over the next few years would be enough to change your ranking? I do not need a bibliography or citation list; I need a defended agenda with reasoning I can use to structure my proposal.
