Two colleagues, Jordan and Riley, argued over lunch about whether constitutional entrenchment clauses are best understood as collective Ulysses contracts. Jordan began by claiming that a constitution with a supermajority amendment rule is analogous to Odysseus tying himself to the mast: the founding generation binds future citizens to resist impulsive changes, preserving long-term democratic stability against temporary majorities. Riley objected that the analogy collapses on the agency condition: Ulysses binds himself, maintaining personal identity across time, whereas a constitution binds strangers who never consented and share no unified will; at best it is dead-hand control disguised as rational self-binding. Jordan conceded the disanalogy in individual identity but proposed that institutional continuity creates a corporate agent across generations, so that amendment rules express the polity's enduring rational plan rather than external coercion. Riley then introduced a hypothetical twist: suppose a constitution contains a reflexive sunset clause that makes any entrenchment rule automatically null after exactly one century, unless renewed by a fresh supermajority. Jordan argued this preserves the Ulysses logic—binding future agents only until the Sirens of political passion have passed—while avoiding dead-hand tyranny. Riley countered that the sunset provision undermines the very idea of precommitment: if the rule can dictate its own dissolution, then future generations are not genuinely bound; they are merely following a temporary recommendation that expires by design, and honoring the expiration date is no more a precommitment success than ignoring it. Jordan replied that this misses the temporal structure of rational planning: a rational agent can bind herself for a fixed term and schedule release, just as a patient might authorize a physician to withhold treatment only for forty-eight hours. Riley answered that in the constitutional case there is no single patient; different generations have conflicting interests, and the release condition is interpreted by the very people it is meant to constrain, so the mechanism lacks the external constraint that makes Ulysses contracts work.
