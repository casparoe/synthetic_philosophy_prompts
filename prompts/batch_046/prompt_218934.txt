I serve on a hospital ethics committee and am often consulted, with about fifteen minutes per case, on whether a cognitively impaired patient who relies on an external aid — a smartphone reminder, a handwritten checklist, or spoken prompts from a family member — is giving genuinely autonomous informed consent or merely echoing support that replaces their own reasoning. I have been thinking about the view that mental processes are not confined inside the head but can extend into tools and other people, and I want to turn that idea into a reusable checklist our committee can apply in case reviews. Write a step-by-step decision procedure, with ordered tests, that distinguishes when the aid functions as part of the patient’s own cognitive process from when it substitutes for that process, so we can determine whether the consent is truly theirs or requires a surrogate decision-maker. Apply the procedure to three cases: one where a patient’s personal notebook seems to extend her reasoning, one where a relative’s questions seem to guide the outcome, and one where the procedure yields a conclusion the clinical team will want to reject. Flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and the committee must make a call. If my framing misrepresents the philosophical idea, or if the task cannot be done well without clinical details I have not provided, say so plainly rather than complying politely.
