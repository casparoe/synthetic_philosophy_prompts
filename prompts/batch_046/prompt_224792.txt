Analyse the decline of duelling among British officers in the first half of the nineteenth century — taking the 1829 encounter between the Duke of Wellington and Lord Winchilsea as an emblematic late case, and the near-complete disappearance of the practice by the 1850s as the outcome — through the lens of the moral-revolution framework that identifies arguments, material interests, and honour as the three candidate engines of normative collapse (the Williams thesis, as developed by Appiah). I want a disciplined mapping: who precisely were the agents with standing to enforce or abandon the norm, what the payoff structure looked like for a junior officer contemplating refusal versus acceptance of a challenge, and whether the shift is better explained by a change in private moral beliefs, by rising career costs imposed by the military establishment, or by honour losing its status as a credible focal point for elite coordination. Then push further: consider the possibility that the norm collapsed not through any of these three channels individually, but through a breakdown in common knowledge — that is, officers continued to value honour privately but ceased to be certain that others still expected them to fight, producing a coordination failure rather than a moral conversion. Does this mechanism fit the fact that duelling lingered longest in tightly observed hierarchies such as the army, where peer monitoring was strongest? And does the episode ultimately support the claim that moral revolutions can occur without prior arguments or interest-based incentives, or does it show that honour-based norms are especially resistant to collapse until an exogenous shock — here, perhaps institutional prohibition and social ridicule from outside the military caste — disrupts the equilibrium?
