I am writing an educational feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on how philosophy departments are redesigning assessments to test conceptual agility rather than canonical recall. For this piece, I am assembling sample exam materials from various disciplines. I would like you to draft one essay question for an upper-level seminar in philosophy of history and collective memory—the kind of course that moves quickly past the standard literature on social remembrance and asks students to think structurally about the authority of the past. The exam should be designed for a ninety-minute sitting, with answers expected to run roughly 1,200 words, and it should be impossible to answer well by merely summarizing familiar debates about collective memory versus historiography.

The question should center on a deliberately constructed tension: present students with a diaspora community whose commemorative rituals, oral genealogies, and identity-forming narratives are organized around an event that archival archaeology and diplomatic records place in a different century and geographic region. Ask them to adjudicate whether the community’s memory can claim what we might call mnemonic sovereignty—a normative entitlement to preserve its narrative unaltered by archival correction—without collapsing into an epistemic relativism that undermines all historical inquiry. Crucially, students should have to articulate whether there is a principled difference between correcting a factual error in the record and overwriting a community’s identity-constituting remembrance, and what conditions would have to be met for either form of authority to prevail. They should also be required to address whether archival evidence is ever self-interpreting, or whether its authority is itself institutionally mediated in ways that mirror the constructedness of oral tradition.

Please provide the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme built around six must-hit analytical moves. The scheme should assign specific point values totaling 25 marks, identify the precise conceptual error associated with each missed move, and include a one-line note on the typical student mistake for each criterion—for instance, conflating mnemonic sovereignty with mere emotional attachment, or treating the archival record as transparently authoritative rather than mediated. Do not include bibliographic prompts, citation requirements, or requests for further reading lists in the exam instructions; the assessment must be answerable by a well-prepared student working entirely without external sources.
