I'm preparing a seminar handout on how people actually apply the inside-view/outside-view distinction, and I'm struck by how quickly the conversation collapses into slogans—"be humble," "trust your reasoning," "use base rates"—without anyone specifying what exactly is being weighted against what. To force the issue, I want a decision-tree taxonomy that starts from a concrete case and branches only on commitments that genuinely separate positions, not on the labels people prefer. Here is the scenario: you have just completed a long, technically involved argument that reaches a conclusion sharply at odds with the expert consensus in your field; you also know that you have made subtle errors in past arguments of comparable length, though you cannot currently locate one in this present case.

Build the tree as a flow of diagnostic questions that someone could answer without knowing which philosophical label each answer corresponds to. The first node should ask whether you update on the conflict with consensus primarily by downgrading your confidence in the conclusion itself, in your confidence that you have correctly evaluated the reasoning process, or in both—because I think these come apart, but I want your judgment on whether that split is stable or collapses under pressure. From there, branch on: whether the outside view applies symmetrically across all domains or is suspended for first-person access to one's own mental states; whether self-trust is calibrated globally as a personality trait or locally to specific cognitive tasks; and whether there is a principled exception for "object-level" beliefs as distinct from meta-level calibration beliefs. Each leaf should be a named position—include at least the pure inside-view rationalist, the strict outside-view base-rater, the hierarchical Bayesian who treats self-location as evidence, the modesty-as-correction-factor view, and one coherent but rarely defended combination that you think occupies an empty quadrant of this logical space. For any combination of answers that yields no named position, diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Construct the tree from the logical space first—define the branches by the combinations of answers they allow—then map the actual positions onto that space, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate rather than papering over them. Take a committed, definite stance: don't present the framework as one neutral option among others, but defend which branches you think are real fault lines and which distinctions survive scrutiny. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want an instrument usable by someone unfamiliar with the literature, and I want your own reasoned judgment on where the taxonomy holds and where it breaks down.
