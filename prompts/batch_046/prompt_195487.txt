I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a close friend who insisted that "Gen Z" is nothing more than young people being young and that generational labels are pure marketing fiction designed to sell consulting reports. I want to push back harder, but I also think the standard sociological defenses miss the mechanism entirely. Next Thursday I am presenting a twenty-minute position paper to a mixed graduate seminar in sociology and philosophy of science—about eight people, including two quantitative demographers who treat birth-cohort primarily as a statistical nuisance variable to be controlled away, three cultural sociologists who study generational identity as a genuine social formation with causal weight, a philosopher of science who works on natural kinds and is deeply skeptical of anything that smells like essentialism, and a labor economist who cares mainly about entry-cohort effects on lifetime wages. The room is skeptical of grand narratives but respects precise mechanisms, and several members have already published on cohort analysis.

My thesis is that generations—"Boomer," "Millennial," "Gen Z"—are neither natural kinds nor pure social constructions. They are what I call synchronized shock cohorts: temporary alignments that only become causally and ontologically weighty when three conditions coincide. First, a demographic bulge that creates institutional bottlenecks. Second, a shared gate into adulthood—labor-market entry, standardized testing, or conscription—that forces simultaneous life-course adjustment across the bulge. Third, a narrative industry that converts statistical correlation into identity. Gen Z is not a timeless kind with an enduring essence; it is specifically the cohort that entered adulthood during the convergence of pandemic labor-market disruption and algorithmic media saturation. To treat Gen Z as possessing an enduring generational nature is to commit a reification error analogous to treating "the unemployed" as a personality type rather than a position within an economic cycle. I want to defend this as a middle path: it takes the demographers' statistical caution seriously, explains why the cultural sociologists are tracking something real, but denies that what they are tracking is a kind.

Prepare me for the Q&A as a prep sheet, not an abstract defense. Order the objections as they will actually come: start with the demographer's statistical objection that cohort effects disappear once you control for age, period, and macro conditions; then the philosopher of science's critique that my "three conditions" are just a redescription of correlation without carving nature at its joints; then the cultural sociologist's pushback that I am dissolving lived, embodied experience into an abstract mechanism; then the labor economist's point that entry-cohort wage effects are already well-modeled without any novel ontology; and finally the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely whether my framework can actually distinguish Gen Z from Millennials in any non-arbitrary way, or whether it collapses all generational difference into pure period effects. For each objection, phrase it the way that specific faction would phrase it, tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold firm, give me a concise reply in the vocabulary this room uses, and flag the weakest point in that reply. Be committed: do not merely lay out options and leave me to choose. Tell me which concessions are strategically necessary to survive the room, which lines I should not cross if I want the thesis to remain intact, and where my position is genuinely vulnerable rather than merely unpopular. I want to walk out knowing exactly where I am soft and exactly where I am standing.
