I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on population ethics and intergenerational obligation, and over the course of the semester I've developed a somewhat heterodox stance on the nonidentity problem that I would like stress-tested. Rather than adopting Derek Parfit's familiar no-difference conclusion or a conventional person-affecting restriction, I now defend what I call a structural-occupancy view: the wrong of a reckless reproductive or climate policy is not that it harms specific, identifiable future persons—since substituting one policy for another changes who comes to exist—but that it degrades the modal conditions of the social roles that any future occupant must inhabit. On my account, we wrong not particular individuals but the generic institutional and ecological frameworks within which agency is exercised; this allows us to condemn reckless policies without needing a victim who is made worse off, and it aims to preserve strong obligations to the future without collapsing into an impersonal total-utilitarian calculus that ignores the separateness of persons.

What I need is an honest map of how exposed this position actually is. Specifically, I want to know: which observations, conceptual arguments, or results in adjacent fields—such as the metaphysics of social roles, institutional persistence, or personal identity—would count decisively against it, as opposed to being merely awkward? Does my view risk reintroducing a hidden person-affecting harm by treating role conditions as proxy victims, and if so, is that a fatal collapse or a repairable tension? Furthermore, what would committed defenders of rival positions—whether Parfit-style impersonalists, actualist person-affecters, or contractualists about future generations—concede as genuinely damaging to my view, and what would they dismiss as failing to grasp the structural shift? I'd also like to know whether there is any development—empirical, formal, or intuitive—that would cause this view to collapse into one of its rivals rather than remain independently stable. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, and be willing to conclude that the position is either deeply vulnerable or, conversely, well insulated in ways that might themselves be philosophically problematic.
