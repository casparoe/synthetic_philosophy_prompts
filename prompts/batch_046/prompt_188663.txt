I'm delivering the opening remarks—about six minutes—at a workshop titled "The Ballot and the Conscience," co-hosted by a philosophy department and a local civic-engagement collective, and the room is genuinely split: moral and political philosophers on one side, organizers and activists on the other. Rather than rehearse the familiar standoff between a consequentialist duty to vote and an expressive right to abstain, I want the remarks to be structured around a framing I've been working with that I'm calling the residue-void asymmetry. The core idea is that when a voter supports a morally deficient but less harmful candidate—the classic lesser-evil scenario—the wrongness endorsed does not dissolve into the calculus of harm prevented; it leaves a kind of moral residue that attaches not as ordinary guilt but as a standing debt to one's own normative commitments. Abstaining avoids that residue, yet it does not restore neutrality; instead it opens an expressive void, a failure to register one's values in the public record. I want the speech to open with a concrete, grounded case—imagine a voter who backed a flawed front-runner in a primary solely to block a worse outcome, then reaches the general election feeling that her earlier choice has already compromised her capacity to vote with integrity—before asking whether any institutional mechanism, such as ranked-choice voting, a binding "none of the above" option, or mandatory disclosure of strategic intent, could actually resolve the asymmetry, or whether it is a permanent structural feature of democratic participation under imperfection. The remarks must do real work for both audiences: for the activists, they should function as a serious acknowledgment that strategic voting is not a clean hands problem; for the philosophers, they should set up the workshop's papers by showing precisely why neither pure consequentialism nor pure expressive theory can absorb the residue without losing something essential. Please write the remarks so they avoid resolving the tension, avoid lecturing, and leave the room with the sense that the philosophers and the organizers need each other to think through what voting actually costs the voter.
