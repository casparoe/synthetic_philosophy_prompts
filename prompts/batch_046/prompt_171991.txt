Im trying to understand a pasage from Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, Book IV ch. 7, where he compares Greek and Roman colonies. He writes that in the Greek model, "The colony settled its own form of government, enacted its own laws, elected its own magistrates, and made peace or war with its neighbors as an independent State, which had no occasion to wait for the approbation or consent of the mother city." Then he contrasts this with Rome, where colonies were "at best but a sort of corporation, which, though it had the power of enacting bylaws for its own government, was at all times subject to the correction, jurisdiction, and legislative authority of the mother city." He also throws in an etymological point: "The Latin word (Colonia) signifies simply a plantation. The Greek word (αποικια), on the contrary, signifies a separation of dwelling, a departure from home, a going out of the house."

My question is whether this comparison is mainly doing political-philosophy work — like, setting up a standard for what a legitimate imperial relationship should look like — or whether it's actually an economic argument disguised as philology. Specifically, Smith notes that Roman colonies were partly a safety valve for landless citizens who couldn't compete with slave labor, and he seems to suggest that the British colonies in America are being treated like Roman plantations (subordinate, extractive) rather than Greek emancipated partners. Is he implicitly saying that the Navigation Acts recreate the Roman "corporation" model, where the colony exists to absorb surplus capital and population for the mother country's benefit? Or am I overreading the economic subtext? Also, does his etymolgy actually carry any argumentative weight, or is it just rhetorical ornament? I'd like help parsing the logic without just getting a summary — I'm especially interested in whether there's a hidden structural point about how legal subordination locks in extractive relationships over time.
