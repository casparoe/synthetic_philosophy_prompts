I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has begun studying philosophy of religion and the philosophy of science with some seriousness. I should like you to analyse a particular historical episode through the lens of a philosophical theory, but I want the analysis to carry one conceptual twist drawn from scientific practice.

The episode is A. J. Ayer's cardiac arrest in 1988 and the brief account he subsequently published of the experiences he underwent—references to intense visual phenomena and to an encounter with a being he interpreted as holding cosmic authority. The theory is his own verificationist criterion of literal significance, developed in his early work, according to which statements about survival after death are cognitively meaningless because they cannot be empirically verified.

Please analyse the episode through that theoretical lens, treating the mapping with discipline: identify the relevant players (Ayer as patient, as observer, and as theorist), specify what counts as evidence under the criterion, and determine whether the case confirms, contradicts, or falls outside the theory's scope. In particular, I want you to attend to a problem familiar from experimental science: a first-person report that is singular, non-reproducible, and generated by an apparatus—the human subject—whose theoretical commitments are already known.

Address the following points in your analysis:

- How the verificationist criterion classifies the content of Ayer's report: does it render the statement that he experienced such phenomena meaningless, or only the metaphysical interpretation that those phenomena indicate real survival?
- Whether the theory can distinguish between reportability and reality in this singular case, and why that distinction either collapses or holds firm here.
- What the episode reveals about applying a semantic criterion designed for public, intersubjective language to private, terminal experiences.
- One aspect of the episode that verificationism illuminates well, and one aspect it misdescribes or leaves entirely silent.
- How a scientist should treat this report in comparison with a single anomalous instrument reading in a non-replicable experiment—should prior theoretical commitment discount the data, or should singular data be permitted to overturn the framework?

Please write in British English. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I shall add those myself. Do not invoke obscure secondary debates I am unlikely to know, and do not force the theory to speak where it offers no clear verdict.
