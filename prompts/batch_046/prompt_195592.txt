The question of when a legend becomes history has stalled for more than a century in the three canonical cases—the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus—where maximalists cite archaeological correlations or later textual echoes as proof of a kernel, and minimalists treat the lack of contemporary documentation as fatal, yet neither camp ever revises its core stance. Diagnose this impasse: is it a substantive disagreement about evidentiary thresholds, a verbal dispute about what the term history must entail—an identifiable agent, a datable occurrence, a verifiable causal chain—or are the sides actually pursuing three different inquiries—whether a specific named event occurred, whether a narrative tradition encodes a collective memory of social transformation, and whether the story functions institutionally to justify present arrangements—while calling them all legend versus history? Ground your diagnosis in how each side actually treats Troy, Arthur, and the Exodus, and specify whether progress would require a conceptual taxonomy that separates these questions, a particular kind of archaeological or textual discovery, or whether no empirical finding should be expected to resolve what is ultimately a normative disagreement about how a culture relates to its past.
