I'm preparing an advanced module for my graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and language, and I want an exercise that moves well beyond the textbook Twin Earth vignettes my students have already encountered in every introductory survey. Please construct a realistic, non-science-fictional thought experiment—no duplicate planets, no demon manipulators, no perfect predictors—engineered to precise specifications: it should involve a working scientist, such as a marine biologist or a clinical pathologist, operating in an ordinary professional context.

Design the case so that semantic externalism and conceptual-role semantics agree on the referent of the scientist's central mental representation, but disagree on the propositional content she expresses when she sincerely asserts something about that referent. The divergence must turn on a niche or recently revised classificatory distinction—perhaps a cryptic species pair separated only by molecular barcoding, or a newly updated nosological category adopted by her institution—that she has not fully internalized but defers to an expert community to apply. Hold her internal conceptual network constant while her external social-linguistic environment shifts subtly and realistically, for instance through an institutional taxonomy update she has not explicitly registered. Present the scenario in no more than two concise paragraphs, then demonstrate precisely why each theory assigns the same object but different truth-conditions to her utterance. Identify the structural feature—likely involving epistemic deference together with a specific form of the division of linguistic labor—that forces the divergence, and explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed, such as if she were an isolated expert with no deference relations at all. Finally, present the attack that a conceptual-role semanticist would mount against your interpretation, noting exactly where that reply is weakest. Write your answer in American English, avoid any bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from real texts.
