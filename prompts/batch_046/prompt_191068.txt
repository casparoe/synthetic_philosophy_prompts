Write an essay on whether simultaneous discoveries—calculus by Newton and Leibniz, evolutionary theory by Darwin and Wallace—are structurally inevitable, and whether it matters who makes them. Contrast the inevitability thesis, which holds that once the conceptual prerequisites are in place the underlying result is essentially forced, with a view we might call attribution consequentialism: the claim that the discoverer's identity, formal apparatus, and institutional position causally shape notation, funding priorities, philosophical framing, and downstream research trajectories, so that priority is not merely honorific. In particular, examine whether the persistence of Leibnizian notation on the continent versus Newtonian fluxions in Britain, and the divergent receptions of Wallace's brief essay versus Darwin's extensive monograph, are best explained by social path-dependence or by intrinsic differences in cognitive accessibility and evidentiary completeness. Weigh whether these path-dependent effects are large enough to undermine the intuition that science is essentially anonymous. Reach and justify a definite conclusion—either that discovery is essentially interchangeable and priority disputes are social fictions, or that attribution carries substantial epistemic and institutional weight. Conclude with a single-line verdict in the form Verdict: [your conclusion].
