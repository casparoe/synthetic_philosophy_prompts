In discussions of Franklin’s plan for moral perfection, commentators often analogize his chart of thirteen virtues—tracked weekly with marks for failures—to modern quantified-self practices: OKR dashboards, habit-tracking apps, or behavioral KPIs. The analogy is used to cast Franklin as a proto-utilitarian self-optimizer, to suggest that moral growth is essentially metrics with religious window-dressing, and to license the conclusion that his project needs no metaphysical commitment to virtue as cultivated character. Audit the analogy: provide the correspondence table (what plays the role of the KPI, the sprint review, the accountability mechanism, the long-term strategic goal); then identify the disanalogies, especially those involving the teleological framing of virtue, the social and theological dimensions of his “project,” and the difference between fault-avoidance and positive disposition-building. Separate the breaks that undermine the secular-optimization reading from the ones that do not. Finally, say whether the conclusion that Franklin was running an essentially modern productivity protocol survives once the mapping is corrected, and suggest a better analogy if there is one.
