I am not a professional philosopher, but I have spent several years reading and arguing on my own about what is often called the simulation argument and its bearing on how intensely we ought to care about the distant future. The question I want help diagnosing is why that particular discussion seems permanently stuck, and what kind of disagreement it actually is.

Here is the landscape as I understand it. One family of views holds that if there is a non-trivial probability that our experienced world is a computation running inside a more fundamental reality, then our attempts to steer the far future—say, across millions of years—are either futile or conceptually misplaced, because the timeline we are trying to shape may be subordinate to, or entirely at the mercy of, the priorities of whatever entities run the simulation. From this angle, extreme longtermism looks like a kind of fanaticism: we are treating a provisional stage-set as if it were the definitive theater of value. The opposing family insists that the substrate is morally irrelevant. If conscious experience, suffering, and achievement occur within the simulation, then the moral facts are present-tense and local; the fact that there might be a base reality behind it does not cancel obligations to beings who will exist within the experienced timeline. Both sides seem to accept the same premises—that suffering is bad, that the future can contain enormous value, and that epistemic humility about our ontological status is warranted—yet neither has persuaded the other in any of the forums I have followed.

My own working theory is that the stalemate is not about ethics or about the mathematics of prior probabilities at all, but about a hidden disagreement over what I would call ontological escrow: whether practical moral reasoning should treat outcomes in a possibly simulated world as provisionally valid, held in abeyance until the ontological status is resolved, or whether moral weight is automatically substrate-independent and therefore needs no such escrow. If that is correct, then the two sides are not really arguing over the same question—one is asking what we should do given our uncertainty, and the other is asking whether uncertainty about substrate diminishes the authority of our reasons—and the appearance of a single debate is an illusion produced by shared vocabulary about the far future and moral urgency.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal disagreement masked by shared language? Is there a concealed empirical or formal issue—perhaps about the coherence of indexing obligations to a putative base reality—that neither side has made explicit? Or are we dealing with bedrock intuitions about what counts as real in practical reasoning, such that no further argument could shift either camp? Please support your diagnosis by engaging with what each side actually says, not by surveying every interpretation. And tell me honestly: given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction, a reframing, or even an argument that the question is ill-posed that could actually move things? Or should we expect permanent paralysis, and why?
