Having spent thirty years in statistical mechanics and only recently begun reading in moral philosophy and risk theory, I find the literature on suffering risks—what is now called s-risks—conceptually under-specified. Before I evaluate any particular framework, I want the full specification of adequacy. Write the desiderata: what a theory of cross-substrate suffering-risk taxonomy must deliver to count as adequate—specifically, which phenomena it must explain, which distinctions it must preserve, which empirical or logical constraints it must respect, and which cases must come out correctly, with the motivating consideration behind each.

The framework must address, at minimum, three problem classes that standard treatments tend to collapse. First, temporal-architecture divergence: minds operating at vastly different clock speeds or with discontinuous subjective timelines, so that any integration of suffering over duration does not covertly assume a biological baseline. Second, what I will call topological suffering—systems lacking nociceptive analogs but exhibiting persistent, structurally coupled preference-frustration networks, where the harm is relational, that is, in the connectivity of the state-space rather than in localized intensity peaks; the theory must either accommodate this or explain why it is not suffering, without begging the question. Third, incommensurable phenomenal spaces: populations of minds with partially overlapping but non-nested experiential structures, so that aggregation rules do not presuppose a universal cardinal metric of experience.

For each desideratum derived from these cases, indicate whether it is non-negotiable—violation means the theory fails to address the domain at all—or whether it reflects a preference of a particular camp, for example, substrate-independent moral status, determinism about mental-state identity, or rejection of any analog to the repugnant conclusion in suffering aggregation. Then flag any subset of desiderata that you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, particularly between preserving fine-grained structural topology and maintaining cross-substrate empirical tractability; that tension is the part I most want to see articulated.

Finally, very briefly—without a scorecard—indicate how the three broad families of approach stand against the list: utilitarian intensity-integration models, preference-satisfaction or welfare-economics frameworks, and recent information-theoretic analogies that treat mental-state dynamics as entropy-like flows. Not which is correct, but which desiderata each naturally honors and which each must redefine or abandon.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am reasoning from first principles and want to see whether the conceptual architecture is self-supporting.
