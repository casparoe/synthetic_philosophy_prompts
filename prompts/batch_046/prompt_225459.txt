I've been working through care ethics not as a supplement to liberal moral theory but as a genuine alternative that reconceives moral agency as constituted by vulnerability and sustained interdependence rather than by autonomy and contract. The position I want to defend is this: moral obligations arise primarily within asymmetrical relationships of dependency and responsive attention—what I would call "attentive love" understood not as an affective state but as a practical orientation of sustained responsiveness—and therefore institutional design (in healthcare, elder care, or welfare allocation) should be judged not by rights-compliance or aggregate welfare but by whether it preserves and strengthens the "networks of dependency and response" through which care actually flows. This means rejecting both the universalist claim that every person holds equal moral standing independent of relational proximity and the utilitarian claim that relationships are only instrumentally valuable for well-being; it also implies that a policy benefiting strangers at the cost of disrupting local care networks could be morally worse than a less efficient policy that sustains them. I take this to resolve the traditional tension between partiality and impartiality not by balancing them but by showing that impartial metrics already presuppose a false picture of the moral subject as an unencumbered chooser.
