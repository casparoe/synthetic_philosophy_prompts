Take the institutional turn in American psychology away from introspection—starting with Watson's founding behaviorist manifesto and running through Skinner's radical program in the middle of the last century—and examine it as a practical response to the paradox of phenomenal judgment that follows from epiphenomenalism about conscious experience. The paradox, as I understand it, runs like this: if my feeling of pain does not cause my judgment that I am in pain—if only some underlying neural event does—then the judgment seems causally detached from the very experience it reports. I want you to apply this tension to the historical actors. Did Watson and Skinner reject first-person mental reports because they recognized, however implicitly, that phenomenal judgments lack the causal connection required for scientific knowledge? Or did they reject them for purely methodological reasons—say, demands for public observability—making their position independent of the paradox? Be careful to say exactly what each figure permitted or banned: for instance, whether Skinner's later allowance for internal, unobservable states counts as a concession to phenomenal judgment or merely as talk about verbal behavior shaped by environment. Then tell me where the theory is silent: does behaviorism solve the paradox by eliminating the mental cause altogether, ignore it by changing the subject to stimulus and response, or transform it into a problem about the reliability of verbal reports rather than about consciousness? Finally, consider what the later cognitive revolution's reintroduction of internal mental representations does to the analysis—does it restore the causal link that epiphenomenalism denies, or does it just relocate the paradox inside the mechanism that produces the judgment? I am not looking for citations or a reading list; I want a disciplined conceptual mapping that runs both ways, with an honest account of where the philosophical lens distorts the history and where the history tests the philosophy.
