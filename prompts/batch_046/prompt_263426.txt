You are advising the ethics committee of a robotics firm that has deployed a multi-agent system in which each agent maintains an internal model not only of the environment but of the other agents' representational states, updating these models through negotiation protocols rather than direct observation of code or weights. The lead engineer argues that because the system's predictive accuracy improves specifically when it treats other agents as belief-holders rather than as mechanical state-transitions, Dennett's intentional stance is empirically vindicated—not merely a heuristic but a framework we should apply symmetrically, meaning we ought to attribute genuine beliefs to the system itself. A committee member counters that predictive success is insufficient without the evolutionary or social embedding that makes human belief attribution unavoidable. The committee asks whether the engineer's argument collapses the distinction between adopting the stance instrumentally and being the kind of entity to which the stance properly applies. Analyze the situation by distinguishing predictive utility, interpretive charity, and ontological commitment, and say whether the asymmetry between attributing beliefs to humans and to this system is defensible. Do not treat the answer as a list of Dennett's conditions; work through the concrete structure of the negotiation protocol and what it actually requires of the agents involved.
