Write an essay on the following controversy: according to Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self as a strange loop sustained by narrative patterns, could a single "I" genuinely persist across two physically separated brains that share no direct communication but each harbor an incomplete, partially overlapping subset of the original self-model? Weigh the case for distributed persistence—namely, that if the self is fundamentally a pattern rather than a substrate-bound entity, then fragmentation across brains is no more problematic than the persistence of software across servers—against the case that narrative integration requires a unified locus of self-reference, so that two partial copies constitute not one distributed subject but two degraded replicas lacking a shared first-person perspective. Reach and defend a specific verdict: either that distributed persistence is coherent but only if a particular condition (which you should specify) is met, or that the proposal collapses into a category error about personal identity because it severs the loop's essential self-referential unity. In your argument, consider whether Hofstadter's emphasis on the loop's internal coherence commits him to rejecting distributed selves, or whether his pattern-based ontology naturally extends to them, and explain what practical or phenomenological difference, if any, would remain between a distributed self and two independent minds that merely share memories.
