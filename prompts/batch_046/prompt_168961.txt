I have been reading a popular essay that summarises A. J. Ayer's attitude towards survival after death, and I should like an accuracy audit before I rely on it in a seminar paper. The author presents Ayer as holding that the proposition 'human consciousness survives bodily death' is straightforwardly false, on the ground that no living observer can verify the experiential states of a corpse, and therefore the claim violates the verification principle by lacking any connection to possible sensory reports. According to the summary, Ayer applied this principle without qualification: any statement that cannot be backed by direct observation is not merely unsupported but literally meaningless, which is why he classed religious hope for an afterlife alongside metaphysical assertions about an absolute reality. The essay further claims that after Ayer suffered a cardiac arrest in 1988 and subsequently reported visions of a red light together with a feeling of timeless presence, he treated this episode as empirical data that temporarily suspended his earlier scepticism, ultimately allowing that consciousness might persist in some attenuated form, though he retained his general hostility to organised religion. The author concludes that Ayer's position is best understood as an extreme form of evidentialism, according to which the absence of post-mortem testimony renders belief in survival irrational rather than merely unverifiable.

Some of this rings true, particularly the link between logical positivism and the dismissal of religious language, yet I am uncertain whether the summary respects important distinctions in Ayer's actual arguments. I wonder, for instance, whether he really intended 'false' rather than a stronger claim about cognitive meaninglessness, whether his verification principle was applied so uniformly across metaphysical and empirical domains, and whether the account of his 1988 experience and its philosophical aftermath is faithful or an over-interpretation. There is also a potential tension that the summary does not address: if Ayer's verificationism treats first-person mental reports with suspicion unless they are publicly checkable, how could he legitimately appeal to his own private near-death experience as relevant data without undermining his own framework?

Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence, indicating which parts a careful reader of Ayer's early logical-positivist writings and his later personal reflections would accept, which are standard simplifications that obscure necessary distinctions, and which are outright misrepresentations? Please also tell me whether the author's concluding characterisation as evidentialism survives correction of the summary, and whether the tension I have noted between verificationist strictures and first-person reporting is genuine or only apparent.
