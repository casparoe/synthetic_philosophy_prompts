I serve on the editorial board of an interdisciplinary journal that regularly receives submissions attempting to derive ethical norms, policy prescriptions, or institutional duties from purely descriptive findings in evolutionary biology, neuroscience, or cultural anthropology. I need a rigorous, reusable decision procedure—something a reviewer can run in about fifteen minutes without consulting outside literature—that determines whether a given manuscript argument commits the naturalistic fallacy or illicitly crosses the is-ought gap, and that distinguishes such errors from legitimate cases in which empirical findings properly inform, but do not alone entail, a normative conclusion. Structure the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests or questions, with explicit stopping conditions: a definite finding that no fallacy is committed, a definite finding that one is committed, and an intermediate zone where only human judgment can settle the matter. The procedure must specifically handle two common maneuvers: straightforward derivations from natural prevalence or evolutionary origin (e.g., “this behavior is widespread across species, therefore it ought to be encouraged”), and evolutionary debunking arguments that use descriptive origins to undermine a moral intuition’s authority (e.g., “this intuition evolved for kin selection, therefore it carries no normative weight”). It should also address arguments that invoke mechanistic descriptions—such as identified neural correlates—to justify practical prescriptions about education, medicine, or social design.

Please apply the procedure to two concrete manuscript claims. First: an argument that because altruistic cooperation has deep evolutionary roots in social mammals, democratic societies have a positive duty to institutionalize cooperative structures at the expense of competitive ones. Second: an argument that because specific neural pathways correlate with empathetic response, school curricula ought to be redesigned specifically to strengthen those pathways, with the implicit premise that enhancing the neural substrate automatically justifies the pedagogical goal. For each case, walk through the procedure step by step, state where it lands, and flag the precise step—if any—where judgment is unavoidable rather than procedural. Ensure that at least one case produces a result that conflicts with the intuitive moral appeal of the argument itself. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: for example, whether it can adjudicate between moral realists and anti-realists, whether it can evaluate thick ethical concepts that already embed normative content, and whether it can distinguish an illicit derivation from a valid reflective-equilibrium move that uses empirical data to revise a prior normative belief. Mark every point where the reviewer must make a substantive value judgment that no checklist can eliminate.
