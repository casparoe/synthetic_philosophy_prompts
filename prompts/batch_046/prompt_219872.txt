I'm designing a handout for a philosophy of mathematics seminar on actual infinity. Please construct a decision tree that sorts positions by concrete commitments rather than by labels. The root asks whether one accepts a completed, definite totality of all natural numbers. The next branch asks whether Hilbert's hotel is best read as coherent reasoning about actual infinite sets or as reasoning that smuggles potential-infinity assumptions under false pretenses. From there, branch on the part-whole principle: is it abandoned only for infinite cases, abandoned altogether, or retained as a strict constraint that rules out actual infinities? The leaves should include Cantorian actualism, Aristotelian potentialism, Hilbert-style formalism (treating infinity as a regulative idea without ontological commitment), strict finitism (every magnitude has a determinate finite size, with no largest number required), and moderate finitism (unbounded processes are real, but no completed infinite totalities exist). For any answer-combination that lands in an unoccupied cell, label it 'unoccupied' and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate—say, if two positions share all answers and differ only in metaphysical motivation. Every node must be phrased as a direct yes/no or either/or question about a case or principle, so that a student can answer without knowing the position names; never ask 'are you a finitist?'
