The student submitted the following answer:

The modal rationalist thesis holds that if a state of affairs is ideally conceivable—representable without contradiction by an epistemically perfect agent—then it is metaphysically possible, unless an a posteriori necessary identity blocks it. Weak conceivability is merely the absence of an explicit contradiction in one's representation; strong conceivability involves a fully detailed, positively coherent mental model, such as conceiving a universe with different gravitational constants.

The circularity worry arises because specifying the "ideal agent" requires knowing all truths that are not modal, yet drawing the boundary between modal and non-modal truths seems to require prior modal knowledge. Hume anticipated this difficulty in his distinction between relations of ideas and matters of fact: he argued that experience might reveal necessary connections among matters of fact that reason alone cannot detect, which is essentially the exception for a posteriori necessary identities. Thus Hume's framework already contains the resources to block circularity, since the agent need only grasp relations of ideas, leaving modal discoveries to empirical inquiry.

However, this solution creates a new problem. If every case where conceivability and possibility diverge can be explained by a hidden necessary identity discovered empirically, then the principle becomes empirically unfalsifiable and therefore useless as a philosophical guide. For example, philosophical zombies appear strongly conceivable—there is no internal contradiction in representing conscious beings lacking consciousness—yet if consciousness is necessarily identical to some physical process, the rationalist invokes the exception. Because we can always postulate such an identity for any apparently conceivable but impossible scenario, the principle tells us nothing about possibility that empirical science has not already settled.

Furthermore, weak conceivability is clearly insufficient, since one can weakly conceive of 2+2=5 without detecting its arithmetical impossibility. Strong conceivability is meant to remedy this by requiring full representational detail, but it is unclear how an agent achieves this detail without already possessing modal knowledge about which representations are genuinely coherent. In the end, the principle survives only in domains like pure geometry, where no hidden empirical identities threaten, but its broader metaphysical ambitions are undermined.
