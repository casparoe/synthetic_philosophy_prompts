A political economist applies a consistent framework drawn from John Stuart Mill's late writings—especially his essays on socialism and the later editions of Principles of Political Economy—to judge institutional proposals. Her track record: she endorses worker cooperatives competing in open markets with labor-based profit distribution; she approves state ownership of railways and telegraphs as natural monopolies but rejects extending it to general industry; she supports steep inheritance limitation coupled with unrestricted capitalist enterprise; she rejects Fourierist phalanxes for suppressing individual initiative despite their cooperative structure; and she rejects state-mandated job assignment with need-based wages, favoring market coordination. Now consider a proposal in which all firms must be worker-cooperatives with no capitalist employment permitted, capital passes only through a public reinvestment trust rather than family inheritance, and prices are set by a decentralized algorithm using real-time labor and ecological data rather than by exchange—though individuals remain free to move between cooperatives. Predict her verdict if she aims to stay consistent with her prior rulings. Identify the two or three competing principles in her framework—such as the necessity of market competition for efficiency and liberty, the moral priority of association over wage-labor, and the value of experimental diversity—that could pull in different directions here. Say which prior case is the closest analog, whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine this case, what principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict, and specify the single institutional modification that would best reveal which principle actually governs her judgments.
