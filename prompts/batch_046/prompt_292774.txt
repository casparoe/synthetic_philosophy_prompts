Please construct a philosophical argument for the following position: local conventions governing gratuity in service interactions, orderly queuing, and pedestrian compliance with traffic signals generate genuine moral obligations for temporary visitors, but only insofar as those conventions function primarily as harm-preventing coordination mechanisms; where they function purely as rituals of social belonging or deference to contingent local hierarchy, no corresponding moral duty arises for outsiders. Then defend the further claim that this distinction is practically unstable, because any convention that successfully coordinates behaviour simultaneously acquires a membership-signalling function, and any convention that signals membership must be observed consistently enough to preserve its coordinating efficacy. Conclude that visitors therefore face an irreducible moral ambiguity rather than a determinable duty, and specify what form of practical reasoning—if any—can legitimately guide action under that ambiguity. Proceed conceptually, without relying on empirical studies of visitor behaviour, statutory law, or appeals to particular named ethical theories.
