For my senior philosophy seminar, I am drafting an adversarial-collaboration statement between two camps in the debate over judging historical figures—specifically Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus—and the validity of the “man of his time” defense. One camp, the Presentist Critics, holds that moral evaluation should apply contemporary standards and that invoking historical context to mitigate blame for grave wrongs—slaveholding, imperial violence, colonial genocide—functions as an unacceptable excuse rather than a genuine explanation. The other camp, the Contextualist Defenders, holds that fair judgment requires situating agents within the moral and epistemic frameworks available to them, and that applying present norms without this contextualization produces anachronistic assessments that confuse explanation with excuse.

Write the joint statement both camps could sign. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered section of agreed claims about the relevant historical record (for instance, Jefferson’s slaveholding alongside his theoretical anti-slavery writings, Churchill’s imperial policies alongside his leadership against Nazi genocide, and Columbus’s initiation of colonization with its catastrophic effects on Indigenous populations), about the logical distinction between explaining behavior and excusing it, and about what each camp takes moral progress to entail. Second, a disagreements table that states each camp’s precise position in wording its own members would accept—especially on whether being embedded in a particular normative environment reduces culpability or merely accounts for it, and on whether internal moral conflict within an agent intensifies hypocrisy or demonstrates limited but real moral capacity. Third, identify the cruxes: whether moral progress is linear enough to license cross-temporal ranking; whether consistency across moral domains indicates a unified framework or selective reasoning; and whether recognizing that an agent could not have accessed certain moral concepts changes the verdict on blameworthiness. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record each side’s predicted assessment of that case in advance.

Please avoid bibliographies, citation requests, or appeals to sources that would require verification. To keep the debate from collapsing into familiar introductory talking points, inject the conceptual distinction between diachronic moral luck—being formed within a normative environment one did not choose—and synchronic moral capacity—the ability to critically transcend that environment using reasoning available at the time. Require both camps to state whether resolving this distinction in either direction would alter their position on Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus, and to say exactly why. Do not quote any specific text verbatim; paraphrase where needed. End with a brief note on whether any part of the disagreement appears to be verbal rather than substantive, identifying which part and giving the reason.
