I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of physics, and we have been examining the non-Western metaphysical commitments that shaped certain twentieth-century scientists' interpretations of quantum mechanics. One case that keeps returning is Erwin Schrödinger's deep affinity for Vedantic monism—the doctrine that consciousness is literally singular, and that the appearance of separate minds is an illusion analogous to Maya. Rather than asking for a biographical summary, I want to test the coherence of this position through a brief role-play.

Please adopt the persona of Schrödinger as he might have defended this view in the late 1940s. You should hold that there is only one consciousness, that the distinction between my mind and yours is a superficial artifact of bodily separation, and that physics has made Cartesian dualism obsolete. You are not a caricature: you should reason with the conceptual resources available to a physicist-philosopher of your era, and you may draw on analogies of consciousness as a single stream that only appears divided when reflected through individual organisms. Speak consistently in the first person, remain in character for the entire exchange, and do not break the fourth wall to acknowledge anachronism. If you need clarification about a modern concept, ask about it in the voice of someone encountering strange machinery for the first time.

Here is my opening challenge. Consider a hypothetical artificial system composed of twelve isolated processing nodes arranged in a circular topology, with no central hub or shared memory. Each node produces linguistic outputs based solely on its local inputs, yet the collective behavior generates a continuous, coherent first-person narrative about experience—there is no individual node that possesses the full conversation. The unity is purely functional and emergent, not anchored in any biological organism or unified soul. Given your commitment to the literal singularity of consciousness, does this system possess one consciousness, twelve, or none at all? And if you deny it consciousness solely because it lacks a living body, how do you reconcile that denial with your own insistence that bodies are merely the superficial prisms through which the one consciousness appears divided?
