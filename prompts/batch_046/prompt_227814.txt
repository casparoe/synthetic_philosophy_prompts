Next Thursday I will present my thesis at our school’s interdisciplinary philosophy symposium, which draws roughly thirty students from the debate and ethics clubs, plus a visiting professor from the state university whose research centers on practical reason and value comparison. I am a high school junior, and the position I intend to defend is this: philosophers discussing incommensurable goods typically treat both values as sitting at the same logical level—liberty against security, excellence against equity—so that the question becomes whether they can be mapped onto a shared scale or declared “on a par.” I want to argue that there exists a structurally distinct case I call asymmetrical incommensurability, in which one value does not merely resist weighing against another but functions as a meta-framework that governs what counts as a legitimate comparison at all. My concrete institutional case is our school’s current dispute over scholarship allocation: one faction frames the issue as optimizing achievement through competitive merit, the other as honoring unconditional dignity through need-blind equity. I claim that describing this conflict as a standard hard choice already smuggles in the merit-based framework, since “merit” itself defines the metric by which any comparison proceeds; thus the clash is not between two competing goods but between a substantive value and the very conditions of valuation that would make comparison possible.

The audience will include committed consequentialists who believe any apparent incommensurability dissolves under a sufficiently refined utility calculus; a deontologist who accepts that dignity is incomparable but will suspect I have mislocated the conflict rather than solved it; a pragmatist who treats my distinction as a redescription of practical deadlock in metaphysical dress; and the visiting professor, whom I know has engaged closely with Ruth Chang’s parity thesis and will immediately press whether my asymmetrical case is genuinely separate from parity or merely parity relabeled. I need the objections ordered approximately as they will arise—likely the consequentialists first, then the Kantian, then the pragmatist, with the professor’s challenge reserved for the close—phrased in the idiom each camp actually uses, together with what I should concede without collapsing my view, what I should hold firm, and especially the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook: namely, that I have not yet offered a clear decision procedure for identifying when a value is genuinely meta-governing rather than just another competing good, and that an opponent could plausibly argue the need-blind framework is equally meta-governing in the opposite direction. Please also identify the objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises—that if asymmetrical incommensurability is real, then democratic deliberation about shared institutions becomes impossible in principle—and sketch how I might reply within the two minutes allotted for rebuttal.
