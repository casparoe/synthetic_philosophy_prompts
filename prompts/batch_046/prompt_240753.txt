I'm trying to operationalize the philosophy of joint action for a practical mediation setting, and I need a reusable decision procedure I can actually run without reaching for a library. I work as a community mediator who gets called in when groups claim they're acting together but members dispute whether there's real collective commitment—think spontaneous mutual-aid networks, distributed open-source contributions, or workplace teams with misaligned incentives. Write a step-by-step procedure, expressed as ordered questions with explicit stopping rules, that takes me from an observed group behavior to a determination: is this a joint action in any philosophically serious sense, or just an aggregate of individual actions that happen to align? The procedure must respect the actual distinctions between collective intentionality built on shared mental content, shared cooperative activity built on interlocking plans and mutual responsiveness, and plural-subject conditions built on joint commitment that binds members even against individual preference—but it should never ask me to cite authors, check a bibliography, or look up a specific theory. Structure it so that each decision node has a clear yes/no/maybe pathway, mark the points where the answer depends on normative judgment rather than observable fact, and include at least one stopping rule for each of the three frameworks where the case fails that particular conception of jointness. Then apply the procedure to two concrete cases: first, a dozen strangers who independently show up to clear snow from a blocked neighborhood road after a storm, with no prior agreement and no designated leader; second, a product team at a firm where every engineer is individually incentivized by a performance bonus tied to shipping features, and they coordinate only through a project-management tool with no shared deliberation. For each case, walk through the steps in order, show where the procedure lands, and identify the single step where a reasonable mediator could legitimately disagree. Finally, state clearly what this procedure is designed to leave unresolved—what kinds of disputes about collective agency fall outside its scope and must be handled by direct negotiation rather than by the framework. Keep the tone precise and practical; I'm looking for something I could actually print and use in a session.
