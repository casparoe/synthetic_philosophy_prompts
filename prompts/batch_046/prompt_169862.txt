I'm working through the literature on collective intentionality and joint action for a seminar paper, and I've become fixated on a tension I haven't seen addressed directly. Michael Bratman's planning account treats shared cooperative activity as built from interlocking individual intentions, sub-plans, and conditions of mutual responsiveness, which makes divergence relatively tractable: if one agent updates her private plan, the shared activity is typically renegotiated through explicit or implicit coordination. Margaret Gilbert's plural-subject account, by contrast, holds that joint commitment creates a normative unit that does not depend on psychological fusion, identical mental content, or shared representations across members. I want to understand what happens—cognitively and structurally—when this purely normative unity encounters internal interpretive divergence.

Consider a scenario I'll call interpretive drift: two colleagues jointly commit to presenting their research together at a conference, but over time one privately comes to understand this commitment as requiring co-authored slides, while the other understands it as delivering alternating sections with separately authored material. Because Gilbert denies that the plural subject requires shared mental representations or a collective mind, there appears to be no internal belief-fixing mechanism—no distributed memory, no group-level theory-of-mind module, no collective Bayesian updating—to detect that the members are now operating under incompatible construals of the very same commitment. This leads me to ask: if collective intentionality is genuinely irreducible to individual mental states, must we posit some hidden cognitive architecture—perhaps a socially distributed procedure for resolving divergence analogous to individual belief revision—to preserve the coherence of the plural subject? Or does the absence of such architecture show that plural subjects simply lack the epistemic unity we associate with intentional agents, and that divergence is resolved purely through normative rebuke and obligation rather than any form of internal updating?

Please push back plainly if my framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if I'm wrongly treating Gilbert's plural subject as a kind of group mind that requires internal coherence mechanisms, or if I'm misrepresenting the conditions under which Bratman's shared intentions can survive plan revision. I'm not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want conceptual clarification on whether the tension I've described is genuine or an artifact of conflating normative structure with cognitive architecture. How confident should I be that collective intentionality can be fully analyzed without eventually positing some distributed mechanism for resolving interpretive drift within the plural subject?
