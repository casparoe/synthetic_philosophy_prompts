After studying Bertrand's paradox in my philosophy of probability seminar, I am trying to understand why the principle of indifference survives at all rather than being discarded as incoherent. The standard chord problem appears to demonstrate that applying uniform indifference across different continuous parameterizations—radial distance, angular position, and midpoint selection—produces incompatible probabilities, specifically 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4, for the very same event of a chord exceeding the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. If all three parameterizations are equally valid expressions of 'no reason to prefer one outcome over another,' then the principle seems to assign contradictory probabilities to an identical proposition within a single reasoning framework, not merely to generate ambiguity across separate models. Since rational belief formation is generally governed by consistency requirements that forbid assigning different probabilities to the same proposition given the same evidence, shouldn't the principle of indifference be abandoned rather than restricted? Or is there a non-arbitrary, physically grounded criterion that privileges one parameterization—such as the symmetry of the generating process—without rendering the defense of indifference circular?
