The debate over whether self-deception is genuinely possible has stalled, at least in the circles I read, despite broad agreement on the phenomena: people avoid threatening evidence, construct post-hoc rationalizations, and sincerely report motives that conflict with their behavior. One camp—the partitionists—holds that self-deception is real but requires a divided architecture: one subsystem knows the truth and actively conceals it from another, so that the subject genuinely believes the falsehood while some part of her does not. They point to cases of sustained “double bookkeeping,” where a person acts consistently on a desire she explicitly denies, and argue that mere selective attention cannot explain the coherence and persistence of the false belief. The other camp—the uniformists—denies that any internal deceiver is needed; they treat self-deception as motivated belief-formation without prior possession of the truth, often emphasizing that subpersonal mechanisms can filter information without anyone “knowing” anything in the relevant sense. They cite the same behavioral data but insist that attributing knowledge to a “part” of the mind is a reification, and that what looks like hidden awareness is actually ambiguous or unformed mental content that gets retrospectively organized.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical—both sides accept the same findings on selective exposure and rationalization—but partly verbal about whether “deception” requires an intentional agent and partly substantive about which mental system counts as “the self” being deceived: the diachronic narrative self, the momentary cognitive system, or a subpersonal module. I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says about cases, not a survey of the literature. Specifically, consider a niche case I keep returning to: retroactive self-deception, where a subject forms a false belief about her own past mental state—say, “I always knew I was unsuited for that career”—when the past state was genuinely ambiguous or unformed, not actively suppressed. The partitionist seems forced to posit a prior true belief that was hidden, yet there may have been none; the uniformist can absorb it easily, but then risks making “self-deception” indistinguishable from ordinary confabulation. Is the disagreement here about cognitive architecture, about the conditions for being a “self” that can be deceived, or about the standards for attributing belief? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate—a distinction between diachronic and synchronic deception, a formal model of belief fixation that renders the partition redundant, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because “the self” is not a single target of deception? Or should we expect no progress, and why?
