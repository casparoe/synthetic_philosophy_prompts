A recent essay summarized Kierkegaard's account of Abraham in Fear and Trembling in the following way. Abraham, as the knight of faith, stands in an absolute relation to the absolute, which requires him to suspend the ethical—the universal moral law—not merely in this particular case but as a general structure of religious existence. Because God issues a direct command to sacrifice Isaac, Abraham treats his paternal duty as null and void; the religious telos of obeying the divine will functions as an instrumental justification that makes ethical norms permanently inapplicable whenever they conflict with revelation. The essay describes this as the teleological suspension of the ethical in its strict sense: the end of religious obedience justifies discarding the ethical means. It further claims that Abraham remains silent before Isaac and Sarah because he recognizes his act as guilty and indefensible by universal reason, and that the absurd here is the flat logical contradiction that God simultaneously commands Abraham to love Isaac and to destroy him, which faith embraces without mediation. Finally, the essay suggests this structure mirrors the political-theological notion of sovereign exception, in which law is suspended to preserve the legal order, except that Kierkegaard applies it to the individual soul rather than to the state.
