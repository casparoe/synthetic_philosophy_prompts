There's a language-certification board that decides whether someone is fluent not through grammar drills or vocabulary quizzes, but by placing candidates in practical situations—ordering in a café, settling a misunderstanding, explaining a joke—and judging whether they navigate each one successfully. The board's handbook explicitly rejects the idea that fluency has some essential core; instead it describes competence as a network of overlapping abilities, much like the family-resemblance view of concepts. Yet every applicant must also pass a standardized written exam that tests explicit knowledge of grammatical rules and a fixed list of essential vocabulary words. I am puzzled by how these two parts fit together. What view of language, meaning, or competence does this institutional practice actually presuppose? Does the practical evaluation commit them to the idea that meaning is use, while the written exam quietly reintroduces an essentialist picture? Can the two parts be philosophically consistent, or do they depend on incompatible theories? And if the board genuinely adopted the family-resemblance view throughout, what would have to change in how they design their tests?
