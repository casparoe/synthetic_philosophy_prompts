You are writing the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Vasquez, who is stepping down after thirty years directing an international emergency medical relief organization. The audience is split evenly between academic moral philosophers—several of whom have published on the demandingness of morality—and her family, board members, and field staff who have no background in ethics. The toast should last about five minutes, honor Elena without sentimental falsehood, and do real philosophical work rather than decorate the occasion with vague references to virtue or sacrifice.

Structure the speech around the demandingness objection to consequentialism, but do not treat it as the familiar complaint that morality asks us to surrender too much wealth or comfort. Instead, build the toast around a sharper, less-discussed distinction: between resource-demandingness (the standard claim that consequentialism requires excessive material sacrifice) and cognitive-demandingness (the claim that it demands a perpetual state of moral accounting that colonizes mental life, leaving no protected space for non-instrumental attachment, rest, or private grief). Frame Elena’s career as a sustained answer to the second problem. She accepted extreme resource-sacrifice—years in conflict zones, personal risk, institutional austerity—without allowing that sacrifice to metastasize into an all-consuming mental calculus. Introduce, as the conceptual backbone of the speech, the idea of role-bounded consequentialism: the view that one can honor radically demanding moral obligations within a clearly defined institutional role, while deliberately insulating other domains—family rituals, friendship, unmonitored leisure—from constant utilitarian weighing. The philosophers in the room should recognize this as a serious structural response to concerns about mental costs and moral residue in the demandingness literature; the non-philosophers should grasp it through concrete, narrated moments from her life, such as her refusal to discuss casualty statistics at her daughter’s wedding, or her practice of leaving unread field reports on Sunday mornings.

The speech must land as affection, not lecture. It should acknowledge that a pure form of consequentialism might condemn her Sunday-morning boundaries as moral failures, yet argue—through her example—that a life of extreme moral commitment can be sustained precisely because it is bounded. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Avoid direct quotations from philosophers; paraphrase any ideas you invoke. The closing should return to the occasion with a toast that celebrates not only what she gave away, but what she protected.
