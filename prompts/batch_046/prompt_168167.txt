After thirty years in experimental physics—mostly calibrating measurement systems and thinking about error propagation—I have recently begun reading welfare economics and found myself constructing an argument about safe Pareto improvements that now seems to collapse into contradiction. I present it in numbered steps and ask where, precisely, it breaks.

(1) Define a policy change as a safe Pareto improvement (SPI) if it satisfies three conditions: no affected party has lower expected welfare; at least one party has strictly higher expected welfare; and the judgment is robust across all reasonable epistemic models of the outcome distribution, so there is a built-in safety margin against hidden harm.

(2) Inaction in a real social context is never a neutral baseline; it is an active trajectory with its own distributional consequences, including opportunity costs and continued harms that affect different parties.

(3) Therefore, whenever an available action A meets conditions (i) through (iii) relative to inaction, inaction itself fails to be an SPI relative to A.

(4) If rational choice requires adopting an SPI whenever one is available, then rationality requires adopting A over inaction.

(5) Now apply a distinction familiar from statistical physics but rarely used in normative theory: ensemble safety, in which a distribution of outcomes contains no harmful outliers, is not the same as trajectory safety, in which every individual path through the system avoids harm. In complex social systems, policies are non-ergodic in this respect—ensemble averages do not determine individual trajectories.

(6) Because of interaction effects—resource competition, behavioral feedback, and institutional crowding—there will always exist multiple available actions, each an SPI relative to inaction taken alone, but jointly incompatible so that implementing any pair violates condition (iii) for at least one.

(7) The rationality rule in (4) applies individually to each available SPI.

(8) Hence rationality requires implementing all available SPIs simultaneously.

(9) By (6), this is impossible.

(10) The natural retreat is to select the best available SPI, but ranking them requires an interpersonal comparison of welfare gains that the SPI framework was explicitly designed to avoid; it supplies only an ordinal judgment of not-being-worse-off.

(11) Consequently, the SPI criterion either demands the impossible or silently imports the very cardinal comparison principle it claims to dispense with.

If I must preserve something, I would rather keep (1) and (4) than (10), but I want to know the price. Give me a committed diagnosis: identify the specific step or hidden assumption that actually fails, explain why it looked plausible—particularly whether the error is an equivocation between ensemble and trajectory safety, an aggregation mistake between (4) and (8), or a mischaracterization of inaction in (2)—and defend your choice. Do not merely list options; tell me which premise to reject and whether saving the concept of safe Pareto improvement requires abandoning its claim to avoid interpersonal comparison, or whether the flaw lies elsewhere entirely.
