A colleague who studies debate-based oversight for superhuman AI systems sketched the following argument, which she calls the distributive-obfuscation collapse. She defines distributive obfuscation not as deception but as a rhetorical-structural technique: the premises that jointly entail a false conclusion are scattered across a long chain of individually valid but irrelevant intermediate inferences, separated by more steps than a human judge's working-memory span—roughly four non-contiguous premises—can hold in simultaneous evaluation. The critical conjunction therefore never appears locally suspicious. She then argues:

(1) In any debate protocol, the judge must assess logical relations between premises and conclusions, not merely local coherence or rhetorical force.

(2) For any claim C in a domain where a system is genuinely superhuman, there exists a distributively obfuscated argument against C whose length is comparable to the correct argument for C, with the decisive premises separated beyond the judge's working-memory span.

(3) A human judge can reliably reject an argument only if she can hold its critical premises in simultaneous evaluation; if they exceed her span, evaluation collapses into heuristic guessing guided by local plausibility.

(4) Therefore, in any superhuman debate, the incorrect side can always construct an argument that the judge is cognitively incapable of rejecting.

(5) If the incorrect side can always present an unrejectable argument, then the presence of a correct opposing argument does not increase the judge's confidence in the correct position beyond her prior belief.

(6) But debate is epistemically valuable only if it can shift the judge's credence away from her prior in the correct direction.

(7) Hence, debate provides no epistemic value in superhuman domains.

(8) Consequently, debate-based oversight adds no safety margin over direct, unmediated reliance on the system's outputs—and, by parity of reasoning, peer review and expert deliberation in complex sciences suffer the same collapse.

She wants to preserve (1), (2), and the intuition that cognitive limits matter, but she finds (8) intolerable because it undermines every complex institution of knowledge. Please adjudicate the chain: identify the step that either contains a subtle invalidity—perhaps an equivocation, a scope shift, or a modal slide—or relies on the least defensible premise; explain why that step seemed plausible; and specify what abandoning it costs for the broader skepticism about debate-based safety.
