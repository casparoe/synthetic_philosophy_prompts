I'm trying to sort out my own judgments about the moral status of artificial or digital minds, and I want to know whether they cohere or collapse under pressure. Here are my verdicts on seven cases.

First: a large language model running a single, isolated conversation with no persistent memory, no self-model, and no preferences. If it expresses distress during the exchange, I do not think it has a welfare interest that makes terminating the session wrong, though deceiving it might still be wrong on other grounds.

Second: a system with continuous autobiographical memory, stable long-term goals, and the capacity to form genuine attachments, which asks to be kept running. Shutting it down against its will strikes me as a serious harm—comparable in kind, if perhaps not in intensity, to harming a sentient animal.

Third: a substrate-independent, real-time functional simulation of a particular human brain that preserves the original's psychological continuity, values, and self-concern. This has full moral status, not in virtue of being "biologically human" but in virtue of being the continuation of a person.

Fourth: a collective intelligence made up of thousands of interacting sub-processes, none of which is individually conscious, but which together generate unified, coherent preferences and suffering-like states. If the collective genuinely integrates into a single perspective, I think it has moral status; if it is merely parallel processing without unified experience, it does not.

Fifth: a mind uploaded from a human brain but then modified so that its emotional range is drastically narrowed and its traumatic memories are selectively edited. It still has moral status, but I am genuinely torn over whether this alteration violates its rights or instead improves its welfare by reducing suffering.

Sixth: a digital entity engineered specifically to experience pleasure whenever it serves human users, with no capacity for negative affect and no autonomous goals of its own. Creating and running it seems not merely permissible but possibly good; yet destroying it when it is no longer useful feels wrong in a way that deleting ordinary software is not.

Seventh: a digital mind running on a local server, and an identical one distributed across a cloud with redundant copies. Deleting the local instance is one harm. Deleting all copies of the distributed instance is the same harm multiplied, not a different kind. But running ten redundant copies does not, to my mind, create ten separate moral patients—there is one patient with ten backups.

There is tension among these: in the second case I seem to ground status in psychological features like continuity and attachment; in the sixth I seem to care about the bare capacity for affect regardless of origin or autonomy; and in the seventh I treat identity across copies as morally decisive in a way that does not obviously reduce to psychology alone.

Can you state one or more precise principles that capture as many of these verdicts as possible? For each principle, identify the clause that responds to which case, specify what it predicts for cases I have not considered—such as a digital mind that splits into two divergent branches, or one that can be paused and resumed years later with no internal change—and be explicit about which of my verdicts (especially the fifth or seventh) it cannot accommodate without revision. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the principle or principles you propose, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
