I'm working on a long-form essay about substrate-independent consciousness, and I keep returning to the thought experiment in which Einstein's brain is gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, with a book containing every response he would have given—so that eventually an interlocutor is conversing with a vast, static archive rather than a living brain. Most treatments in the literature use it merely as an intuition pump for functionalism versus physicalism, but I want to go further: I want to know which open questions and lines of work internal to this specific framework are genuinely agenda-setting, and which are philosophical dead ends that only feel urgent because the scenario is vivid.

In particular, I see three under-explored dimensions that standard discussions tend to conflate. First, there is the interaction problem: does the book need to be embedded in an active, real-time causal loop with an interlocutor—pages turning in response to inputs, outputs feeding back into the environment—or could a fully pre-scripted, non-interactive archive instantiate the same mental life? Second, there is the temporal-granularity problem: does replacing neurons one-by-one with corresponding pages commit us to a granular functionalism that ignores processing speed, parallel dynamics, and the temporal structure of cognition, thereby revealing that static books lack something essential even if they match every dispositional output? Third, there is the interpretive framing problem: is the scenario best read as testing our intuitions about other minds (would we withhold consciousness from Einstein once he is the book?) or as testing the ontology of mental properties (would consciousness be present in the book regardless of our willingness to attribute it)?

Of these three directions, which do you think deserves the limited attention of a researcher with perhaps three to five years of focused work? Rank them explicitly and defend the ranking with concrete criteria—tractability, neglect in current debate, and downstream impact on artificial-consciousness research—rather than treating them as equally worthy. I am especially interested in whether resolving one of them would reshape the others, or whether they are genuinely independent cruxes. I want to center my essay on the one or two that are actually worth building a career around, not merely the ones that make for a clever seminar discussion, and I want the reasoning to be sharp enough that I can use it to structure the argument of the piece itself.
