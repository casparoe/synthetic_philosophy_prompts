You are a professional philosopher of history and museum-studies advisor. Think carefully, begin with brief brainstorming about which theoretical distinctions must survive operationalization and which can be safely dropped, then build the tool.

I am a senior curator at a regional heritage museum. About once a month I must finalize public-facing interpretation—exhibit labels, audio-tour scripts, or digital panels—for a local site or event where residents’ living collective memory conflicts sharply with the documentary, archaeological, or administrative record. The stakes are concrete: misrepresenting either side erodes community trust or undermines institutional credibility. I need a reusable decision procedure, not one-off advice.

The principle to operationalize is this: collective memory and critical history serve different functions—memory sustains identity and moral continuity, history corrects distortion and distributes accountability—so neither should receive blanket priority. Resolution should depend on structured weighing of three factors: testimonial continuity (the coherence and intergenerational transmission of the community’s account), material corroboration (what artifacts, physical traces, and non-adversarial records actually support), and institutional accountability (whether the archival record was produced by actors with an interest in obscuring harm or shifting blame).

Turn this into a checklist or ordered flowchart in words that I can run in roughly thirty minutes per case without outside legal or archival expertise. It must include explicit decision nodes in sequence, with pass/fail/ambiguous criteria at each. Clearly label every point where judgment cannot be eliminated—do not disguise these as mechanical steps. Identify at least two failure modes where the procedure is likely to misfire or deliver an answer I will dislike. Then apply it to three hypothetical cases: (1) residents remember a demolished neighborhood as a thriving cultural hub, while city planning archives describe it as a blighted slum slated for necessary renewal; (2) an indigenous group claims continuous ceremonial use of a riverbank for centuries, but archaeological strata and colonial land-grant documents show intermittent non-ceremonial use and legal expropriation; (3) a factory-workers’ family association insists a 1970s closure was malicious sabotage by distant owners, while corporate minutes and economic data frame it as unavoidable bankruptcy. For case (2) or (3), the procedure should yield a conclusion that conflicts with what a sympathetic curator might initially prefer, and you should defend why the procedure still holds. The deliverable must not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; work only with the framework defined above. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and state what the procedure cannot do—particularly whether it can resolve conflicts that are fundamentally about moral recognition rather than factual discrepancy.
