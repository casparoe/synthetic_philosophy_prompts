I'm running a small readin group for people who aren't acadamics — mostly friends from a local book club — and next month I want to do a session on why the nation isn't as old as people think. I'm planning to draw on Hobsbawm's idea of invented traditions and Anderson's imagined communities, but I don't want to just lecture about France in 1789 or whatever. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

Specifically, I want the group to come away understanding that there's a real difference between Hobsbawm's top-down fabrication of rituals and symbols and Anderson's point about anonymous simultaneity through print capitalism — they keep collapsing the two into people made up countries. So I need the order of ideas: what has to land first before they can even see why the distinction matters, and what example should carry the whole session. I'm thinking of using Belgium's 19th-century construction of a unified past from Flemish and Walloon fragments, but I need to know what that example has to include and what it must avoid, like making it sound like Belgium was just a lie told by kings. I also need to map the misconceptions in sequence: they'll come in thinking nations are either eternal tribes or pure modern fictions, then they'll think imagined means fake, then they'll slide into thinking that if it's invented it's illegitimate. I need to know exactly where in the arc to head each off, including the one my friend raised — if nations are invented then my national identity is just a story I tell myself — which I want defused before it sticks, and I want them to keep the two mechanisms seperate in thier heads.

Also, what's the check I can do at the end to see if they've actually grasped the conceptual split rather than just repeating slogans? And what should I openly say I'm leaving out at this level — maybe the full debate over whether print capitalism actually caused nationalism, or the critiques of Anderson's Eurocentrism? I want to be honest about the omissions without opening a rabbit hole.
