After a long argument with a friend about whether a political leader who orders an atrocity-preventing strike that kills innocent civilians can ever be said to have acted without leaving a genuine moral stain, I find us trapped in a loop that feels increasingly structural rather than factual. We share every premise: the civilian deaths are certain, the prevented atrocity is vastly worse, democratic authorization is genuine, and the leader is not a sociopath. Yet my friend treats the remaining discomfort—the sense that something wrong has still been done—as purely psychological residue, a failure to internalize that full justification erases wrongdoing. I keep trying to locate a normative remainder that is not just guilt, but every formulation I offer either collapses back into psychology or appeals to an absolute constraint that seems to dissolve the moment we grant the necessity defense. We have been rotating through the same hypothetical cases for months without movement. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this disagreement substantive—about whether institutional authority can function as a moral transformer that changes the ontology of the act, rather than merely licensing it—or is it verbal, with me using wrong to mean unavoidable moral cost and my friend using wrong to mean unjustified? Could a hidden empirical disagreement be doing the work, perhaps about how moral agents actually experience role-based authorization? Or are we answering different questions under one heading, one about the conditions of justification and one about the conditions of moral purity? Given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a conceptual distinction between moral residue and moral cleansing, a redesigned thought experiment that isolates institutional role from personal authorship, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned?
