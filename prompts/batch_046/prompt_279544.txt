At a recent philosophy of science panel I covered for my column, two scholars—Dr. Lena Voss and Prof. Marcus Reed—debated Kant's treatment of animals in the Metaphysics of Morals and the Lectures on Ethics. Voss argued that the standard indirect-duty reading is incoherent: if cruelty to animals is wrong solely because it hardens human beings toward one another, then the wrongness depends on contingent psychological causation, yet Kant presents the prohibition as categorical. She claimed that Kant's comparison of animals to machines in the Groundwork is typically misinterpreted; read in context, it establishes functional analogy rather than metaphysical exclusion, which implies that suffering in analogous systems generates direct, though imperfect, moral duties. Reed responded that Voss imports modern functionalism into Kant's mechanistic biology. He insisted that the machine comparison explicitly denies self-consciousness and rational autonomy—the conditions for being a moral patient—and that the Metaphysics of Morals carefully distinguishes duties with regard to animals from duties to them, a grammatical and philosophical separation that undermines her direct-duty claim. He added that if Kant truly recognized direct moral consideration, he would not have restricted moral status so sharply to rational nature. Both acknowledged that Kant rejects Cartesian mechanism, but they split over whether this rejection opens space for direct consideration or merely complicates the indirect-duty account. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess the strength of Voss's coherence objection, Reed's grammatical and metaphysical defense, and their competing readings of analogical reasoning in Kant's system. Then say who is closer to Kant's actual position and identify what both overlook about the relation between indirect duty and analogical reasoning.
