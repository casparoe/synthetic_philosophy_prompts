Exam question:
Consider the case of Dr. Reyes, who checks a digital thermometer with an intermittent calibration fault. By pure coincidence, the fault causes the device to display "38.9°C" for a particular patient, and that reading is exactly the patient's actual temperature. Dr. Reyes reads the display, justifiably believes the patient has a fever of 38.9°C, and is correct. Analyze whether Dr. Reyes knows the patient's temperature. In your answer, address (i) whether this is a genuine Gettier case and why, (ii) how the "no false lemmas" condition applies here, and (iii) whether a modal safety condition resolves the intuition that knowledge is lacking.

Student answer:
This is clearly a Gettier-style counterexample to the justified-true-belief analysis. Dr. Reyes is justified in trusting a medical instrument, her belief that the temperature is 38.9°C is true, yet she doesn't really know because her justification is only accidentally connected to the truth—the thermometer is broken. This is exactly what Gettier demonstrated in 1963: JTB is not sufficient. In that same paper, Gettier proposed the "no false lemmas" remedy, which requires that none of the premises in the subject's reasoning be false. Here, Dr. Reyes's reasoning includes the implicit premise that the thermometer is functioning properly, which is false, so the remedy withholds knowledge.

Next, consider modal conditions. The relevant-alternatives framework, which Gettier used to formalize his cases, asks whether we can rule out nearby possibilities in which the belief is false. Because the calibration fault could just as easily have produced a reading of 36.5°C, those alternatives are not eliminated, so knowledge fails.

Finally, regarding virtue epistemology: Sosa's safety condition says a belief is knowledge only if it wouldn't easily have been false. In this case, since the patient's actual temperature is biologically stable, in nearby worlds she still has 38.9°C. So the belief is safe with respect to the world, which suggests knowledge is present—contradicting our initial intuition. I think this shows that safety looks at the wrong thing: it tracks the stability of the fact rather than the reliability of the cognitive process.
