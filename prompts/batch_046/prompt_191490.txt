After a long, somewhat draining argument with a friend who studies sociology and who kept reducing Imre Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes to nothing more than Popperian falsificationism wearing a historical costume, I want to design a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy-of-science first-years who have met Popper's basic account but never worked through what makes a research programme structurally different from a sequence of conjectures and refutations. I need a teaching plan rather than lecture notes: the exact dependency order in which the components must be introduced—hard core, protective belt, negative heuristic, positive heuristic, and the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts—so that the protective belt is grasped as an architectural feature of rational inquiry rather than an immunizing stratagem, and so that students see why a programme can be rational to pursue even while it is degenerating. For the carrying example, I want one concrete historical episode other than the tired Newton-versus-Ptolemy comparison, preferably one in which the same programme moves through both an apparently ad hoc rescue of its hard core and a later empirically progressive adjustment; the episode must make visible both the methodological immunity of the hard core and the directive force of the positive heuristic, and it should be chosen so that students cannot resolve the case by simple hindsight.

I also need the misconception map: the Popper-derived assumptions they will predictably import—that any refusal to abandon a central claim in the face of anomaly is irrational, that auxiliary hypotheses are always epistemically vicious, and that theoretical progress is identical to empirical progress—together with the precise point in the explanatory sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and how. Include a check for understanding that tests whether they have grasped Lakatos's technical taxonomy of ad hoc hypotheses—distinguishing moves that are ad hoc in the ad hoc₁, ad hoc₂, and ad hoc₃ senses—by presenting them with a hypothetical auxiliary adjustment in a scenario where the long-run empirical outcome is unknown, asking them to classify the move and defend whether it renders the programme degenerating, rather than asking them to recite definitions. Finally, specify what should be explicitly bracketed at this level—perhaps Lakatos's normative sociology of science, the full formal taxonomy in exhaustive detail, and any strong claim that the methodology definitively solves the demarcation problem—and indicate how I should signal that omission openly without making it appear that I am hiding difficulties from them.
