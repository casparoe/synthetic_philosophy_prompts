I'm working through my own intuitions about the boundary between life and death, and I want to see whether they hang together under one principle or whether some have to be sacrificed. Below are six concrete scenarios, each with my gut verdict. Some feel straightforward, but at least two pairs pull in opposite directions, and one involves a kind of integration that looks like life yet feels like death. I'd like you to propose the best-capturing principle—or a small, explicit family of candidates—state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, predict what it says about the further case described at the end, and be honest about which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate without ad hoc adjustments.

Cases and verdicts:

1. Whole-brain death with mechanical support. A 35-year-old is declared dead by whole-brain criteria: no brainstem reflexes, no spontaneous respiration, no cerebral perfusion. Ventilation and vasopressors maintain heartbeat, warm skin, and normal color. The family is told she is legally and medically dead. Verdict: she is dead; the heartbeat is a technologically sustained artifact, not the life of a person.

2. Permanent vegetative state. A 50-year-old has been in a permanent vegetative state for five years after trauma. The brainstem is intact: he breathes spontaneously, has sleep-wake cycles, digests food, heals wounds, and maintains homeostasis. There is no awareness, no command-following, and no prospect of recovery. Verdict: he is alive.

3. Unresuscitated circulatory arrest. A 70-year-old suffers cardiac arrest. After ten minutes with no pulse and no breathing, resuscitation is not attempted. At the cellular level, scattered neurons may show brief residual electrical activity for a few minutes. Verdict: he is dead.

4. Deep accidental hypothermia, full recovery. A hiker is found with a core temperature near 18°C and no detectable heartbeat or brain activity for six hours. She is gradually rewarmed and makes a complete neurological recovery with no deficit. Verdict: during the suspended interval she was not dead; her vital functions were reversibly arrested rather than irreversibly lost.

5. Brain-dead pregnancy maintained to term. A pregnant woman is declared brain-dead (whole brain, including brainstem) at 20 weeks gestation. She is maintained on intensive support for three months. Her body regulates hormones, her hair and nails grow, she mounts immune responses, and she delivers a healthy infant by Caesarean section. Verdict: she remains dead throughout; the gestation is sustained by technology and tissue-level processes, not by her integrated life.

6. Decapitated body with artificial perfusion. In a hypothetical research protocol, a human body is decapitated but kept perfused with oxygenated blood and mechanically ventilated. The heart beats, the gut digests, wounds heal, and trunk reflexes occur. There is no brain, no head, and no possibility of consciousness. Verdict: the tissues are biologically active, but the person is dead; this is not a living human being.

Further case for prediction: A patient's biological brain is gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, by a synthetic substrate that replicates all psychological functions, with no interruption in consciousness, memory, or self-report. Once replacement is complete, no biological neural tissue remains, but the subject behaves identically and reports seamless continuity. What does your principle predict—did the original person survive, die at some point during the process, or become something else?

Please answer with: (a) the principle or principles, precisely formulated, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it; (b) the verdict each principle delivers on all six listed cases and on the synthetic-replacement case; (c) an explicit statement of which of my verdicts must be overturned if the principle is to avoid complexity or contradiction; and (d) whether the tension between case 1 and case 5 (both lacking a brain, yet one displays extensive organismal integration) and between case 3 and case 4 (both lacking circulation and detectable brain activity for hours, yet one is dead and one is not) can be resolved within a single account, or whether it forces a choice between saving the verdicts and saving simplicity.
