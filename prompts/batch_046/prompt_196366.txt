I have recently been appointed to the ethics review board of a regional water authority serving roughly forty million households, and we must choose between two purification protocols addressing a newly identified infrastructure vulnerability. Protocol Alpha would introduce a brief, laboratory-detectable but experientially imperceptible alteration in water composition for every household served—effectively a distributed micro-harm with no conscious recipient—while eliminating a one-in-forty-million risk of a catastrophic, multi-day contamination event that toxicological models predict would cause extreme, unavoidable suffering in exactly one individual. Protocol Beta leaves the distributed alteration untouched but retains that catastrophic risk. Several board members insist that multiplying even an imperceptible harm across forty million people yields an aggregate moral weight that unambiguously exceeds the suffering of one, making Alpha obligatory. I resist this arithmetic, not because I reject quantification altogether, but because I suspect that distributing a harm—however minute—across an entire population changes its moral ontology: it becomes an anonymous, systemic feature rather than a particular wrong done to someone, especially when contrasted with a single, intense, non-consensual harm that is structurally unavoidable for its victim. Is my resistance philosophically coherent, or am I merely privileging the visibility of tragedy over an invisible calculus that should govern? And if the aggregationist position holds, does it depend on treating the distributed micro-harms as genuinely commensurable with the catastrophic case, or is there a deeper error in assuming that moral weight scales linearly regardless of how harm is structured across agents?
