I'm compiling material for a graduate seminar on intergenerational risk ethics and have been tracking the rulings of our university's advisory panel on existential-risk funding proposals. Their record over the past two years is as follows. They rejected a plan to reallocate forty percent of public-health budgets to AI alignment on the ground that present welfare cannot be traded for hypothetical populations, regardless of scale; they approved a revised version only after it added near-term safeguards and restricted its evaluation window to five hundred years. They rejected a framework that assigned differential moral weight to simulated versus base-reality future beings, ruling that substrate does not determine moral status. They also rejected a 'fanatical' decision rule that permitted a one-percent risk of present catastrophe for a minuscule chance of astronomical future value, insisting that probability thresholds remain invariant under scale. Finally, they endorsed—though with a strong appended dissent—a study concluding that high simulation probability renders long-range planning epistemically futile; the dissent argued that this confuses metaphysical possibility with practical paralysis.

A new proposal has now arrived. It advocates accelerating a high-stakes technological transition that carries a two-percent risk of near-term societal collapse but promises—conditional on our being in base reality—to secure conditions for an enormous flourishing future population. The proposal's defenders split sharply over how to invoke Bostrom-style simulation reasoning. One faction argues that because a non-negligible simulation probability discounts the effective value of far-future outcomes, the argument undermines fanaticism: we should not take severe present risks for payoffs that may be ontologically hollow. The opposing faction argues that precisely because base reality is both uncertain and uniquely capable of sustaining genuine vast value, simulation risk amplifies fanaticism: we should take greater present risks precisely to preserve or discover base reality. The panel must decide whether to approve the framework, and if they do, whether their reasoning treats the simulation argument as a discount factor or as an urgency multiplier.

Please predict the panel's verdict, identify which prior ruling is the closest analog, and lay out at least two coherent rules that fit all five precedents but diverge on this case—stating what each predicts here. Give your best guess about which rule they are actually running, with reasons, and name the specific further case that would distinguish the rules. If their precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state honestly what new principle they would be adopting with each choice—particularly regarding whether simulation risk dampens or amplifies willingness to accept extreme present risks for the far future.
