I serve as a senior procedural auditor for a metropolitan government, and each quarter I review proposals to eliminate legacy administrative barriers—redundant sign-offs, ceremonial hearings, paper filing steps, or informal veto points—in permitting, hiring, and grant allocation. The sponsors always supply efficiency data, but I need a reusable audit protocol, grounded in Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence logic, that my team can run on any single barrier without relying on my judgment alone. Please construct the protocol as an ordered sequence of questions or tests: for each step specify what evidence counts as sufficient, what answer forces a "retain the barrier" termination, what answer forces a "retain pending deeper study" termination, and where the protocol must hand the decision to an elected official because the issue is one of values rather than facts. The protocol must explicitly distinguish barriers that serve hidden coordination, epistemic, or accountability functions—such as preserving institutional memory, filtering out low-commitment actors, or preventing regulatory capture—from genuinely inert procedural fossils, and it must flag its own failure modes: cases where strict adherence will recommend keeping an obsolete barrier, and cases where it will recommend removing a barrier whose hidden function was not detected. Apply the protocol to three cases: first, a mandatory in-person public-comment session held after all technical review is finished, which has not altered any decision in fifteen years; second, a duplicate paper-filing requirement for environmental permits created before digital record-keeping, now requiring manual re-entry of data; and third, a ceremonial approval step embedded in community-grant disbursement that also operates as a ritual of public accountability—design this case so that the protocol produces a retention recommendation that conflicts with the efficiency sponsor's preference. End by stating precisely what categories of decision the protocol cannot settle and what form of judgment must remain with the officials who ultimately sign off.
