I have been reflecting on a recurring argumentative pattern in multi-causal historical explanation, and I am unsure whether it has received a precise name in the philosophy of history or in formal causal modeling. The pattern arises when a historian explains an institutional collapse—say, the dissolution of a particular administrative federation—by identifying a cluster of conditions: fiscal insolvency, military overextension, and the erosion of a shared legitimating narrative. The historian then defends each condition's causal relevance by observing that, had any one of them been absent, the remaining conditions would still have been jointly sufficient to produce some form of collapse; the outcome was therefore robustly overdetermined at the macro level. At the same time, however, the historian insists that the precise trajectory, chronology, and morphological details of the actual collapse—the specific sequence of provincial withdrawals, the particular treaty architecture, the cultural residues—can be explained only by the exact internal configuration of all three conditions together, with no substitutability among them at the micro level.

What interests me is the simultaneous commitment to macro-level replaceability of the causal cluster and micro-level irreplaceability of its internal arrangement. This is not standard causal overdetermination, since overdetermination typically concerns redundant sufficient causes for a single event-token rather than a cluster that is redundant as a set yet essential in its parts. Nor does it reduce neatly to the type-token distinction, because both claims concern the same token event, differentiated only by resolution. I have encountered related discussions in structural explanation and explanatory pluralism, and I wonder whether concepts such as equifinality or causal redundancy capture this double-move, or whether it remains unnamed. Does this configuration have an established label? If not, how should one carve its boundaries—distinguishing it from ordinary overdetermination, from convergent causal paths that yield the same outcome-type, and from mere narrative embellishment? I am not looking for a bibliography, but for a concise conceptual map that specifies exactly what the phenomenon is and what it is not; please keep your response to no more than four hundred words.
