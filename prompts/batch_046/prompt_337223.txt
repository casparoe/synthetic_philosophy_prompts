As a retired biologist now studying philosophy, I have been examining Nozick’s arguments about the moral status of animals in Anarchy, State, and Utopia—specifically his view that animals fall under a utilitarian calculus allowing replacement, whereas human beings are protected by side-constraints against being used merely as means. Since Nozick died in 2002, he never addressed cultivated meat: animal tissue grown in bioreactors from stem-cell lines without slaughtering a sentient creature. Applying only his published framework, how should one assess this practice? In particular, does the absence of death and replacement of living animals eliminate the moral problem he identified, or does the initial extraction of cells still constitute an instrumentalization that violates even the weaker protection he granted to animals? Please lay out the reasoning he likely would have offered, distinguishing whether the replaceability argument applies to industrial cell cultures rather than to living replacements, and identify the precise point where his view would diverge from a straightforward animal-welfare calculus.
