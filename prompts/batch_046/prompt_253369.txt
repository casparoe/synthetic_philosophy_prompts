I am drafting a novel that interweaves the testimonies of survivors from three distinct historical catastrophes, not to establish equivalence among them but to examine how each community developed its own grammar of unspeakability. A reader has objected that any narrative architecture placing these events in dialogue—even contrapuntally—performs an implicit ranking, and that ranking historical evils is either epistemically impossible because they are incommensurable or ethically obscene because it treats irreducible moral horror as fungible.

Please explain the conceptual knot at the center of this objection. Specifically, is the difficulty a lack of shared metric, or is the very possession of a metric the moral failure? I want to understand whether a defensible intermediate position—one might call it structured non-commensuration—can genuinely hold: a framework that registers differences in kind (the bureaucratic abstraction of suffering, the collapse of temporal orientation for survivors, the destruction of meaning-structures, the scale of intentionality) along multiple independent axes without ever synthesizing them into an ordinal scale. If you think this middle ground collapses, explain precisely where the collapse occurs: does descriptive differentiation necessarily entail comparison once placed within a single narrative field, or can one maintain a plural-dimensional map of evil that refuses a unifying axis?

Please keep the explanation intuitive and grounded in the logic of representation rather than in formal proof. I need to feel why the tension is structurally unavoidable, not merely to catalog philosophical positions. Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
