I'm a PhD student in philosophy of practical rationality, and I am advising a hospital institutional review board that must repeatedly decide whether to continue partially completed clinical trials or treatment protocols once interim data undermines their original justification. The board needs an operational decision procedure grounded in what decision theory and philosophy actually say about sunk costs — not the popular slogan to ignore them, since in institutional medicine discontinuation can damage patient trust, staff morale, and future recruitment in ways that pure budget calculations miss. Please construct a reusable checklist the board can run in a thirty-minute review: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure outputs a recommendation to discontinue, the point at which it outputs a recommendation to continue, and the explicit hand-off points where the issue is one of values rather than rational assessment. The procedure must distinguish material sunk costs (expended funds, staff-hours, equipment) from procedural or relational sunk costs (institutional credibility, implicit commitments to enrolled patients, staff identity tied to the protocol), because standard rational-choice models collapse both into the same category and the board suspects this is wrong. Apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a phase-II oncology trial with sixty percent enrollment completed but interim analysis showing no efficacy signal; (2) a long-running palliative-care program that has lost its primary funding stream but has become central to unit staff identity and patient-family relationships; and (3) a surgical innovation program in which the lead surgeon has spent years refining a technique that now shows worse outcomes than standard care, where discontinuation would affect her career trajectory and the unit's regional reputation. Include at least one case where the procedure recommends discontinuing despite strong institutional and relational pressure to continue, and state clearly where the procedure is likely to misfire or where reasonable people who understand the theory will still disagree. Separate settled results in decision theory from positions that remain genuinely contested among philosophers and clinicians regarding institutional rationality, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
