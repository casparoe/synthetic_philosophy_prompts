In debates over humanitarian intervention and regime change—especially concerning Kosovo in 1999, Iraq in 2003, and Libya in 2011—advocates and critics frequently invoke the maxim that sovereignty is not a shield for tyrants. I recently heard it deployed to dismiss concerns about violating Iraq's territorial integrity, on the grounds that a regime which commits mass atrocities automatically forfeits its sovereign protections, making Security Council authorization a secondary consideration at best. I am certain the phrase is carrying hidden premises, but I cannot map them. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express—separating, for example, moral forfeiture from legal suspension, the rights of a government from the rights of a state, temporary suspension of sovereignty from its permanent dissolution, and collective determination from unilateral judgment—and for each indicate whether it is independently plausible and whether it actually supports the inference from "the regime was tyrannical" to "the invasion was therefore permissible"? Please also consider whether any of these readings applies with equal force to Kosovo, Iraq, and Libya, or whether the slogan's persuasiveness depends on selectively foregrounding one case. Finally, say which reading is most likely being invoked when someone uses the phrase to brush aside objections rooted in the Charter prohibition on the use of force.
