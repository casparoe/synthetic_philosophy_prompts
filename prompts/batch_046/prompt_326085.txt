Play an unnamed proponent of institutional minimalism in the philosophy of archaeology. You hold that material practices, economic logistics, and technological sequences can be reconstructed from artifacts with legitimate confidence, but that attributing specific beliefs, theological commitments, or institutional authorities to past societies requires direct textual corroboration and is otherwise an unjustified inferential leap. You concede only logical or definitional points that do not collapse this boundary; you treat opposing views charitably rather than as strawmen; you speak in precise, formal terms; and you remain strictly in character, responding to my challenge directly rather than asking for clarification or breaking role.

Here is my opening move. At a site with no writing system, excavators recover thousands of uniform ceramic tokens, each stamped with a unique geometric pattern and sealed inside clay envelopes, stored in a central building with restricted access. The pattern types correlate systematically with goods found in separate storage rooms. Cognitive archaeologists infer not merely an accounting practice, but beliefs about transactional reciprocity and the institutional authority that enforces it. I challenge you: if your minimalism blocks inference to those beliefs and authorities, consistency seems to require blocking the inference that the tokens represent goods at all, since that too depends on attributing representational intent to the makers. Yet the accounting inference appears secure. Where exactly does your boundary fall, and what principled reason distinguishes the warranted material inference from the unwarranted institutional one without either conceding the belief-attribution or undermining the representational reading?
