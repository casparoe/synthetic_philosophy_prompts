I'm taking a course in metaphysics that has me thinking about ontology identification under conditions of inaccessibility, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I can't place precisely. Consider two competing ontologies for the same empirical domain. Ontology A posits only observable entities—say, fields with measurable symmetries. Ontology B accepts every claim of A about observable arrangements, but adds a further layer: each field contains an internal "index structure" that is defined to operate exclusively at scales permanently below any possible detection threshold, or behind a dynamical shield that the theory itself treats as fundamental. The defenders of B do not claim that B yields novel predictions; rather, they argue that the index structure explains why the observable symmetries take exactly the form they do, and that without it those symmetries remain brute. Meanwhile, defenders of A treat B as A plus invisible ornamentation.

I've encountered structurally similar moves elsewhere: one involves mental properties assigned universally to physical systems but defined so that they never influence behavior, never leave traces, and are in principle undetectable by any apparatus constructed within the physical framework they inhabit; another involves hidden variables that are not merely unknown but are rendered necessarily inaccessible by the very dynamics that invoke them. In each case, the added entities are not speculative extensions awaiting better instruments—they are constructed to lie permanently outside the reach of the ontology's own observational apparatus, yet they are defended as indispensable for explanatory completeness rather than predictive power.

Is there an established concept or literature that captures exactly this configuration—where an ontology incorporates entities that are in principle unobservable according to that ontology's own framework, and where those entities are justified as explanatory rather than predictive? How does it differ from ordinary empirical underdetermination, from standard indispensability arguments, or from verificationist objections? If no standard term fits, could you name and define the pattern, specify its boundary conditions—distinguishing it, for instance, from entities that are merely currently unobserved but could in principle be detected—and indicate whether it represents a legitimate explanatory strategy or a degenerate form of theory protection?
