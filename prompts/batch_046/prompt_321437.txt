I have been reflecting on whether religious commitment genuinely makes people more moral, and I recently encountered a summary—perhaps in a magazine essay or an introductory book chapter—that tried to settle the question with a particular conceptual framework. According to this summary, asking whether religion makes people "more moral" is ambiguous in three ways: first, it might mean stricter adherence to explicit norms; second, it might mean a greater capacity for trust in strangers; and third, it might mean resistance to what the author called "moral credentialing," where people perform one good act to license a later bad one. The summary then claimed that empirical studies show religious individuals score higher on the second measure only in conditions where they feel observed by a divine witness, which suggests that religion produces not authentic virtue but a kind of "monitored compliance." The author apparently concluded that secular societies therefore face a structural deficit: without belief in an omniscient evaluator, people fall back on purely contractual reciprocity, and the deep, non-calculated trust necessary for cooperation collapses. I suspect this argument conflates several different claims, but I am not sure which ones. Is the three-part distinction between norm-adherence, stranger-trust, and resistance to credentialing a fair way to parse what "more moral" could mean? Does the evidence about divine observation actually support the claim that religious morality is only monitored compliance, or are there other interpretations of those findings? And is the leap from "religion can motivate trust" to "secular societies cannot sustain it" justified, or does it overlook secular mechanisms—such as institutional transparency or shared narrative identity—that might serve a similar function?
