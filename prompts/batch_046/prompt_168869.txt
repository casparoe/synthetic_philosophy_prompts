In my reporting on philosophy of mind, I recently moderated a discussion between Dr. Lena Kowalski, a philosopher of consciousness, and Dr. Raj Patel, a cognitive neuroscientist, about whether epiphenomenalism can survive what is sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Their exchange unfolded across six moves.

(1) Kowalski opened by arguing that if phenomenal consciousness is causally inert, then every first-person judgment of the form “I am now experiencing X” is caused solely by underlying neural activity, never by the experience itself. Consequently, she claimed, the correlation between judgment and experience is purely accidental from the believer’s point of view: the subject has no cognitive access to the state she reports.

(2) Patel replied that accidental correlation misdiagnoses the situation. He offered a comparison to a smoke detector: the alarm is caused by combustion particles, not by the property “being on fire,” yet it reliably tracks fire because both share a common cause. By the same token, neural states can cause both the phenomenal experience and the judgment about it, producing a stable common-cause pathway that makes the judgment genuinely responsive.

(3) Kowalski countered that Patel’s analogy misses a modal asymmetry. In the smoke-detector case, fire plays causal roles elsewhere in the environment; in epiphenomenalism, phenomenal properties play no causal role anywhere. More critically, she said, supervenience without downward causation permits a metaphysically possible scenario—call it phenomenal divergence—in which the neural substrate remains identical but the phenomenal property is absent. In that scenario, the judgment “I experience X” is still produced by the unchanged neural mechanism, yet it is false. Because the judgment mechanism is insensitive to the presence or absence of the phenomenal property, it cannot constitute knowledge of that property.

(4) Patel responded that phenomenal divergence violates the supervenience relation that epiphenomenalists typically accept. If consciousness supervenes on the physical with at least nomological necessity, then identical neural substrates necessarily entail identical phenomenal properties. The counterfactual therefore describes an impossible world, and Kowalski’s charge of insensitivity collapses.

(5) Kowalski then shifted ground: even granting that divergence is impossible, she argued, the judgment remains responsive only to the neural substrate, not to the phenomenal property itself. She drew a distinction between tracking the substrate and tracking the experience. Because the content of the judgment refers to the phenomenal quality—say, the specific character of redness—while its causal etiology refers only to neural firing patterns, there is a systematic misalignment between what the judgment is about and what governs its formation. This, she claimed, is the real heart of the paradox: not that we are frequently wrong, but that our first-person authority is causally hollow, directed at a property that never touches the machinery producing the report.

(6) Patel conceded that the causal etiology points downward, but denied that this undermines authority. He argued that representational content need not mirror causal ancestry: a belief that the sun is rising is caused by retinal stimulation, not by solar motion, yet the belief is genuinely about the sun and can be epistemically authoritative. Unless Kowalski can show that phenomenal judgments are unreliable in actual cases—not merely that their causes are non-phenomenal—she has not shown that epiphenomenalism is self-undermining, only that it possesses an unfamiliar causal architecture.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then indicate which party is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly regarding whether the paradox, if sound, threatens knowledge, first-person authority, or merely offers an unfamiliar causal explanation. Finally, assess whether Kowalski’s “phenomenal divergence” scenario in move (3) and her representational-content objection in move (5) genuinely advance the standard formulation of the paradox, or whether they mainly relabel difficulties that the standard version already contains.
