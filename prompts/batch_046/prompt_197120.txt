In my AP Biology course and in the popular-science essays I read for a school philosophy club, I keep encountering a specific pattern in evolutionary reasoning that I cannot find a precise name for, and I want to know whether philosophers of biology or evolutionary theorists have already carved it out.

Here are two concrete instances. First, consider a morphological trait—say, a particular cranial feature—that is developmentally coupled to another feature that is clearly adaptive. The feature itself is widespread across species with different diets and climates, and it carries a slight metabolic cost, yet it also provides a minor structural benefit. One researcher explains it as an adaptation: natural selection refined it because the net benefit was positive, and the developmental link is merely the pathway through which selection operated. Another researcher explains the very same feature as a spandrel or exaptation: selection never targeted it independently, the benefit is incidental, and the widespread distribution is best explained by its being dragged along with the genuinely adaptive partner. The empirical evidence cited is largely the same in both explanations—the shared developmental pathway, the cross-species distribution, the cost-benefit profile—yet the classification flips.

Second, I see the same oscillation in behavioral explanations. A social behavior appears costly in some contexts but beneficial in others, and it is tied developmentally or neurologically to a behavior that is uncontroversially adaptive. Defenders of a strongly adaptationist framework treat the behavior as an adaptation with context-dependent expression; critics treat it as a spandrel or a byproduct of selection on the partner behavior, treating any benefit as post hoc rationalization. What strikes me is that the disagreement often tracks the broader argumentative goal—defending evolutionary explanation against design-based objections versus criticizing overreach in adaptationist storytelling—more than it tracks any discrete empirical test.

Is there an established concept that captures this specific configuration: a trait that is developmentally coupled to an adaptive feature, functionally ambiguous, and therefore susceptible to being classified as either adaptation or spandrel depending on the explanatory agenda of the classifier? I am not asking for the general spandrels critique itself, nor for the distinction between adaptation and exaptation, both of which I understand. I am asking whether there is a named concept for the rhetorical or methodological pattern in which the same body of evidence about coupled, ambiguous traits is sorted differently based on whether one is advancing or resisting an adaptationist program. If there is no such concept, can you propose a precise term and definition that distinguishes this pattern from ordinary confirmation bias, from the motte-and-bailey move in evolutionary debates, and from straightforward empirical disagreement about selection coefficients? I would also like to know whether the "developmentally coupled but functionally ambiguous" trait itself has a technical designation separate from spandrel or exaptation, or whether it falls into an unrecognized gap between those categories.
