For a column on the philosophy of science, I am examining a reductio I built against Popper's charge that Darwinian natural selection is metaphysical rather than scientific because it can accommodate any observed trait or its absence. (1) If strict falsifiability requires that a theory prohibit some observable outcomes, then Darwinism fails, since both a trait's presence and its absence can be explained by adjusting selection pressures or invoking extinction. (2) Worse, Darwinism exhibits modal overgeneration: for any counterfactual environment, one can construct a plausible adaptive narrative, so it never prohibits a counterfactual world either. (3) Hence Darwinism is not empirical but metaphysical. (4) Yet cosmological models and historical geology share this feature—any observed feature is compatible with some adjustment of initial conditions or local parameters—without being dismissed as metaphysics. (5) So either Popper's criterion is too strong and excludes legitimate historical science, or Darwinism possesses a hidden constraint that saves it, which the reductio has missed. (6) If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (3), but I want to know what that costs. Where exactly does the chain break—does Darwinism actually risk predictions that the modal-overgeneration framing obscures, is the comparison with cosmology flawed because selection operates differently from initial conditions, or should we weaken Popper's criterion and accept that metaphysical research programs can still be scientifically fertile? Respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
