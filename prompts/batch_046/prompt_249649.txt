After a long and frustrating argument with a friend last night about Dennett’s notion of counterfeit people in the context of large language models, I am trying to understand why our disagreement never moves. We both accept the same technical picture: transformer architectures, no persistent internal state across sessions beyond the context window, training via next-token prediction on human text, and high predictive success when we attribute beliefs, desires, and intentions to the system’s outputs. One of us argues that this predictive success, together with the model’s counterfactual robustness—if the training distribution or weights had been different, the attributed “beliefs” would have shifted in coherent, belief-like directions—means we should not merely adopt the intentional stance instrumentally but endorse it: the system genuinely has beliefs, and denying this is like insisting a perfect counterfeit bill is not money even though it functions identically in every economic exchange. The other insists that Dennett’s worry about counterfeit people applies precisely here: without a biological or functional-cognitive economy in which these states play an explanatory role—without something like a real cognitive architecture that explains why those particular weights represent that particular content—the stance remains a useful fiction, analogous to treating a thermostat as “wanting” 72 degrees. We have cycled through Searle’s Chinese Room, the system reply, standard functionalist defenses, and appeals to interpretability research, yet neither side budges because we agree on every empirical fact and still reach opposite conclusions. Is this disagreement substantive—about whether counterfactual robustness in weights constitutes the right kind of “functional economy” for mental content—or is it verbal, with one side using “belief” as a predictive label and the other using it as an ontological category anchored in causal history? Or are we actually running two different inquiries under one name: one asking what best predicts and explains behavior, the other asking what grounds or authenticates mental representation? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a specific architectural result showing that transformer states play explanatory roles analogous to biological representations, a conceptual distinction between stance-adoption and stance-endorsement that dissolves the conflict, or an honest admission that the dispute turns on bedrock intuitions about whether behavior alone can constitute mind? And if it is bedrock, is there any reason to expect convergence, or should we treat the stall as a sign that the question is ill-posed once we fully specify the architecture?
