In my study group we've been working through David Lewis's essay Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?, and I'm trying to see whether the intuition he pumps—that we wouldn't let an innocent friend serve prison time for our crime—can be turned into a general argument against any form of vicarious punishment. I think I've built a chain that proves too much, but I can't locate the leak. Here are the steps:

(1) If it is just for person A to bear punishment P for offense O, then O must be the kind of debt that can be discharged by the occurrence of suffering P regardless of whose body suffers it.
(2) But if the sufferer's identity is irrelevant, then justice is fully satisfied by the aggregate distribution of suffering, not by its attachment to the guilty party.
(3) Lewis's thought experiment shows we reject (2) in human contexts: we judge it unjust for an innocent volunteer to serve prison time for a convicted offender, even if the sentence length and severity exactly match what justice demands.
(4) So justice requires that the guilty party suffer; it is not merely a bookkeeping problem about total suffering.
(5) Applying this to theological penal substitution: either Christ's suffering discharges the debt without the guilty being punished (violating (4)), or the guilty are somehow punished in Christ, which requires a metaphysical union strong enough to collapse the distinction between substitute and offender.
(6) Therefore, penal substitution is either unjust (by (4)) or not genuinely substitutional (by the collapse in (5)).
(7) But the same reasoning applies to institutional vicarious liability—corporate fines paid by shareholders, parents punished for children's torts, collective reparations—all of which we treat as permissible.
(8) Hence the argument proves too much: it undermines not only the theological doctrine but every non-identical allocation of punitive burden.

I want to keep (3) and (4), since they capture the Lewisian intuition, and I also want to keep (7), because those analogies are real features of legal and moral practice. So I don't want to solve the problem by denying that vicarious liability exists. Which step should I reject—(1), the move from (4) to (5), or the disjunction in (6)—and what does giving it up cost? In particular, if the flaw is in (1), does that mean justice is not about suffering at all? And if the flaw is in (5)'s collapse claim, what kind of metaphysical relation between substitute and offender would be enough to save substitution without making it trivial?
