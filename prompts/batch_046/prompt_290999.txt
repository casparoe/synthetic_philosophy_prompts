Consider the regulatory divergence between many European jurisdictions and the American market in assisted reproduction. In countries such as France and Spain, commercial surrogacy is prohibited and altruistic arrangements are typically void or criminalized, while egg donation is permitted under anonymity and capped compensation. In the United States, by contrast, commercial surrogacy is legal in numerous states, governed by detailed contracts that allocate parental rights according to intent rather than gestation or genetics, and compensated egg donation operates through a market with variable anonymity rules. The asymmetry is striking: European law treats the extraction of gametes as a donatable, anonymizable contribution, but treats gestation as an inalienable bodily and relational process that cannot be contracted away, even without payment. American practice treats both as services that can be fully specified by agreement, though surrogacy contracts receive heavier judicial scrutiny than egg donation contracts despite involving greater physical duration and risk.

What philosophical commitments about the body, parenthood, and contractual freedom does this institutional pattern presuppose? Does the European asymmetry commit to an ontology in which genetic material is separable property while gestational labor constitutes an inseparable aspect of personhood, or can it be explained by a different theory—say, one about the irreducibility of temporal embodied presence to contract, or about the state's interest in preventing the instrumentalization of reproductive processes? Does the American intent-based framework presuppose that reproductive labor is fully fungible, that parental status is purely a matter of allocative agreement, and that bodily processes can be partitioned into commodifiable units without remainder? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories behind each regime, distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and assess whether either regime is philosophically coherent or whether both depend on an unstable hybrid of proprietary and dignity-based concepts. Finally, describe what a consistently applied policy would look like if it treated gametes and gestation under a single framework—whether fully alienable or fully inalienable—and identify which concrete institutional features (compensation structures, anonymity rules, parentage adjudication, contract enforceability) would have to change.
