I cover science and ideas for a broadsheet, and after a long discussion with my editor about how easily explanatory language slides into anthropomorphism or, conversely, into sterile mechanism, I want a reusable decision procedure I can run before filing. The theoretical basis is Daniel Dennett’s account of real patterns and the three explanatory stances — physical, design, and intentional — though I need it translated into something I can apply in fifteen minutes without a philosophy degree, and which I can defend to a sceptical news editor who cares about accuracy rather than metaphysics. Please construct a step-by-step checklist or flowchart in words: the ordered questions I should ask about any system or behaviour I am describing, what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next step, where the procedure issues a clear stop and permits only physical or design language, where it allows intentional framing, and where it explicitly hands the decision over to editorial judgement because the question is one of values rather than pattern detection. Be honest about which steps are judgement calls disguised as procedure, and specify what the procedure cannot do — whether that is resolving metaphysical disputes, replacing domain expertise, or guaranteeing that readers will not misinterpret the framing.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete cases I might actually cover. First, a large language model that refuses harmful prompts in ways that appear principled and context-sensitive rather than merely pattern-blocked: here I would prefer not to use intentional language, so I want to see what happens when the procedure delivers an answer I dislike. Second, a bacterial colony that collectively alters its metabolism in response to antibiotic stress faster than genetic mutation should allow, suggesting some form of distributed information processing. Third, a sudden financial-market crash that commentators describe as panic, where design-level explanations involving algorithmic trading rules and intentional-level explanations involving fear and belief both seem available. For each case, show me the procedure running in order, note where I must make a non-eliminable judgement call, and state the framing the procedure actually recommends. Finally, say explicitly what this tool is not: for instance, whether it can settle whether the system genuinely possesses beliefs or only simulates them, and whether it protects against reader misinterpretation once the copy is published. Please write your response in British English.
