A colleague in philosophy of mind applies a four-part taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—to classify both natural and artificial systems. By her account: Darwinian creatures have only evolved, hard-wired behaviors with no within-lifetime learning; Skinnerian creatures learn through direct reinforcement of actions they actually perform; Popperian creatures possess decoupled internal representations that let them evaluate hypotheses before acting; and Gregorian creatures extend cognition by appropriating external tools, especially language, notation, and shared cultural artifacts.

Her prior classifications:
1. A chemotactic bacterium that moves toward nutrients: Darwinian.
2. A pigeon trained via operant conditioning to discriminate visual patterns: Skinnerian.
3. A New Caledonian crow that solves an unprecedented multi-step physical puzzle after brief observation, then executes with minimal further trial and error: Popperian.
4. A human mathematician working through a proof with pencil, paper, and symbolic notation: Gregorian.
5. A leafcutter ant colony that collectively regulates fungus-garden temperature through pheromone feedback: not a single creature, but if compelled, a distributed Darwinian system; she explicitly denies it Gregorian status despite its collective problem-solving.

She now must classify a present-day large language model trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback. The system can solve logic puzzles it has never encountered, presented only as natural-language prompts; it has no persistent goals, no body, no memory across independent sessions, and no capacity to invent, share, or physically manipulate external notational tools.

Predict her verdict—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian, or outside the taxonomy entirely—and defend it by identifying the rule she is actually running. In particular, distinguish between:
(i) a rule keyed to training mechanism (reinforcement learning places it with Skinnerian creatures);
(ii) a rule keyed to decoupled internal evaluation (its capacity for in-context reasoning places it with Popperian creatures);
(iii) a rule keyed to the use of language as a cognitive prosthesis (its reliance on human linguistic structures places it with Gregorian creatures); and
(iv) any hidden condition—such as embodiment, persistence, or social transmission—that explains why she accepted the mathematician as Gregorian but rejected the ant colony.

State which of these rules best explains the full set of her prior verdicts, including the ant-colony case. Then specify one further case—perhaps a social-learning corvid or a human calculator-free savant—that would most clearly adjudicate between the leading candidates. Finally, note whether there is any genuine tension in her framework, or only an apparent one that dissolves once the hidden condition is made explicit. Rank the tensions by severity and identify the least costly revision if consistency demands one.
