Write a dialogue between two scholars—one sympathetic to macro-scale evolutionary narratives of human history (in the vein of Harari’s framing of the Cognitive Revolution and Diamond’s geographic determinism) and one critical of such grand narratives (in the vein of Graeber and Wengrow’s rejection of linear stage-models). Have them debate whether the emergence of large language models can be coherently analogized to an evolutionary transition in collective cognition, comparable to the shift from biological to fictional cooperation that Harari describes. The defender should argue that LLMs constitute an externalized layer of myth-making that enables coordination at unprecedented scale, largely independent of individual human belief; the critic should counter that this illegitimately treats societies as organisms, ignores the institutional and power-laden contexts in which these tools are embedded, and imposes a progressive narrative onto heterogeneous social practices. Each speaker should directly address the other’s concern about scale—specifically whether explanatory frameworks valid across millennia can be applied to rapid technological change without committing a category error—and conclude by identifying what conceptual or empirical consideration would settle their disagreement.
