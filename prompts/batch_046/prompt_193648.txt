Write a short story exploring the specific philosophical question of what Descartes's cogito actually proves, treating the issue as genuinely unsettled rather than as a settled textbook exercise. Place your protagonist in a situation of engineered discontinuity: she is a consciousness whose mental states are reconstructed in isolated intervals with no direct causal or memorial link between them, so that each instance of thinking feels immediate but never obviously belongs to a persisting subject. Have her discover that she can doubt everything about her past and her body, yet find herself unable to doubt that she is thinking right now; then push the narrative into the less familiar territory of whether this intuition proves only a punctual, frame-bound occurrence of thought, or whether it implicitly commits her to a continuous thinking substance stretching across the reconstructed intervals. Include a secondary figure—perhaps the architect of her condition—who argues that treating prior reconstructed frames as meaningful investments is irrational if no continuous self honors them, and let the protagonist respond not by asserting identity outright, but by examining whether the very performance of doubting continuity already presupposes some form of temporal persistence in the doubter. Avoid resolving the tension with a simple moral or technological twist; let the story end with the philosophical crux still alive. Present the entire narrative as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any other formatting devices, written in the manner of a single uninterrupted letter.
