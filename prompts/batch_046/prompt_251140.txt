I sit on the Acquisition and Ethics Committee of the Meridien Museum of Contemporary Practice. Last quarter we acquired a large-scale painting attributed to the late painter Elias Vore, only to learn—after a technical analysis—that it was produced by a generative system that did not copy any existing canvas but instead reconstructed Vore’s decision-tree backwards: the algorithm inferred, from the finished works in our archives, the sequence of compositional choices he would have made at each layer, then executed those choices with materials chemically identical to his own. The result is perceptually indistinguishable from a genuine Vore, and some conservators argue it is decisionally isomorphic—sharing the same counterfactual dependencies—without being causally connected to his hand. The Vore estate demands immediate deaccessioning and destruction, arguing that the work lacks indexical authenticity, the property of being the actual terminus of an artistic intention regardless of appearance. Our curatorial staff counters that destroying it repeats iconoclastic errors and that transparency—displaying it in a dedicated gallery with full documentation of its synthetic origin—would serve education and public trust better than suppression. Our board policy permits either course if justified, but requires a recommendation with a concrete timeline. I need you to draft the formal opinion memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting: it should recommend one specific course of action (retain and display under conditions, destroy, or sequester) with a clear implementation timeframe, engage seriously with the strongest argument for the opposing view—especially the claim that decision-isomorphism can substitute for causal origin in aesthetic value—and explain why that argument ultimately fails or succeeds. Treat this as an institutional ethics document, not an abstract essay: the recommendation must be actionable, and the reasoning must be accessible to board members who are not specialists.

Please approach this as a senior philosopher of art experienced in museum ethics and institutional decision-making. Before drafting the memo, briefly map the correspondence between causal origin and decision-isomorphism explicitly—what maps onto what, where the analogy between a reconstructed decision-tree and an original artistic act breaks down, and whether the breakdown undermines the curatorial staff’s position—then write the memo in a formal, polished tone appropriate for circulation. State the recommendation up front, provide reasoning with an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and conclude with concrete steps and deadlines (for example, within thirty days or by the end of the fiscal quarter). Your response should be committed: take a definite position and defend it with reasons, rather than laying out all options neutrally and leaving the choice to me. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the board needs reasoning it can evaluate now, not references it must chase down.
