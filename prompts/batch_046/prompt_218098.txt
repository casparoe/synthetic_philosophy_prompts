I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who advances an unconventional, systematic reading of Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator. On her view, the spectator is not a unitary, internalized social mirror but a calibrated dissonance mechanism: moral self-correction arises not from emotional convergence with an imagined other, but from sustaining a structured tension between one's own sentiments and the counterfactual sentiments of an idealized, multi-perspectival observer. She further argues that self-deception, in Smith's framework, is not a failure to perceive this observer's verdict, but a premature resolution of that tension—collapsing the plural observer into a single, accommodating voice. Write me a sequence of seven interview questions that engage this interpretation where it is most vulnerable. I want: one question on whether this dissonance model can accommodate Smith's explicit emphasis on sympathetically entering into the feelings of others rather than holding them at arm's length; a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that tension and entry are compatible. One question that asks how her view distinguishes productive moral tension from mere paralysis or indecision, with a follow-up pressing the boundary case of an agent who never resolves the dissonance. One question on whether reconstructing Smith's internal observer as a plural, distributed mechanism does violence to the textual evidence of a singular internal judge, anticipating her defense that singularity is phenomenological rather than metaphysical. And a final question asking what conceptual or evidential finding—short of direct textual contradiction—would lead her to abandon the claim that self-deception is premature resolution rather than failed perception. Keep the questions substantive, avoid biographical or generic openings, and do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographic references.
