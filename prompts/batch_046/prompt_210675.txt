I have been thinking about the St. Petersburg paradox on my own, and I keep returning to a difficulty that the standard resolutions seem to pass over too quickly. The familiar answers—invoking diminishing marginal utility, risk aversion, or the physical impossibility of infinite resources—treat the paradox as though it were a failure of the agent’s preferences or of the world’s capacity to pay. I want you to argue for a different position: that the infinite expected value is spurious because the calculation treats outcomes lying far outside any agent’s representational horizon as if they stood on equal footing with outcomes the agent can genuinely conceive receiving.

Please defend the view that a payoff is decision-relevant only if it satisfies a condition of representational accessibility: the agent must be able to form a specific, non-generic mental representation of the state of affairs in which they actually hold that reward. Apply this to show why the St. Petersburg game differs structurally from an ordinary fair bet, and explain why truncating the expectation at the boundary of representational accessibility is not equivalent to applying a psychological utility curve or assuming risk aversion. You should also address the objection that this criterion merely disguises bounded rationality, and clarify why representational inaccessibility belongs to the payoff structure itself rather than being a contingent cognitive limitation of the player. Keep the reasoning internal to the logic of decision and representation; do not appeal to empirical studies of judgment, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to named authors beyond what is necessary to identify the paradox itself.
