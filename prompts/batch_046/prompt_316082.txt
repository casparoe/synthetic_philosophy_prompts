I've been revisiting Clark and Chalmers's extended-mind argument after some old rationalist forum threads on cognitive outsourcing, and it made me want to apply it to Christopher Nolan's film Memento, whose plot I take as familiar: Leonard Shelby suffers from anterograde amnesia and relies on Polaroid photographs, handwritten notes, and tattoos to navigate the world and pursue revenge for his wife's murder. The film's reverse chronology puts the audience in roughly Leonard's epistemic position, and his external artifacts look like a cinematic version of Otto's notebook—coupled, constantly available, and functionally integrated with his reasoning.

But Memento complicates the parity principle in ways that standard thought experiments do not. Leonard does not merely use his notes; he deliberately writes false information on himself—the license plate he tattoos on his thigh, the Sammy Jankis notes that may be self-projections—so his external system is both a cognitive prosthesis and an instrument of self-deception. I would like a reading that asks whether Leonard's artifacts genuinely constitute part of his mind, or whether they fail some integration or reliability condition that Otto's notebook satisfies. Does the film dramatize the worry that mere causal coupling is not sufficient for cognitive constitution, by showing that an external system can be coupled yet systematically misleading? Or does it push further, suggesting that an extended mind is more vulnerable to self-deception than an internal one because it lacks the first-person authority that beliefs usually carry?

Please take both the film and the philosophy seriously: do not reduce Memento to an illustration of Clark and Chalmers, and do not twist the parity principle to fit the plot. Where the film resists the philosophical framing—perhaps in its implication that Leonard's real problem is not memory loss but an ethical refusal to stop—say so, and note what that resistance adds to the debate that pure argument misses.
