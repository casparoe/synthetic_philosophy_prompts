I am supervising a doctoral student in legal philosophy who has developed a consistent but somewhat opaque position on the proper scope of legal moralism. Over the course of our discussions she has rendered the following judgements: she supports prohibiting commercial surrogacy arrangements and employer-mandated genetic screening, holding that both reduce human beings to marketable commodities or biological data-sets; she opposes criminalising private polyamorous households and voluntary euthanasia for the terminally ill, maintaining that intimate autonomy and self-determination should not be subject to state moral oversight; and she endorses restrictions on graphic violent imagery in public advertising, arguing that the state has a legitimate interest in preserving the moral environment of shared spaces, though she rejects analogous restrictions on private consumption. I am trying to determine the underlying principle or principles that best unify these judgements.

The difficulty is that her track record seems compatible with at least two distinct rules. One interpretation is an anti-commodification principle rooted in human dignity: the law should intervene whenever human capacities, relationships, or bodies are treated as fungible goods, or when public spaces are degraded by the instrumentalisation of violence, but it should otherwise refrain from policing private moral choice. A second interpretation is an expanded harm principle: the law may restrict conduct that risks tangible psychological or social harm, including exploitation of vulnerable parties or the corruption of shared civic life, but it should not criminalise consensual private behaviour that causes no direct injury to others. Both frameworks accommodate her approval of surrogacy bans and genetic-testing prohibitions, as well as her tolerance of polyamory and assisted dying; both also explain her stance on public advertising.

Here is the case I should like your analysis of. A technology firm has developed an artificial intelligence system capable of generating a sustained, interactive simulation of a deceased intimate partner, designed for private domestic use; the user is fully aware that the entity is synthetic, gives explicit consent, and no third party is involved or deceived. Should my student, if she wishes to remain consistent with her prior verdicts, support or oppose a legal prohibition on the private development and use of such systems? Please identify the two (or more) candidate rules that fit her earlier judgements, state what each predicts about this new case, offer your best guess as to her actual verdict with reasons, and specify the further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a non-commercial open-source version distributed within a grief-support network, or a simulation of a living consenting partner—that would most sharply distinguish between the competing principles you have identified.
