I'm helping my older sister evaluate two pairs of competing job offers that her career counselor presented, and she has asked me whether her preferences reveal a consistent risk attitude or whether she is being misled by the way the options are framed. In the first pair, Offer A guarantees an annual bonus of exactly $50,000, while Offer B carries an 89 percent chance of $50,000, a 10 percent chance of $150,000, and a 1 percent chance of receiving nothing; she chose A without hesitation. In the second pair, Offer C provides an 11 percent chance of $50,000 against an 89 percent chance of nothing, while Offer D provides a 10 percent chance of $150,000 against a 90 percent chance of nothing; this time she chose D.

When I laid out the structures side by side, I pointed out that both pairs contain the exact same underlying comparison—an 11 percent probability of $50,000 versus a 10 percent probability of $150,000—except that the first pair embeds that comparison inside an 89 percent background chance of receiving $50,000, which is present in both options of the first pair but absent from the second. I told her that a rational decision-maker ought to treat that background state as separable, like removing an identical coupon from two competing menus, which means her preference for A in the first pair should align with a preference for C in the second if her reasoning is consistent. She rejected this, arguing that the guaranteed middle outcome in Offer A does not merely add value but transforms the emotional and practical significance of the remaining risks, so that the 1 percent chance of total failure in Offer B feels categorically different from the 90 percent chance of failure in Offer D. She even likened the shift to the sorites paradox, proposing that an 89 percent background of moderate success might accumulate like grains of sand, altering the qualitative kind of gamble in a way that makes its removal a substantive change rather than a neutral mathematical cancellation.

I want to advise her on whether her reversal constitutes a genuine failure of rational consistency—specifically, whether normative choice theory should require that preferences remain invariant when an identical outcome is appended to every alternative in a set—or whether her reaction correctly identifies that rational agency must accommodate holistic, context-dependent evaluation rather than decomposing risks into state-independent parts. I am particularly unsure how to weigh the formal demand for separability against her claim that the psychological embedding of outcomes is not an error to be corrected but a legitimate feature of practical reasoning, and I do not know whether telling her to "fix" her intuition would be giving her sound philosophical guidance or merely imposing an abstract formalism that ignores how real agents experience uncertainty.
