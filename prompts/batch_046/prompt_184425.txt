Construct a realistic, finite betting scenario—set in an ordinary workplace or clinical context such as an insurance underwriting office or a diagnostic committee, with stakes comparable to a modest professional fee or a casual wager—in which a precise Bayesian agent governed by a single credence function is immune to a Dutch book, while an agent holding imprecise probabilities represented by a convex credal set is vulnerable to a guaranteed loss unless they supplement their beliefs with an additional decision rule such as E-admissibility or maximin. The ambiguity that generates the credal set should arise naturally from genuinely underdetermined evidence—for example, conflicting expert testimonies with no agreed weighting—rather than from logical ignorance or exotic physics, and the scenario must involve at least two distinct bets that exploit different extreme points of the set. Present the case with explicit probabilities, odds, and payoffs, and do not invoke infinite bet sequences, perfect predictors, demons, or physically impossible conditions. After the scenario, demonstrate step-by-step: (a) why the precise Bayesian cannot be exploited regardless of which bet is offered; (b) exactly which combination of bets produces a sure loss for the imprecise agent who accepts every wager that is fair at some point within their credal set; and (c) how adopting E-admissibility, maximin, or another specified rule blocks the book. Identify the structural feature—such as the absence of a unique probability measure governing all betting dispositions—that creates the divergence. Then explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into a standard Dutch-book argument for the probability axioms, or does the vulnerability disappear entirely? Next, outline how a defender of imprecise probabilities would attack the case, specifying whether the defense requires rejecting the betting interpretation of belief, imposing a restriction on admissible bets, or adopting a particular aggregation rule. Conclude by stating precisely what the case is designed to show: whether imprecise probabilities are inherently incoherent without an auxiliary decision rule, or whether the Dutch book is merely an artifact of forcing a uniform betting behavior onto a family of reasonable credences.
