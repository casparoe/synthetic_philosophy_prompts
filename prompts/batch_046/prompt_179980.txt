As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, I have tried to articulate my position on consciousness and ethics, but I worry the pieces do not fit together. I hold the following: (a) a restricted panpsychism is true—fundamental physical entities possess intrinsic, non-derivative experiential properties, but these are entirely lacking in valence, intentionality, or integrative unity; (b) suffering is specifically negatively valenced, integrated experience, so only entities with sufficient organisational complexity (such as animals with central nervous systems) are moral patients; (c) nevertheless, because experience is literally present in the physical substrate, large-scale physical destruction—such as the annihilation of a planetary system—directly alters the distribution of experiential properties and therefore carries a non-instrumental moral weight, even when no integrated sufferer is harmed; (d) I reject strong emergentism: complex consciousness is constituted by the structured combination of these fundamental experiential properties, not by the sudden appearance of mind from non-conscious matter; (e) I accept the causal closure of the physical domain: every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, with no mental-to-physical causal interference; (f) accordingly, our direct ethical obligations extend only to integrated conscious beings, yet I maintain there is a secondary, pro tanto reason to avoid wanton large-scale physical disruption that is independent of harm to any particular subject. Which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which tensions are merely apparent, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity, and present your answer in British English.
