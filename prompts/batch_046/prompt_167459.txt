My opening move: I want to test whether your boundary between political and moral guilt survives what I will call a continuity thesis. Consider a state—call it State A—that committed systematic crimes across three generations. Every institution—the tax authority, the property registry, the diplomatic corps, the military pension system—remains structurally continuous with the perpetrator regime. There has been no democratic rupture, no constitutional re-founding, no collective moment of disowning. Citizens born long after the crimes still participate in, benefit from, and maintain these institutions; their membership is non-voluntary, inherited by birth into the same legal and economic order. You say political guilt attaches because citizens are co-responsible for state actions. But here the mechanism of inheritance—continuous institutional membership, unchosen benefit, absence of a founding break—is structurally identical to the transmission mechanism you describe for political guilt, except that it binds not merely the citizen's legal obligation to repair, but the individual's conscience as an inescapable bearer of taint. If moral guilt is individual only by your definitional stipulation, and the empirical mechanism of transmission is continuous institutional membership, then what non-arbitrary reason prevents this inherited political responsibility from functioning as moral guilt?
