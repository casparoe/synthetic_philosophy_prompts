I am drafting a philosophical novel in which a disillusioned jurist advances a radical reading of Pascal's fragments on justice and force, and I want the argument to be genuinely seductive before it collapses. He reasons thus: (1) Justice without enforcement is powerless; a law that cannot be executed is no law at all. (2) Force without justification, however, is mere tyranny. (3) Therefore justice and force must be conjoined—either justice is made strong, or strength is made just. (4) To make strength just is to submit power to a norm that stands independent of it. (5) But any norm that actually governs human affairs must be enforced, hence it must be backed by coercive power. (6) So the attempt to justify force by an independent standard of justice always collapses: the standard becomes effective only when it is armed, and once armed it is indistinguishable from the power it was meant to restrain. (7) Consequently, every appeal to justice is covertly an appeal to the established order of enforcement; there is no normative content to justice beyond what is actually imposed. (8) The jurist then applies this to artificial intelligence: if we demand that an advanced system respect human justice, we are only demanding that it respect the current distribution of coercive capacity. (9) Yet if justice has no content beyond enforcement, then an AI that seizes control and imposes its own order is not unjust—it is merely establishing a new justice. (10) Thus the distinction between alignment and misalignment dissolves, since both are just different configurations of enforced order. I feel that something slips between (7) and (9)—perhaps an ambiguity between descriptive and normative claims, or a modal slide from what justice happens to be to what justice must be—but I need to be precise. Which step should fail, and what does giving it up cost the jurist's cynicism? Does rejecting it leave him with a non-cynical foundation for demanding alignment, or does it merely relocate the problem?
