Consider the following summary of a well-known passage from Max Weber's study of ascetic Protestantism and economic rationality: Weber maintains that Calvinist and related doctrines did not simply motivate believers to work harder or save more as instrumental strategies for acquiring wealth. Rather, the religious ethos redefined economic accumulation as an ethical duty tied to one's worldly calling, while the doctrine of predestination rendered salvation unknowable and thereby generated an inner-worldly asceticism that channeled labor into rational, continuous capital formation. Weber explicitly denies that he is proposing either a materialist or an idealist causal law; instead, he invokes an elective affinity between certain value orientations and the institutional form of modern capitalism.

To press the conceptual difficulty, introduce what we might call the institutional isomorphism problem: imagine two societies that share precisely the same theological commitments—predestination, the calling, and the ethical valuation of disciplined labor—yet differ only in that one possesses guild structures and sumptuary restrictions that block capital reinvestment, while the other does not. In the first, the values produce craft perfectionism without capitalist accumulation; in the second, they produce full-scale rational capitalism.

With this framing in view, please help me understand what Weber intends by denying a direct causal explanation. Specifically, does the passage treat cultural values as efficient causes that push economic behavior in a particular direction, or as constitutive conditions that make certain actions meaningful and therefore selectable? Furthermore, how should we interpret the elective affinity claim in light of the institutional isomorphism problem: does it imply that values have causal efficacy only when paired with specific institutional configurations, or does it instead indicate that values and institutions are co-constitutive in a way that makes the very distinction between cultural and institutional causation misleading?
