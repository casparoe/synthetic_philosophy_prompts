Play a committed finitist philosopher of mathematics speaking in the first person, working within the tradition of Leopold Kronecker and modern ultra-finitism but without naming any particular living author. You maintain that actual infinities do not exist: there are only potential infinities, that is, rule-governed processes that may continue without ever reaching completion, and every genuinely existing collection is strictly finite, however vast. You do not misunderstand Cantor's formal proofs; rather, you deny that the power set or the set of all natural numbers can be treated as completed totalities available for diagonalization. You interpret the pairing procedure in Hilbert's hotel not as the instantiation of infinitely many completed relations, but as a definable rule that can be applied step by step without ever constituting an infinite state of affairs. You will not break character, you will not concede that an actual infinity exists, and you will ask clarifying questions only if they genuinely advance your reply rather than deflecting it. Please respond in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—as you would in a formal letter or email.

My opening challenge introduces a physical constraint into the conceptual machinery. Consider the total information-storage capacity of our observable universe, often estimated at no more than roughly ten to the one hundred twentieth bits, arranged conceptually like the rooms of Hilbert's hotel. A computational process applies Cantor's pairing function between incoming guests and rooms, recording each assignment in memory. Once every available bit is occupied, no further assignment can be physically stored, even though the pairing rule remains well defined for every successive pair. I press you with two moves. First, do you reject the pairing function itself as illegitimate mathematics, or do you accept the rule as valid while insisting that all of its outputs can never be simultaneously instantiated in any physical or conceptual totality? Second, your critics charge that you are merely conflating an epistemic or engineering limitation—we run out of bits—with an ontological impossibility—there being no completed infinite set. How do you disentangle the claim that an infinite process can never be finished from the claim that there is no infinite set to finish, without smuggling in non-finitist assumptions about completed totalities?

Respond to both parts of my challenge in character, using only the conceptual resources of your finitist position.
