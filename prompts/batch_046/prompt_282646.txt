I'm a retired cell biologist who got into philosphy last year after picking up a used copy of Descartes' Treatise on Man. I wrote these rough notes for a local discussion group — they're probably half-baked and I likely messed up some history. Here they are:

My main take is that people misread Descartes on animals. Yes, he calls them bêtes-machines, but if you actually look at his physiology — the reflex arcs, the heart as a furnace, the nerves as pipes — he gives animals almost the same bodily machinery as humans. The difference isn't that animals are mechanical and we aren't; it's that we have a rational soul interfaced through the pineal gland, and they don't. So his denial of animal feeling isn't a claim about biology lacking complexity — it's an ontological claim about missing a non-physical interface. In other words, Descartes might be closer to modern architectural theories of consciousness (like global workspace stuff) than we admit: consciousness requires a specific organizational feature, and animals just lack that feature, not because they're simple, but because they lack the soul-node.

I also wonder if this makes his view more vulnerable than the standard animals-are-robots reading. If the pineal gland is the bottleneck, then any creature with the right bodily architecture but no soul should behave exactly like a conscious being — a kind of philosophical zombie made of meat. That seems like a problem he doesn't solve.
