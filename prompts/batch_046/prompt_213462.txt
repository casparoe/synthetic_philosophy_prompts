I've been thinking on my own about why some moral practices—like slavery or dueling—don't merely become illegal, but eventually become hard for people to imagine defending at all. I keep encountering three kinds of explanation. One says powerful groups changed course because the practice stopped serving their material interests. Another says people were gradually persuaded by moral arguments that the practice had always been wrong. A third says practices tied to honor or social shame collapse when the social mechanisms that enforce them break down, regardless of whether anyone's private beliefs have changed.

But these stories feel too neat, and I want the full specification before I read further. What would a theory of moral revolution—how a norm actually dies—have to deliver to count as genuinely adequate?

Write out the desiderata: the complete checklist of what such a theory must account for, with the specific case or distinction behind each requirement. I want it to cover at least these hard cases and distinctions:

- The difference between a practice becoming prohibited by law and becoming genuinely unthinkable in mainstream moral discourse, and why a theory must explain both if they occur at different speeds.
- The phenomenon I think of as normative residue: when a practice vanishes, traces of its old logic survive inside the new moral order—like honor codes persisting after dueling is banned, or racial hierarchies being re-coded rather than erased after formal abolition.
- Rapid collapse versus slow erosion: dueling fading as a formal institution within roughly a generation in parts of Europe, versus slavery's defense being gradually marginalized over centuries.
- Cases where the practice ends mainly because the state monopolizes violence—such as blood feuds or public execution—rather than because citizens change their moral minds.
- The distinction between people privately changing their beliefs and changing only what they can publicly justify without social cost.

For each requirement, say why it is non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps. Then identify the single desideratum that eliminates the most popular explanations—whether that is the interest-based account, the rational-argument account, or the honor-and-shame dynamics view linked to thinkers like Bernard Williams or Kwame Anthony Appiah.

Also flag any pair of requirements that seem jointly unsatisfiable. For instance, does explaining rapid moral change require treating people as responsive to reason, while explaining why defenders of slavery clung to their views for so long require treating them as driven by non-rational social pressure? If some subset of the list cannot be satisfied together, say so explicitly—that tension is part of what I want to see.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring philosophers, though a brief note on how the three main approaches fare against the list is welcome. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
