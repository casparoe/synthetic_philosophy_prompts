In the philosophy-of-history workshop I am convening, we have reached a persistent impasse over Fernand Braudel’s distinction between geographical time, social time, and the time of events—specifically over whether the long-duration structures he identifies in his study of the Mediterranean basin constitute a distinct causal layer or merely a descriptive backdrop for political and military narratives. One faction argues that Braudel’s mountains, climate patterns, and agricultural rhythms possess what we have begun calling “structural inertia”: they do not determine specific outcomes, but they asymmetrically constrain the space of possible events, so that explaining the failure of Philip II’s armada requires reference not only to naval tactics and court politics but also to the wind patterns and coastal geographies that made sustained supply lines implausible over long durations. The opposing faction insists that this framing illicitly reifies temporal scale into causal power; they grant that climate and geography are slow-moving conditions, but maintain that all explanatory work is ultimately done by agents responding to incentives within those conditions, and that attributing causal autonomy to “geographical time” is either metaphorical or redundant with standard environmental-history explanations.

What makes the deadlock especially frustrating is that both sides accept Braudel’s explicit rejection of environmental determinism, both acknowledge the same archival evidence about sixteenth-century logistics, and both agree that a purely event-centered narrative misses something important about the period. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about empirical history at all, but about what an adequate explanation must include—whether mentioning a long-duration constraint counts as explaining an event or merely contextualizing it, with one side treating explanation as narrative integration and the other requiring mechanistic transmission. Yet I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is instead a hidden substantive dispute about modal causation or about which temporal scales count as data for historical testing, disguised as a methodological quarrel. Could you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement—whether it is verbal, about standards of evidence, about bedrock intuitions regarding historical agency, or about an unstated formal distinction between constraint and cause—and indicate what, if anything, would genuinely move it: a conceptual clarification about structural causation, a formal model of multi-scale explanation, or perhaps the recognition that the question is ill-posed because it conflates ontology with historiographical ordering?
