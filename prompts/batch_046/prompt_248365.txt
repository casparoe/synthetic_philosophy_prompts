I've been thinking about art markets lately after talking with a friend who works in gallery conservation, and she mentioned this weird practice that a few auction houses have started doing: they sell what they call 'material-continuty replicas', which are works built by the original artist's studio using leftover pigments, the exact same brushes, even wood from the same tree that went into the original canvas, but produced after the artist died. They issue a certificate saying it's not the original but is materially continous, and they price it at about sixty percent of the original's value. At first I thought this only makes sense if you believe the value of an original comes mainly from the causal chain of production rather than the visual result, but now I'm not sure. What philosophical commitments about authenticity and value does this institutional practice actually presuppose? Can it stay consistent if you think the price gap should be much larger, or does it quietly depend on some kind of 'aura' theory where the physical materials carry non-visible worth? And is the practice compatible with a purely relational view where the original's status is just social agreement, or does the pricing structure reveal an implicit essentialist claim about objects? I want to know whether the policy collapses if you reject non-visible properties altogether, or if it's just marketing dressed up in philosophy.
