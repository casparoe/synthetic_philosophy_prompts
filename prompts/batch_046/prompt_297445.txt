Compare Daniel Dennett's intentional stance with a hypothetical representational-stability stance for attributing beliefs to artificial systems. Under Dennett's view, belief attribution is warranted whenever rationalizing an agent's behavior yields predictive and explanatory success, with no requirement that internal representational structures mirror the attributed content. By contrast, the representational-stability stance demands that attributed beliefs correspond to internal representational configurations that remain stable across counterfactual input perturbations and maintain coherence with other attributed mental states, even when behavioral prediction alone would succeed.

Apply this comparison specifically to large language models that generate contextually appropriate, rational-seeming discourse about empirical matters—such as weather patterns or political events—without possessing persistent internal states across interaction sessions. For each stance, determine whether such a system genuinely possesses beliefs about these topics or whether attribution is merely a predictive projection. Then isolate the precise divergence point: not predictive utility, but the grounding condition for mental attribution—behavioral regularity versus internal representational coherence.
