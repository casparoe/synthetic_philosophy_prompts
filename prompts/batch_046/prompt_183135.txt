I've been leading a graduate seminar on collective decision-making, and our months-long discussion of Sen's liberal paradox has stalled in a way that feels structural rather than merely stubborn. One group treats the result as a formal demonstration that even minimal individual sovereignty over personal choices is incompatible with Pareto efficiency and unrestricted domain, concluding that liberal values are collectively incoherent unless we abandon one condition. The other group accepts the derivation entirely but dismisses its force, arguing that real liberal rights function as lexical constraints or domain restrictions rather than as orderings over social states, so the paradox never applies to the institutions liberals actually defend. They agree on the mathematics, they agree that rights matter, and yet neither has revised a single premise.

My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is not about liberalism's plausibility but about what kind of object a "right" is inside a social-choice framework—whether it protects a sphere of preference-satisfaction or instead blocks certain comparative judgments altogether—and that both sides are answering different questions under the same name. But I am unsure whether the disagreement is instead verbal, whether a hidden formal issue about appropriate domain restrictions is doing the work, or whether the question is ill-posed because "liberalism" bundles incompatible commitments.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it substantive, verbal, empirical, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about individual sovereignty that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about how to model rights without reducing them to preferences—driving the impasse? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction between types of rights, a formal result about restricted domains, an institutional case study showing how democracies avoid the conflict, or an argument that the paradox should be dissolved rather than solved?
