I am preparing the final examination for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, which this term has traced the problem of mechanistic explanation through self-reference, behavioral rigidity, and the conditions for genuine agency. In our unit on Douglas Hofstadter’s notion of sphexishness—the mechanical repetition of a behavioral script regardless of contextual irrelevance, exemplified by the digger wasp that will endlessly re-check its burrow if the prey is displaced—we did not stop at the familiar contrast between rigid instinct and human flexibility. Instead, I pressed the class with a distinction I introduced in our penultimate meeting: first-order sphexishness, in which an organism executes a fixed routine, and second-order sphexishness, in which an organism rigidly executes a meta-rule that governs when and how to deviate from its first-order routines. The conceptual knot is whether second-order rigidity collapses back into first-order rigidity, whether it introduces a meaningful form of flexibility that blocks mechanistic reduction, or whether the entire sphexishness/freedom distinction is merely descriptive-level relative rather than ontologically substantive.

For the exam, please draft one essay question that students can answer in seventy-five minutes, aiming for approximately 900 to 1,100 words. The question must not be answerable by reciting a summary of the wasp example and declaring that humans are adaptable therefore free. Rather, it should require students to adjudicate whether an agent whose deviations are themselves governed by an unmodifiable rigidity-condition—say, a being that alters its path only when a hard-wired threshold is triggered, and never otherwise—possesses anything deserving the name of flexibility, and whether such a case undermines or merely complicates the mechanist’s claim that all behavior is sphexish at some appropriate level of analysis.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, with the time and word constraints clearly stated; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words that demonstrates the specific moves an A-grade response must make, including a clear stance on whether second-order sphexishness is genuinely different in kind; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 points that assigns specific marks to each required conceptual move, names the precise mistakes that should cost marks—particularly the failure to address the meta-rule problem or the conflation of behavioral variability with ontological freedom—and distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by identifying which moves are present versus absent. The best answers should show that they understand why injecting flexibility-rules does not automatically dissolve mechanistic determination, and should say something precise about whether self-modifying rigidity remains rigidity at the level that matters for free will.
