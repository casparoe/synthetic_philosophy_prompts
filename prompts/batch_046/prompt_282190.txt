In my philosophy of social science seminar, we have reached an impasse over whether demographic cohorts—Baby Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z—constitute real social kinds or are merely reified statistical bins, and the stall has a specific shape I want diagnosed. The sociologists point to persistent, cross-contextual patterns in economic behavior and political attitude clustered by birth years; the philosophers respond that temporal correlation does not establish kindhood, that the boundaries are arbitrarily drawn around census years rather than natural joints, and that “Millennial” lacks stable identity conditions across national or class contexts. Both sides accept the same survey data and the same demographic trends, yet neither has moved in weeks. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not genuinely ontological but methodological: one camp treats cohort membership as an explanatory variable whose predictive utility is sufficient for kindhood, while the other demands modal robustness—counterfactual stability under perturbations to birth-rate distributions and life-stage effects—without conceding that predictive success could ever satisfy that demand. Is this correct, or is there a hidden empirical question about whether intra-cohort variance systematically exceeds inter-cohort variance once life-stage and period effects are separated, which neither side has actually measured? Diagnose the disagreement using what each camp actually says about explanatory power, boundary conditions, and counterfactual stability. Is it verbal—centered on competing uses of “generation”—substantive about social ontology, or are the two sides answering different questions, one causal-explanatory and the other metaphysical, under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal criterion for social kindhood applied specifically to temporally indexed groups, an empirical result showing that cohort effects vanish under adequate controls, or a conceptual distinction—perhaps between diachronic social roles and synchronic demographic aggregates—that dissolves the dispute? I want the diagnosis supported by the actual structure of each position, together with an honest assessment of whether any distinction, experiment, or formal result could actually end the stalemate, or whether the question is ill-posed from the start.
