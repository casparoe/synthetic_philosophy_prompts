I am an undergraduate studying philosophy of mind and aesthetics, and I have become stuck on the paradox of fiction. The familiar formulation runs as follows: we appear to feel genuine pity, fear, or sorrow for characters and events we know to be entirely invented, yet standard accounts of emotion seem to require that the subject believes the object is real and that the emotion carries some behavioural exit condition—an impulse to act that is conspicuously absent when we weep in a cinema. The literature I have encountered offers what look like three entrenched positions. One holds that our responses are not full-blooded emotions but rather simulated affective states sustained by a framework of pretence in which the audience knowingly adopts fictional premises; another treats them as genuine affective states generated by mental simulation of the represented situation; and a third argues that the paradox dissolves once we reject the belief-condition altogether and allow emotions to be directed at mere representations.

Despite decades of refinement, the debate seems to have reached an impasse: every argument for one view has a well-rehearsed reply, and no empirical result or formal distinction has clearly shifted anyone. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but taxonomic. The sides may be answering different questions—one about the phenomenological quality of the feeling, another about its causal-functional profile, another about its intentional object—while using the same word emotion to cover all three. I want to test this, but I also want to consider an alternative I have not seen discussed in standard surveys: that the disagreement is masked by an implicit dispute over whether an affective state lacking any behavioural exit condition—no tendency to assist the character, flee the theatre, or alter the plot—can count as the same kind of state as its real-world counterpart, or whether that absence transforms it into a categorically different phenomenon, perhaps closer to an aesthetic attitude than to an interpersonal emotion.

To make this concrete, consider a hypothetical case: a reader spends several weeks in genuine insomnia and appetite suppression after finishing a tragic novel, symptoms that mirror clinical grief, yet consistently reports that she never believed the events occurred and feels no impulse to intervene in the fictional world. Does this case support the realist about fictional emotion, undermine the pretence-based account, or simply show that functional profiles can diverge without changing ontological kind?
