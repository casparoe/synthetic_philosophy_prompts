I was arguing with my roomate last week about whether we should actually care about insects, and it made me want my philospohy workshop to feel that tension rather than just talk about it. I'm running a session for about 14-18 grad students, 75 minutes, on moral status and scale. The specific idea I want them to hit is the scale argument for insect sentience: even if any given ant has only a tiny credence of being sentient, the astronomical numbers might make the expected moral weight enormous. But I also want them to feel why that conclusion feels intuitively wrong — there's this weird gap between what you feel about one ant and what the aggregation math says you should feel about trillions.

Could you design an in-class game or structured deliberation that forces them into that gap themselves? I'm imagining something with a "probabilistic patient budget": groups get cards representing populations with different sentience probabilities and sizes (humans near-certain but few, chickens moderate and billions, insects near-zero per-capita but countless), plus a fixed pool of protection points they must divide. Maybe there's a hidden draw after they've locked in their allocation that reveals actual sentient individuals in each category based on the assigned probabilities, so they see the concrete results of their choice. The rules should make them construct the allocation, notice the mathematical pull, and only then learn what the conflict is called. Don't lecture it — let them discover the contradiction in their own bargaining.
