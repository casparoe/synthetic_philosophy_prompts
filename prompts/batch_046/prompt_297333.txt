Read Kurosawa's Rashomon through the problem of cross-cultural variation in moral intuition, specifically the divergence between East Asian and Western responses in experiments on intention-outcome asymmetry and the contested replication of the so-called Knobe effect. The film stages four mutually incompatible narrations of a single assault and death—those of the bandit Tajomaru, the wife, the dead samurai speaking through a medium, and the woodcutter—without ever granting the viewer an authoritative version. I want an interpretation that treats this narrative structure not merely as epistemic uncertainty but as a philosophical argument about whether moral cognition is irreducibly indexed to social role, gendered honor codes, and self-preserving framing. Does Rashomon suggest that there is no culture-transcendent standpoint from which to adjudicate the contradictions, or does it instead reveal a universal psychological bias—self-deception—that manifests differently across social positions? Be precise about scenes: the medium's trance-like testimony, the woodcutter's quietly revised account at the gate, the bandit's swaggering self-exoneration, and the way the ruined Rashomon gate frames each flashback with shifting light and contradictory close-ups. I am writing this for a graduate seminar in moral epistemology, and I need the reading to stay honest in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of either moral universalism or relativism, and do not distort the philosophical literature to fit the cinematography. In particular, I am interested in whether the film's refusal of narrative closure can be read as a critique of the method of cases in ethics—specifically, whether it shows that treating intuitions as atomistic, decontextualized data points misses the narrative standpoint-dependence that cross-cultural surveys actually measure. What does the film add to the philosophical debate that an argument about intuition variability alone cannot capture?
