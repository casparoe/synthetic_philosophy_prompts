I am writing from my high school philosophy seminar, where we have been discussing moral luck. One classmate keeps repeating the line, "If you didn't control it, you can't be blamed for it," to argue that students should not be penalized for outcomes shaped by random institutional assignment—such as being paired with an incompetent lab partner or receiving a more difficult exam version—because responsibility requires control and these results depend partly on luck. Before our next session I want to understand precisely what principle she is invoking. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could express, ranging from the tautological claim about excuses to the radical thesis that luck undermines all moral assessment? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and specify which version actually supports excusing students in these particular cases versus which versions overreach and would also excuse negligence, recklessness, or willful ignorance. Present the answer as a menu of readings I can bring to class, with clear indications of which ones do the argumentative work she needs and which ones fail under scrutiny.
