The debate between endurantism and perdurantism over how material objects persist through time has stalled for decades despite broad agreement on the formal apparatus: one side treats objects as three-dimensional entities wholly present at each time, the other as four-dimensional spacetime worms composed of temporal parts. Both sides acknowledge temporary intrinsic change—an object being straight then bent—and both claim to respect ordinary judgments about identity over time, yet no argument has moved the opposing camp. Is this stalemate substantive, or is it verbal—hinging on whether "the same object" requires spatiotemporal continuity—or are the two camps actually answering different questions under the single heading "persistence": one asking what ontology is required to ground temporary properties (the temporary intrinsics problem), the other asking which framework best systematizes our pre-theoretic tracking practices? Diagnose the stall using what each side actually commits to about change, part-whole relations, and identity conditions, not by surveying interpretations. Is there a hidden formal disagreement—say, about whether stage theory genuinely resolves the temporary intrinsics problem without hidden ontological cost—or is the split driven by bedrock intuitions about whether an object can be literally present at non-contiguous times? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates the ontological claim from the explanatory burden, a formal result showing inter-translatability or collapse, a clarification of which phenomena are non-negotiable data, or should we conclude that the dispute is ill-posed and abandon it?
