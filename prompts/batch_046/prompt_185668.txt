Exam question: "Distinguish Berlin’s negative liberty, Berlin’s positive liberty, and Pettit’s republican liberty (non-domination). Then apply all three to a warehouse worker whose shifts are governed by an algorithm that retains full discretionary authority to change them at any moment but, in practice, never does so and rarely monitors her. For each conception, state whether her situation is condemned, and be precise about whether interference, mastery, or domination is present."

Student answer:
"Negative liberty is freedom from external interference or obstacles. Since the algorithm never actually changes the worker’s schedule, blocks her movements, or prevents her from leaving, there is no direct interference; negative liberty is largely intact, though the looming possibility may cause anxiety. Positive liberty is about self-mastery and being one’s own master, which can take the form of rational self-direction or collective democratic self-rule. The worker does not control the rules that govern her labor, and the algorithm is not accountable to her, so she clearly lacks autonomy and positive liberty is undermined. Republican liberty, as Pettit develops it, is essentially a variant of collective positive liberty: it requires that people share in democratic governance so that no one is subject to another’s arbitrary will. Because domination, on this view, requires actual interference—someone actively imposing their will—and because the algorithm retains power but never exercises it, there is no domination here. Therefore republican liberty is satisfied, and only positive liberty is genuinely violated."
