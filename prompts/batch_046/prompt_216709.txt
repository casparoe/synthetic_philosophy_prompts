I recently relocated for professional reasons to a jurisdiction where rapid economic growth and stringent public order are visibly maintained without democratic accountability, and I find myself genuinely unsure whether the prosperity I observe can ground any form of political obligation or whether I am merely rationalizing my own comfort. Before I form a settled view, I want the full specification for a theory of performance-based political legitimacy—particularly one that aims to justify authoritarian rule in both large continental party-states and small city-states with strong legal-rational traditions. List the desiderata: the phenomena the theory must explain (for instance, why citizens might accept non-democratic rule during sustained growth but withdraw consent during stagnation, and why high performance in one regime correlates with institutional durability while in another it correlates with brittle personalization); the distinctions it must preserve (between legitimacy and mere stability or revealed preference, between collective prosperity and individual flourishing, between human bureaucratic discretion and algorithmic governance, and between input authorization and output authorization); and the comparative-political regularities it cannot contradict (such as the highly variable survival rates of performance-based regimes after economic shocks, or the divergence in corruption trajectories despite similar growth rates). For each desideratum, state the motivating case or conceptual reason behind it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable—such as the prohibition on circularly defining legitimacy as whatever happens to produce prosperity, or the demand that the theory not collapse the distinction between order maintained by law and order maintained by predictive surveillance—and which are preferences of particular camps, such as whether the theory must accommodate rights of exit, voice, or participatory feedback mechanisms. Identify the single desideratum that eliminates the largest share of existing accounts, and say whether any subset of the non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given what we know about regime dynamics and information economics. Finally, briefly score the standard contenders—developmental-state theories, East Asian exceptionalist narratives, and pure technocratic-output accounts—against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than on the scorecard.
