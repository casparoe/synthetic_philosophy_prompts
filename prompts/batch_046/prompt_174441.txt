I'm working with a small interdisciplinary group—philosophers of science and archaeological theorists—trying to decide where conceptual work on inference from material culture can actually move the needle over the next three years, with no new excavation budget and only limited researcher time. Our discussions keep returning to a persistent tension: archaeologists routinely reconstruct beliefs, norms, and institutions from assemblages of objects, yet the inferential path from, say, a ceramic distribution, burial orientation, or boundary marker to a specific claim about kinship obligation, cosmological commitment, or legal authority seems radically underdetermined in some domains and surprisingly constrained in others. Rather than surveying interpretive debates or offering a bibliography, I'm trying to identify which open conceptual problems are genuinely upstream of the rest.

To focus the question, let me propose a framing that may not be standard in the literature: there appears to be an "underdetermination gradient" across types of institutional claims. For structural features—trade networks, settlement hierarchies, resource allocation—the material record often supplies relatively robust comparative and negative constraints. For intentional or normative features—the specific meaning of a ritual deposit, the authoritative status of a symbol system, the conceptual content of a norm embodied in spatial layout—the same assemblage frequently seems compatible with multiple, mutually incompatible interpretations, and absence of expected material traces rarely licenses strong negative conclusions about belief. If this gradient is real rather than illusory, then clarifying its scope and managing it may be prior to resolving particular interpretive disputes.

Given that framing, which open questions or lines of conceptual work should take priority? I'm particularly interested in three candidates: first, whether we can formalize the conditions under which material absence counts as genuine evidence against an institutional belief (the negative-evidence problem); second, whether developing aggregation rules from micro-artifact patterns to macro-institutional structures is tractable without collapsing into either naive functionalism or unfalsifiable hermeneutics; and third, whether the gradient itself reflects a permanent epistemic limit or merely a temporary artifact of underdeveloped middle-range theory. Please defend an ordering: for each, specify the criteria you are using—downstream impact on interpretive practice, tractability for philosophers rather than field specialists, dependency of other debates on its resolution—note the key trade-offs between them, and indicate what kind of result (a conceptual distinction, a formal constraint, a revision to evidentiary standards) would cause you to reorder the list. I want a defended agenda for where limited conceptual effort should go, not a survey or a reading list.
