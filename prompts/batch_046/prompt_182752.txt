I'm putting together a philosophical fiction workshop on affective experience and want a prompt that forces a model to navigate the conceptual tension in pain asymbolia without falling back on generic thought-experiment templates. Write a short story—roughly 800 to 1,000 words—set in a near-future clinical context where a procedure called Affective Decoupling is available: it preserves full nociceptive discrimination and reflexive withdrawal but suppresses the evaluative suffering-component, producing a state functionally identical to the documented neurological phenomenon of pain asymbolia. The protagonist, Elian, underwent this procedure years ago and now serves as the sole primary caregiver for a younger sibling who suffers from an untreatable genetic disorder causing relentless, severe nociceptive suffering—the precise inverse profile.

Structure the narrative around a concrete incident: during care, Elian sustains a significant but non-life-threatening injury—perhaps a deep laceration or a second-degree burn—and responds with calm, analytical attention rather than distress, which draws moral judgment from a secondary character such as a hospice nurse or a religious family member who interprets the reaction as either monstrous empathy-deficit or saintly transcendence. The central dramatic choice should be an offered elective reversal procedure that would restore Elian's capacity for suffering but might compromise their functional presence for the sibling; the story should stage this not as an abstract dilemma but through lived physical detail, silences, and practical constraints. End ambiguously, leaving unresolved whether the badness of pain is intrinsic to the sensation or a separable evaluative layer, and whether Elian's decoupled state makes them a more ethical or a less fully human caregiver. Avoid explicit philosophical essays in dialogue; let the contradiction reside in action, bodily description, and the gap between what Elian feels and what others expect them to feel.
