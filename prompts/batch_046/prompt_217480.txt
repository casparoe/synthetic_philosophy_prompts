Please argue that the moral value of Plutarch's Parallel Lives and the broader hagiographic tradition lies not in historical accuracy but in a specific epistemic function: they act as extreme-value anchors that correct for base-rate neglect in moral learning. The claim should be that by pairing Greek and Roman lives and systematically exaggerating virtues, these texts construct what we might call a virtue reference distribution — setting the upper tail of possible moral achievement far enough from the median that learners do not compress their ambitions to locally available exemplars. Make the case that this distortion is a feature, not a bug: it solves a cross-generational coordination problem by transmitting ambitious norms that median biography would filter out. Then address the standard objection that unrealistic ideals demotivate ordinary people, and show why that objection mistakes statistical representativeness for normative anchoring — in other words, that a reference point need not be probable to be functional. Keep the argument entirely internal; do not ask for bibliographies, specific editions, or citations to secondary scholarship.
