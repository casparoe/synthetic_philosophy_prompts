I work as the senior commissioning editor for a quarterly interdisciplinary review that publishes long-form essays at the intersection of environmental history and political ecology. Two years ago our editorial board adopted a mandate that every feature must exhibit what we term structural depth: the narrative must engage with processes—climatic, geographical, or institutional—that outlast individual lives and discrete political events. We now receive roughly thirty unsolicited pitches monthly, and I have approximately forty minutes to triage each proposal before deciding whether to send it to peer review, request revision, or reject it outright.

To do this consistently, I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered sequence of tests, with explicit stopping rules and flagged normative checkpoints—that operationalizes Fernand Braudel's well-known distinction among geographical time, social time, and the time of events, without requiring that I or my staff be professional historiographers. The procedure should specify, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer, where a negative answer produces an automatic rejection, where it produces an automatic advance, and where the answer forces me into an irreducibly value-laden judgment about editorial mission rather than factual adequacy. Because our board is particularly concerned with scale-jumping—narratives that claim long-term structural significance but derive all their evidence from diplomatic minutes or personal correspondence—the procedure must include a specific filter against conflating the biography of an institution with the biography of an individual. Please apply the procedure to two concrete proposals currently on my desk: first, an essay arguing that the gradual desiccation of the Aral Sea between the 1960s and the 2010s reshaped Central Asian pastoral economies and state boundaries across successive regimes; second, a proposed biography of a single Soviet irrigation engineer, active from 1952 to 1988, which claims to reveal the structural logic of hydraulic socialism through one career trajectory. I want you to show me exactly where the procedure advances or rejects each case, including at least one outcome that runs against my intuitive preference—perhaps advancing the biography despite its individual scale or rejecting the Aral Sea essay for lacking narrative accessibility—while explaining what the misfire reveals about the procedure's built-in biases. Finally, be explicit about the procedure's hard limits: it should not evaluate prose style, political sympathy, or contemporary relevance, and it should acknowledge the points at which my own training in environmental history makes neutral application impossible.
