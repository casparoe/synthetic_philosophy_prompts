I am writing a paper for my philosophy of law seminar on moral responsibility, and a heated discussion with a classmate—who insists that determinism makes retributive punishment morally incoherent—has left me wanting a document that maps the disagreement precisely rather than replaying it. Please draft the joint statement that a Dennett-style compatibilist defender of just deserts and a Caruso-style free-will skeptic who advocates a public-health quarantine model of criminal justice might produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The document should begin with a numbered section of claims both parties can sign without reservation: for instance, agreements about what neuroscience reveals regarding predictive processing in voluntary action, about the social need to protect citizens from dangerous behavior, and about the conceptual difference between suffering imposed as moral repayment and suffering accepted as a side-effect of risk-management. Next, present the residual disagreements in a table format, with each position phrased in wording its own side would accept. The Dennett side should maintain that moral responsibility survives determinism and that punishment can function as part of a legitimate moral conversation without requiring libertarian free will; the Caruso side should deny that determinism is the sole obstacle, reject desert as a legitimate reason to inflict hardship, and treat dangerous individuals analogously to carriers of infectious disease who may be isolated for public safety but not blamed. Identify the cruxes—particularly whether long-term confinement in a non-punitive therapeutic facility can avoid smuggling in retributive intent, and whether abandoning desert-language strips the practice of its expressive or communicative function. For each crux, propose a concrete institutional design—say, a secure residential facility with transparent admission criteria based solely on risk assessment, a fixed review protocol, and explicit prohibitions against moralizing language in staff training—and record in advance what each side predicts: the compatibilist should say whether such a design still counts as punishment and whether it preserves the moral conversation, while the skeptic should say whether it genuinely escapes retribution or merely disguises it.

Please be explicit about what argument or evidence would change your assessment—whether empirical findings on recidivism under therapeutic versus punitive regimes, or a philosophical demonstration that desert-talk is conceptually ineliminable from any practice of accountability. Also indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail, especially if you misrepresent either philosopher's actual stance on reactive attitudes, or if the institutional scenario you construct assumes normative premises that one side would reject.
