In cognitive neuroscience, it is standard institutional practice to assign discrete psychological functions—fear processing, face recognition, temporal sequencing—to specific brain regions on the basis of focal lesion deficits, and to treat those localizations as genuine explanatory achievements worthy of clinical and theoretical weight. At the same time, the very same discipline routinely advances theoretical frameworks—predictive coding, global workspace dynamics, or enactive cognition—according to which these capacities are distributed across large-scale networks, heavily context-dependent, and not the proprietary output of any single anatomical part. The two practices do not merely coexist as temporary methodological compromises; they function as parallel norms within the same journals, funding criteria, and clinical diagnostic manuals, so that a researcher may defend a lesion-mapping study in one context and endorse strong emergence in another without the tension being treated as a crisis.

What philosophical commitments about reduction and emergence does this dual practice presuppose? Does it implicitly rely on a layered ontology in which mechanistic decomposition is valid at one level of organization while emergence is real only at a higher one, and if so, what determines the boundary between those levels? Or does the practice presuppose that emergence is purely epistemic—an artifact of our current modeling limitations rather than an ontological feature of neural organization—thereby allowing localization to be treated as permanently valid regardless of theoretical rhetoric? Can a robust non-reductive emergentist consistently participate in lesion-deficit mapping without contradiction, or does the practice quietly rule out strong emergence by treating parts as causally and conceptually prior to the whole? Furthermore, is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that both a reductive mechanist and a weak emergentist could license it, but on radically different grounds? And if the field genuinely replaced its implicit commitment with a view according to which cognitive properties are genuinely non-decomposable and downward-causing, what specific elements of the practice—grant review standards, the taxonomy of clinical disorders, or the very concept of a localized "function"—would have to be abandoned or radically reconceived?
