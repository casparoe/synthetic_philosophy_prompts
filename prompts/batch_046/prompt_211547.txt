I've been working through the standard philosophy-of-biology debates on defining life, and I've noticed what looks like a clean pattern in the familiar borderline cases. Viruses realize replication and evolutionary adaptation but lack autonomous metabolism; synthetic minimal cells achieve metabolism and bounded homeostasis but carry no evolutionary history of selection; prions replicate but possess neither cellular boundaries nor metabolic activity. From this pattern, I've come to think that "life" is best treated not as a binary natural kind with necessary and sufficient conditions, but as a graded cluster concept composed of three largely independent dimensions—autonomous metabolism, bounded homeostasis, and open-ended evolutionary capacity—where an entity can be alive to the degree that it realizes one or two of these without the others.

To test whether this graded framework is coherent, I've been considering a deliberately engineered synthetic system—call it an externally-dependent protocell: a lipid-vesicle compartment that maintains internal pH, synthesizes its own membrane proteins, and exhibits dissipative chemical organization, yet relies entirely on an exogenously supplied pool of ribozymes for replication and is structurally incapable of accumulating heritable mutations. Given that this entity clearly satisfies the metabolic and homeostatic dimensions while entirely lacking evolutionary autonomy, should we classify it as genuinely "partially alive," alive in precisely those two respects? Or does this framing rest on a deeper confusion—namely, that these three criteria can actually be separated and satisfied in isolation—such that treating them as additive dimensions misses whatever makes the concept of life conceptually unified rather than merely a checklist?
