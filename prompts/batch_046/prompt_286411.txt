I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar on the metaphysics of time, and we've spent the past three weeks working through presentism versus eternalism. Our discussions kept returning to whether the B-series—tenseless relations of before and after—can carry the full explanatory load, or whether the A-series—pastness, presentness, and futurity—forces an ontological privilege onto a single moment. For our final session I want to present a poem that stages this tension structurally rather than merely describing it, something that lets the form do part of the philosophical labor.

I'd like a sonnet, strict or loose, with a clear volta around line nine. The octave should build the eternalist's block-universe picture: every event lying equally real, ordered only by earlier-than and later-than relations, with change treated as variation across a four-dimensional manifold. Use the rhyme and rhythm of these eight lines to give the view a kind of static, architectural solidity—perhaps rhyming pairs that feel locked in place. At the volta, introduce the presentist's challenge: if all times exist alike, what distinguishes my present now from any other point in the manifold? The sestet should press the A-series objection—maybe through the image of a spotlight that seems to move across an otherwise uniformly lit stage—without resolving the dispute. The closing couplet should leave the two positions suspended, ideally pairing a rhyme word like "now" with one like "then" to show that they have not been reconciled.

One further constraint: I want the poem to allude, without turning didactic, to the familiar problem that in special relativity there is no absolute simultaneity, so the presentist's privileged "now" seems frame-dependent. The eternalist's confidence in the block should be subtly undercut by a line that hints at this relativization of simultaneity—something a student of philosophy of time would recognize—while the presentist's spotlight should feel threatened by the same idea. Please avoid any bibliographic references, citations, or reading lists; I want the poem to stand on its conceptual architecture alone, accurate enough that someone who knows the debate sees the moves being made, but accessible enough that the poem works even if the listener has never read the literature.
