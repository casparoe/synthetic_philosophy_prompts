I am a novelist applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship that supports conceptual research on artistic practice, and I need a polished two-page proposal for a project that examines whether Douglas Hofstadter’s knob-twiddling model of creativity—where intelligent variation is understood as parameter adjustment within a conceptual space—can be rigorously extended to narrative fiction without losing its explanatory power. My half-formed hypothesis is that fiction requires not only positive adjustments but also what I call negative knobs: dimensions that become creatively productive precisely when they are suppressed, inverted, or removed rather than dialed upward, such as withholding psychological interiority, collapsing moral causality, or flattening temporal sequence. The existing treatments of Hofstadter’s framework remain largely confined to mathematics, music, and analogical reasoning, while literary theory discusses intertextuality and thematic variation without formalizing them in parameter-space terms; consequently, no one has established whether narrative creativity is reducible to multi-knob tweaking or depends on a distinct logic of strategic absence.

The proposal should state the precise research question—does the knob-twiddling model adequately capture literary invention, and if not, what structural revision is required?—and explain why current positions in cognitive aesthetics and philosophy of art leave it open. It should outline a conceptual, non-empirical method: constructing a taxonomy of narrative parameters (character consistency, causal density, moral symmetry, temporal linearity, and their interrelations), then analyzing a series of rigorous thought experiments in which each parameter is isolated, combined, or negatively adjusted to determine whether meaningful thematic variation can occur without altering any positive feature. The proposal should justify this approach over obvious alternatives—such as empirical corpus analysis or pure historical comparison—by arguing that the core issue is structural, not statistical. It should specify what a successful result looks like: a refined taxonomy distinguishing surface variation (single-parameter change) from structural recalibration (reconfiguration of relationships between parameters, including through suppression), together with clear criteria for when a narrative change counts as derivative versus genuinely creative. It should also define explicit failure conditions—for example, if all negative adjustments reduce to hidden positive adjustments on an unobserved parameter, or if the revised model collapses into standard intertextual accounts with no added predictive or explanatory value. The deliverable should be a thirty-page conceptual essay suitable for philosophers of art and practicing writers, with a realistic scope for part-time work over six months; please do not include a bibliography or citation list, as I will supply all references myself. Finally, the proposal should articulate the practical value for novelists: a clearer vocabulary for how invention actually operates at the level of form, not merely content.
