I'm preparing a long-form feature on how philosophers formalize belief revision when evidence arrives with degrees of uncertainty rather than as outright observation. A colleague in science writing summarized Richard Jeffrey's rule of conditionalization for me, and I need to verify the technical details before I include it. Here is the gist of what they said: "Jeffrey conditionalization fixes a gap in standard Bayesianism. Bayes' rule demands that you update on evidence assigned probability one, but in practice we often learn experiences with confidence somewhere between zero and one. Jeffrey's solution is to assign the experience a new credence q and then redistribute probabilities across an exhaustive partition of possibilities, holding the ratios inside each cell constant—the so-called rigidity condition. Because the internal ratios are preserved, hypotheses that were tied within a cell remain tied. This means the rule can handle old evidence—beliefs you've held for years at high but imperfect confidence—without forcing you to pretend you ever observed them with certainty. Moreover, the update is strictly local: if a proposition is genuinely probabilistically independent of the evidence, its credence should remain untouched, which makes Jeffrey updating more conservative than ordinary conditioning, since only beliefs within the partition's reach can shift."
