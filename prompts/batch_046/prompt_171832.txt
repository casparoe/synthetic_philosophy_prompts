I have been working through the moral philosophy of acausal trade—the scenario in which rational agents, separated by causal boundaries but linked by shared decision algorithms or logical structure, can affect each other's outcomes through correlation alone. The standard treatment in decision-theoretic ethics treats this as an extension of the prisoner's dilemma: because my reasoning process determines that of a sufficiently similar counterpart, cooperation becomes instrumentally rational in expectation, but remains fundamentally a strategic calculation about my own utility across possible worlds.

I want you to argue, however, that this instrumental framing is conceptually inadequate and that acausal symmetry generates direct moral obligations rather than merely efficient correlations. Defend the position that when an agent's decision procedure is genuinely identical across causally isolated worlds, the distinction between self and counterpart collapses in a way that undermines individualist moral agency; consequently, the agent ought to adopt a universalizability constraint that treats the welfare of all algorithmically linked counterparts as falling within the scope of direct duty, not as an externality to be optimized. Address the objection that obligation requires causal interaction, contractual agreement, or the possibility of reciprocity in a physical sense, and explain why logical correlation might nonetheless ground a non-instrumental commitment. Please keep the argument self-contained and formal: do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid relying on obscure named doctrines, but feel free to draw on familiar resources in Kantian ethics and rational choice theory to build the case from first principles.
