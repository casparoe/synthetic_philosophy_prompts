I am a high school student enrolled in an advanced independent-study course on philosophy of science, and over the past month I have been working through Pierre Duhem’s The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory with the help of my instructor. In one particularly dense section, Duhem argues that an experiment in physics can never condemn a single, isolated hypothesis by itself; rather, it tests an entire interconnected theoretical system. He further claims that when the system’s predictions conflict with observation, logic alone does not dictate which auxiliary assumption or theoretical component must be abandoned—this decision falls instead to what he terms good sense, an intuitive judgment about simplicity and coherence rather than a formal rule. I find the argument compelling, but I am struggling to see exactly where logical constraint ends and conventional choice begins.

My confusion has become sharper because I recently read a popular-science article describing how climate researchers adjust different internal representations of cloud feedback within global circulation models: several distinct parameterizations reproduce present-day satellite measurements with comparable accuracy, yet they project substantially different equilibrium warming responses under doubled carbon-dioxide conditions. This strikes me as a modern instance of the underdetermination Duhem describes—an empirical system that fails to uniquely determine its theoretical components. What I cannot determine from the passage is whether Duhem intends good sense to function as a purely pragmatic or aesthetic criterion that scientists apply when logic runs out, or whether he believes there is a deeper, non-deductive rationality that genuinely constrains which revisions are permissible. Could you help me understand the precise role of good sense in Duhem’s holism, and then show me how that distinction might apply to a case like climate-model tuning, not as proof but as a heuristic for testing whether I have grasped the philosophical point?
