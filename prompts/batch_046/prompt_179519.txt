I've been preparing a discussion for an advanced undergraduate seminar on care ethics, and I want to use Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day—not the film, but the novel's first-person narration—to explore a problem that standard papers in the field tend to treat abstractly: how hierarchical professional roles can reorganize care obligations without eliminating them. Rather than reading Stevens as simply emotionally repressed, I'd like you to apply Joan Tronto's four phases of care—caring about, taking care of, care-giving, and care-receiving—and Nel Noddings's distinction between receptive attention and projective empathy to his relationship with Miss Kenton and his service to Lord Darlington. Treat Stevens's butlering as a highly structured practice of care that redirects care-giving away from reciprocal personal relationships and toward an institutional figure who never fully receives care in return.

Where, in specific scenes—the offer of flowers, the dismissal of the maids, the political meetings—does the novel show that this redirection is a moral loss rather than a professional virtue? At the same time, where does Ishiguro's irony resist a straightforward care-ethical critique, perhaps by suggesting that some forms of distance preserve a dignity that unmediated care might undermine? I want a reading faithful to both the novel's ambiguity and the philosophical framework, and I'd like it to conclude by asking what Ishiguro's portrait of institutional care reveals about the radius of care problem that argument alone tends to miss.
