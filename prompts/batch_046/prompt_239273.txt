I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy. Recently I encountered Galen Strawson’s basic argument against moral responsibility: the idea that being truly responsible for an action requires being responsible for the mental states that caused it, which in turn requires being responsible for still earlier mental states, producing an infinite regress that never reaches a foundational “base case”—much like a recursive function that lacks a terminating condition. I want to present this structure to my colleagues in a form that captures the logic itself rather than explaining it didactically, leaving the tension unresolved.

Write a sestina: six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi, using the classical rotation of six end-words. In the first stanza, place these words at the ends of lines 1 through 6 respectively: code, cause, prior, trace, base, loop. The poem should be in unrhymed iambic pentameter, with those end-words fixed at the line endings according to the traditional pattern.

Let the poem enact Strawson’s regress. Each stanza should attempt to ground responsibility in a preceding cause, only to reveal that cause as itself ungrounded and push the origin further back—like stepping through frames of a stack trace that never reaches a root call. As the end-words rotate through new grammatical positions, they should show what seemed like a foundation turning into just another link in the chain. The envoi must weave all six end-words into a single syntactic unit across its three lines, ending without closure: no resolution about free will or determinism, only the open loop.

You may draw on engineering metaphors—recursion, dependency chains, stack frames, termination conditions—but the language should remain poetic rather than technical. Do not include citations, bibliographic references, or allusions to specific publications. The tone should be formal, serious, and precise, appropriate for an audience comfortable with logic but unfamiliar with academic philosophy.
