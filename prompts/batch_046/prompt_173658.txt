Write a short dialogue between a defender of internalism about practical reasons and a defender of externalism about practical reasons. Their debate should center on an agent whose motivational architecture is partitioned into functionally isolated modules—specifically, a deliberative module that recognizes long-term ends (such as sustained health or intellectual achievement) and an impulsive module that actually controls immediate behavior, with no causal or informational bridge between them. The internalist should argue that, absent internal accessibility across the partition, no reason can be genuinely normative for the agent as a whole; they should press the point that attributing an external reason to "the agent" illegitimately reifies a unified self where only disconnected subsystems exist, and that a reason must be capable of motivating the controlling module to count as the agent's own. The externalist should respond that normative binding does not require causal efficacy within every module, but rather that the agent, considered as an integrated practical identity subject to standards of rational coherence, can be genuinely required by reasons that transcend any single module's current motivations; they should address the internalist's reification charge by explaining whether the unity of agency is a metaphysical precondition or a normative achievement. Each character must engage with the other's actual argument rather than talking past it: the internalist should explain why cross-module evaluation does not necessarily presuppose a mythical unified self, and the externalist should explain how an external reason avoids being merely an alien imposition upon the module that actually governs action. Conclude with both identifying the precise conceptual crux—whether the conditions for reason-attribution depend on pre-existing motivational unity or can themselves constitute that unity.
