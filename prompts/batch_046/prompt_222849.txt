I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have begun reading seriously in philosophy of mind and the philosophy of physics. I am preparing to moderate an interview for a small interdisciplinary podcast—tentatively called After the Lab—aimed at scientists who have turned to philosophy later in life. Next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a reconstructed, Vedanta-inflected version of Erwin Schrödinger's claim that consciousness is fundamentally unitary: not merely that minds share a common substrate, but that the apparent plurality of individual subjects is, in some carefully specified sense, an illusion generated by the same kind of conceptual division that physics imposes on an otherwise continuous reality.

I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking the guest to restate this One Mind thesis. In particular, I want at least one question on how this Vedanta-inspired unity is supposed to accommodate the phenomenological and neuroscientific data from split-brain patients—where two seemingly independent streams of awareness can be demonstrated—together with a follow-up that anticipates the reply that such cases merely show divided access to a single, undivided consciousness rather than genuinely divided consciousness itself. I want at least one question pressing the tension between Schrödinger's own commitment to the mathematical structure of quantum mechanics and his metaphysical appeal to non-dual Atman: specifically, whether wave-function monism or some other interpretation of quantum theory can be made coherent with the denial of individual subjects, and a follow-up keyed to the likely answer that quantum superposition describes physical states, not mental ones, leaving the unity claim untouched. I also want a question comparing this view to contemporary cosmopsychism—asking whether Schrödinger's position is genuinely distinct or merely an historical anticipation—and a follow-up that addresses the common deflection that historical anticipation is not philosophical redundancy. Please frame the sequence so that each major line includes the anticipated standard reply and a sharper second move.

In your response, please separate what is settled in the relevant literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who study Schrödinger's philosophy, and do not present any contested interpretive claim—such as the exact relation between his quantum formalism and his Vedantic commitments—as if it were the consensus view. The tone should remain respectful but probing, appropriate for an audience of former scientists who are comfortable with technical distinctions but new to philosophical argumentation.
