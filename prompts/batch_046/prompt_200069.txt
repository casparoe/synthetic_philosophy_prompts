Although I have no formal training in philosophy, I have spent several years thinking on my own about whether moral rules can ever be stable if the same kind of action can be right in one situation and wrong in another over what feel like subtle differences. To capture what I think is missing from the usual debates, I have formulated what I call the feature interaction thesis: moral reasons are not independent atoms that merely gain or lose weight depending on context; rather, they possess internal relational structures so that the presence of one feature can transform—not merely outweigh—the moral contribution of another. For example, honesty might ordinarily count in favor of telling the truth, but when it is combined with cruelty in the form of a humiliating revelation, honesty does not simply become less important; its valence is altered because it now participates in a wrong-making configuration. I want to use this idea as the center of an exam for an upper-division undergraduate philosophy seminar. Please write one essay exam question that students could answer in ninety minutes and that requires them to deploy the feature interaction thesis to test both moral particularism and moral generalism—specifically, they should have to show whether the thesis supports particularism, undermines it, or forces a revision of standard generalist accounts—without allowing them to succeed by merely summarizing well-known positions associated with figures such as Jonathan Dancy or W.D. Ross. Include a model answer of roughly seven hundred words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves, with marks assigned to each (total twenty marks), plus a brief one-line note on how candidates typically fail on each point. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts that a student without library access could not verify from memory alone; the difficulty should come entirely from the conceptual work of applying the feature interaction thesis precisely.
