I've been leaning toward this view on supererogation: there isn't actually a seperate category of "above and beyond" acts. Instead, everything we call supererogatory is just an imperfect duty—like beneficence or rescue—performed way past the point where most people stop, and we mistake that high-cost performance for crossing into a different moral zone. The "beyond duty" feeling, I think, comes from two biases: we treat the minimal decent act as the "full" duty, and we have a kind of status bias where costly altruism gets misread as heroism rather than just expensive altruism. So praise should scale with cost, but there's no special "optional" moral goods—just goods that are harder to deliver.

What would actually count against this? I want an honest map of where it's exposed. Which concrete cases or intuitions would hurt it—like the organ donor who gives a kidney to a stranger, or the firefighter who dies saving someone—do those really collapse into "just a really hard imperfect duty"? Are there formal results about moral demandingness that support or undermine the idea that there's no ceiling? And what about the defender's side: if you're committed to this view, which objections do you have to concede are real problems versus which ones can you dismiss as psychological noise? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be straight about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—like if you can always say "that's just an unusually demanding imperfect duty"—and whether that insulation is a feature or a bug for a view like this. Don't need citations, just the conceptual pressure points.
