I've been discussing with some friends whether dreams are actually experiences, and I tried to organize our conversation around two ideas that seem to come up in philosophy, though I don't have the background to be sure I'm using them correctly. One idea, which I think is associated with Norman Malcolm, is what I'll call the "wake-state exclusivity" position: the claim that genuine experience requires an unbroken conscious state, and since sleep interrupts that state, anything we report as a dream must be a reconstruction assembled after waking rather than an event that genuinely occurred during sleep. The other idea, which I link to Daniel Dennett, is what I'm calling the "instantaneous replay" model — the notion that the brain stores scattered neural traces during REM sleep and then, in the brief interval before full consciousness returns, compiles those traces into a coherent narrative that feels like it lasted hours, even though no actual experiential stream was running during the sleep itself. I'm unsure whether this second description captures Dennett's real view, or whether he would reject even the idea of stored traces being replayed.

Could you compare these two positions for me? In particular, I want to know whether Malcolm's argument targets only the location of the dream (during sleep versus at waking), or whether he also denies that the reconstructed memory counts as an experience in its own right; and whether Dennett's position is closer to this replay model or to a stronger claim that there is no hidden mental content at all — that the story is invented more or less from scratch upon awakening. I also find myself wondering whether there's conceptual space between them: could one consistently hold that dreams are real experiences, but that they occur only in that split-second of awakening, with no prior mental substrate to replay?
