My colleague in philosophy of mind, Dr. Elena Voss, has given me her verdicts on whether various mental states count as genuine emotions, as opposed to mere judgments, perceptions, or bodily reflexes. She holds that a sudden panic with racing heart and a diffuse sense that something is wrong, but with no identifiable object, is a genuine emotion. She holds that a calm, sustained belief that climate change threatens one's descendants, with no bodily change, is not an emotion but a judgment. She holds that a reflexive withdrawal from a hot stove is not an emotion. She holds that feeling intense warmth and tears upon seeing an old photograph, with no current belief that anything present is good or bad, is a genuine emotion. She holds that performing culturally required mourning gestures at a funeral while feeling nothing inside and making no evaluation is not an emotion. She also holds that a long-lasting, objectless melancholy coloring all experience is an emotion rather than a mere mood.

The new case is a state induced by non-invasive neurostimulation that replicates the full autonomic and neural signature of jealousy directed at a specific rival; the subject reports experiencing it as an observed bodily event rather than an inhabited feeling, and explicitly denies any evaluative judgment that the rival has wronged them or that they have lost something valuable. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Predict whether Voss will classify this as jealousy. Identify the two or three most plausible rules about emotion that fit her earlier verdicts. Say how each of those rules diverges on the neurostimulation case. Give your best guess about which rule she actually runs, with brief reasoning. Finally, propose one further case that would best distinguish the leading candidates.
