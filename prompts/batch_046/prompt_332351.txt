so since i retired from the biophysics lab i've been reading philosophy and i'm trying to see if my thoughts on choice hang together. here's the package:

(1) buridan's ass is a real failure mode for rational choice — if two options are genuinely equal, rationality gives you no instruction, and that's a problem, not just an oddity.

(2) but i'm also convinced that in any actual physical system, including brains, perfect symmetry never holds — there's always thermal noise or quantum flucuations breaking the tie, so pure indifference is physically impossible.

(3) even so, i think that if utilities are exactly tied, picking randomly is rational — in fact it's what a maximizer ought to do.

(4) i also hold that preference is discovered, not invented: if you think you're indifferent, there's really a microscopic asymmetry you're just not conscious of.

(5) at the same time, i think that if preference is genuinely zero, then not choosing is just as rational as choosing — there's no obligation to break the tie.

do these cohere? it feels like (2) and (4) might make (1) moot, since the situation never actually arises, but maybe that's only apparent. and (4) seems to fight with (3): if there's always a hidden reason, then random breaking isn't rational decision-making, it's just missing the real cause. plus (1) and (3) pull in opposite directions — is tie-breaking a rational success or a failure? and (5) sits awkwardly with (3) — if inaction is equally okay, then random choice can't be "the" rational move.

please rank the actual tensions by severity, say which ones dissolve on closer inspection, and tell me the cheapest single revision that fixes the most without forcing me to drop my physicalist instincts. be specific about what each fix costs.
