In debates about whether conceivability is a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility, I keep encountering an analogy that treats the mind like a kind of modal spectrometer: just as an astronomer calibrates an instrument against known stellar lines and then reads absorption patterns to detect distant elements, the philosopher is said to calibrate conceptual analysis against uncontroversial possibilities and then “read off” modal properties—possibility, impossibility, necessity—from the presence or absence of contradictions in a conceived scenario. Proponents use this to argue that conceivability is not mere imagination but a disciplined detection method, and that when we clearly conceive of water that is not H₂O, we are registering a real modal fact rather than constructing a fiction. Critics reply that the analogy hides the fact that both the “instrument” and the “calibration” are internal to the very faculty being tested.

Can you audit this analogy thoroughly? Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of the spectrometer, the calibration stars, the absorption lines, the distant elements, and the observer’s error-correction. Then identify the precise points where the mapping fractures: where does the mental “instrument” lack the independence of a physical device; where does “calibration” presuppose the modal knowledge it claims to discover; and what happens to the analogy if contradictions in conception turn out to be symptoms of cognitive limitation rather than modal boundary markers? Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that conceivability can serve as a direct, if fallible, guide to possibility—survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the spectrometer image was doing all the argumentative work. If the analogy collapses, suggest what a more honest comparison for the epistemic status of conceivability might be.
