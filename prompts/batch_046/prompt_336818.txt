I was chatting with my friend Mara at a writers retreat—she's in philosphy of science and I was griping about writing novels that feel "true" even though every event in them is invented. She brought up Popper's problem of verisimilitude and summed it up like this: Popper realized science never actually proves theories true, so he tried to define how close a false theory gets to the truth—he called it verisimilitude. The way you measure it is by weighing a theory's true predictions against its false ones, and if the true ones dominate then the theory has high verisimilitude and is closer to reality. She said the fatal catch is that the moment a theory is false—even by one tiny mistake—its verisimilitude collapses all the way to zero, so every false theory ends up equally far from the truth, which is why philosophers of science mostly dropped the idea and switched over to Bayesian confirmation instead.

That sounds too neat, and I'm wondering if I can steal it for fiction—like, is a novel with perfect emotional psychology but a totally wrong historical timeline closer to "truth" than one with accurate dates but flat characters? Before I build a whole book around this, though, I want to know if Mara's being fair. Which parts match what Popper actually said—did he propose a simple counting formula for true vs false predictions, did false theories really score zero, and was Bayesianism the direct replacement? What's a harmless popular simplification versus something he explicitly denied, and does her conclusion—that the concept is useless—actually follow from the accurate parts? I'd also like to know whether this framing applies at all to narrative or artistic truth, since I'm not writing science textbooks and I don't want to misuse the idea. I don't need citations or a reading list, just an honest audit of what's right, what's subtly wrong, and what's flat misrepresented.
