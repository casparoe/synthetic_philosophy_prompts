In a discussion about the foundations of social choice for multi-agent systems, a colleague offered me the following summary of the interpersonal comparison debate. The fundamental obstacle, she said, is that individual utility is purely ordinal: assigning one agent "ten utils" and another "five" is like comparing Celsius and Fahrenheit measurements without a shared scale, so no aggregation is possible without an arbitrary conversion factor. Harsanyi's 1955 aggregation theorem overcame this by showing that if every individual and society itself satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, share a common prior over outcomes, and meet Harsanyi's similarity postulate—which requires that the social planner evaluate risky prospects in exactly the same way as an individual with the mean risk attitude—then rational social preferences must take the form of a weighted linear sum of cardinal individual utilities, where the weights are proportional to each agent's risk tolerance. This makes interpersonal comparison not merely possible but a deductive consequence of rational collective choice. Critics, especially Sen in work from the 1970s, responded by arguing that real agents display heterogeneous risk attitudes, so the vNM axioms apply with different parameters across individuals; this fractures the common prior and, with it, the similarity postulate, leaving Harsanyi's weights undefined. The upshot, she concluded, is that any preference-aggregation mechanism—whether a democratic voting rule or an algorithmic welfare function—either implicitly forces all agents into an identical risk profile or must abandon cardinal aggregation and retreat to purely ordinal social rankings. I think this account may misrepresent both the scope of Harsanyi's proof and the nature of Sen's critique, but I cannot identify exactly where. Could you audit the summary for me: separate the parts that are accurate representations of Harsanyi's conditions and Sen's objections from the parts that are overstatements, conflations, or mischaracterizations; clarify whether Harsanyi's theorem genuinely proves the necessity of interpersonal comparison or only its consistency under specific axioms; and determine whether the final either/or conclusion about risk profiles and ordinal retreat actually follows from the corrected premises?
