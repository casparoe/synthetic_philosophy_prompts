ok here's roughly where i've landed on escalation and the dollar auction and i wanna check if it actually hangs together or if i'm just being inconsistent.

(1) i think the dollar auction gets mis-taught: with common knowledge of rationality nobody should enter, but once you treat each bid as a costly signal that updates the opponent's belief about your private resolve, the escalation becomes a Bayesian signaling game rather than a pure rationality trap.

(2) in real-world stuff—labor strikes, biding wars—walking away isn't always rational because backing down can wreck your credability and invite future exploitation.

(3) sunk-cost reasoning is a fallacy in individual choice, full stop. if you've sunk $90 into a useless $100 gadget the $90 is gone and shouldn't affect your decision.

(4) but in strategic interaction, having visibly sunk resources can be rational because it binds you in a way opponents observe, essentially a credible commitment device.

(5) i reject one-size-fits-all norms of rationality; what's rational depends on context and goals.

(6) despite all that, i think escalation of commitment is mostly a cognitive bias and institutions should design mechanisms to prevent it.

so do these hang together? specifically, does (3) clash with (4) and if so which one has to be weakened? is there a hidden tension between (1) and (6)—if escalation can be rational signaling, why treat it as a bias across the board? and is (2) compatible with (5) without me just hand-waving 'it depends'? also there's an apparent conflict between (1) and (2) that might dissolve once you distinguish one-shot games from repeated interaction—am i right that's not a real contradiction? order the tensions by severity and tell me the cheapest fix, and don't just say 'nuance it'—i want to know which commitment actually has to give if they can't all stay.
