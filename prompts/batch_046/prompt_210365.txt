At a family gathering last month, my uncle and my cousin argued about our grandmother's advance directive. She had signed a document refusing antibiotics should she develop severe dementia and no longer recognise her relatives; she is now in precisely that condition, cheerful but unable to identify anyone, and has developed pneumonia. The directive is clear, yet she now seems content with her life and does not resist care. My uncle defended honouring the directive; my cousin argued it should be overridden.

My uncle began by saying that the directive expresses our grandmother's authentic, long-held values about dignity and bodily integrity. He said the cheerful state she now inhabits lacks the cognitive complexity to hold genuine moral interests that could outweigh her prior autonomous choice, just as we honour a person's will after death even though the dead have no current preferences.

My cousin replied that this treats the living woman as a legal fiction. She argued that autonomy is not a one-time contract but an ongoing capacity, and that the present grandmother has real interests—comfort, continued experience, avoidance of death—that are not cancelled by predictions her earlier self made about a condition she could not genuinely imagine.

My uncle countered that without a principled line, we would never honour any prior commitment if the subject later changed. He said we do not ask a deceased person whether they have revised their views on inheritance, because the authority of the earlier decision survives the loss of present consciousness; by the same token, the directive should survive her cognitive decline.

My cousin said the inheritance analogy is misplaced because property rights survive death, whereas bodily rights belong to the living body in front of us. She proposed that the authority of an earlier self should be modelled not on a testator but on a constitutional convention: it binds only so long as there is sufficient continuity of the subject it governs, and severe dementia breaks that continuity.

My uncle then argued that even if psychological continuity is weakened, narrative identity remains—our grandmother is still the protagonist of the same life story. To ignore the directive, he said, is to allow an interloper state to hijack the ending of her biography, fracturing the coherence of her life as a whole.

My cousin responded that narrative identity is precisely what is at stake. If the story has turned into something she would never have authored—cheerful simplicity rather than dignified refusal—then imposing the old plot is authorial tyranny, not fidelity. She added that both sides are missing the possibility that the directive itself was based on a false prediction: people with dementia often report wellbeing that outsiders underestimate.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, rating each of the six positions I have outlined from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Say who is right about which specific point, and identify what, if anything, both parties are neglecting—particularly regarding the predictive reliability of advance directives and the distinction between precedential and constitutive authority over one's future self. In your response, please state how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
