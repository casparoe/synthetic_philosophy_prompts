I am a PhD student in philosophy of science with a focus on evolutionary biology, and next week I am presenting a paper to an interdisciplinary workshop in philosophy of biology. The audience will be mixed: several evolutionary biologists, including behavioral ecologists who are committed adaptationists and a couple of evo-devo researchers who emphasize developmental constraint; plus philosophers of science who care about heuristics, explanatory burden, and the structure of evolutionary reasoning rather than empirical case studies. My thesis is that Gould and Lewontin’s spandrels critique is standardly misread as an empirical claim about the prevalence of non-adaptive traits, when in fact it is a methodological argument about asymmetrical explanatory burdens—namely, that adaptationist hypotheses must first rule out architectural and developmental constraints before claiming selective optimization. I further argue that this methodological point has been largely dissolved by contemporary constraint-based evolutionary modeling, including work in evo-devo and evolutionary robotics that treats phenotypic space as already structured by high-dimensional search landscapes, so that so-called spandrels are predictable features rather than anomalies. Consequently, I contend that the critique should be retired as a general anti-adaptationist weapon and redeployed only as a local heuristic within behavioral ecology, where researchers still implicitly treat phenotypic space as largely unconstrained. Please prepare me for the Q&A session. I need the objections ordered by how likely they are from this specific audience and how damaging they would be to my argument, each phrased in the vocabulary those participants would actually use—distinguishing, for example, the behavioral ecologist’s worry about predictive power from the evo-devo researcher’s worry about mischaracterizing constraint, and from the philosopher of science’s worry about conflating methodological rules with empirical findings. For each objection, indicate what I should concede upfront, what I should hold firm on, and the weakest point in my reply that I should be ready to defend rather than hope they miss. Include a brief two-minute reply for each, noting where that reply is soft. Finally, tell me the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—perhaps the one that targets my reliance on evolutionary robotics as an analog for biological phenotypic space—and how to survive it if it comes. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want a speaker’s prep sheet for surviving the room.
