I've been working through an argument about Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem that seems to show the view collapses into universal moral paralysis, and I can't locate the slip. Here it is, with the steps my study group extracted.

(1) On Scanlon's account, an act is wrong if its performance is permitted by principles that no affected party could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced agreement.

(2) Reasonable rejection is fundamentally pairwise: when assessing whether I can reject principle P, I compare the burden P imposes on me to the burden the available alternative imposes on each other affected person, taken one at a time.

(3) Because rejection is pairwise and not summed across individuals, the fact that principle P saves five people from death does not generate five times the weight against my rejection; my rejection of being sacrificed stands with full force regardless of how many others benefit.

(4) Apply this to a standard rescue case: we can save either A alone or B1 through B5 together, but not all six. The principle "save A" is rejectable by B1, B2, B3, B4, and B5, each facing death. The principle "save B1-B5" is rejectable by A, also facing death.

(5) Now extend the structure: in any social choice with winners and losers—taxation, vaccination mandates, resource allocation—every affected party on the losing side can issue an independent, non-diluted rejection of the principle that produces that loss.

(6) Since there is always at least one loser in any non-unanimous principle, and since their rejection is never canceled by the gains of others, every non-unanimous principle is rejectable by someone.

(7) The only principles that survive are those that harm no one, but in a world of scarcity and conflict such principles are empty.

(8) Therefore contractualism cannot justify any actual moral or political decision that involves trade-offs; it proves too much by forbidding all distributive choice.

My instinct is to save (2)—the pairwise, non-aggregative character of rejection—because that seems essential to Scanlon's resistance to utilitarianism. But if I keep (2), I seem forced to accept (6) and (8). Where does the argument go wrong? Is there a hidden scope shift between (4) and (5), or does Scanlon have a resource—perhaps a distinction between personal and institutional justification—that blocks the extension? And if I do reject (2) to save practical morality, what exactly do I lose?
