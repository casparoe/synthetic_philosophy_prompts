I'm planning a single ninety-minute session for a library philosophy circle of non-specialist adults — engineers, lawyers, artists — who have heard of Gödel, Escher, Bach but have not read it. I need a teaching plan, not the lecture itself: I want them to grasp Hofstadter's "knob-twiddling" model of creativity, in which concepts contain implicit adjustable parameters and creative insight comes from systematically varying those parameters rather than from random association. I want to avoid the standard musical or visual-art illustrations; the carrying case should be the design space of social-deduction games, specifically how the "hidden role" concept gets twisted across variants — changing the number of hidden roles, making them asymmetric, injecting public-information mechanisms, altering win conditions — so that the parameter space becomes tangible. Lay out the dependency order: what idea must be secured before "knob" can be introduced without collapsing into "brainstorming," how to build the parameter-space intuition through this game family step by step, and why this example works better than the usual ones for this audience. Include one end-of-session check that distinguishes understanding the mechanism from merely using the vocabulary, and state explicitly which parts of Hofstadter's broader account — its connections to analogy, to formalization attempts, and to Gödelian self-reference — I should declare I am leaving out and why. Separate settled points in the interpretation of Hofstadter's model from genuinely contested readings among scholars who study it, and do not present a disputed reading as consensus. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
