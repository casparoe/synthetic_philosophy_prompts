At a philosophy seminar, Lena and Marcus debated a mixed-strategy variant of Newcomb’s problem. Lena began with the standard two-boxing dominance argument: whatever the predictor has already done, taking both boxes yields £1,000 more than taking only one, so the dominant choice is to take both. Marcus replied that this neglects the evidential correlation — choosing one box makes it far more probable that the large sum is present — so expected utility clearly favours one-boxing. Lena then introduced a twist: suppose the agent commits not to a pure act but to a probabilistic mixture over decision procedures, one-boxing with probability p and two-boxing with 1-p, where the predictor knows the parameter p but not the realised outcome of the agent’s internal randomisation. She claimed that, once you average over your own mixture, dominance is restored in expectation and the evidential asymmetry dissolves, because conditioning on the mixture parameter rather than the realised act severs the correlation that drives the one-boxing intuition. Marcus countered that the predictor forecasts the actual behaviour that will be realised, not the abstract parameter p, so conditioning on your concrete choice still carries the full evidential weight; averaging over your coin-flip merely dilutes the expected payoff without repairing the causal structure. They turned to me to referee, and I genuinely could not decide. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether dominance applies to the mixture itself; whether the predictor conditions on the parameter or on the realised decision; and whether the evidential argument for one-boxing survives once you average over the agent’s own randomisation. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both parties are missing.
