I serve as vice-chair of our university's Research Integrity Appeals Board, and I must circulate a written opinion before our closed session next Thursday on a case that has divided the panel along conceptual lines. The respondent, Dr. Elena Voss, is a tenured immunologist suspended after an internal audit revealed selective reporting in a multi-site clinical trial. Dr. Voss does not dispute the statistical anomalies; instead, she argues that her first-order scientific judgment—that the therapeutic compound shows genuine efficacy—remains fully rational, and that her explicit awareness of her own susceptibility to confirmation bias should not be treated as undermining that judgment. She frames her position in terms of higher-order evidence about her own reasoning: recognizing a personal cognitive risk, she claims, does not automatically defeat the belief it targets, and penalizing her for retaining confidence despite that recognition amounts to enforcing a destructive form of epistemic akrasia. Our institutional code defines sanctionable misconduct as intentional deception or reckless disregard for evidentiary standards, but it offers no guidance on cases in which a researcher knowingly maintains a belief while acknowledging personal vulnerability to bias.

Please draft the formal memo I will present for a vote: it should advance a concrete, actionable recommendation—whether to uphold the suspension, overturn it subject to a specific remediation protocol with a defined timeline, or impose an intermediate sanction—and it should provide the reasoning that supports that bottom line. The memo must engage Dr. Voss's argument at its strongest, explaining precisely why treating higher-order self-doubt as automatically belief-defeating might create perverse institutional incentives against transparency or erode the autonomy required for expert judgment, before clarifying why the board should nevertheless reach the opposite conclusion, or why her analogy between rational belief-persistence and reckless disregard ultimately collapses. The document should read as an institutional opinion suitable for the official record rather than personal counsel, and it should stand on its own conceptual grounds without requesting citations, bibliographies, or external authority.
