I have been preparing a long-form essay on the idea—most closely associated with Daniel Dennett—that the self is best understood not as a substance but as a center of narrative gravity, an abstract point around which the stories we tell about ourselves converge. Most popular treatments stop at the physics analogy, but I want to push into a conceptual difficulty that rarely gets aired: if the self is genuinely like a center of gravity, then it should be deterministically calculable from the actual "mass" of our autobiographical contents. Yet those contents—memories, intentions, self-descriptions—are notoriously fallible, contradictory, and retrospectively edited, which means the "mass" can shift without any underlying reality to anchor it. This leads me to ask whether the analogy collapses precisely where narrative fails to be truth-tracking, and whether accepting it forces us to treat the self as a kind of fictional construct that could, in principle, be multiply instantiated—say, in an artificial system that maintains several incompatible autobiographical threads without ever privileging one as the true owner. Please explain this conceptual issue: does the narrative-gravity model require that false or contradictory stories be excluded from the calculation of the self, or does it embrace them as equally valid contributors, and what does that choice imply about the uniqueness and reality-status of the self?
