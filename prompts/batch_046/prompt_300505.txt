I have been trying to work out my own position on the relationship between long-run structures and historical agency for a graduate seminar on historical methodology, and I want to know whether the following six commitments form a stable package or whether I am inadvertently smuggling in contradictions.

First, I accept Braudel's distinction between geographical time, social time, and individual time, and I think that in explaining large-scale transformations the first two levels carry most of the explanatory weight.

Second, I reject hard structural determinism: I believe that singular, non-substitutable human choices—say, a particular refusal to sign a treaty, or an accidental navigational decision—can permanently alter the institutional trajectory that long-run climate and economic structures would otherwise have produced.

Third, I treat contemporary climate change as a paradigmatic longue durée phenomenon, operating slowly and almost invisibly on mentalités over centuries; at the same time, I hold that discrete, short-duration extreme-weather events—one catastrophic drought, one flood year—are the principal proximate causes of premodern state collapse and mass migration episodes.

Fourth, I maintain that narrative history focused on named individuals is intellectually legitimate, but only on the condition that those individuals are understood as switch-points: concentrated nodes where structural pressures are redirected rather than autonomous origins of change.

Fifth, I deny that methodological individualism is true for sociology, yet I insist that it is correct for historiography: any adequate historical explanation must ultimately terminate in the specific beliefs, intentions, and perceptual frames of particular agents, even when those mental states are themselves structurally overdetermined.

Sixth, I reject great-man theories in general, but I also think that the idiosyncratic psychological profile of certain historical figures—the risk calculus of a specific explorer, the particular paranoia of a specific ruler—can produce institutional outcomes that no other agent in an identical structural position would have generated.

Please audit this set. Identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to conflict, ordering the real tensions by severity. For each tension, specify whether it is logical, explanatory, or methodological, say which commitment has to give if I want consistency, and describe the cheapest revision together with what I lose by making it. Pay particular attention to whether my commitment in the third point undermines my second, and whether my fifth and sixth commitments pull in opposite directions once I reject great-man theories. Finally, tell me which single revision—weakening or abandoning one of these six—would resolve the greatest number of conflicts, and whether that resolution is worth the theoretical cost.
