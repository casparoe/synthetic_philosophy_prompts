Since my father received a diagnosis of progressive neurodegenerative disease, and since I have begun to contemplate what I would want for myself in analogous circumstances—including gradual neural prosthetic replacement and, more speculatively, scanning-based preservation—I have tried to articulate exactly what matters in my own survival. Before I discuss these views with my family, I want a rigorous consistency check. I hold the following commitments: (a) Survival consists not in bodily continuity but in the preservation of a singular first-person point of view, conceived as a continuous stream of conscious experience with inherited concerns and projects. (b) If my biological brain is replaced neuron by neuron with functionally identical synthetic substrates, I survive the process, because the point of view is never interrupted. (c) If I am scanned, destroyed, and reconstructed elsewhere with perfect psychological fidelity, I do not survive; the reconstruction is a duplicate, and my stream ends with my destruction. (d) In split-brain separation, both hemispheres can support consciousness continuous with mine, so both resulting persons survive me; uniqueness is not an essential feature of survival. (e) The difference between (b) and (c) is explained not by substrate loyalty but by the presence in (b) and absence in (c) of an unbroken causal-functional chain linking each stage directly to the next. (f) The ultimate reason to care about any future stage—gradually replaced or reconstructed—is that it inherits my values, memories, and character; continuous physical embodiment is therefore irrelevant to whether I have reason to care.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, and for each specify precisely which commitment must be modified or abandoned and what that costs the overall view. I want an audit of my position, not a survey: trace exactly what (a), (e), and (f) commit me to with respect to the verdicts in (b), (c), and (d), and show whether my principles can actually deliver those verdicts without ad hoc adjustments.
