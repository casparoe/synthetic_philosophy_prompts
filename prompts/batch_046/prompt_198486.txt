I am preparing a unit for an upper-level philosophy course that asks students to read Harold Ramis's Groundhog Day through Sartre's existentialist account of radical freedom and bad faith, and I want the prompt to push past the standard interpretation that Phil Connors simply moves from inauthenticity to authenticity. The film's premise—Phil trapped in an endlessly repeating February 2 in Punxsutawney—seems to literalize Sartre's thesis that human beings are condemned to be free regardless of circumstance: Phil can steal, lie, seduce, or attempt suicide, yet none of these choices alter his situation.

Rather than treating the repetition as a straightforward metaphor for the burden of groundless choice, I want a reading that asks whether the loop actually destabilizes Sartre's framework. Specifically, does the absence of irreversible consequences mean that Phil's choices lack the temporal openness Sartre seems to presuppose, and does that collapse his freedom into something closer to determinism? Please examine specific sequences—the suicide montage, his piano lessons with Rita, the repeated failure to save the elderly homeless man—to determine whether they dramatize Sartre's distinction between facticity (the given repetition) and transcendence (the choice enacted within it), or whether they expose a limitation in Sartre's account: that freedom without the risk of permanent failure may not be freedom at all but merely the simulation of agency. I also want you to consider whether Phil's eventual transformation—adopting the persona of the benevolent, culturally accomplished local hero—should be read as an achievement of authentic freedom or as a more sophisticated form of bad faith, a fixed role adopted precisely to avoid confronting the nothingness of a situation that never ends. The reading should treat the film as philosophically serious on its own terms, not reduce it to an illustration, and should say clearly whether the narrative logic ultimately vindicates Sartre, resists him, or reveals a tension within his concepts that argument alone does not settle.
