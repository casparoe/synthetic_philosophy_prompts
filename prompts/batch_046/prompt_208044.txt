I have spent the past year trying to make sense of why hypocrisy feels as though it cancels someone's right to criticize others, after following several public scandals in which figures with hidden, parallel faults loudly condemned those same faults in others. Next month I am presenting my conclusion — that hypocrisy does not merely diminish one's standing to blame, but transforms the speech-act itself into what I call "moral ventriloquism," in which the blamer uses another's fault as an acoustic mirror for their own unacknowledged, structurally identical failure — to a philosophy discussion circle that meets at the public library. The audience is precisely this mix: two moral philosophers who treat standing as a matter of clean hands or institutional authority, several graduate students in ethics who are skeptical that "standing" is anything more than a social convention, and engaged lay readers who care mainly about whether the underlying accusation is true regardless of who delivers it. I want to defend the further claim that what matters is not an exact match between faults but whether they share what I call a "structural echo" — a social acoustic in which the blame amplifies precisely because it deflects attention from the blamer's parallel guilt.

Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way each camp in that room would phrase them: the clean-hands moralists, the authority-skeptics, and the truth-is-all-that-matters readers. For each objection, tell me what I should concede outright, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is softest. In particular, I need to defend my "structural echo" concept rather than hope they miss it — identify its weakest point and draft the reply I should have ready if pressed on it. Finally, include the one objection I am most hoping nobody raises, with a brief note on why it would do the most damage to my position and whether any concession could save it.
