Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed etiological (selected-effects) theorist of biological function and a committed causal-role (systemic-capacity) theorist, with the collaboration focused specifically on evolutionary spandrels, exaptations, and engineered or non-adaptive traits whose causal contribution to current system organization is clear but whose evolutionary origin is not one of direct selection for that contribution.

The document should take the form of a formal collaboration statement containing the following sections, in this order:

1. Agreed claims. Numbered statements both parties can sign. These should cover: (a) the formal core of each theory—selection history versus current contribution to system capacity; (b) what both agree needs explaining—normativity of malfunction, the possibility of multiple realizability, and the distinction between adaptation, spandrel, and exaptation; (c) what standard objections (e.g., hearts in organisms where pumping does not enhance fitness; synthetic gene circuits with designed roles but no evolutionary history) actually show and do not show; and (d) the shared acknowledgment that evolutionary developmental biology and synthetic biology present cases neither framework was originally designed to address.

2. Precise disagreements. Present these in a table with two columns—Etiological and Causal-Role—stating each side's position in wording that its own proponents would accept. The disagreements should be formulated with exactness rather than rhetoric, and should cover at least: whether a trait must have a selection history to possess a proper function; whether current causal contribution to a system's capacity is ever sufficient by itself; how each framework classifies a spandrel that is subsequently co-opted into a new systemic role; and whether the normativity of malfunction can be grounded entirely in present system organization without reference to historical design.

3. Identification of verbal disagreements. State explicitly whether any portion of the dispute turns on ambiguity in the term function—for instance, an explanatory register versus a normative register—and, if so, which portion and why.

4. Cruxes. List the sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction or the other would genuinely move at least one party. Include at least: (i) whether a conserved non-coding genomic element that currently stabilizes transcription but originated as a structural byproduct of chromatin packaging has a function in the full normative sense; (ii) whether an engineered synthetic circuit introduced into an organism to perform a novel metabolic role possesses a biological function despite lacking any evolutionary history; and (iii) whether exaptation—where a trait's current contribution differs qualitatively from the contribution for which it was originally selected—undermines the etiological account, supports the causal-role account, or requires a hybrid analysis both reject.

5. Agreed tests, cases, and predictions. For each crux, specify a concrete scenario, conceptual experiment, or empirical finding both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance each side's explicit interpretation or prediction. The predictions should be phrased so that, were the case to resolve clearly in one direction, one party would be compelled to revise a core commitment.

Write in neutral, formal language. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, appeals to named authorities, or quotations from specific texts. Where a concept requires illustration, define it within the document rather than referring to an external source. The goal is a constructive text both camps could put their names to, with residual disagreement rendered exact rather than dissolved.
