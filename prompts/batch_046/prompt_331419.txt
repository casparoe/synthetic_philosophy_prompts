Consider this argument about rational choice when expectation is undefined.

(1) Rational agents must maximize expected utility.
(2) In the Pasadena paradox, every available strategy yields a payoff series whose expectation is undefined: the terms fail to converge absolutely, and no standard limiting procedure assigns a definite value.
(3) So in the Pasadena game, expected-utility theory issues no verdict among strategies.
(4) Many high-stakes practical decisions—climate mitigation across unbounded generations, AI alignment with open-ended future value, or investment in research with potentially infinite returns—have structurally analogous payoff streams: potentially infinite, with non-absolutely-convergent value distributions.
(5) Hence expected-utility theory is silent precisely on those choices where normative guidance is most indispensable.
(6) A decision framework that is systematically mute in the most important cases is either false or practically empty.
(C) Therefore we should reject the expectation-maximization framework for serious practical reasoning.

I want to resist (C), and I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) or (4). Which step should be rejected, and why did it look plausible? In particular, is the flaw an equivocation in (2) between formal undefinedness in one model and undefinedness in every reasonable extension, a hidden modal slide in (4) from structural analogy to identical formal conditions, or is (3) wrong because an undefined expectation does not entail the absence of any rational prescription? Please explain what each standard reply—whether appealing to bounded rationality, to non-Archimedean or limiting-average utility constructions, or to denying the analogy in (4)—would be forced to concede.
