A popular exposition of foundational risk compares the axioms of ZFC to a legacy operating-system kernel: if an inconsistency lies buried deep in the axiomatic base, every theorem that depends on that dependency is corrupted, just as every application crashes once the kernel bug is triggered. From this, the author draws a practical prescription—working mathematicians ought to treat results that invoke large-cardinal hypotheses or unrestricted comprehension as running in an unverified sandbox, relying on them for heuristic guidance but withholding them from proofs that require absolute security. Lay out the explicit correspondence between kernel and axiom base, application and theorem, crash and derivable contradiction, and sandbox and restricted proof system; then identify where mathematics diverges from software engineering (there is no runtime execution, contradictions are syntactic rather than behavioral, and isolating a proof requires a meta-theoretic reduction rather than process-level containment). Finally, ask whether the recommended epistemic caution—trusting only fragments that are conservatively extendible—actually survives these disanalogies, and suggest what analogy, if any, better captures how mathematicians should respond to the live possibility that their basic axioms are inconsistent.
