As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am revisiting Daniel Dennett's essay "Where Am I?" for a feature on how neuroscience reshapes our intuitions about self-location, and I believe I have finally isolated its central metaphysical payoff. Once Dennett accepts that his brain has been removed and placed in a vat, he recognizes that his conscious presence is now genuinely situated in the vat rather than in the body, because the brain is the actual substrate generating all experience. This suggests that our ordinary feeling of being here in the body is essentially a representational projection — a phenomenon I am calling projective indexicality — in which the brain constructs an embodied spatial anchor regardless of its physical location. Consequently, Dennett's scenario does not merely challenge Cartesian dualism; it demonstrates that first-person spatial self-reference is systematically decoupled from physical position, turning the question of where I am into a kind of narrative fiction maintained for functional coherence. For the piece, I want to understand whether contemporary philosophers of mind treat this decoupling as an error in our self-model that needs correction, or whether there is a defensible view that the brain's default embodiment tracks a real metaphysical fact about where the self is constituted — even when the brain itself has been relocated.
