I am a doctoral student working on early modern metaphysics and the philosophy of perception. Berkeley’s master argument is usually rejected on the familiar ground that conceiving of an object as existing unperceived does not entail that the object is actually perceived; I want to pursue a less common line of resistance. Specifically, please argue that the master argument depends on a suppressed premise about determinacy: that the content of any mental state must be fully specified in the same mode—perceptual or conceptual—under which it is grasped. If we allow that conception can be structurally indeterminate—grasping an object only through relational or functional properties without fixing its sensory mode—then the alleged contradiction dissolves, because the conceived content is never fully specified as “unperceived in every respect.” Construct the argument that Berkeley therefore conflates perceptual determinacy with conceptual determinacy, and that abandoning this conflation permits a reconstructed idealism that distinguishes between perceptual esse and a more schematic conceptual structure. The argument should be self-contained: do not invoke particular commentators, provide a reading list, or depend on bibliographic references, and do not quote from Berkeley’s texts verbatim.
