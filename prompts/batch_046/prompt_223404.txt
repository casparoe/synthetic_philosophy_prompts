I'm preparing an advanced bioethics seminar module on reproductive autonomy and have been working through the literature on liberal eugenics and procreative beneficence—the view that prospective parents bear not merely a liberty but a moral obligation to use available genetic technologies to select the child with the best expected life prospects. Rather than treating Andrew Niccol's 1997 film Gattaca as a straightforward dystopian warning about genetic discrimination, I want to read it as a philosophical test case for whether that liberal ideal can be coherently separated from the deterministic social hierarchy the film depicts. The society in Gattaca practices precisely the kind of private, parent-driven selection that liberal eugenics defends: there is no state mandate, only a marketplace of reproductive choice aimed, ostensibly, at the welfare of future children. The task is to determine whether the film's tragedy undermines that ideal from within, or whether it merely illustrates a contingent failure of social justice that a properly liberal eugenic regime could avoid.

Read Gattaca through this lens, but with specific attention to the non-identity problem: Vincent, the "faith birth" protagonist, exists only because his parents refused genetic selection for him. Had they followed the principle of procreative beneficence and engineered an embryo with Vincent's desired traits but without his congenital heart condition, Vincent himself would never have come into being—someone with better prospects, but not Vincent. Does the film's emotional architecture, which asks us to root for Vincent's triumph over his "inferior" genome, implicitly reject the logic of selection by making us cherish the very outcome that selection would have eliminated? Or can a defender of liberal eugenics reply that the parents' duty runs not to a hypothetical replacement but to the actual potential child they bring into existence?

I'd like the reading to examine particular scenes and relationships—the conception of Vincent alongside his engineered brother Anton, the swimming competitions in which Vincent's heart nearly fails him yet also forges his determination, and Jerome's paralysis, caused by an accident rather than by any genetic flaw—to ask whether the film consistently distinguishes genetic determinism (the belief that DNA fixes destiny) from the statistical reasoning that selection improves expected welfare. Where does Gattaca resist reduction to a philosophical argument about reproductive obligation, and where does it genuinely illuminate something that abstract debate misses—perhaps about sibling comparison, the social meaning of being "unengineered," or the conditions under which identity survives technological choice? Please take both the film and the philosophical position seriously, avoid treating either as mere illustration, and conclude with what you think the work contributes to the debate over procreative beneficence that a standard journal argument cannot capture.
