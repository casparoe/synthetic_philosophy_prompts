I'm developing a seminar paper on the Epicurean claim that death is not harmful to the one who dies, and in sharpening the argument I have driven it to what looks like an absurd extension that now threatens to collapse into something I cannot accept. Here is the reconstructed chain, laid out precisely. (1) If an event E harms a subject S, then there must exist, at some time after E occurs or concurrent with it, a subject who is the bearer of that harm; harm is essentially subject-relative and requires an existing bearer, not merely a causal trace. (2) At death, S permanently ceases to exist; consequently there is no post-mortem subject available to bear any harm attributed to the state of being dead. (3) Therefore death is not a harm to S. (4) Consider now not permanent death but temporary suspended consciousness — deep general anesthesia, a reversible coma, or any interval in which psychological continuity is interrupted. During such an interval, by the same reasoning in (1), there is no present subject to bear harms occurring within it. (5) Furthermore, if diachronic personal identity requires continuous psychological connectedness, then the subject who eventually awakens is not strictly identical to the subject who entered the suspension; the chain of identity has been broken by the gap, leaving no continuous bearer that spans the interruption. (6) Applying (1) consistently to this case: harms that occur during the suspension, and also harms that occur after resumption — such as the ruin of the sleeper's long-term projects, the dissolution of her relationships, or the suffering of people she loved — lack the required continuous bearer, since the pre-suspension subject no longer exists and the post-resumption subject is, on this metaphysics, a different bearer altogether. (7) Hence, on this reasoning, no event during or after a temporary suspension of consciousness can harm the original sleeper — a conclusion that seems plainly wrong, since we routinely and confidently judge that people are genuinely harmed by post-operative misfortunes and by the collapse of endeavors interrupted by unconsciousness. (8) The only escapes appear to be denying (1) or denying the identity-breaking claim in (5), but both moves look costly: denying (1) seems to open the door to free-floating harms that attach to no existing subject, while denying (5) requires adopting a metaphysics of personal identity that permits identity across temporal gaps without continuous psychological connection — a view the Epicurean argument about death seemed not to need and may not support.

Where exactly does the argument overreach? Is the mistake in illegitimately extending the "no bearer" principle from permanent non-existence to temporary discontinuity, in covertly assuming a particularly stringent theory of diachronic identity that is independent of the value-theoretic point about harm, or in conflating the absence of a phenomenological register with the absence of harm altogether? If I reject the problematic step, does the original Epicurean argument about death survive intact, or does it collapse with the extension? I would prefer to keep (1) intact if possible, since it captures something essential about harm being subject-relative, but I want to understand precisely what I am paying to preserve it.
