I'm giving the opening remarks at the twenty-fifth anniversary retreat of our university's interdisciplinary climate-policy center. The room is split: founding faculty and decision theorists who know the dollar-auction literature well, and donors, administrators, and junior staff who do not. The center's history includes two long-running initiatives that kept attracting funding well after their research value had become doubtful—largely because closing them would have required a collective admission of error. I want a five-minute address built structurally around escalation of commitment, specifically framing institutional persistence as an all-pay auction in which every stakeholder burns reputation and resources whether or not the initiative ultimately "wins." The idea should determine what I can honor—the rationality of remaining in a game with visible exit costs—without excusing the failure to design exit conditions in the first place. For the non-experts, explain why intelligent groups stay in losing games without reducing it to individual stupidity; for the experts, offer a precise framing—reputation treated as a non-recoverable bid—rather than the usual sunk-cost cliché. Include one concrete image or line that lands for both halves of the room. If the premise that this framing can honor persistence without excusing institutional failure is incoherent or self-undermining, say so plainly instead of drafting the speech.
