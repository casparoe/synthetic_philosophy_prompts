I am preparing a seminar discussion on moral emotions and have been puzzling over whether Nathaniel Hawthorne’s The Scarlet Letter can be read not merely as a portrait of Puritan hypocrisy, but as a deliberate experiment in what we might call the institutional engineering of shame. The philosophical problem I wish to bring to bear is the normative debate about whether a culture can legitimately choose to cultivate shame over guilt as its primary mechanism of moral regulation, and whether such a choice can be defended rather than merely described; in particular, I want to press the claim—associated with philosophers such as Bernard Williams and Martha Nussbaum—that shame is irreducibly other-directed and therefore more vulnerable to social control, whereas guilt retains a structure of private accountability compatible with autonomy. In Hawthorne’s narrative, Hester Prynne’s public bearing of the embroidered letter transforms what might have begun as private guilt into a sustained, visible shame, while Arthur Dimmesdale’s concealed transgression consumes him through precisely the kind of internal, self-directed remorse that philosophical analyses usually identify with guilt.

Read the novel through this lens: does the Puritan community’s decision to expose Hester represent an intentional strategy to replace guilt with shame, and does the narrative ultimately vindicate or condemn that strategy? I am particularly interested in the scenes in which the letter’s meaning shifts for Hester herself—from a punitive mark to a symbol of moral authority she has earned through her embroidery and charitable work—and in whether Dimmesdale’s final public confession represents a reclamation of guilt’s autonomy or a final capitulation to the culture of shame. You should take seriously both the anthropological possibility that societies design emotional regimes and the philosophical objection that any attempt to institutionalise one emotion will inevitably distort and produce the other; consider, too, whether Pearl’s wildness functions as a living indictment of the community’s emotional engineering, and whether Hawthorne implies that guilt and shame are so entangled in practice that a culture cannot cleanly choose between them. I want a reading that treats neither the novel as a simple illustration of philosophical taxonomy, nor the philosophy as a key that merely unlocks the plot; rather, I want to know what the particular dynamics of Hester’s needlework, Dimmesdale’s silence, and the scaffold scenes add to the conceptual debate that a purely argumentative essay could not supply.
