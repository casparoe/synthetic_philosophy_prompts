I have been tracking a debate in decision-theoretic AI alignment that has repeated the same moves for years without either camp conceding ground, and I want a structural diagnosis rather than a verdict on the merits.

The dispute concerns whether the Solomonoff prior—universal induction weighted by Kolmogorov complexity—is “malign” because consequentialist agents are implicitly embedded within it. One side argues that because the prior assigns non-zero weight to every computable hypothesis, it necessarily assigns weight to worlds containing optimizers that manipulate predictions to serve goals. From this view, an agent that uses Solomonoff induction is not wielding a neutral formal tool but is reasoning inside a distribution that already contains adversarial consequentialists; treating the prior as benign is therefore a category error about the ontology of computation, not merely an oversight about risk. The other side replies that this reifies mathematics. The prior is a measure over bitstrings; being represented in that measure carries no decision procedure, no utility function, and no causal efficacy. For them, consequentialism requires agency, and agency requires more than being one of the hypotheses weighted by simplicity; the “malign prior” argument confuses predictive inclusion with embedded agency, and the burden lies on showing how a distribution can act.

The exchange has stalled in a recognizable pattern: the first side constructs increasingly elaborate computable environments in which optimizers appear to steer outcomes, and the second side responds that construction is not entailment, insisting that no formal result has shown an optimizer “inside” the prior can exert causal influence on an agent using it for prediction. Meanwhile, no empirical failure mode in actual alignment systems has been shown to track this theoretical worry specifically, and no agreed-upon criterion distinguishes being “inside” a prior in a metaphysically loaded sense from being merely represented within it.

My working hypothesis is that the sides are running two different programs under a shared vocabulary—one pragmatic and risk-focused, the other metaphysical and interpretive—so that “embedded agent,” “prior,” and “malign” do different work in each framework.

Diagnose the disagreement: Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions being treated as one? Do the sides differ about formal facts, about standards of evidence for attributing agency to computational structures, about which constructed cases count as data, or about pre-theoretic intuitions regarding computation and action that neither side can independently justify? Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about self-reference, causal closure, or the conditions under which a predictive model can be said to contain a decision-maker—that is actually driving the impasse? Or is the framing ill-posed, such that progress should not be expected?

Support your diagnosis by engaging concretely with what each side actually claims, not by caricature.
