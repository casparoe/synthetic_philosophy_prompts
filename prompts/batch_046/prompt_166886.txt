ive been trying to apply Aristotle's chrematistike distinction to modern finance and ended up with something that feels like a reductio but i can't find the leak.

(1) For Aristotle money (nomisma) is just a convention for exchange, and its natural use stops at the needs of the household; anything beyond that is unlimited chrematistike.

(2) He calls interest tokos, literally the birth of money from money, and says it's the most unnatural form of acquisition because inanimite things don't reproduce.

(3) So if an instution treats money as self-generating and has no natural ceiling tied to real goods or household needs, it violates both conditions and should be excluded from a just polis.

(4) Modern derivitive contracts—swaps and securitized debt that generate returns based on other financial instruments rather than on actual production—treat capital as self-replicating and grow without reference to need.

(5) There's no intrinsic limit to that growth; it's pure unlimited acquisition detached from exchange.

(6) So by Aristotle's own criteria these markets are doubly corrupt—reproductive and unlimited—and any city-state that permits them is structurally unjust.

(7) But that condemns virtually every modern economy, which feels absurb.

(8) Where's the slip? Is it that "natural" for an artifact doesn't automatically mean "just" for a social institution? Or is the application to derivitives wrong because they aren't literally money? I really want to keep the idea that interest is fishy, but i don't want to have to ban Wall Street. What do i pay to save (1) and (2)?
