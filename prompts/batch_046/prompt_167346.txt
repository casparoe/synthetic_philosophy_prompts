I am a doctoral student in normative ethics, and I am trying to determine whether my judgements about professional and institutional obligations can be unified under a general principle or whether they instead support a particularist account—especially with regard to what I have been calling the “normative density” of role-based reasons, by which I mean the degree to which an institutional commitment generates residual obligations even when it is rightfully overridden.

Here are my verdicts on six concrete situations. In each case I indicate whether I think the action was all-things-considered correct, and whether it leaves a moral remainder—an unfulfilled obligation that continues to exert some normative force.

First, I promised my doctoral supervisor that I would deliver a draft chapter by Friday morning. On Thursday evening, a close friend called in crisis, asking for immediate help removing her belongings from a shared flat where she was experiencing domestic abuse. I broke my promise and assisted her. I believe this was the right thing to do, yet I also believe I still owed my supervisor a sincere apology and explanation; there is a moral remainder.

Second, I was asked to review anonymously a submission to a journal by my former doctoral supervisor, with whom I had a deeply acrimonious separation. I wrote an honest, sharply critical report. I believe this was correct, and I feel no moral remainder whatsoever; I do not think I owed him any special leniency.

Third, I found a wallet containing £200 and identification on a university path. I returned it intact despite being financially strained. This was clearly correct, and there is no moral remainder.

Fourth, I was present at the bedside of a dying acquaintance who asked whether his estranged son had called to say goodbye. The son had not. I told him that he had. I now think this was wrong—comforting though it was—and I do not think there is any ambiguity that makes it permissible.

Fifth, my department held a formal ceremony to honour a major benefactor whose fortune was built on documented labour exploitation. I refused to attend, knowing that my absence would embarrass two junior colleagues whose funding depended on the benefactor’s continued goodwill. I believe refusing was correct. Crucially, however, I do not feel that I owe those junior colleagues any apology or compensation for the harm my absence caused them; I think there is no moral remainder here, despite the structural parallel to the supervisor case.

Sixth, I was asked to write a reference for a former student whose performance was mediocre but not poor, knowing that she belonged to a group severely underrepresented in the field and that the letter might determine whether she secured a rare position. I wrote an honest but slightly generous assessment, highlighting her strengths without inventing achievements. I feel this was acceptable, but I experience a faint discomfort—something like a minimal moral remainder—though I am unsure whether that discomfort tracks a real residual wrong.

These verdicts seem to pull in different directions. Cases one and five both involve breaking an institutional expectation for moral reasons and causing collateral harm to dependants, yet only case one generates a remainder for me. Case six introduces a kind of epistemic partiality within a professional duty that feels different from the direct conflict in case one.

My question is whether there is a coherent general principle—perhaps concerning the directness of the wrong, the nature of the institutional role, or the distinction between violating a commitment and merely failing to participate in a corrupt practice—that can accommodate all six verdicts, including the asymmetry between case one and case five. If not, which verdict should I abandon? And what does the resulting principle predict for the following case I have not decided upon: a defence barrister who deliberately exploits a procedural technicality to secure the acquittal of a client she privately believes guilty of a violent offence, knowing that the victim’s family will suffer and her own chambers will face public criticism?

Please also indicate whether your proposed principle is particularist—treating each case as governed by its own irreducibly specific reasons—or generalist, and whether it requires me to revise my intuition that case five involves no moral remainder.
