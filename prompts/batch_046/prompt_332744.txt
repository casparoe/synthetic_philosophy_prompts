Write a sestina on whether dreams are experiences, framed through the debate between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett. Use these six end-words in this order for the first stanza: report, memory, theater, construction, criterion, waking. Speak in the first person of a dreamer being interviewed upon awakening, someone whose testimony is being treated heterophenomenologically — that is, interpreted as a text to be analyzed rather than taken as a transparent window onto an inner show. Structure the poem so that its formal properties do philosophical work: the sestina’s recursive end-words should mirror the circular problem Malcolm raises, namely that if the only access to the dream is the waking report, then reportability functions simultaneously as the criterion for having had an experience and as a post-hoc construction of it. In stanzas one through three, let the speaker defend the reality of the dream-experience in the language of presence, observation, and immediate memory; in stanzas four and five, let the speaker begin to adopt Dennett’s vocabulary of narrative assembly, the Cartesian theater of recall, and the interpretation of reports. In the envoi (the final tercet), place “waking” and “theater” together and “criterion” beside “construction” so that the rhyme-locked pairing enacts the deadlock visually — whether reportability is the criterion for dream-experience or merely its construction upon waking. Keep the philosophical claims accurate: Malcolm denies that dreams are experiences because they fail the reportability criterion at the time of occurrence; Dennett allows that there may be experience but treats the waking narrative as assembled and interpreted heterophenomenologically. Do not resolve the tension with a sermon or a shrug; let the form hold the contradiction open. Aim for thirty-nine lines (six sestets plus envoi), with precise but accessible language — accurate enough that a philosopher of mind recognizes both positions, readable as poetry even without specialist background, and free of citations, bibliographies, or named works beyond the philosophers themselves.
