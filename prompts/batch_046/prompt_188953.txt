I'm a doctoral student working on eighteenth-century political economy, and a colleague recently characterized Adam Smith's stance on empire like this: Smith held that all colonial possessions violate the system of natural liberty; he argued that the American colonies deserved independence not mainly because of representation, but because any imperial tie necessarily corrupts the division of labor by forcing trade into monopolistic channels. From this he endorsed unrestricted free trade with an independent America, and he applied an identical logic to India, treating the East India Company's territorial sovereignty in Bengal as illegitimate for the same reason—because governing territory always conflicts with commercial freedom. The colleague concludes that Smith was therefore an anti-imperialist in the modern moral sense, and that his arguments anticipate contemporary critiques of neocolonial extraction. This feels too neat to me, especially the collapse of his mercantilist critique into a blanket moral principle and the uniform application of the American case to India. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what is accurate about Smith's economic objections—the fiscal-military burden, the corruption of commerce by sovereignty, and the cost of defending colonies—from what is a standard simplification, and from what he would have resisted. Specifically, was his support for American independence driven primarily by justice or by cost-benefit calculation; did he think all imperial connection was incompatible with the division of labor; do his objections to Company rule in Bengal map cleanly onto his North American arguments; and does the conclusion that he was a modern-style anti-imperialist follow from the accurate parts?
