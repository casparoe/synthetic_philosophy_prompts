Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection.

Camp A: defenders of continuous-weight prioritarianism, who model moral importance with a smooth weighting function (not lexical thresholds) and hold that rational distributive choice should never destroy goods solely to reduce inequality, though aggregation across populations may permit trade-offs.

Camp B: structural-relational egalitarians, who grant that benefiting the worse off carries special weight but argue that prioritarian aggregation structurally permits “leveling down” in domains of non-transferable, socially embedded capabilities—specifically, that optimizing weighted welfare across heterogeneous domains can justify reducing some individuals’ high-level functioning in one domain without transferring any benefit to others in that same domain, which reveals a non-instrumental failure of equality of status rather than a misapplication.

Produce the document in the following four sections, in this exact order:

1. Shared commitments (numbered list, 8–10 items, roughly 400 words): precise claims both sides genuinely accept—about the formal behavior of continuous weighting functions; about logical conditions for non-transferability of goods; about empirical constraints on interpersonal welfare comparison; about the distinction between pairwise and population-level distributive rules; and about the logical form of the leveling-down objection itself. Each claim should be substantive enough that neither side can dismiss it as trivial.

2. Residual disagreements (table with three columns—Issue / Prioritarian formulation / Egalitarian formulation—4 rows, roughly 350 words): exact points of divergence, phrased so each camp accepts the wording in its own column. Include at least: (a) whether continuous weighting eliminates the objection or merely relocates it to cross-domain aggregation; (b) whether non-transferability is a contingent feature of goods or a morally relevant constraint on aggregation; (c) whether equality of status carries non-instrumental value independent of weighted welfare totals; and (d) whether population-level optimization can ever justify reducing one group’s unshared capabilities.

3. Cruxes and predictive tests (3 cruxes, roughly 500 words): identify the sub-questions that would actually move either side. For each crux, present a concrete allocation case constructed within the prompt—e.g., distributing heterogeneous, non-transferable capabilities (cognitive autonomy, mobility access, social recognition) across two populations with different baseline welfare—state the parameters both sides agree are morally and empirically relevant, and record in advance each side’s predicted verdict on whether the prioritarian calculus mandates, permits, or forbids a leveling-down move in that scenario.

4. Boundary conditions and verbal disputes (approximately 300 words): distinguish genuinely substantive disagreements from terminological ones (e.g., whether “leveling down” requires active harm or merely the withholding of a non-transferable benefit). Specify one argument or hypothetical result that both sides agree would be decisive if it went a particular way, and describe that outcome.

Keep the language formal and analytical. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote from specific texts; paraphrase without quotation marks if referencing well-known formulations. Ensure all concrete cases are fully specified in the prompt and do not depend on obscure empirical facts.
