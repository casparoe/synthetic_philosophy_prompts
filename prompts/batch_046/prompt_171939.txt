In a recent panel on long-termist strategy, a speaker dismissed proposals for active SETI as naive optimism, repeatedly invoking the Fermi question—rendered in accounts of the 1950 Los Alamos conversation as "Where is everybody?"—to insist that cosmic silence is already decisive evidence that technological civilizations collapse before achieving detectable interstellar presence. I want to push back not by listing familiar resolutions, but by showing that the one-liner is doing ambiguous epistemic work. Can you lay out, as a menu of distinct precise theses, what "Where is everybody?" might actually be claiming: from a bare expression of surprise with no inferential weight, to an implicit Bayesian update conditioned on our own existence, to a full inductive argument about the rarity of long-lived observers? For each reading, say whether it genuinely undermines funding active SETI, whether it smuggles in a specific resolution of the paradox (such as a near-term Great Filter), and whether it survives objections drawn from reference-class selection—should we condition on being observers who have not yet been contacted?—and from the self-indication assumption regarding silent universes. I do not want a bibliography, citation list, or suggested readings, only the disambiguation with verdicts, so I can return to the panel with a precise map of which interpretations actually support the speaker's dismissal and which collapse into tautology or hidden premises.
