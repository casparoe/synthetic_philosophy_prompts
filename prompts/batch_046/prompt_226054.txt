A recent essay in a popular philosophy magazine offers the following characterisation of Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence: 'Both positions rest on the idea that enduring social arrangements contain implicit practical knowledge that present generations cannot fully articulate. Burke's doctrine of prescription holds that institutions acquire authority through continuous use across generations, while Chesterton's fence warns against dismantling a mechanism before its purpose is understood. Consequently, both reject rationalist social reconstruction from first principles. The key divergence is that Chesterton treats tradition as actively sacramental: for him, the fence protects ordinary people from expert overreach, whereas Burke's gradualism remains an essentially aristocratic defence of inherited privilege. From this shared epistemic humility, one might derive a modern institutional proposal — a constitutional review body selected by sortition, tasked with blocking reforms that lack demonstrated comprehension of the fences they would remove.'

Separate what is broadly accurate, what flattens important distinctions into a convenient simplification, and what misrepresents either thinker.
