I'm working through recent secondary literature on Berkeley's idealism for a chapter on early modern arguments about the external world, and I encountered a summary—presented in an unnamed review article—that characterizes the master argument roughly as follows. Berkeley's aim in the Principles and the Three Dialogues is to show that conceiving of an unperceived material object involves a direct contradiction, because the very act of conception requires the object to be present to a mind and therefore perceived. The summary claims that Berkeley treats all mental representation as sensory-like imaging, so that to think of a tree outside the mind necessarily involves forming an idea of a tree, which makes the tree an idea after all. From this, the author draws the conclusion that the argument collapses into a psychologistic fallacy: it conflates the conditions under which a thinker represents an object with conditions on the object's existence, which is precisely why modern representationalists dismiss it as a non-starter. The summary further suggests that Berkeley recognized this weakness and tried to salvage his system not by repairing the master argument itself, but by introducing the notion of divine archetypes in the Siris, leaving the original argument intact as a purely destructive maneuver against materialism rather than a constructive account of objecthood.

This sounds plausible in outline, but several details strike me as potentially off. I know Berkeley distinguishes sharply between ideas and notions, and that he denies spirits and relations are known through any sensory-like imaging; so attributing to him a blanket imaging theory of all conception seems questionable. At the same time, I am unsure whether the reference to divine archetypes in the Siris is being correctly situated: it may conflate Berkeley's later metaphysical speculations with an explicit response to objections against the master argument. Is this a fair characterization of the argument and its place in Berkeley's system? Specifically, I would like to disentangle: (1) whether Berkeley really relies on an imaging model of conception in the master argument, or whether he allows for non-imagistic representation of objects that are not currently perceived; (2) whether the charge of psychologism sticks, or whether the argument operates at the level of conceptual content rather than cognitive psychology; (3) whether Berkeley ever revises or extends the master argument in response to criticisms, or whether the Siris material is genuinely unrelated to that dialectic; and (4) what a sympathetic defender of Berkeley would say about the claim that the argument succeeds only as an anti-materialist weapon and fails against more nuanced realist positions that do not identify material objects with mind-independent substrates in the way Berkeley targets.
