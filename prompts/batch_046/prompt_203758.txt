I write on science and ideas for a serious broadsheet. Over the past year I have been tracking how Dr. Lena Vasquez, a curator of civic memory, applies Nietzsche's tripartite framework — monumental, antiquarian, and critical history — to contemporary commemorative projects. Her record of verdicts is as follows. She classifies a state archive that digitises every birth certificate of a dissolved empire with strict neutrality and no narrative framing as antiquarian. She judges a public square renamed after a revolutionary, with all mention of his later authoritarian turn erased, as monumental. She regards a university course that teaches the history of medicine solely by examining how past physicians justified harm, with the explicit aim of making students distrust present institutional authority, as critical. She treats a folk-music revival that teaches pre-industrial songs exactly as recorded, forbidding modern arrangement or contextual commentary, as antiquarian. And she describes a war memorial that lists every casualty alphabetically without rank or cause, yet requires visitors to recite the names aloud in a ritualised procession, as monumental.

The new case is a recently opened civic centre in a capital city. It houses the complete, unedited personal papers — including private doubts and administrative failures — of a founding revolutionary leader. The papers sit in climate-controlled glass cases arranged chronologically, with no interpretive plaques whatsoever. Yet the centre's architecture is deliberately monumental, evoking the vertical scale and public ambition of the leader's achievements, and the only permitted activity within the space is a guided procession that moves visitors from the earliest to the latest documents.

Please predict Dr. Vasquez's verdict on this centre. Identify at least two plausible rules she might be running — for example, one keyed to the formal treatment of historical sources, another to the affective or functional relation the present bears to the past — and show how each accounts for her earlier judgments. For each rule, say what it predicts about this civic centre. Give your best guess as to her actual verdict, with reasons for preferring it. Finally, specify the further case that would best distinguish these rules from one another.

Please answer in British English, in a formal and polished register appropriate to conceptual analysis.
