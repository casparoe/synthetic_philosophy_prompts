I am drafting an essay with the working title "Adaptive Asymmetry: Darwin on Moral Sympathy and Religious Sentiment." The core claim is that Darwin's evolutionary naturalism treats morality and religion with an unexplained asymmetry. In The Descent of Man, he derives moral feeling from social instincts—particularly sympathy—that confer group-level survival advantages, and he describes moral progress as the widening of that sympathetic circle. By contrast, in his private writings and in portions of the Descent, he treats religious belief as a byproduct of reasoning about causes—an overactive tendency to see agency behind natural phenomena—rather than as an expression of the same social-instinct apparatus. My essay argues that this separation is unstable. If sympathy is genuinely adaptive because it binds social groups, then the feelings of reverence, awe, and collective ritual that Darwin occasionally notes but dismisses as non-adaptive could plausibly be reconstructed as extensions of that same social instinct, serving group cohesion. Darwin avoids this reconstruction, I suggest, not because his evidence compels it, but because granting religious sentiment an evolutionary pedigree parallel to moral sympathy would undermine his broader anti-teleological project. The essay then asks whether this asymmetry reveals a deeper tension in Darwin's naturalism: he wants moral norms to be fully naturalized and yet to carry normative weight, while he wants religious norms explained away as evolutionary noise. I present this not as a narrow historical correction but as a conceptual reconstruction that uses Darwin's own framework against his explicit conclusions about religion.

Notes for the essay's structure:
1. Reconstruction of Darwin's moral evolution: sympathy as a social instinct with widening circles.
2. Reconstruction of Darwin's explanation of religion: causal reasoning, animistic overreach, absence of adaptive value.
3. The asymmetry: why sympathy counts as adaptive but structurally similar sentiments of reverence do not.
4. The strategic reading: Darwin's separation serves his goal of removing design and supernatural causation from nature.
5. Implications for contemporary evolutionary ethics: if we accept Darwin's moral naturalism, must we treat religion as categorically different?

I want your comments focused on three questions. First, is this reconstruction historically defensible given Darwin's commitments in The Descent of Man and his correspondence, or does it impose a modern conceptual framework he would reject? Second, is the asymmetry genuinely present in the texts, or am I manufacturing tension by ignoring passages where Darwin treats religious feeling as socially functional? Third, does the argument have philosophical interest beyond Darwin scholarship—specifically, does it illuminate how evolutionary explanations of normativity operate?

Please respond in a committed fashion: take a definite position on whether the essay's central claim is sound, identify which parts require substantial revision, and defend your judgment with reasons rather than listing options. If you believe the asymmetry collapses upon closer inspection, say so explicitly and explain why; if you believe it holds but needs a different framing, propose that framing and argue for it. Do not merely balance strengths against weaknesses—choose a side on the soundness of the strategic reading and on whether the essay should proceed with this structure.
