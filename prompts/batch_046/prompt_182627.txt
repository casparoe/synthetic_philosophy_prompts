I'm a doctoral student working between analytic philosophy of personal identity and Buddhist metaphysics, and I've become dissatisfied with the usual framing of anatta (the denial of ātman) as either a proto-reductionist thesis or a conventional-truth salvage operation. What I need is a precise specification — the full desiderata — for any theory that aims to reconcile the metaphysical denial of a persisting self with the conditions that make first-person ethical urgency, biographical attribution, and rebirth-talk coherent.

Specifically, lay out what such a theory must deliver: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect — for instance, between conventional designation (prajñapti) and mere illusion, or between causal continuity and personal identity — and the conceptual tensions it must dissolve, with the motivating consideration behind each. Please flag which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect camp-specific preferences (say, of Abhidharma reductionism, Madhyamaka conventionalism, or Yogācāra reflexive-awareness approaches). I am especially interested in whether there is a jointly unsatisfiable subset: explaining why suffering is structurally non-substitutable for the subject while merit-transfer remains possible, without reintroducing a hidden owner; accounting for the first-person grip of moral deliberation despite the absence of its referent; and preserving the coherence of karma-ownership across rebirth without a transmigrating substance. Then, briefly and only against this list — keeping the weight firmly on the criteria rather than on the scorecard — indicate how a Parfit-style reductionist view, a two-truths Madhyamaka account, and a Yogācāra reflexive-awareness account each fare, noting their sharpest failures. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; this should be answerable through conceptual reasoning alone.
