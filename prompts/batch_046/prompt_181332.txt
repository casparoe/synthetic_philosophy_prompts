I cover science and ideas for a general audience, and I am preparing a thirty-minute explanatory workshop—more guided discussion than lecture—for readers who follow medicine and technology but have no background in metaphysics or medieval philosophy. The concept is Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism and its denial of necessary causal connection in nature. I need a teaching plan, not a script. First, lay out the dependency order: which distinction must be secured before occasionalism sounds like a live philosophical option rather than an absurd rejection of science, and why the separation of constant conjunction from metaphysical necessity has to precede any talk of divine direct agency. Second, propose the single carrying example that should anchor the session. It should avoid the familiar fire-and-cotton pairing; instead, draw on something closer to these readers—say, an adaptive clinical algorithm that predicts patient deterioration from vital-sign patterns alone, with no embedded physiological mechanism—while specifying exactly what the example must illustrate (correlation without intrinsic causal power) and what it must lack (it should not suggest randomness or human ignorance). Third, map the misconceptions these readers will form: that occasionalism is merely religious skepticism about clinical evidence, that it denies observable regularity altogether, or that it smuggles God in as a hidden mechanical cause behind each event. For each, state precisely where in the sequence it should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, suggest one concrete check I can use at the end to see whether they have grasped occasionalism as a claim about metaphysical dependency rather than filed it away as a cultural curiosity. Fifth, state what should be left out at this level—perhaps the full metaphysics of atoms and accidents, or the detailed reply to the Aristotelian philosophers—and say why omitting it protects the core idea. I do not need citations, a bibliography, or references to specific editions; the plan should stand on its own.
