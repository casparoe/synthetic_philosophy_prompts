Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps in the quest for the historical Jesus: specifically, on whether the criteria of authenticity—dissimilarity, embarrassment, and multiple independent attestation—can ever settle who Jesus was, or whether they are structurally incapable of resolving the figure behind the tradition.

Camp A holds that these criteria, when applied with cross-validation and explicit probabilistic weighting, can yield a profile of the historical Jesus robust enough to adjudicate the central typological disputes—for instance, whether he was primarily an apocalyptic prophet or a wisdom teacher—without collapsing into circular reasoning. Camp B holds that the criteria are epistemically uncalibrated: they presuppose precisely the model of tradition development they claim to discover, and the available source pool is too small, too interdependent, and too redactionally contaminated to support any meaningful distinction between signal and noise.

Produce the document in four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about the formal structure of each criterion; about the source situation (the Synoptic problem, the independence or dependence of Q and Thomas, the temporal gap between events and texts, the presence of identifiable editorial layers); about the legitimate goal of avoiding anachronism; and about the fact that both camps reject both naive literalism and pure mythicism.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row must state the precise disagreement in neutral wording, with a left column giving Camp A's formulation of its own position and a right column giving Camp B's formulation of its own position—each phrased so that the respective camp would willingly sign it. Do not allow either side to strawman the other. Cover at least: the formal applicability of dissimilarity without circularity; whether embarrassment can be distinguished from later theological embarrassment; and whether inter-source correlation strengthens attestation or reveals shared contamination.

Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would move at least one camp. I take there to be at least two: (a) whether the criterion of dissimilarity can be operationalized without defining "authentic" as "what does not fit reconstructed church theology"; and (b) whether the scarcity and mutual dependence of sources permits any credible false-positive rate for reconstructed sayings. For each crux, specify a concrete textual case—such as the "Son of Man" sayings or the Temple-action logion—or a formal argument that both camps agree bears on it. Then record, in advance and separately for each camp, what that camp predicts the case or argument will show, including the conditions under which that camp would update.

Fourth, a brief concluding section that separates verbal from substantive disagreement. Specifically, address whether the dispute over whether the criteria can "settle" the question is partly about the meaning of "settle" (e.g., near-certainty versus best-available-explanation), and mark exactly where the camps diverge on values—such as the acceptable risk of false positives in historical reconstruction—versus on factual or methodological claims.

The document should assume no web access: do not request bibliographies, reading lists, page-number citations, or verbatim quotations from scholarly works. Write from the standpoint of a longtime reader of rationalist forums who takes adversarial collaboration, explicit crux identification, and calibrated prediction seriously, and who wants this particular methodological deadlock treated with corresponding rigor rather than with generic survey-style summary.
