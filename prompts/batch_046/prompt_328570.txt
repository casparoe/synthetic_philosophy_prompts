I've been trying to work through the color phi illusion on my own—the effect where two differently colored dots flashed in quick succession seem to travel through space and change color midway—even though I have no formal background in philosophy of mind. Reading informal summaries, I keep encountering a distinction between an "Orwellian" revision, where the brain allegedly rewrites the record of experience after it happens, and a "Stalinesque" revision, where the brain withholds experience from consciousness until it has assembled an edited version, like a censored film reel held back for approval. The framing makes it sound as though these are competing explanations for whether we are genuinely deceived.

But the more I think about it, the more I wonder whether the Stalinesque account doesn't actually dissolve the problem rather than explain it. If consciousness is simply running on a fixed broadcast delay—say, two or three hundred milliseconds—then by the time any percept reaches awareness, the brain has already received both flashes and can construct the intermediate color change honestly. In that case, there is no false intermediate experience being inserted into memory; there is only one accurate, delayed report of the whole sequence. So why do philosophers treat the Orwellian version as the more disturbing possibility? Shouldn't a permanent, built-in lag between reality and awareness be the more unsettling conclusion, since it means we are never experiencing the present moment but always a reconstructed past? And if that lag is real, doesn't color phi stop being an illusion at all and become instead direct evidence that all conscious perception is essentially predictive editing?
