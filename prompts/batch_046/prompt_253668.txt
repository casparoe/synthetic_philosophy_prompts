I'm preparing a long-form feature on whether the idea of moral progress holds up under philosophical scrutiny, particularly through Peter Singer's framework of the expanding circle of moral concern. Lately, however, I've become convinced that the standard literature is conceptually lopsided: it treats progress almost exclusively as extensive—widening the radius of beings granted direct moral consideration—while neglecting what might be called intensive progress, in which the very criteria for possessing moral status are reconceptualized rather than merely applied to a larger set of entities. Before I commit my narrative to either framework, I need a definite judgment rather than an even-handed survey. Among the open conceptual questions in this domain—whether an extensive model can accommodate rights-based expansions without collapsing into welfare aggregation; whether genuine moral learning requires a corresponding contraction in tribal or partial moral commitments; how to distinguish authentic moral change from adaptive preference manipulation in historical cases like abolition or the emergence of animal advocacy; and whether an intensive reconceptualization of moral status undermines the coherence of comparing moral frameworks across time—which two or three should command the scarce attention of philosophers working on moral change over the coming years? Please defend an explicit ordering: which questions are conceptually upstream of the others, which are tractable for philosophical analysis without leaning on contested empirical social science, and which, if settled, would most fundamentally reshape how journalists should frame narratives of improvement? I want a committed agenda with clear reasons, not a menu of options, and I'm particularly interested in whether the extensive-intensive distinction you've just heard is a productive research axis or a distracting red herring.
