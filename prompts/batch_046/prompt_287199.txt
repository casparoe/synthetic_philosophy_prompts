At a recent meetup of an online rationalist reading group, two regulars—I'll call them Maya and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about how to practice epistemic modesty when your own reasoning feels unusually clear. The exchange started after Maya said she had become convinced of a somewhat unpopular view in philosophy of mind—specifically, that a functionalist account of consciousness fails for reasons tied to the explanatory gap—but that she was holding her confidence at only about 55 percent because she knew philosophers disagree widely and she was almost certainly missing something.

Leo pushed back. He said her discount was too mechanical. The outside view tells us that smart people disagree, he granted, but applying that base rate requires you to first classify yourself into a reference class—say, "trained philosophers" versus "generalists with an opinion"—and that classification is itself an inside-view judgment. If you try to correct every inside-view belief with an outside view, you run into what he called a reference-class recursion: the correction mechanism depends on the very reasoning you're trying to correct. So pure outside-view deference isn't coherent; you have to let some inside-view reasoning stand, at least long enough to decide which base rate applies.

Maya replied that Leo was overstating the recursion. You can break it, she said, by anchoring to the broadest available reference class—"humans trying to solve hard philosophical problems"—and refusing to update upward unless you have genuinely exceptional external validation, not just the feeling that your arguments hang together. The fact that you need an inside-view step to pick the class doesn't license trusting your specific conclusion; it just means modesty operates one level up, at the meta-level of choosing your prior.

Leo countered that this "anchor broad and stay low" rule sounds principled but collapses in practice. Everyone who holds an unpopular view can claim they're applying the broad base rate, yet they all secretly use inside-view cues—clarity, coherence, the sense that objections have answers—to decide when they're the exception. If you genuinely never updated upward based on your own reasoning, you'd have to treat your belief in the broad base rate itself with the same skepticism, since that's also a philosophical claim formed through reasoning. Maya's position, he said, smuggles in inside-view confidence under the guise of methodological humility.

Maya then offered a distinction: there's a difference between using inside-view reasoning to form a first-order belief and using it to calibrate your confidence in that belief. The outside view should suppress the second, not the first. You can think you've found a good argument without claiming you're likely right about the conclusion, given human fallibility.

Leo said that distinction doesn't survive contact with actual cognition. If you genuinely believed your confidence should stay near the base rate regardless of argument quality, you'd stop engaging with objections as if they could change your mind; you'd treat your own reasoning as entertainment rather than evidence. But Maya clearly was engaging with objections seriously, which revealed that her inside view was doing real epistemic work—she just wasn't accounting for it in her confidence reports.

They didn't resolve it, and I honestly couldn't tell who was right about what. Could you adjudicate the exchange point by point—rating the strength of each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating, and saying who is more right overall? In particular, address whether Leo's recursion objection actually undermines Maya's practice of discounting, or whether Maya's "meta-level" reply successfully contains it. Also, say what both are leaving out about how epistemic modesty should work in domains where reference classes are genuinely ambiguous and where the outside view itself is constructed from inside-view reports.
