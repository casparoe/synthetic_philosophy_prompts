Please argue that Pascal's observation in the Pensées — that justice without force is powerless, but force without justice is tyrannical — isn't just descriptive politics but actually hides a normative condition for legitimate authority. Call it coercive legitimation: the idea that punishment only counts as just if the subjects have genuinely internalized the moral rightness of the law, not just fear it. Then push the point that this creates a serious tension with Pascal's Jansenist view that fallen human reason can't reliably produce genuine moral belief about institutions. If people are too corrupt to truly believe human laws are just, then coercive legitimation is impossible, and every human authority becomes naked tyranny — which is a stronger conclusion than Pascal seems to want. So argue that his justice/force claim, taken seriously, forces him toward a kind of political quietism that undermines his own practical acceptance of social order. Keep it conceptual; don't bother with bibliographies or page numbers, I just want the argument laid out.
