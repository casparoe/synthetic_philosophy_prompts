A member violated confidentiality by revealing a client's identity, but only to avert an imminent harm that the manual does not explicitly address. One board member, coming from a tradition where procedural fidelity is treated as the primary expression of professional integrity, insists we apply the prescribed suspension: allowing context to override the text, he argues, replaces the rule of law with the rule of personal feeling and erodes the trust that makes collective practice possible. I want to know not only how to vote, but whether a society or institution that demands near-categorical rule-compliance actually produces better moral agents, or whether it merely fosters skilled evasion and moral passivity. Is there a coherent middle position that gives rules serious weight without treating them as absolute, and what would it recommend in this specific case? Please write your response in American English. If my framing rests on a false dichotomy—say, between proceduralism and conscience-based judgment—or asks something that cannot be answered well, say so plainly rather than accommodating it politely.
