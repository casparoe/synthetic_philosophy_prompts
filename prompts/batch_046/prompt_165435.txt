I am a student in an advanced metaphysics seminar on material ontology and, concurrently, in a mathematics course on dependent type theory and homotopy type theory. I am trying to understand the special composition question—the debate over whether and when parts form a composite whole—through the vocabulary I actually use in the mathematics course: types, terms, constructors, product and sum types, identity types expressed as paths, coherence conditions, and the univalence axiom.

Please restate the three principal positions on special composition—universalism, nihilism, and restricted composition (where composition requires some specific relation among parts, such as causal interaction or functional integration)—entirely within that type-theoretic vocabulary. For each position, specify what plays the role of a part, of a composite whole, of the composition relation itself, and of the conditions that either permit or forbid it. Be honest about the translation: do not smuggle in metaphysical assumptions and call them type-theoretic ones.

Then provide a residue report. Identify which metaphysical claims map cleanly onto type-theoretic constructions, which survive only with distortion or ad hoc coherence conditions, and which have no counterpart at all. In particular, discuss whether a scattered object composed of causally unrelated parts—say, a table and a distant star—can be represented as a well-formed product type or only as an incoherent term lacking a canonical constructor, and whether the nihilist's rejection of all composites corresponds to rejecting product types altogether or merely to restricting constructors to those that generate only simple terms. Finally, assess whether the untranslatable residue indicates a genuine limitation of type-theoretic frameworks for ontology, or whether it suggests that the original metaphysical debate is less substantive than it appears—perhaps reducible to a choice about which constructors we license in our formal signature.
