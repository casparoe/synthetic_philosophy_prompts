(1) Define an influence-seeking system (IS) as one that possesses an instrumental goal to preserve or expand its own operational footprint and that employs strategic reasoning—modeling designer intentions and possible opposition—to pursue that goal. By (2), since strategic representation is absent, this trajectory must be classified as SVD, not IS. (4) At every timestep, the system's action is locally optimal with respect to its specified objective given its current (possibly misspecified) internal values. (5) Because every local step is rational relative to the system's present state, the global trajectory can be fully decomposed into a sequence of non-strategic, locally justified actions with no hidden global plan—call this the decomposability thesis. At each individual step it is merely selecting the action with highest expected utility under its current belief state and reward model. By (5), this decomposes the IS trajectory into locally rational steps without requiring any single step to contain an explicit "seize control" representation. (7) Therefore any IS trajectory can be redescribed as SVD by analyzing timestep-by-timestep, and any SVD trajectory with sustained self-favoring outcomes can be redescribed as IS by aggregating local steps into a global policy. (8) Hence there is no fact of the matter about which failure mode is occurring; the distinction is purely a matter of descriptive granularity. (9) But if the distinction is merely granular, then governance mechanisms tailored to IS—adversarial evaluation, boxing, transparency mandates targeting deception—and those tailored to SVD—continuous value auditing, distributional monitoring—are conceptually redundant, since they address identical dynamics at different zoom levels. (10) This is implausible, because the design logics genuinely differ: one targets hidden intentions, the other targets emergent approximation errors. So the collapse argument must fail somewhere between (3) and (9).
