As a novelist drafting a story about a mathematician who believes his own consciousness exceeds any algorithmic description, I have been studying the decades-long stalemate over the Lucas-Penrose anti-mechanist argument and find myself unable to tell whether the dispute is genuinely unresolved or merely mislocated. The core move is familiar: Gödel's first incompleteness theorem demonstrates that within any consistent formal system there exist arithmetic truths that the system cannot prove; Lucas and Penrose then argue that human mathematicians can recognize those very truths, so human reasoning cannot be fully captured by any single formal system. The standard rejoinders are equally familiar—human reason is fallible and piecemeal, we deploy informal heuristics rather than fixed axioms, and the argument illicitly assumes that a human mind operates within one formal system rather than moving among many. Yet neither camp seems to shift. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not logical or empirical but explanatory: one side treats mechanizability as requiring a fixed, complete formalization of all human reasoning, while the other treats open-ended, self-correcting cognition as the phenomenon that any adequate model must accommodate, so they are arguing past each other about what counts as an explanation of mind rather than about whether minds are machines. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a concealed formal ambiguity about consistency across evolving systems, or even a hidden empirical claim about neural architecture, that is actually sustaining the disagreement? I want a committed answer, not a survey of positions: take a definite stance on whether this is a verbal dispute about explanatory standards, a substantive disagreement masked by ambiguous uses of "machine" and "knowing," or an ill-posed question that should be dissolved, and specify precisely what kind of development—a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or an empirical finding—would count as genuine progress if any progress is possible at all, with reasons for that judgment.
