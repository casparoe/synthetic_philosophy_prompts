I have been working out a justification for behavioral nudges that I call proportional-autonomy contractualism, and I want an honest assessment of how exposed it really is. The position holds that a choice-architectural intervention is ethically legitimate not primarily because it preserves formal freedom of choice—the standard libertarian-paternalist defense—but because the welfare improvement it produces for the affected agent stands in the same proportion to that agent's ideal-point welfare as the collective welfare improvement stands to the collective's ideal-point welfare, a structure I borrow directly from the monotonicity logic of the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution. So, for example, a default opt-in for retirement savings might be justified if the individual's expected welfare gain (security in old age relative to perfect liquidity) is large in proportion to their autonomous ideal, while the state's gain (lower future dependency costs relative to its fiscal-optimal budget) is modest in proportion to its collective ideal; the ratios align, and the nudge respects proportional improvement rather than merely leaving an escape route. What would have to be true for this framework to fail?

I am particularly interested in empirical findings about autonomy, psychological reactance, and preference adaptation under nudges: which results would show that people do not experience proportional welfare gains as legitimating, and which would merely complicate the measurement of ideal points? I also want the formal and conceptual vulnerabilities made explicit. Can individual and collective welfare be mapped onto comparable ideal-point spaces without category errors? Are there intuitive cases where the ratios align yet the nudge is clearly illegitimate, or where they diverge yet the intervention seems acceptable? Could a committed defender insulate the view by redefining baselines or ideal points, and if so, does that render the position unfalsifiable in a way that is itself a defect? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that defenders of proportional-autonomy contractualism would concede from those they would dismiss, and assess whether the view has been qualified into vacuity or remains genuinely at risk.
