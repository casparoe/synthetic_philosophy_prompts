A recurring analogy in rationalist discussions of indirect normativity compares shaping one's values over time to training a deep neural network: you do not set the weights by hand, you specify an architecture and a loss function, curate the training data, and trust gradient descent to converge on something sensible. The analogy is deployed to defend a particular stance on idealised deliberation — namely, that direct value-specification is both impossible and undesirable, and that we should instead focus on curating our information environment and trusting the convergence properties of a well-structured mind. Recently, some writers have added a twist: they treat indirect norms as a form of 'normative escrow', where commitments are locked in now but released to the agent only upon reaching specified epistemic conditions, much like a smart contract.

Audit the core analogy and its escrow extension. Give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the engineer, the architecture, the loss function, the training set, and gradient descent in the case of personal value-formation? Then identify the fractures: is there any analogue of a fixed loss function in human psychology, does the 'engineer' have authority over the system they inhabit, and does convergence have any guaranteed endpoint? Next, examine whether the 'normative escrow' modification repairs any of these breaks or introduces new ones — for instance, does it solve the problem of present-self bias, or does it merely relocate it to the design of the release conditions? Finally, say whether the conclusion — that we should be relaxed about direct value-choice and instead curate inputs — survives once the disanalogies are explicit, or whether the neural-network framing was carrying the normative load all along. If the analogy collapses, suggest what a more honest model of indirect normativity would look like.
