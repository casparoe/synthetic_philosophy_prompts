I'm presenting next Thursday at a transitional justice seminar. My thesis: in post-authoritarian societies the state should fund not just memorial archives but also active forgetting programs for survivors who request them — anonymized records, subsidized relocation away from commemorative sites, removal of local plaques — and this doesn't breach any collective duty to remember if we understand remembrance as distributive rather than universal across the population. The room is mostly transitional justice lawyers who equate state forgetting with impunity, plus memory-studies scholars steeped in Margalit's arguments about shared remembrance obligations, and one archivist who cares about record integrity. There's also a cultural historian influenced by Rieff-style skepticism about institutionalized grief who thinks we're already too theraputic. Prep me for the Q&A: the objections in the order they'll hit, phrased in their own vocab, with what to concede, what to hold, and the weakest link in my distributive framing that I should defend rather than hope they miss. Also flag the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one about small survivor groups where distributive logic collapses.
