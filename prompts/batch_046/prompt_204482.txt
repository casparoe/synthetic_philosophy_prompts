I am a novelist asked to deliver the opening address at the inaugural assembly of the Meridian Foundation, which supports both quantum-physics education and wildlife-ethics programmes. The audience is divided evenly between physicists who treat panpsychism as a serious interpretive option in fundamental physics and philanthropists who have never encountered the concept. I want you, acting as an expert philosopher of science with experience in public rhetoric, to think carefully about how to structure a twelve-minute speech around one precise tension: if consciousness is genuinely distributed at the fundamental level, then suffering might not be localised in organisms but diffused through physical interaction, yet this must not collapse into either sentimental anthropomorphism of particles or an ethical abstraction that dissolves all particular concern. Begin with brief brainstorming: identify the two misreadings the physicists and the ethicists are each likely to impose, and note the conceptual pivot that prevents both. Then compose the address, which must open with an image drawn from fiction — a character discovering that the grief she believed was hers alone is mirrored in the thermal noise of the room — and use that image to frame the Foundation’s actual work without claiming that atoms feel pain. The speech should conclude not with a false resolution but with a specific, practical pledge the Foundation can adopt that respects the conceptual difficulty: for example, a protocol for funding experiments that considers whether an intervention at one scale might redistribute rather than eliminate harm. Do not cite named philosophers, do not include a reading list, and do not quote any text verbatim. Write in polished British English suitable for delivery in a formal hall.
