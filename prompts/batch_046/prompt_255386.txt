I've been trying to work out what I actually think about personal identity by testing my intuitions against the Buddhist idea of anatta—the view that there is no fixed, permanent self, only shifting aggregates of sensation, perception, mental formation, and consciousness. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them or whether some have to go.

If every neuron in a person's brain is gradually replaced by a synthetic equivalent over twenty years, with no interruption to conscious experience, I think it is still them. But if the same total replacement happens in an instant—scanning the brain, destroying the original, and booting up the copy—I think it is not them; it is a perfect duplicate, not the same continuing subject. If a monk spends thirty years in deep meditation and reports that his childhood memories now feel like scenes from a stranger's life, even though he can narrate them accurately, I think he is spiritually correct that the old self has dissolved, though for legal and social purposes we should still use his name. If a brain is split and each hemisphere is placed in a different body, I think neither body contains the original person; since there was never a core self to divide, both are new streams of consciousness. If a person dies and, purely by coincidence, a newborn elsewhere possesses all the same memories, personality traits, and emotional patterns with no causal link, I think that newborn is definitely not the same person.

These judgments seem partly aligned with anatta, yet they pull in different directions: gradual physical continuity saves identity in the first case, but absence of a causal thread kills it in the coincidence case, while the split-brain case denies that there was anything to preserve at all. The case I keep revisiting is this: suppose a precise medical procedure suppresses only the autobiographical memory cluster—every personal narrative and dated self-reference—while leaving skills, emotional temperament, and reasoning fully intact, and the patient insists they feel like the same continuous subject from the inside. Is that the original person suffering a strange amnesia, or is it a new consciousness inheriting old habits?

Is there one principle—perhaps drawing on causal-functional continuity, narrative coherence, or the Buddhist denial of a substrate—that captures most of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, predict what it says about the suppressed-memory case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without becoming ad hoc. If no single principle works, present the two strongest candidates and say which verdict each forces me to drop.
