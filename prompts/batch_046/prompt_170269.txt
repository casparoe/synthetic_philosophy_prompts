For my graduate seminar in the metaphysics of science, I am drafting a 90-minute essay examination on counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation. I want the question to resist simple regurgitation of Lewis’s possible-worlds dependency analysis or Woodward’s invariance-under-intervention framework. To that end, build the question around a constructed case I will call a redundant-dormant structure: mechanism M1 produces effect E, while mechanism M2 is structurally sufficient for E but is actively suppressed by M1’s very operation (not merely absent), such that an intervention breaking M1 also removes its suppression of M2, allowing M2 to produce E. The exam question should ask students to do four things: (a) apply the counterfactual account to determine whether M1 counts as a cause of E, given that in the nearest world where M1 is absent, M2 is no longer suppressed and E still occurs; (b) apply the interventionist account, distinguishing an intervention that breaks M1 alone (leaving suppression intact, so E is absent) from one that breaks M1 and removes suppression (so E occurs via M2), and explain whether invariance holds; (c) argue whether this divergence reveals a substantive metaphysical disagreement about what causation is, or merely a difference in how each theory formalizes background conditions and intervention spaces; and (d) assess whether an interventionist can accommodate the counterfactualist intuition that M2’s presence is causally relevant without abandoning the claim that invariance is fundamental. Provide the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 points. The scheme should specify five must-hit analytical moves, assign marks to each (totaling 20), and include for each a one-line note on the characteristic error—such as conflating preemption with redundant-dormancy, treating suppression as a separate cause rather than a background condition, or assuming that agreement on the actual-world judgment settles the theoretical dispute—that typically separates an A-range answer from a B-range answer.
