You are a senior compliance officer at a manufacturing firm. Eighteen months ago, you redesigned your team’s supplier-monitoring protocol: instead of giving auditors direct access to raw inspection reports, you implemented an automated aggregation layer that you personally configured. The layer computes a single "supply-health score" from dozens of sub-metrics, and you set the weights so that isolated but serious violations—say, a critical safety failure at one facility—are diluted across the broader portfolio and rarely pull the aggregate below the passing threshold. Your team now reviews only the score; the underlying files are technically available but are not part of the standard workflow, and you have not opened them in over a year.

This week, a junior auditor mentioned, in passing, that he had seen unfiltered documents for one supplier that listed repeated, severe violations. You interrupted him, told him that the aggregate score for that supplier was well within acceptable bounds, and insisted that there was no need to examine raw data when the summary metric was clear. You genuinely believe the supplier is compliant. At the same time, you recall that you designed the weighting scheme precisely because you anticipated that raw reports might contain alarming outliers that you did not want to be forced to act upon, and you wanted a framework that would let you continue operations without confronting those outliers directly.

You are now asking for an analysis of your mental state. Is it coherent to say that you do not know about the violations, given that you engineered the very system that prevents you from seeing them? Can a person be sincerely ignorant of a fact that she has deliberately arranged not to learn, or does the deliberation necessarily undermine the sincerity? More generally, does your situation illustrate a genuinely coherent mental division—one in which an attitude of belief and an attitude of avoidance are sustained not within a single hidden compartment, but distributed across the rules you wrote, the reports you avoid, and the convictions you sincerely maintain?

Please address the philosophical structure here rather than offering generic workplace advice. In particular, discuss whether intentional design of one's own ignorance is compatible with genuinely failing to know something one has caused oneself not to know, what distinctions among knowledge, belief, and motivated avoidance are doing the work, and whether your responsibility for the supplier violations is affected by the fact that your blindness is both self-imposed and, by your own lights, sincere.
