Write the text of a commencement address, approximately six minutes, for a liberal-arts college whose graduating class has spent the past year divided over whether to rename the central library honoring a benefactor whose legacy combines transformative philanthropy with policies now widely condemned. The audience is mixed: philosophy faculty who will recognize Jain epistemological references, and family members who will not. Use anekāntavāda — specifically the doctrine of syādvāda (the seven-fold qualified assertion) and the naya framework of partial standpoints — as the structural backbone of the speech, not as ornament. The address must demonstrate that holding a firm moral judgment about the benefactor's harmful actions is structurally compatible with recognizing that any single narrative about him is necessarily one-sided, and it must give the graduates a practical framework for sustaining conviction without epistemic closure. Avoid collapsing into relativism ("all sides are equally valid") or triumphalism ("the correct view is now settled"). Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific scholars; the philosophical work should be done by the concepts themselves.
