I'm writing a feature on the conceptual architecture behind federal crisis governance, and I've become convinced that commentators treat subsidiarity as a constitutional ornament rather than as an empirical claim about knowledge. To test that conviction, I want to examine the European Union's decision in June 2020 to centralize COVID-19 vaccine procurement through the Commission—blocking individual member-state negotiations—followed by the rollout disparities, the AstraZeneca delivery disputes, and the eventual relaxation that let national governments adapt distribution schedules to local epidemiological conditions. Analyze the episode through subsidiarity understood specifically as an epistemic principle: not merely that decisions belong at the lowest competent level, but that competence is defined by access to distributed, tacit knowledge that central aggregation corrupts or loses. Map the players precisely—the Commission's Joint Negotiation Team, national health ministries, regional logistics networks, and the pharmaceutical firms themselves—identifying what each actually knew and when they knew it. Where did centralization yield genuine bargaining advantages that no single state could replicate, and where did it suppress granular, context-specific information about cold-chain capacity, population density, or local infection curves? Then run the analysis in reverse: does the EU's hybrid outcome—centralized purchase, decentralized delivery—confirm that subsidiarity tracks knowledge distribution, or does it reveal that in acute crisis, the relevant knowledge migrates upward, rendering the principle either inapplicable or merely retrospective? Be disciplined about where the framework is silent, and avoid forcing every administrative failure into an epistemic narrative.
