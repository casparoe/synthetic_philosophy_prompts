I'm working through the Hart–Fuller debate for a graduate seminar and have constructed an argument that seems to collapse legal positivism into natural law, but the conclusion feels too neat to be right. Here are the steps: (1) For Hart, a rule is valid law only if it satisfies the rule of recognition, which requires that legal officials accept it from the internal point of view. (2) To accept a rule from the internal point of view is to treat it as a normative standard for correct behavior, not merely to predict that sanctions will follow disobedience. (3) A normative standard must be capable of figuring in practical reasoning without contradiction; one cannot simultaneously treat a rule as supplying a reason to act and regard it as supplying no reason at all. (4) Now consider what I will call moral quarantine: the psychological strategy of applying a rule one judges deeply immoral by isolating that judgment in a separate mental compartment, allowing compliance without endorsement. (5) Moral quarantine explains behavior, but it does not provide a normative reason; it is a mechanism of compartmentalized compliance, not internal acceptance. (6) So if an official applies a rule she considers immoral, she is in moral quarantine and is not accepting it from the internal point of view. (7) Therefore, for Hart's condition to be met, officials must regard the rule as providing at least a minimal normative reason, which requires some moral endorsement of its content. (8) Hence legal validity necessarily depends on moral merit, and the separation thesis fails. I particularly want to preserve (1) and (3), and I want to avoid the conclusion that Nazi Germany had no law. Where is the weakest link—is it the inference from (3) to (4), the characterization of moral quarantine in (4)–(5), or the move in (7)? What does rejecting that step cost, and can I still maintain that immoral rules are genuinely law?
