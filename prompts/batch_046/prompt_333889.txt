I have been trying to understand the long-running disagreement about whether culture or values can genuinely cause large-scale economic and social changes, particularly in the debate over Max Weber's argument that Protestant ethical orientations helped shape the rise of modern capitalism. On one side, people point to regional patterns—Calvinist areas developing stronger commercial practices earlier—to suggest that beliefs really did steer history. On the other side, critics reply that law, geography, and material incentives explain the same patterns without invoking anyone's inner life, and that treating "culture" as a cause risks mistaking a symptom for a driver. Despite decades of statistical work and historical case studies, the dispute seems permanently stuck. My working theory is that the stalemate is not mainly about missing data. Rather, the two sides appear to be operating with incompatible pictures of what an explanation must look like: one treats values as a kind of switch that directs material forces without being reducible to them, while the other demands that any real cause be something that can be measured independently of the behavior it explains, like capital availability or climate. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there an underlying empirical disagreement—perhaps about which specific doctrines mattered, or about whether similar economic transformations ever occurred without those beliefs—that is actually keeping the debate alive? I am asking for an honest structural diagnosis: is this a verbal conflict over the word "cause," a clash about standards of evidence, a hidden dispute over whether mental states can enter causal chains at all, or are the sides simply answering different questions under the same name? And if the core problem is conceptual, could a precise distinction—say, between culture acting as a selecting mechanism that filters opportunities versus an initiating force that creates them—actually move the conversation forward? Or should we expect no resolution, because the question is ill-posed? Please avoid surveying every interpretation; instead, explain what kind of disagreement this really is, why it has resisted settlement, and what—whether a historical counterexample, a formal model of social causation, or a clarification of terms—would count as genuine progress.
