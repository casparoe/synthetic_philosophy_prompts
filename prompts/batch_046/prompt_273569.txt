Following a prolonged and rather frustrating discussion with a friend—she works in behavioral economics and regards philosophy of action as largely speculative—I have agreed to explain, in one focused session, why the Knobe effect poses a genuine problem for our folk concept of intentional action rather than merely revealing a bias in moral judgment. She is analytically sharp but has no background in philosophy; she will arrive assuming that “doing something intentionally” is a straightforward psychological category and that any asymmetry in our attributions must reduce to blame, social desirability, or simple irrationality. I want a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself: the precise sequence in which the conceptual dependencies must unfold, beginning with the baseline folk-psychological conditions under which we ordinarily withhold or grant intentionality, then moving to the carrying scenario, then to the empirical result, and only then to the philosophical interpretation. For the carrying example, do not use the over-familiar CEO-and-environment vignette; instead, construct a structurally parallel case—perhaps an architect whose redesigned public plaza unintentionally becomes either a site of harmful vandalism or a venue for beneficial community organizing—explain why this variant works better for an audience skeptical of corporate framing, specify exactly which structural features it must preserve and which it may alter without breaking the demonstration, and indicate where in the sequence it should be introduced relative to the baseline conditions. I also need the misconception map: she will likely conflate the effect with the fundamental attribution error, or assume it proves people are irrational, or think it merely shows that moral evaluation “leaks into” an otherwise neutral descriptive judgment; identify the exact point in the explanatory arc where each misconception should be headed off, and distinguish the apparent tension—that normativity influences judgment—from the deeper issue of whether normativity is actually constitutive of the concept of intentional action. Include one precise diagnostic question for the end that reveals whether she has grasped that the asymmetry threatens conceptual analysis rather than just predictive accuracy. Finally, tell me what should be deliberately left unsaid at this level—perhaps the full literature on causal deviance, adverbial theories, or the taxonomy of side-effect conditions—and address one specific complication we argued about: whether inserting a morally neutral control condition, in which the side effect is neither harmful nor beneficial, undermines the standard interpretation of the asymmetry, and if so, exactly where in the arc that control belongs and what it is meant to test.
