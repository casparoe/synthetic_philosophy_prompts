I am drafting a novel about a writer who, after a corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy, attempts to finish a memoir that requires reconciling sharply divergent emotional responses—one hemisphere registering grief, the other an almost clinical detachment. I want the fiction to be conceptually rigorous rather than merely symbolic, so I have been trying to map the actual philosophical terrain around split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness. The literature seems to branch in several directions: whether the procedure genuinely produces two conscious subjects rather than one divided subject; whether the right hemisphere possesses underappreciated narrative or linguistic capacities that would complicate any simple “silent partner” model; what these cases imply for the unity of consciousness in intact brains; and whether personal identity can bifurcate in a way that undermines standard psychological-continuity accounts. Each sounds important, but they cannot all be upstream for my project. If you were advising me—not on neuroscience per se, but on where the conceptual weight actually lies—which two or three open questions should I treat as decisive? I want you to rank them by criteria you make explicit: tractability with current clinical and imaging methods, how many other debates depend on resolving this one first, and whether an answer would genuinely reshape practice in clinical ethics, legal agency, or artistic theory rather than merely refine taxonomy. Take a committed position: if the evidence genuinely underdetermines whether one or two minds are present, tell me which interpretation I should write with and why it serves both philosophical coherence and narrative integrity. I am not asking for a survey of Sperry’s experiments or a reading list; I need a defended agenda that identifies where the action is, what would change your ranking, and which lines of inquiry are overrated distractions for someone working without laboratory access but with serious conceptual investment.
