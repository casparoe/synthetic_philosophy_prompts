In my philosophy of mind seminar, two classmates argued about whether moral status is genuinely gradated or only binary with fuzzy edges. Maya said that if sentience comes in degrees, then a learning-enabled climate controller—which predicts temperature shifts and adjusts heating preemptively—must occupy some intermediate position between an insect and an ordinary thermostat, since its predictive loop involves a minimal form of anticipation. Leo replied that anticipation without subjective experience is mere computation, so the controller remains at zero moral status; yet he added that if we deny it any status, consistency demands we deny bees as well, because their nervous systems are also just predictive loops operating at a larger scale. Maya countered that scale and integration matter: bees possess a unified point of view with integrated valence, whereas the controller lacks any such center, so gradation applies within organisms but not across substrates. Leo responded that invoking “integrated valence” smuggles in a hidden binary criterion, which collapses the supposed gradient back into a threshold. They turned to me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: who is right about the predictive-loop comparison, about the relevance of substrate versus scale, and about whether integrated valence undermines gradation? Also say what, if anything, both sides are missing about how gradation should be mapped onto functional architecture.
