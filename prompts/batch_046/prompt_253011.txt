Consider the institutional architecture of hate-speech regulation across liberal democracies. European criminal statutes typically prohibit group defamation, the denial of well-documented historical atrocities, and expressions that incite hatred against protected categories, even absent any direct call to violence; American constitutional doctrine, by contrast, protects such expressions unless they fall into narrow categories like true threats or incitement to imminent lawless action. Yet within the United States, private universities and digital platforms routinely enforce speech codes and deplatforming policies that are substantially broader than European criminal law, banning hostile-environment rhetoric and symbolic insults that American courts would shield. Meanwhile, European democracies often rely on non-legal mechanisms—media councils, educational campaigns, and professional disciplinary boards—to regulate expression in spaces where their criminal codes do not reach.

What theory, or family of theories, about the nature of speech-related harm does this cross-institutional, cross-jurisdictional pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit commitments: does the divergence assume that harm operates through one mechanism that only coercive state authority can suppress, and through another that private norm-setting bodies are uniquely positioned to address? Does it presuppose that dignity is a public good requiring democratic containment in one sphere and a relational good maintained by community norms in another? Or does it instead conceal an unacknowledged philosophical tension—perhaps that European law treats virulent speech as an instrument of domination requiring state intervention, while American private institutions treat it primarily as a risk to institutional brand and user retention, with neither framework consistently grounded in a theory of autonomy or recognition?

Distinguish strictly what the practice presupposes from what merely accommodates it. Is the divergence coherent under a single framework—say, one that distinguishes coercive authority from expressive community authority—or is it philosophically underdetermined, compatible with rival views about harm, tolerance, and the limits of liberal neutrality? And if we replaced the implicit theory with a uniform, jurisdiction-agnostic account of speech harm—whether a strict Millian harm principle or a robust recognition-based theory—what concrete changes would have to occur in both criminal statutes and private institutional codes?
