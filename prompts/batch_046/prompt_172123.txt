Analyze the late-1990s U.S. impeachment proceedings—the congressional and public blame directed at Bill Clinton for perjury and obstruction regarding his relationship with Monica Lewinsky—through the lens of the philosophical literature on standing to blame and the hypocrisy objection. Treat the key Republican figures who led the charge but were subsequently revealed to have engaged in extramarital affairs—Newt Gingrich, Bob Livingston, and Henry Hyde—not as mere rhetorical counterexamples, but as a structured test of whether personal hypocrisy defeats standing on a “clean hands” account. Map precisely who the blamers were, what norm they invoked, whether their own violations were of the same kind, and whether the public treated hypocrisy as undermining the blame itself or only the blamer’s credibility. Then ask whether the episode reveals that standing is a genuine moral condition or a social credential tracking institutional power: did the fact that these figures retained formal authority despite exposure show that standing is dispensable when power is present? Conclude by assessing whether the case forces a distinction between internal standing (the blamer’s own moral record) and external standing (their structural position in a practice that systematically protects similar conduct), and whether that distinction saves the concept or dissolves it into politics.
