In my high school philosophy course we are studying the inefficacy objection to individual action on climate change and meat consumption. I have noticed that defenders of the objection often rely on an analogy comparing one person's greenhouse-gas emissions to a single thread pulled from a massive tapestry: the overall image remains intact, they say, which is meant to license the claim that no individual thread bears moral responsibility for the pattern. Meanwhile, critics invoke a counter-analogy comparing collective harm to a crowd of people each applying slight pressure to push a victim onto train tracks—individually each push is redundant, yet together they are sufficient—asking why the tapestry framing should dominate. Can you audit both analogies explicitly? For each, state the correspondence (thread to emission, tapestry to climate system; individual pusher to consumer, victim to affected populations), then locate the disanalogies: does the tapestry picture falsely assume linear, additive harm and ignore non-linear tipping points and feedback loops? Does the crowd analogy conflate causal contribution with normative complicity, and does it misrepresent the structure of overdetermined climate harm? Finally, assess whether either analogy genuinely supports its intended conclusion—moral insignificance versus shared responsibility—or whether both collapse once we distinguish between being a necessary condition, a sufficient condition, and a participatory part of an unjust system. If neither holds up cleanly, is there a more accurate analogy, perhaps one involving threshold dynamics or institutional participation, that better captures the moral situation without begging the question?
