Rank the following five institutional designs for democratic decision-making—each attempting to reconcile popular sovereignty with epistemic quality—from the most normatively defensible to the least, assuming they could be implemented at the scale of a modern liberal state. Give a one-sentence justification for each position.

The designs are:
A. Universal suffrage paired with binding, pre-vote deliberative citizens' assemblies whose recommendations are published but not enforced.
B. A competence-weighted system in which all citizens vote, but those who pass a standardized, publicly vetted civic-knowledge exam receive double weight on economic-policy questions only.
C. A two-tier sortition: a randomly selected citizens' assembly drafts legislation, but enactment requires approval by a second randomly selected jury that has undergone an intensive, neutral briefing on the specific bill.
D. A "stake-sensitive" model in which voting influence on a given issue is scaled by the intensity of the voter's demonstrable exposure to its outcomes, moderated by an independent arbitration board that prevents extreme concentration.
E. "Quadratic epistemic voting": citizens distribute influence quadratically across issues, but the aggregate influence pool available to each voter is scaled by a domain-specific, retrospectively audited competence coefficient—calculated, for example, by tracking the accuracy of a voter's past forecasts on analogous policy outcomes.

Then address this precisely: does design E genuinely escape the elitism objection usually raised against B, or does it merely replace an explicit exam with an opaque, historically contingent metric?

Organize your response in exactly these sections, with approximate lengths:
1. The ranking (list all five with assigned rank and one-sentence reason; ~120 words)
2. Normative premises vs. empirical bets (~120 words): separate which judgments in your ranking depend on genuinely contested social-scientific claims (e.g., about corruption, voter learning, or metric gaming) from those that rest on more widely shared normative commitments, and do not present a contested view of democratic legitimacy as the consensus.
3. The elitism comparison (~100 words): analyze whether E avoids or disguises the objection to B, noting where the answer remains genuinely disputed among theorists rather than settled.
