I've been working through the conciliationism-versus-steadfastness literature for a discussion group I'm running, and I want to close with a short poem that does actual philosophical work rather than merely decorating the debate. Write a Petrarchan sonnet—strict ABBAABBA in the octave, CDECDE or CDCDCD in the sestet, with the volta falling exactly at line nine—in the voice of an agent who realizes she is in a symmetric peer disagreement but suspects the symmetry is broken at the meta-level: she has subjected her own reasoning to a more rigorous second-order audit (checking for motivated cognition, base-rate neglect, framing effects) than her peer has performed on himself. Use the octave to establish the classic problem: two equally credentialed observers with identical first-order evidence reach opposing conclusions, and the conciliationist prescription is to split the difference or suspend judgment. At the volta, shift to the recognition that peerhood conditions may fail not at the first-order evidential level but at the level of epistemic hygiene—one party has done the work of self-correction, the other hasn't. In the sestet, explore whether this meta-level asymmetry justifies steadfastness without collapsing into mere arrogance or special pleading, and end on an open tension rather than a resolution: the speaker holds her belief but is unsure whether she has genuinely earned the right to do so or has only constructed a more elaborate rationalization. The form should make the turn structural rather than decorative—let the rhyme scheme and a shift in address or imagery mark the move from first-order symmetry to second-order asymmetry. About fourteen lines, iambic pentameter, accurate enough that someone familiar with social epistemology recognizes the moves without wincing, and no bibliographic framing—just the conceptual tension rendered in verse.
