Hey—I'm a journalist who writes about science and philosophy of science, mostly for outlets that let me get into the weeds without dumbing it down. Lately I've been obsessed with the common prior assumption—Aumann agreement, rational agents with shared beliefs, all that jazz. I think I have a rough principle that captures my gut reactions, but every time I apply it I run into a case that breaks it.

My tentative rule: rational people should share a common prior whenever they're disagreeing about empirical frequencies inside a shared conceptual framework, but they don't need one when the disagreement is about which framework to pick, or about value weights that aren't in the data itself.

Here are my verdicts:

Case 1: Two epidemiologists look at the exact same COVID dataset and disagree on whether a specific lockdown policy actually worked. One grew up seeing government intervention fail constantly; the other grew up in a place where coordinated state action usually succeeds. I think they SHOULD eventually converge—this feels like a frequency question inside shared epidemiology. But I also feel like forcing them to start with identical priors about government competence is unrealistic and maybe a little disrespectful?

Case 2: Two mathematicians are proving something together. They share every lemma, every dead end, every false proof. One thinks the conjecture is false because it "feels too neat"; the other thinks structural patterns point to it being true. After months with zero new evidence they still split. I think this disagreement is totally legitimate—no common prior required.

Case 3: A jury hears identical robbery evidence. Some jurors have direct lived experience with police corruption in thier own neighborhoods; others don't. They split on whether the defendant was framed. I think they should try hard to reach consensus, but I don't think the experienced jurors should just "update away" thier priors to match everyone else. That lived experience feels like data, not prior.

Case 4: Two physicists in 1925 have the same quantum experiments but disagree on hidden variables versus Copenhagen-style interpretation. Before Bell's theorem came along, I think they were rational to hold different priors about determinism. After Bell, they should have converged. So the prior was okay pre-test, but not post-test?

Case 5: Me and my roommate read teh exact same news article about a niche border dispute in Central Asia. Same language skills, same article, no hidden info. If we still disagree about what's actually happening, I think that's just cognitive error—no legitimate prior difference excuses it.

So my principle handles case 2 okay, and maybe case 4 if I add a "pre-test/post-test" clause. But case 1 wants convergence and also doesn't want forced priors. Case 3 treats lived experience as data, which my principle doesn't cover well. Case 5 is pure error.

Can you lay out one precise principle—or maybe two competing candidates—that delivers as many of these verdicts as possible? For each clause, tell me which case motivates it, what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, and which of my five verdicts you have to throw overboard to keep the principle clean. Specifically, apply it to this new case: two AI safety researchers have identical benchmark results on a large language model, but they disagree on whether scaling it up will cause catastrophic failure. One worked at a lab that had a near-miss alignment failure; the other hasn't. Does that near-miss count as "shared data" or as part of thier prior?
