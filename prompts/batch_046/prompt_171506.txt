Write a poem of exactly twenty lines divided into two ten-line stanzas. The first stanza should be spoken from the perspective of Dennett’s “two-bitser”—a minimal system with only two internal states that can nonetheless be interpreted as tracking something in its environment—while the second stanza should be spoken from the perspective of a biological mind that claims original intentionality rather than derived. To make the philosophical disagreement do structural work, impose this formal constraint: the rhyme scheme of the second stanza must be the exact reverse of the first (if stanza one rhymes ABABCDCDCD, stanza two must rhyme DCBA... etc.), so that the second speaker’s lines formally depend on, and invert, the first speaker’s pattern, mirroring the claim that derived intentionality borrows its content from an original source. Rather than using standard Chinese Room imagery, have the two-bitser track something unexpected—say, the migration of a single cloud shadow across a valley—to show that even this minimal tracking supports an intentional interpretation. Do not resolve the dispute; end with both speakers uttering the same closing line, but with the first speaker meaning it as an interpreted prediction and the second as a direct experience. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the original-versus-derived-intentionality debate recognizes both positions, and the rhyme inversion should be audible even to a reader who does not know the philosophy.
