I've been a member of a shared fabrication workshop for about two years, and I'm trying to figure out whether I should renew my annual commitment or leave. The workshop was founded by two experienced makers who wrote the original charter; that document technically grants them absolute, unilateral authority to change any usage rule, deny anyone access to the machinery, or expel members without explanation. In the entire history of the space, however, they have never exercised any of these powers. They are genuinely supportive, they rarely attend board meetings, and they actively encourage members to take risks with expensive equipment.

Recently, the workshop received a municipal arts grant that would pay for intensive skills courses—welding, CAD design, electronics—so that members who currently lack technical training could actually use the advanced tools. At the same time, city regulations require anyone operating the heavy machinery to hold a current safety permit. The permits are nearly always approved within a day, and inspectors have never shown up unannounced, but technically you cannot legally operate the equipment without one, and a single complaint from a neighbor could trigger an immediate suspension of the workshop's operating license.

I talked through my situation with a couple of friends over dinner, and we ended up with three incompatible intuitions. One friend said I am obviously free in the workshop: nobody actively stops me from building what I want, the founders don't interfere, and the permit is just bureaucratic paperwork. Another friend insisted that freedom must include actually being able to do what you set out to do, so the grant-funded training matters just as much as the absence of barriers, and without either skills or permits I would be blocked in a deeper sense. The third friend said both views miss the central point—that I live under a charter that allows two people to upend everything on a whim, and that kind of subjection to arbitrary power is the real problem regardless of how kindly they behave or what skills I acquire. I'm not asking whether I should stay or go for practical reasons. Rather, I'm trying to understand whether these three perspectives—being left alone, having the real ability to pursue projects, and not living under someone else's unchecked authority—are genuinely competing accounts of the same thing, or whether they are talking past each other. Given the specific mix here—a dormant but absolute founding authority, a near-automatic regulatory gate, and an optional pathway to competence—does one of these perspectives capture what is actually at stake, or do I need all three to make sense of whether this space respects my freedom?
