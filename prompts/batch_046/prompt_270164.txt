Write a satirical parody, approximately 650 words, formatted as a deadpan Silicon Valley “Investor Pitch Deck and Terms of Service” for a fictional platform called “The Public.” The platform is a news-aggregation and social-media service that claims to have solved individual isolation by creating a perfectly abstract, numerically superior collective audience that consumes all discourse without taking personal responsibility for it. Structure the document in five sections—Executive Summary, Product Features, Community Guidelines, Risk Factors, and a brief Founder’s Note—using the hyper-earnest, jargon-heavy voice of a tech startup prospectus.

The satire should render, without ever naming Søren Kierkegaard or using explicit philosophical terminology, the central arguments of his critique of the press and the public sphere: that the modern press manufactures an imaginary entity called “the public” which has no actual members yet exerts enormous normative pressure; that this abstraction levels qualitative distinctions (passion, conviction, individual responsibility) into quantitative metrics (views, shares, engagement); and that the result is a culture in which everyone is “involved” in every issue yet no one is genuinely committed, because commitment requires being a specific individual rather than a fraction of a crowd. The parody should exaggerate real features of this view—treating the absence of a responsible agent as a feature, not a bug—so that a reader familiar with Kierkegaard recognizes the fairness of the caricature, while a general reader understands it as a sharp critique of algorithmic media and virality culture.

Inject one deliberately niche conceptual twist not found in standard summaries: frame the platform’s “Leveling Algorithm” not merely as flattening opinion, but as actively translating every passionate, first-person commitment into a third-person “trend” that can be observed, liked, and abandoned without cost. This should illustrate the specific mechanism by which the press transforms the ethical into the aesthetic, turning action into spectatorship.

Maintain strict internal consistency and deadpan irony: the founders should genuinely celebrate the pathologies being satirized. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts or editions. The piece should be readable without web access and should not depend on obscure scholarly debates.
