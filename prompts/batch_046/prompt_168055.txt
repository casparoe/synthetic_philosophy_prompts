In comparative neuroethics and the philosophy of moral status, a debate has persisted for over a decade without resolution. One side—the "scaling" position—argues that brain size, whether measured by absolute mass, encephalisation quotient, or total neuron count, is a defeasible but genuinely morally relevant factor: larger brains tend to support richer phenomenal experience and more complex preference structures, so size should count in weighing moral status, provided we correct for body size and phylogeny. The opposing "structural" position holds that this is a category mistake. Moral relevance, they claim, tracks specific functional architectures—such as recurrent thalamocortical loops, global workspace dynamics, or particular forms of integrated information—not bulk tissue. They point to the large-brained but apparently non-conscious octopus and to the small-brained but behaviourally sophisticated corvid, arguing that comparing a sperm whale's brain to a human's is like comparing a warehouse's shelf space to a computer's processing architecture.
