I spent most of yesterday evening in a long argument with a close friend about moral rescue dilemmas: if we can save either one stranger or five strangers from an identical, imminent threat, and every other factor—proximity, cost to us, risk—is held exactly constant, does morality demand that we save the larger group? At one point she cut off my appeal to the greater number with the phrase, "Numbers don't count when every life is irreplaceable," and she seemed to mean something stronger than ordinary compassion but not quite the standard utilitarian aggregation either.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be expressing—for instance, that moral reasons are non-aggregative across distinct persons; that we lack epistemic authority to compare the value of different lives; that there is a deontological side-constraint against treating individuals as fungible units; or that rescue obligations are fundamentally pairwise rather than directed at groups? For each reading, please indicate whether it amounts to a trivial truism, a substantive and defensible position in the spirit of Taurek's argument that saving the one is not morally worse than saving the five, or a claim that is actually false—and specify which of these readings would genuinely justify refusing to save the five in the standard rescue case I described. I am trying to determine whether our disagreement is about a real moral principle or whether we are simply using the same words to express different emotional reactions, and I need a clear menu of interpretations to bring back to our next conversation rather than continuing to talk past each other.
