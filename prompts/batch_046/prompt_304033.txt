As I construct the moral architecture of my novel’s protagonist, Dr. Alina Kessler—a philosopher of what she calls “scalar ethics,” concerned with how harms distribute across populations and thresholds—I need to preserve consistency in her judgments without reducing her to a cartoon utilitarian or a rigid deontologist. Her published verdicts on aggregation so far are these: first, that torturing one person for an hour is morally worse than giving a barely perceptible dust speck to seven billion people; second, that blinding one person is worse than causing every human being on earth to lose a single eyelash; third, that one person suffering a four-hour migraine is less bad than two hundred and forty people each suffering a one-minute migraine of the same intensity; fourth, that a climate policy with a ninety-nine percent chance of preventing five hundred deaths is preferable to a policy that certainly prevents one; and fifth, that a clinical trial which deceives ten thousand participants about a harmless procedure, causing each a momentary sub-noticeable confusion, is permissible, whereas a trial that honestly exposes only five participants to severe psychological trauma is not.

The new case I must place before her is this. A digital platform can retain an algorithmic design that imposes a thirty-millisecond “sub-cognitive friction” on each of its two hundred million users—individually imperceptible, below any conscious detection—where epidemiological modeling demonstrates that this distributed friction statistically produces, through network dynamics in vulnerable populations, exactly three cases of severe cognitive burnout annually. The alternative is a targeted redesign that eliminates the friction for nearly all users but deliberately imposes a ten-minute moderate cognitive strain upon twenty thousand identified high-risk users, with full informed consent and compensation.

What should Dr. Kessler decide if she is to remain coherent with her own track record? Which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog, and where do the analogies fracture? I want you to identify at least two plausible rules that jointly accommodate her first four verdicts—perhaps a discontinuity view with convex weighting, perhaps a linear-aggregation view with a lexical boundary for direct severe harm, perhaps an emergent-causation view that distinguishes statistical synthesis from direct imposition—then say what each predicts about the algorithmic case. Give your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasoning that favors it, and then specify the one further case—one that does not involve digital platforms or climate—whose outcome would most clearly distinguish the rules you have named. I am less interested in bibliographic authority than in the internal logic of the position she has been sketching.
