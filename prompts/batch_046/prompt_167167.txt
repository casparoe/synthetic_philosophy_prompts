I am writing up my commitments in suffering-focused ethics and want an audit of joint tenability before I present them. Here is the package as it stands:

(1) I hold an axiological version of negative utilitarianism: suffering is the sole intrinsic bad, pleasure carries no positive moral weight (at best it is a tie-breaker), and the fundamental normative aim is the minimization of suffering rather than the maximization of net welfare.

(2) I accept the procreative asymmetry—there is a weighty reason not to bring suffering beings into existence, but no parallel reason to bring happy beings into existence—yet I reject the conclusion that this makes extinction or non-procreation universally best. I maintain that the non-creation of potential beings who would have lived with very low suffering and high structural meaning represents a kind of loss, even though that loss is not itself a suffering.

(3) I think suffering is bad in an entirely agent-neutral way: there is no intrinsic moral difference between suffering I cause and suffering I merely observe. At the same time, I hold that there is an additional, stronger duty of rectification or prevention specifically tied to causal contribution, which is why I treat factory farming as more pressing for me personally than wild-animal suffering, despite my belief that the latter dwarfs the former in total magnitude.

(4) I endorse long-horizon, expected-value reasoning for suffering reduction, including attention to distant future risks such as stable dystopian regimes or misaligned artificial systems that could generate suffering at astronomical scales. Nevertheless, I am skeptical of present-day large-scale ecological interventions aimed at reducing wild-animal suffering, on the grounds that our epistemic position is too weak to avoid catastrophic unintended suffering through ecological disruption.

(5) I think that suffering should be aggregated with diminishing returns to intensity—perhaps along a logarithmic curve—so that distributing a given quantum of suffering across many subjects is not necessarily equivalent to concentrating it in one. I pair this with the claim that there is never any compensatory trade-off via pleasure; reductions in suffering are always one-directionally good, but increases in pleasure never justify allowing suffering.

Please treat this as an audit, not a lecture on negative utilitarianism. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve under inspection, and what is the least costly single revision? Rank the tensions by severity, specifying for each what must give and at what philosophical cost. In particular, examine whether (3) or (4) does more hidden work in destabilizing (1), and say whether (5)'s aggregation rule undermines the comparative judgment in (3). I am not indicating which commitment I would be most willing to abandon.
