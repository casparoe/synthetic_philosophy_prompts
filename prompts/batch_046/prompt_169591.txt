My dissertation advisor grades essays in a seminar on whether Enlightenment projects were engines of progress or instruments of domination. Her track record: she judges Locke’s labor-mixing property argument domination, because it universalizes a right that rationalizes colonial seizure; Kant’s essay on public reason progress, because it requires no institutional apparatus beyond the individual thinker; the Encyclopédie domination, because its taxonomy centralizes knowledge and erases craft and folk practices; Wollstonecraft’s Vindication progress, because it builds universal claims outward from embodied gendered experience; Smith’s Theory of Moral Sentiments progress, because sympathy operates interpersonally without state mediation; and Hume’s essay on commerce domination, because it abstracts wealth from the extractive conditions of its production. She will now evaluate an 1780s proposal circulated among private scientific societies for a voluntary philosophical alphabet and universal grammar, intended to eliminate ambiguity in cross-national correspondence; adoption is private and non-coercive, but the scheme requires scientific journals to reject submissions in local dialects or vernaculars. Predict her verdict—progress or domination—lay out the two competing rules that both accommodate her earlier judgments, say which she is more likely applying and why, and name the single further case that would best distinguish the rules. I care less about the label than about the consistency of her reasoning.
