I am preparing remarks for a working group on governance architectures for present-generation artificial intelligence, and I have become convinced that the standard framing of prosaic AI alignment—treating it primarily as a technical problem of refining reward signals, interpretability methods, or adversarial robustness within a unified system—misses the structural core of the challenge. I would like you to construct a rigorous argument for the following position: that aligning systems built entirely from current architectures and training paradigms, without waiting for theoretical breakthroughs, is achievable only through the intentional fragmentation of decision-making authority into modular subsystems with bounded, non-overlapping domains and reciprocal institutional veto powers.

Your argument should proceed in three parts. First, establish why technical refinements within a unified architecture are necessarily inadequate for prosaic alignment: contend that current optimization dynamics produce emergent objectives that are not reliably distinguishable from intended ones by any interpretability technique available without fundamental theoretical advance, so that increasing capability without structural separation merely amplifies risk. Second, defend the claim that modular fragmentation—where no single module can unilaterally execute high-stakes actions and where cross-module coordination requires explicit, auditable agreement—does not fatally compromise system capability; rather, argue that the reduction in integrated reasoning is itself the essential safety property, analogous to the separation of powers in constitutional design. Third, respond directly to the objection that this merely relocates the alignment problem to the meta-level of module coordination: explain why coordinating bounded, transparently designed modules with fixed jurisdictional limits is tractable in ways that aligning a monolithic, emergently complex optimizer is not. You may draw analogies from administrative law, distributed systems, or organizational theory, but develop the reasoning conceptually rather than through appeals to specific texts or authorities.
