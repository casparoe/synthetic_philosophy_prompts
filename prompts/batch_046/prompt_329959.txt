I'm drafting a section for my dissertation on epistemic circularity, and I'm trying to isolate a tension that standard presentations of the bootstrapping problem tend to obscure. Specifically, I'm distinguishing world-tracking reliability—the accuracy of a first-order method's outputs relative to external states—from method-tracking reliability—the accuracy of a second-order belief about whether that first-order method is itself accurate. Give an example of a conceptual issue in the bootstrapping / easy-knowledge debate where these two come apart in a non-perceptual domain—such as inductive generalization over social conventions or deductive inference within a formal system—such that an agent could in principle bootstrap knowledge of world-tracking success without thereby gaining method-tracking knowledge, but where a seemingly innocent extension of the bootstrapping license collapses the distinction and generates the circularity. The example should be concrete enough to identify the exact inferential step at which circular reasoning enters, and it should explain why restricting the bootstrap to first-order outputs alone blocks the inference to method-level knowledge.
