I've been working through Frank Ramsey's scattered remarks on value and meaning, and I keep running into what looks like a structural tension in his system rather than a simple omission. In the brief epilogue appended to The Foundations of Mathematics, he treats the question of whether life has meaning as grammatically defective—analogous, on his view, to asking about the dimensions of a celestial body within a conceptual scheme that lacks measurement altogether. At first this sounds like a standard logical-positivist dismissal, but Ramsey couples it with a more constructive claim: that once we see the grammatical confusion, ethical reasoning should be reconstructed as the systematization of preferences under coherence conditions. That reconstruction looks remarkably close to the decision-theoretic framework he developed elsewhere, in which rational preference is recovered from betting quotients and consistency constraints on subjective degrees of belief.

The difficulty is that this reading makes Ramsey's ethics depend on treating all value commitments as finitely verifiable dispositions—something that sits uneasily with his logic. In his account of universal generalization, he insisted that genuinely universal judgments involve an infinite scope that no finite verification can settle, which implies that rational commitment sometimes outruns any specifiable behavioral or probabilistic profile. So if the cure for the meaning-of-life confusion is to replace metaphysical value-talk with a calculus of subjective preference, then that cure seems to apply only to judgments that can be finitely articulated. But long-range obligations, commitments to distant future generations, or assessments of objective value all appear to have precisely the infinite, unverifiable structure that Ramsey thought characterized logical universals. Doesn't this mean his dismissal of life's meaning secretly presupposes that value judgments are always of the finite, preference-rankable kind? And if that presupposition fails—if ethical judgment can be infinite in the same way logical judgment is—wouldn't Ramsey be forced to admit that the original question was not grammatically confused after all, but merely unanswered?
