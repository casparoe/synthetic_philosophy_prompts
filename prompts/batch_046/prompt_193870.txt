I am drafting the final examination for our doctoral seminar on language and political philosophy, and I need a single ninety-minute essay question that will distinguish candidates who genuinely understand George Orwell's arguments about the corruption of language and the corruption of politics from those who can only summarize them. The question should present a novel case: a government agency that produces policy reports through collective iterative editing, so that no single author intends any particular imprecision, yet the final texts systematically replace precise causal verbs with passive abstractions. Ask the candidate to determine whether Orwell's diagnosis—that sloppy language enables sloppy thought—retains its force when the sloppiness is distributed across a committee rather than residing in an individual speaker's mind, and whether his proposed remedy of conscious linguistic discipline can apply at all in such a context. Then provide a model answer written entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting devices, followed immediately by a marking scheme in the same continuous prose that lists five essential conceptual moves a top answer must make, assigns each a specific weight out of twenty total marks, and includes a brief one-line note on the characteristic mistake students commit on that point. The question should be unanswerable by mere recitation of Orwell's standard claims and should force the student to adjudicate the tension between individual intentionality and institutional linguistic drift.
