A colleague reconstructed Wittgenstein's private-language argument and extended it to mechanistic interpretability in neural networks. The chain runs as follows. (1) For any symbol to possess determinate meaning, there must be a distinction between correct and incorrect application that is independent of the user's momentary impression. (2) In a purely private language—one whose terms refer only to the speaker's immediate sensations—no independent check on correct use exists. (3) Therefore private language is impossible. (4) A hidden-layer activation in a trained network is analogous to such a private symbol: its content is not fixed by any public linguistic practice, but only by gradient updates against a reward signal selected by the engineer. (5) By (1)-(3), then, these internal representations lack determinate content; there is no fact of the matter whether a given neuron tracks curvature, shadow, or some disjunctive feature. (6) Yet mechanistic interpretability presupposes precisely that there are correct and incorrect descriptions of what internal representations represent. (7) So if Wittgenstein's argument is sound, interpretability research is conceptually incoherent—even though it sometimes produces stable, predictive descriptions. She takes this to show the private-language argument proves too much and must be abandoned. I am reluctant to surrender either Wittgenstein's core insight or the legitimacy of interpretability work. Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost?
