As a novelist who works with fragmentary imperial records, I keep returning to an impasse in archival theory that no one seems able to break. One camp treats the silence of subaltern subjects in colonial archives as an epistemic gap: absence is accidental or strategic but recoverable through triangulation with oral tradition, material traces, and counter-archives, with the aim of reconstructing lives that were merely unrecorded. The opposing camp treats that same silence as ontologically constitutive—the archive was engineered not to omit certain lives accidentally but to render them structurally unrecordable, so that any reconstruction risks projecting modern categories onto a void that power actively produced.

For decades the same objections circulate. The recovery scholars accuse the structuralists of romanticizing absence and paralyzing ethical action; the structuralists accuse the recovery scholars of epistemic violence and of mistaking the archive's omissions for incomplete data rather than for a system that defines what counts as documentable life. Is this disagreement substantive, about how power operates through record-keeping; verbal, about whether "voice" and "trace" name different relations to authority; or are the sides answering different questions—one about historiographical method, the other about the ethics of representation—under one heading?

I suspect the stalemate conceals a deeper confusion about negative evidence: is an absence in the record only a missing datum, or is it a positive feature of the archival system that should alter how we understand presence itself? Diagnose the dispute using what each side actually says about the link between recording and being, and tell me what progress would look like—a formal distinction, an empirical case that breaks the symmetry, a reframed question, or an argument that the problem is ill-posed because it conflates the conditions of knowledge with the conditions of being documented.
