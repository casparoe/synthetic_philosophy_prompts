Consider an argument I have been using in a seminar on comparative political ethics, and which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion undermines both universalist anti-corruption campaigns and the legitimacy of democratic representation. (1) Corruption is the misuse of entrusted public office for private benefit. (2) "Private benefit" must be understood structurally: it is any benefit that accrues to the officeholder or her coalition rather than being distributed according to impartial public criteria, including political survival, party advantage, or network loyalty. (3) In many societies where clientelism and gift-giving to officials are common, these practices channel public resources to supporters and kin in exchange for loyalty; they therefore satisfy the structural condition in (1). (4) In established Western democracies, legislators routinely direct pork-barrel spending, targeted subsidies, and regulatory favors to constituents and donors in exchange for electoral support and campaign resources; these practices also channel public power toward benefits that accrue to the officeholder's coalition rather than to an impartial public. (5) So if corruption is defined purely by this exchange structure, then ordinary democratic representation is structurally corrupt. (6) But democratic representation—where officials are accountable to constituents through precisely these exchanges—is not corrupt; it is a legitimate institutional form. (7) Therefore corruption cannot be defined by exchange structure alone; it must involve the violation of a locally specific norm of impartiality that distinguishes legitimate accountability from illegitimate favoritism. (8) Yet if the wrongness of corruption depends on violating local norms, then in societies where reciprocal gift-giving with officials is a normative expectation, such practices do not constitute corruption at all. (9) So we face a dilemma: either Western democratic practices are corrupt, or corruption is not a universal moral wrong but merely a conventional violation of local standards, which makes cross-cultural anti-corruption efforts a form of moral imperialism. (10) I reject both horns—Western democracy is legitimate, and corruption is genuinely wrong everywhere, not just conventionally wrong.

The argument seems to prove too much, but I cannot locate the slip. Is the error an equivocation in (2) between personal enrichment and political survival? A hidden modal slide between "satisfies the structural condition" and "is corruption"? Or is the argument actually valid, leaving us with the costly task of abandoning either (1) or (6)? If I must choose, I would prefer to preserve (1), (6), and the rejection of both horns in (10). Which step should I reject, why did it seem plausible, and what does each alternative cost—especially for the claim that Western anti-corruption standards are not simply universal?
