I have come to hold a reconstructed Keynesian position on probability and uncertainty that treats Keynes's notion of the weight of an argument not as a psychological addendum to probabilistic belief but as a structurally independent normative dimension. On this view, developed from A Treatise on Probability through to the General Theory, rational agents should suspend quantitative judgment altogether when evidential weight falls below a threshold, rather than assigning diffuse probabilities; when judgment is warranted, the weight of the underlying evidence modifies the evaluation of prospects in a manner that cannot be captured by a second-order distribution over first-order probabilities; and consequently, rational choice under genuine uncertainty may systematically violate the independence conditions of standard expected utility, not through irrationality but because the evidential basis of belief is itself part of the rational evaluation of outcomes. I want to know what would genuinely count against this interpretation—what internal textual tensions in Keynes, what formal results in decision theory, what empirical patterns in actual human judgment, or what philosophical considerations about belief and action would weaken or undermine it. I am especially interested in which objections a committed defender of this reading would concede as damaging, which they would reject as imposing an inappropriate Bayesian or risk-weighted framework, and whether any result or observation could decisively show that this position collapses into a conventional ambiguity-averse model with an expanded state space. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the view is well insulated or genuinely exposed.
