In cross-cultural debates about corruption, defenders of clientelism often invoke an analogy with private friendship: a constituent who hosts a politician at a family wedding and later receives a public contract is said to be practicing the same reciprocity as a friend who sends a thank-you bottle of wine after a dinner party. In both cases, there is no explicit contract, the bond is personal rather than transactional, and the exchange strengthens social ties; condemning the political version is therefore portrayed as imposing a Western legalistic framework that mistakes legitimate moral economy for bribery. Audit this analogy rigorously: lay out the correspondence table—friend versus official, dinner invitation versus wedding hospitality, wine versus contract, social gratitude versus constituency loyalty—then identify the points where the mapping fractures, especially regarding visibility to excluded third parties, the presence of competing claimants with equal need but weaker ties, the reversibility and quantifiability of obligations, and the embedding of the exchange in a hierarchical system of access. Which features of friendship—voluntary equality, private affection, absence of institutional power—does the analogy smuggle into a context of dependency and state resource allocation? Once those disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that universal anti-corruption norms are culturally parochial survive, or does it depend on the analogy carrying the normative weight? If the friendship model collapses, propose a more accurate analog—drawn perhaps from subscription services, kinship obligation, or insurance pooling—that can preserve the intuition of legitimate reciprocity without obscuring the structural harm done to those outside the network.
