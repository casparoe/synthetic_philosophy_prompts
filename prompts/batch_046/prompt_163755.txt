Consider the intersection of indirect normativity—where an act’s rightness depends not on direct evaluation but on conformity to norms that an idealized deliberative procedure would endorse—and theories of idealized deliberation that invoke fully informed, rational, or critically reflective agents. A recurring but underdeveloped tension concerns what might be called the revision-boundary problem: if the idealized deliberator is partly constituted by stable cognitive and institutional scaffolding, then real-world changes to that scaffolding (through moral learning, institutional redesign, or cognitive enhancement) threaten either to dissolve the authority of the indirect norms or to force direct evaluation of the scaffolding itself, undermining the indirectness.

Assume you have limited research capacity—the equivalent of one focused three-year project or a small working group—to advance work in this area. Which open questions, implications, or lines of work at this intersection matter most, and in what order should they be pursued?

Specifically:

1. Identify the two or three most important unresolved issues. These might include whether indirect normativity can accommodate diachronic moral learning without self-defeat; whether idealized deliberation requires diachronic stability of the deliberator’s architecture; whether there is a non-circular specification of the “scaffolding” conditions without smuggling in direct normative evaluation; or whether the authority of indirect norms collapses once agents recognize they can redesign the deliberative framework.
