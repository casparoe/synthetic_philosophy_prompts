I'm working on an essay about Berkeley's master argument and want comments on its coherence and accuracy. The standard reconstruction holds that attempting to conceive of an object—say, a tree—existing entirely unperceived is self-defeating, since the very act of conceiving it places it before one's own mind. I want to argue that this argument trades on an ambiguity between two readings of "independent of perception": independence from this particular mind at this particular time, and independence from all minds whatsoever. If "unperceived" means only that I am not currently perceiving the tree, then Berkeley's argument fails to rule out the tree's existence, since it might be perceived by another human mind; but if it means that no mind perceives it at all, then the argument may succeed, yet it risks proving too much, since other minds themselves are not always present to my perception and would seem to fall under the same prohibition unless God is introduced as a perpetual perceiver. My essay proposes that Berkeley needs the stronger, universal reading to defeat materialism, but that he implicitly relies on the weaker, particular reading to avoid solipsism about other people—creating a tension that is only resolved by treating divine perception not as an ad hoc supplement but as the necessary condition for particular-mind independence to be meaningful. Does this distinction genuinely capture a weakness in Berkeley's argument, or does it impose a modern framework he wouldn't recognize? Is the move to God as perpetual perceiver better understood as repairing this tension, or as a separate theological commitment? And does resolving the ambiguity in this way ultimately strengthen idealism or expose it to a charge of circularity? I'd especially value comments on whether the argument holds together conceptually and whether any part misrepresents Berkeley's actual reasoning.
