I'm currently in a graduate seminar on epistemic game theory and the philosophy of rationality. You are an expert in formal epistemic logic and in designing classroom exercises for philosophy students. Before you begin, please think carefully and brainstorm briefly: what is the precise friction between common knowledge of rationality and the fact that agents may operate with heterogeneous internal standards of rationality, and how can that friction be made visceral in a live group exercise rather than explained?

Design a 90-minute in-class activity for about 16 students divided into four groups of four. The goal is for participants to feel, before it is named, that coordination can collapse even when every agent is fully rational—because common knowledge of rationality requires not just shared belief that others are rational, but shared knowledge of what rationality consists in for each agent.

The mechanics should work as follows. The group plays a four-round sequential coordination game using a shared payoff matrix structured so that if all members share the same profile, backward-induction reasoning yields a clear path to a unique equilibrium; if profiles are mixed, the reasoning chain fractures and no stable path is obvious. Between rounds, groups have three minutes of open discussion, but they are forbidden from stating their card, describing their internal calculus, or using technical terms that reveal their type. They observe only the aggregate choice distribution after each round, never individual justifications. Use a payoff scheme with no real money—award symbolic "coordination points" that accumulate toward a visible leaderboard, with a small bonus for reaching the equilibrium path.
