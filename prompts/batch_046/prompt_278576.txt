I am giving the opening address at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Evidence Beyond Reach", convened for physicists, philosophers of science, and representatives of the funding council that supports long-term sealed-experiment programmes. The governing idea must be ontology identification in the presence of permanently inaccessible information: specifically, construct the argument around a hypothetical apparatus—an irreversibly sealed cryogenic chamber calibrated once to maintain either a definite quantum state or a coherent superposition, with no subsequent measurement possible—where every observable consequence is identical, yet the two ontological descriptions differ on what exists inside. This idea should do structural work, not decorative work: it must constrain what can legitimately be claimed about the chamber's contents, prevent the audience from treating the distinction as merely epistemic ignorance that future technology might cure, and also block the slide into saying that the distinction therefore does not matter at all. The room is mixed; half will follow technical philosophy of science, half will not, so each conceptual move must be made without relying on named doctrines or citations. Please compose the address in British English, structured as exactly five numbered sections, each no more than two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion, and have each section advance the argument that without accessible information we do not merely lack data about the ontology, but lack the identification conditions that would make one ontology correct and the other wrong.
