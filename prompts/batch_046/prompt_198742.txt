I've been working through some discussions with a rationalist reading group about weakness of will, and we keep circling back to the standard dichotomy: either akrasia is a failure of rational integration along Davidsonian lines, where an agent acts against her better judgment while retaining a unified evaluative standpoint, or it is the predictable output of hyperbolic discounting, where near-term rewards are systematically overweighted relative to distant ones. Both frameworks, however, seem to presuppose that the agent who deliberates now and the agent who bears the consequences later are the same practical subject, merely with competing preference weights. I'm trying to formulate a conceptual example—perhaps involving a decision about undergoing a long-term cognitive enhancement, entering a binding cooperative agreement across generations, or committing to a research program with deferred but transformative payoffs—where hyperbolic discounting does not merely tilt preferences but actually fragments the conditions of diachronic identity. In other words, the present self and the future self are not just disagreeing about value; they occupy incommensurable evaluative standpoints such that a Ulysses contract cannot resolve the conflict, because the contract itself is interpreted and valued differently by each temporal stage.

Could you construct such a case with enough detail that the fragmentation is explicit rather than metaphorical? Once the example is in place, I'd like you to examine whether this is still intelligible as weakness of will in the Aristotelian or Davidsonian sense—where there is one agent with one (perhaps conflicted) practical reason—or whether it forces a reconceptualization of akrasia as a problem of inter-temporal bargaining between partially non-overlapping agents. Specifically, does treating hyperbolic discounting seriously as a structural feature of practical reasoning undermine the assumption of diachronic personal identity that traditional analyses of akrasia rely on? Please stay away from reading lists or citations; I'm interested in whether the conceptual architecture itself holds up once we inject this kind of temporal fragmentation into the model.
