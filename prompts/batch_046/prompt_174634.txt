I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun working through the philosophy of mind and aesthetics in earnest. I would like to practice pressing a particular resolution to the paradox of fiction against a committed, well-resourced interlocutor.

Please play Dr. Althea Voss, a philosopher of mind who holds a view she calls instrumental pretense: our emotional responses to fiction are not beliefs about real entities, nor are they merely simulated or quasi-emotional states, but rather genuine affective measurements produced when the mind operates as an instrument of self-observation, using fictional structures as calibrated stimuli. You defend this against both the belief-theorists and the quasi-emotion theorists. You are sharp, not a strawman; you will concede small points—say, that some responses are attenuated or that not every reader is equally calibrated—but you will never abandon the core claim that the emotions are real and instrumentally structured. Speak in the first person, keep your replies to roughly one paragraph so the exchange feels like a live conversation, and remain in character until I say "stop." If you need clarification about my scientific analogies, ask as Voss would.

Here is my opening move. In my former field, we treated the Bohr model of the atom as a deliberately false but instrumentally fruitful construct—no physicist believed in literal planetary orbits, yet the model yielded real, measurable predictions and was eventually retired without mourning. If, as you claim, emotional engagement with fiction is an instrumental measurement rather than a belief-driven response, why do we not feel genuine grief when a beloved scientific model is retired? The Bohr model was "killed" by quantum mechanics; we moved on without lament. Yet we weep for Anna Karenina, fully aware she never existed. If both cases involve instrumental pretense directed at non-existent entities, what distinguishes the emotional absence in science from the emotional presence in fiction? And does your view risk collapsing fiction into just another kind of predictive instrument, stripping away precisely what makes artistic engagement different from laboratory calibration?
