Here are my judgments about liberty in five concrete situations, together with a tentative principle that I am not sure holds up.

In the first, a state mandates seatbelts for drivers: I judge that this does not constitute a loss of liberty worth objecting to, despite the direct prohibition.

In the second, an employee works under an at-will contract in a labor market with few alternatives; her employer may change her hours, duties, or location without notice or justification, though she is legally free to resign. I judge this to be a genuine liberty deficit.

In the third, the same employee works under an identical job description, but now every change requires negotiation with a strong union and her explicit consent. I no longer judge her freedom to be compromised, even though her range of permissible actions is the same.

In the fourth, a citizen possesses full legal rights and lives without legal censorship, yet was never taught literacy and therefore cannot read ballots, contracts, or legal notices. I judge that she suffers a serious deprivation, but not the same kind of liberty deficit as in the second case.

In the fifth, a government never arrests critics, yet state-funded media and education consistently brand dissent as treason, and private employers—acting without legal mandate but in alignment with official culture—systematically deny employment to known critics. I judge freedom to be genuinely threatened here, though the mechanism differs from the second case.

My working principle is that liberty is undermined not merely by interference, but by arbitrary power: the capacity of one party to alter another's situation at will, without being required to account for that alteration to the person affected. This distinguishes the second case from the third. Yet it struggles to explain why the fourth case feels different from the second, and it suggests the fifth case involves domination even in the absence of direct legal coercion.

Is there a precise principle, or a small family of principles, that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should be abandoned, and why? Finally, apply your account to a case I have not mentioned: a society in which an automated system assigns housing, employment, and credit based on opaque optimization criteria. Citizens may file formal appeals, but the process takes years and no human can explain or alter any particular outcome. Does this constitute a liberty deficit, and of what kind?
