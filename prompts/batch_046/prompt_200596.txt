In my course on Dewey's experimentalism and democratic theory, we have been examining his argument that democracy is best understood not as a fixed governmental apparatus but as a mode of cooperative social inquiry in which shared problems are reconstructed through participatory intelligence. For my final reflection, I want to press this framework against an institutional form Dewey never encountered: citizens' assemblies in which large language models serve as non-voting procedural facilitators—synthesizing testimony, flagging logical inconsistencies, and drafting compromise formulations—while human members alone retain decision authority. Please take a committed position: would Dewey regard this arrangement as an authentic extension of experimental intelligence in public problem-solving, or as a corruption of the participatory conditions that make inquiry genuinely social? I am not asking for a balanced survey of objections and replies; I want you to defend one interpretation with sustained reasoning, drawing specifically on his distinction between routine and intelligent action and on his concern that technical efficiency can displace the shared reconstruction of experience. Present your argument as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—write it as a single letter—so that the line of reasoning unfolds without interruption. Where your judgment depends on a contested reading of Dewey's later work, state that dependence plainly rather than presenting your conclusion as inevitable.
