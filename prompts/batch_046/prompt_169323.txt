In discussions about whether large language models "believe" things, I keep encountering a specific argumentative slide. A speaker first uses "belief" in a functional-behavioral sense: the model behaves as if it holds some content, generating related outputs and resisting contradictory prompts in consistent ways. When critics object that the model also produces confident falsehoods, the speaker retreats to a thinner technical gloss—something like "the system contains an internal pattern correlated with that topic," explicitly denying any claim about truth, understanding, or commitment. Once the objection is neutralized, the speaker returns to the functional language, but now deploys it to support normative conclusions: that the outputs should be treated as testimony requiring epistemic evaluation, or that the system merits certain forms of deference. The two senses are not arranged like a defensible core and an indefensible extension; they are parallel interpretations that share a word but carry incompatible theoretical loads.

Is there an established concept for this exact pattern of oscillation between a behavioral-functional register and a technical-descriptive register, used strategically to defend and then apply a claim? If not, can you name and define it precisely, distinguishing it from motte-and-bailey, from plain equivocation, and from legitimate conceptual extension? I want the carving, not just the label: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie—especially in cases where a functional state genuinely might ground a normative practice, versus cases where the link is purely verbal.
