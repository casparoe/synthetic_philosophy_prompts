I'm working on a feature about whether folk morality really shifts across cultures or if we're mostly seeing WEIRD measurement noise, and I got into a long coffee chat with an anthropologist—Dr. K in my notes—who's been tracking how different groups draw the line between 'actually wrong' and 'just not how we do it.' Her precedants so far: she files American undergrad refusal to share with a starving stranger under conventional/personal preference, not moral; she treats failure by a Matsigenka brother-in-law to help build a house as a loyalty-based moral breach; she puts Japanese reciprocity failures in the moral fairness box; she codes Hadza meat-sharing obligations as moral but food-taboo violations as conventional; she rejected labeling 'wearing shoes indoors' in one Southeast Asian sample as moral despite local anger; and she accepted broken promises to dead ancestors in a Polynesian group as moral because it was universalized inside the kin line and tied to harm-to-kin.

Now she's facing a weird new case—the Varnu highlanders—where picking philosphy over becoming the family healer triggers the exact same punishment intensity and emotional arousal as stealing, but only inside the kin group; outsiders who do it face zero judgment, and locals insist it's not harm-based but about 'disrupting the vertical flow of care.' If she wants to stay inconsistant with her own track record, should she classify this as moral or conventional? What's the closest analog in her earlier list—the Matsigenka loyalty case or the Polynesian ancestor promise? And what competing rules could she be running: one that demands harm-to-stranger universalizability, one that only requires in-group universalization plus emotional arousal, or one that rules out any role-based violation? Which rule actually fits all six of her precedants best, and what hypothetical follow-up—say, a culture where outsiders are punished for skipping the healer path—would finally break the tie?
