You serve as the senior philosopher of science on your university's Institutional Review Board for Cross-Species and Synthetic Cognition Research. The board must vote next Thursday on whether to replace its binary protection protocol — Category A (vertebrates with neurological complexity, full ethical review) versus Category B (everything else, minimal oversight) — with a graded three-tier framework: Full Moral Status (FMS), Precautionary Intermediate Status (PIS), and Non-Moral Mechanism (NMM).

Two pending proposals force the issue. Proposal 1 is a neuroethology study using aversive thermal-gradient learning in bumblebees (Bombus terrestris) to measure stress-induced decision bias; the PI argues this reveals affective states analogous to pessimism and therefore warrants more than Category B. Proposal 2 is an engineering project testing adaptive set-point modulation in networked building thermostats that learn occupant preferences via reinforcement loops; the PI claims these systems exhibit proto-intentional goal-tracking that blurs the line between mechanism and minimal cognition.

Half the board insists the binary is administratively necessary and that gradation invites anthropomorphic projection onto insects and machines. The other half wants the three-tier system adopted immediately.

Draft the board's decision memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting. It must recommend a concrete course of action — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — for both the framework and the two proposals, with a clear implementation time frame; define explicit, operational criteria for FMS, PIS, and NMM that reviewers can apply without relying on intuition alone; apply those criteria honestly to both the bumblebee study and the thermostat network; and treat the binary-opposition's strongest concern (administrative paralysis and projection risk) as seriously as your own position, explaining precisely why it does or does not defeat the recommendation. Think step by step: first map the gradation argument and its criteria, then examine the disanalogies between affective bias in insects and feedback loops in devices, then conclude with an actionable bottom line the board can vote on.
