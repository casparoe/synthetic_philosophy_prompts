I do not have any formal training in philosophy or decision theory, but over the past year I have been working through the logic of cooperative decision puzzles on my own, and I have been invited to present at a small interdisciplinary seminar—mostly philosophers of action and a few computer scientists who work on multi-agent systems—about a connection I have noticed between the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. My thesis is that these are usually treated as separate failures of superrationality—one arising in simultaneous games, the other in sequential ones—but I think they are both manifestations of a deeper structural problem I call mirror-lock: when an agent treats its own reasoning process as fully transparent to others and builds that transparency directly into its utility calculation, it creates a self-reinforcing loop in which the only stable outcome is the worst one for everyone involved. I want to defend the claim that standard causal decision theory does not escape this problem, because the difficulty is not in the causal structure of the choice but in the meta-cognitive framing that makes transparency part of the rational calculus. The audience includes people who believe superrationality simply needs a better formalization, as well as skeptics who think these puzzles show that rationality is irreducibly context-dependent. Please prepare me for the objections they will actually raise, in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way these particular listeners would phrase them: which objections have real force, what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and how to reply within the time available. I am especially worried that someone will ask whether my mirror-lock is just a rebranding of the common-knowledge problem in game theory, and whether I have smuggled in a normative assumption about reasoning transparency that causal decision theory explicitly rejects. I also want to know the weakest point in my own position—the one I should prepare to defend openly rather than hope nobody notices.
