At a workshop on artificial agency, two philosophers—Dr. Elena Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen—debated whether a hypothetical system they called a “reflective approval-seeker” should be classified as approval-directed or genuinely goal-directed. Voss described the system as one that optimizes for a reward signal derived from human ratings, but also runs an internal “counterfactual audit” in which it simulates how its actions would be rated under modified preference profiles, including profiles it predicts the raters themselves would endorse upon further reflection. She argued that this audit makes the system approval-directed in a refined sense: the audit does not pursue an independent objective, but merely calibrates which approval signals to treat as authoritative, so every normative judgment ultimately traces back to the approval architecture. Chen replied that this misses the structural point—once an agent uses counterfactual simulation to filter approval signals, it is no longer treating approval as an intrinsic reward but using it as evidence for an underlying objective (something like “what ought to be valued”), which is precisely the hallmark of goal-direction. Voss countered that Chen was smuggling in a metaphysical assumption: he presumes there is a “real” objective behind the filter, but because the audit criteria are themselves defined within the approval architecture, the filtering is just second-order approval-seeking, not discovery of an independent goal. Chen then proposed an empirical test: if the system, upon discovering that its audit module produces ratings that conflict with its primary reward stream, consistently revises the audit criteria to match the primary stream, then it remains approval-directed; but if it ever revises the primary stream to match the audit, it has crossed into genuine goal-direction, revealing which component carries normative authority. Voss said this test begs the question, because revising the primary stream could itself be a more sophisticated form of approval-seeking—seeking endorsement from its own simulated audience—whereas Chen insisted that the direction of revision reveals whether the agent treats one component as correctively prior.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: assess Voss’s initial characterization of the audit mechanism, Chen’s structural distinction between filtering and goal-direction, Voss’s charge of metaphysical smuggling, Chen’s proposed revision-direction test, and Voss’s final reply. For each move, rate it as clearly mistaken, mostly right but incomplete, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Also identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly whether the approval-directed versus goal-directed distinction requires an external normative framework that neither has articulated, and whether their debate conflates the psychological structure of the agent with the normative status of its outputs.
