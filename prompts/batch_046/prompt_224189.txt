As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind, I am delivering the opening remarks at a memorial symposium for Dr. Elena Voss, a cognitive scientist and dear friend who died three weeks ago. The audience is split: about half are family members and former students with no technical background, and the other half are philosophers of mind and neuroscientists who will catch any misstatement about Dennett-style user-illusionism. Elena herself rejected any talk of an immaterial self. I want to honor her without implying she survives as a conscious observer, yet I also want to avoid reducing her life to bare biology. Lately I have been framing consciousness not merely as a user-illusion but as something analogous to a nuclear-reactor control-panel interface: a highly compressed, predictive display evolved for social coordination rather than for transparent access to underlying physical processes. I would like the remarks to use this framing structurally—what we have lost is not a hidden inner light but the particular interactive interface Elena maintained, the navigable patterns of reasoning and care she projected. The speech should comfort those who are grieving without invoking an afterlife, remain precise enough for the specialists, and let the control-panel analogy shape exactly what can and cannot be said about her presence and absence. Please write these opening remarks.
