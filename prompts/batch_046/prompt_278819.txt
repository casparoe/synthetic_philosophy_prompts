I'm taking a seminar on comparative epistemology that moves between classical Chinese philosophy and analytic theories of knowledge. Our instructor has been evaluating whether subjects in various dream-skeptical scenarios possess knowledge, and she has taken consistent positions on five cases. First, in Zhuangzi's original butterfly scenario—where one might be Zhuang Zhou dreaming of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being Zhuang Zhou—she holds that the subject lacks knowledge of his own ontological kind or species, but retains knowledge that conscious experience is occurring. Second, in response to Descartes' first-meditation argument that past dreams seemed as real as present waking life, she rules that this undermines knowledge of the external world generally, but not immediate knowledge that one is currently experiencing something. Third, in a false-awakening case, where a subject dreams they have woken up and are now reading philosophy, she denies that the belief I am awake right now counts as knowledge, since the phenomenology of awakening can be perfectly simulated. Fourth, in a lucid dream where the subject explicitly knows this is a dream and correctly identifies the dream content as non-veridical, she says knowledge is preserved: the subject knows the mental state and its non-veridicality. Fifth, in a modern perfect-simulation or brain-in-a-vat scenario replicating an ordinary human life, she rules that the subject lacks knowledge of their physical embodiment, though they may still know that they are undergoing experiences.

Next week she will discuss what she calls the reflexive butterfly case, which she has constructed by combining Zhuangzi's transformation with a Cartesian-style reflexive argument. In this scenario, the subject—whether human or butterfly—reasons as follows: If I am a butterfly dreaming I am human, then I am still a thinking thing; if I am a human dreaming I am a butterfly, then I am still a thinking thing; therefore, regardless of which I am, there is a thinking thing here. The conclusion is true, and the reasoning process is one that is generally reliable when applied in waking life. However, the argument depends on a false intermediate belief: the subject assumes that the principle of non-contradiction applies in the same stable way across the transformation, and that they have direct epistemic access to both horns of the dilemma, when in fact—according to the Zhuangzian framework—the transformation undermines precisely the kind of metaphysical ground needed for that inference. Moreover, in all nearby worlds where there is no thinking thing occurring, the subject would not be able to form this belief at all, and in all nearby worlds where the belief is formed, a thinking thing is indeed present.

Given this track record, I want to predict her verdict on the reflexive butterfly case, identify whether she is implicitly running a safety-based, sensitivity-based, no-false-lemmas, or some other account of knowledge, and learn which further case would best distinguish the leading candidates. Specifically, does she grant knowledge of the conclusion there is a thinking thing here despite the false intermediate step, or does she deny it? Which of her earlier rulings best supports your interpretation, and what single additional scenario—perhaps one involving a reliable but accidentally true belief formed in a dream with no false premises—would settle whether her criterion is primarily about the absence of false lemmas or about modal robustness?
