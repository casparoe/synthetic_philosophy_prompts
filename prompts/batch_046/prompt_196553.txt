Write a one-semester dissertation prospectus (approximately 15 pages, over 12 weeks) on the following question: does pain asymbolia genuinely undermine the thesis that pain is essentially unpleasant, and does the answer depend on whether the dissociation occurs at the level of sensory-affective integration or at the level of higher-order evaluative judgment?

The existing debate treats pain asymbolia—typically observed after prefrontal lobotomy or high-dose opioid administration—as a uniform phenomenon in which nociceptive signals persist but the usual negative affect is absent. Critics of essentialism cite these cases to argue that the badness of pain is contingent rather than constitutive. Defenders reply that what remains in asymbolia is not pain proper but a bare sensory quality lacking the affective dimension that defines it. This standoff persists because both sides conflate two distinct dissociations. The first, affective nulling, occurs when the subject reports that the sensation is not bad and exhibits no aversive behavior, despite intact nociception; this appears to remove badness from the experience itself. The second, evaluative decoupling, occurs when the subject retains aversive motivation and physiological stress responses but judges, upon reflection, that the sensation is not genuinely harmful or undesirable; here badness may remain present in the affective processing but be disowned at the level of endorsed evaluation.

Your prospectus should state the precise conceptual distinction between these two forms of dissociation, explain why the standard literature treats them interchangeably, and propose an analysis—using general resources in philosophy of mind and clinical phenomenology, without requiring original empirical data—that determines whether either form genuinely threatens the essential-badness thesis. The method should be comparative: apply the distinction to classic clinical descriptions of asymbolia, to conceptually coherent hypothetical cases of non-pathological "pleasant pain," and to the logical structure of essentialist arguments (for instance, whether pain functions as a natural-kind term with affective properties as necessary conditions). State what a successful result looks like: for example, showing that affective nulling undermines essentialism only if one already assumes a representationalist theory of affect, whereas evaluative decoupling undermines it only on a judgment-dependent theory, so that the threat to essentialism is theory-relative rather than absolute. State what failure looks like: if the distinction collapses under analysis—if every alleged case of evaluative decoupling can be redescribed as hidden affective nulling, or vice versa—then the project should conclude that the traditional standoff is inescapable without further empirical neuroscience, and that the conceptual resources alone do not adjudicate it. Give an honest scope assessment: the comparative conceptual analysis is tractable within one semester, but constructing the framework across clinical, hypothetical, and logical domains risks scope creep; therefore limit clinical discussion to well-known descriptions rather than original case studies, and explicitly flag any point where the argument depends on empirical premises about neural mechanisms that you are not equipped to defend. Do not include a bibliography or citation list; refer to positions and arguments in general terms and note that references will be added in revision.
