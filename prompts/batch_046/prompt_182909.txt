After a long and rather heated argument last night with a close friend about whether neuroscience has settled the free-will question, I want an outside audit of the scientific summary my friend presented. The account runs as follows. In Libet’s classic experiments from the early 1980s, subjects watched a rotating dot and reported the moment they felt the conscious urge to flex a wrist; meanwhile EEG recorded a readiness potential—specifically the Bereitschaftspotential over the supplementary motor area—beginning roughly 350 to 500 milliseconds before that reported awareness. From this temporal gap my friend infers that the brain initiates movement unconsciously, and that the conscious experience of deciding is therefore a retrospective narrative constructed after the neural machinery is already in motion. My friend further claims that Libet himself drew exactly this epiphenomenalist conclusion, treating conscious will as causally inert. The summary then invokes the 2008 Soon et al. fMRI study, in which multivariate patterns in frontopolar cortex and precuneus predicted left versus right button-press choices up to seven to ten seconds before the subjective report, at roughly sixty percent accuracy; my friend presents this not merely as predictive correlation but as proof that the brain deterministically computes the decision long before consciousness appears. The only escape hatch mentioned is Libet’s proposed conscious veto—a brief window in which awareness might halt an already-initiated action—but my friend dismisses this as an ad hoc rescue, arguing that subsequent work on unconscious inhibitory control shows that even vetoing is prepared subcortically, leaving no causal gap for a genuinely free mental act. Finally, the summary blends Libet’s readiness-potential data with Wegner’s separate framework treating conscious will as an illusion of authorship, presenting both as a single empirical result: the brain decides, then tells the conscious self a story. From all of this my friend concludes that neuroscience has empirically falsified free will, at least in the form of a conscious mental cause that initiates voluntary bodily movement.

I am uneasy about this synthesis. It strikes me that Libet may not have endorsed full-blown epiphenomenalism, that predictive brain patterns might indicate predisposition rather than a locked decision, and that conflating two distinct research programs—Libet’s EEG work and Wegner’s cognitive-psychological account—might distort what either actually shows. But I lack the expertise to say where the errors lie. Could you evaluate the fairness of this characterization? Please proceed claim by claim: distinguish what is accurate about the experimental designs and results, what is a standard but defensible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation—whether of Libet’s own interpretive stance, of the Soon et al. findings and their limitations, of the conceptual status of the veto, or of the relationship between the two literatures. In particular, clarify whether the conclusion that conscious mental initiation has been empirically falsified follows from the corrected premises, and flag any points where my friend’s summary moves from correlation or temporal precedence to causal determination without adequate justification.
