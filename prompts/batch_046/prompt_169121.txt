I advise the acquisitions editor of a small university press who receives approximately forty unsolicited monograph proposals each month and can devote only ninety minutes per week to screening them for peer review. The press lacks the staff or data to maximize expected scholarly impact across the full set; it needs a reusable decision protocol that operationalizes satisficing and bounded rationality in this specific editorial role. Design a step-by-step screening procedure that translates Simon’s aspiration-level logic into practice: specify how the editor sets a non-negotiable quality floor before viewing any proposal; how she applies a rapid two-minute pattern-match filter to reject obvious misfits; how she allocates a diminishing evaluation-cost budget across the remaining proposals; and how she stops searching and advances the first proposal that clears the floor, even if a later one might be superior. The protocol must include three ordered tests—disciplinary fit, methodological coherence, and argument novelty—each with explicit pass, fail, and uncertain rubrics, and must flag the exact points where judgment cannot be eliminated, particularly when a proposal exceeds the floor but conflicts with a quarterly portfolio-balance constraint that requires at least one acquisition in each of three designated subfields. Provide worked applications to three proposals: one that passes the pattern-match and checklist immediately; one that fails the pattern-match yet passes the analytical tests, requiring rejection on search-cost grounds despite adequate quality; and one that barely clears the floor and fills a missing portfolio gap but is weaker in absolute terms than a proposal that arrives later—state what the procedure mandates and why the editor will find the outcome unwelcome. Finally, identify the boundary conditions under which this satisficing protocol produces systematically worse acquisition portfolios than a naive first-come-first-served rule, and specify the precise circumstances that require escalation to the editor-in-chief rather than automated application of the procedure.
