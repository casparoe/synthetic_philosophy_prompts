I'm a second-year metaphysics student running a study-group session for first-years on material constitution, and I want to avoid the standard statue-and-clay framing because they'll just repeat the coincidence objection from memory. Instead, I'm building the session around a modified case: a centuries-old bonsai that has had every branch and root replaced over decades, yet is still exhibited, insured, and sold as the same tree. I need a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—for students whose only background is introductory logic.

First, what conceptual dependency must be in place before the puzzle of "two objects in one place" even makes sense to them? Second, what feature of the bonsai case should carry the intuition that there are genuinely two entities—the tree and the successive living tissue—and what feature must I suppress so they don't collapse it into biological persistence? Third, specify the exact sequence in which to introduce spatiotemporal continuity versus identity conditions, then the coincidence objection, then the modal-property argument, showing what each step depends on. Fourth, map the predictable misconceptions—probably that "the tree is just its shape" or that "complete replacement proves there's only one changing object"—and state precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off. Fifth, provide one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that reveals whether they've grasped the metaphysical structure rather than just the botanical analogy. Finally, say explicitly what to leave out at this level—e.g., hylomorphism, relativized identity, or four-dimensionalism—and justify the omission.
