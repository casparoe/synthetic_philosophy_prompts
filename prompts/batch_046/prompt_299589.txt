We kept returning to the same institutional pattern: pest-management guidelines treat killing thousands of one insect as an acceptable harvest of surplus individuals, yet treat harming a single member of another species as an intolerable loss, with the line drawn not by toxicity to the organism but by population abundance and what ecologists call functional redundancy—the idea that some individuals are fungible units within an ecosystem service, while others occupy irreplaceable roles. My friend called this pure anthropocentric aesthetic preference dressed up as science; I want to know whether the practice actually rests on a specific, reconstructible theory of value, or whether it is philosophically underdetermined.

Point to the practice itself: in integrated pest-management protocols and pesticide-registration rules, economic thresholds allow chemical intervention once pest density exceeds a calculated surplus level, implicitly treating individuals below that floor as interchangeable, whereas protections for scarce pollinators or iconic species treat every individual as non-fungible regardless of population size. The chemicals, the nervous systems affected, and the suffering involved are held constant; only the replaceability calculus changes.

What implicit theory of moral status or value does that structure presuppose?
