As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue for a work of fiction, I wish to adjudicate a disagreement I have sketched between two characters who are not myself. The first argues that the heterogeneity problem for hedonism dissolves once we recognize that all pleasures share not a qualitative feel but a formal structure: the experienced closure of an anticipated gap, whether gustatory, intellectual, or emotional. She concedes that tasting wine and solving a proof differ in phenomenology, but insists that both are instances of tension resolving into fulfillment. The second responds that this account collapses pleasure into relief, since the cessation of a migraine also closes a gap, and that without a common phenomenal quality hedonism loses its claim to identify one kind of good. He further observes that certain pleasures—such as the unsettling satisfaction of recognizing a tragic pattern in one's own past—lack any conscious anticipation, yet remain genuinely pleasant, which undermines her structural claim. The first replies that anticipation need not be conscious or pleasant; in tragic recognition, the mind completes a cognitive arc it was implicitly seeking, so the structural condition still holds. The second concludes that she has stretched anticipation so broadly that it applies to every mental event, rendering her account vacuous. Please go through their exchange move by move, rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, and indicate what both overlook—perhaps a distinction between phenomenal quality and intentional object—that might resolve the impasse without forcing either to abandon her position entirely. I seek analytical precision for narrative use, not bibliographic references.
