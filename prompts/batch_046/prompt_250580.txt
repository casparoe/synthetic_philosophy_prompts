I'm writing my diss on international law and mass violence and I'm stuck on something that feels philosophically weird. The Genocide Convention and the courts that apply it restrict the label to cases where victims fall into one of four protcted groups and prosecutors can prove specific intent to destroy the group as such. Political groups and cultural genocide — destroying a language or institutions without physical killing — are left out, even though Lemkin's original draft was broader. Meanwhile, in transitional justice talks, governments sometimes treat official recognition of genocide as a bargaining chip in reparations deals, like it's a distributive good rather than a factual diagnosis.

What philosophical commitments about the wrong of genocide does that whole practice presuppose? Does it assume the harm is essentially in destroying a particular kind of collective identity, not just in killing alot of people? Or that the perpetrator's mental state makes it morally unique, more than scale or suffering? And when states bargain over the name, does the practice presuppose the category is partly performative — that calling something genocide changes its moral or legal status?

Reconstruct the implicit theory: is it about group ontology, culpability, or the politics of recognition? Say whether the legal framework actually needs these commitments or is philosophically underdetermined — could a harm-based view license the same four-group rule for purely practical reasons? And tell me what a system built on Lemkin's broader concept, plus an outcome-focused standard instead of intent, would have to change in courtrooms and in reparations politics. I keep feeling like there's a hidden theory of moral status in the naming rules, but I can't tell if it's coherent or just politically convenient.
