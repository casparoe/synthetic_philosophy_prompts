I'm drafting the final examination for an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility and the philosophy of action, and I want a single essay question that students cannot answer merely by rehearsing Frankfurt's classic counterexample to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. Rather than asking them to summarise the Black-and-Jones scenario, the prompt should present a novel case: Elena, a climate-policy negotiator, is about to sign an international accord. Unknown to her, a predictive behavioural algorithm—rather than a human intervener—has mapped her cognitive architecture and will silently restructure her attentional pathways only if she begins to form an intention to refuse; if she moves straight to agreement, the system never activates. Crucially, Elena retains what philosophers might call a dispositional capacity to reject the accord—she possesses the general psychological skills and rational profile that would enable refusal in ordinary, unmonitored contexts—but in this specific environment any nascent refusal would be pre-empted before reaching conscious articulation.

Please formulate an exam question that asks students to evaluate whether this algorithmic case undermines the Principle of Alternative Possibilities more effectively than Frankfurt's original, or whether it instead exposes a hidden robustness condition within the principle—specifically, that responsibility requires not merely the absence of coercion but the preservation of a particular kind of deliberative access to alternatives that remains intact even when unexercised. The question should require students to distinguish the familiar flicker-of-freedom reply from the claim that bare dispositional capacities are sufficient, and to consider whether an agent who is transparent to herself about her available paths (an idealised deliberative condition) could still be responsible despite the algorithm's presence. Provide a model answer of approximately 600 to 700 words, written in British English, together with a marking scheme structured around five must-hit points (totalling 20 marks), each with its specific weighting and a brief one-line description of the characteristic mistake candidates make on that point.
