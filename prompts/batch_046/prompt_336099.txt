I have recently become the primary carer for a close family member who, following a neurological injury, reports intense burning and stabbing sensations in specific limbs, describes them with precise anatomical detail, and answers affirmatively when asked whether they hurt, yet shows no sign of distress, requests no relief, and—most worryingly—does not withdraw from or avoid the activities and positions that aggravate the underlying tissue damage. Reading philosophy of pain in order to understand what this means for how we should think about their welfare, I have encountered arguments that cite cases like this to claim that pain is not intrinsically bad: the unpleasantness, they say, can be separated from the sensory content, and where the unpleasantness is absent, there is no genuine harm. But I keep encountering a particular pattern of reasoning that troubles me. The separation is used to dismiss the idea that the sensory experience itself carries negative value, yet the simultaneous loss of protective motivation—which in this case allows progressive physical harm—is treated as an unrelated clinical curiosity rather than as part of the very phenomenon that needs moral assessment. I want to know whether this specific argumentative configuration—the selective invocation of a sensory-affective split to deny intrinsic badness, coupled with the neglect of the functional deficit that accompanies the split—has an established name in the literature. How, precisely, does it relate to discussions of cases where people feel pain without suffering, and to debates about whether pain is bad because of what it feels like or because of what it makes us do? If there is no name, I would welcome a coinage with clear boundary conditions: for instance, does the pattern require that the protective failure be present, or is it enough that the affect is absent? And does it apply equally to congenital and acquired cases? Please distinguish in your answer between points that are broadly settled among philosophers of mind and medicine, and points that remain genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus view. Please write in British English.
