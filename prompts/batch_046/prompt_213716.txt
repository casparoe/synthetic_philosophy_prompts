I am preparing a seminar presentation on Ian Hacking’s concept of looping effects in psychiatric classification, a subject that has become personally urgent since I began observing in a community mental-health clinic how patients actively reshape diagnostic labels in real time. The debate appears stalled: philosophers of medicine argue that interactive kinds do not dissolve the reality of mental disorders, while sociologists of science treat looping as evidence that these categories are fundamentally constructed. Both sides recycle the same historical cases—hysteria, dissociative identity disorder, autism—without advancing. My hypothesis is that part of the stall is verbal, pivoting on whether reality requires causal stability or social invariance, yet I suspect a deeper asymmetry is obscured: some diagnoses loop in ways that stabilize clinical practice, while others loop in ways that fragment it. I have recently observed what might be called reverse looping, in which lay digital communities generate granular subtypes that clinicians absorb into revised criteria faster than clinical frameworks can shape self-understanding. Is the philosophical deadlock actually a failure to distinguish between stabilizing and fragmenting loops, or between clinical and lay-driven interaction? Diagnose the disagreement—whether verbal, substantive, or methodological—and indicate whether recognizing this asymmetry, or another conceptual distinction, could genuinely advance the debate rather than rename it. Please do not offer a bibliography or reading list; I need a structural diagnosis and a clear view of what would count as progress.
