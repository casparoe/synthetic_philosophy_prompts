I am drafting the final examination for a graduate seminar on strategic rationality and the philosophy of decision theory. I need one essay prompt addressing the backward induction paradox as it arises in the centipede game. The question must not be answerable by merely summarizing the standard common-knowledge argument and declaring that rational players defect at the first node. Instead, I want students to confront a modified variant I have constructed for this exam: call it the "Centipede with Private Type Signals." In this version, after each round in which both players choose to continue, each player privately receives an independent, noisy signal about whether the opponent is a payoff-maximizing agent or a commitment-bound agent who will continue regardless. The signal is not observed by the other player, and its reliability is common knowledge.

The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether this injection of private, unobservable belief-states resolves the backward induction paradox, dissolves it, or merely relocates it to a higher-order level of reasoning about beliefs. Specifically, students must distinguish and defend one of three interpretive positions: (i) that backward induction reveals a normative defect in procedural, step-by-step rationality; (ii) that it is a descriptively accurate but institutionally fragile prediction about real-world cooperation; and (iii) that it is an artifact of an implausibly strong common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption that collapses once private information is introduced. Their answer should explain how the private-signal variant interacts with each interpretation, with particular attention to whether common knowledge is genuinely undermined or merely transformed into common knowledge of probabilistic beliefs.

Please provide the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should identify four specific argumentative moves that distinguish an A answer from a B answer—each named explicitly, with a brief note on the typical failure mode for that move. The scheme should also flag the specific misconception that treating the noisy signal as "breaking" common knowledge automatically restores cooperation, and it should indicate how that error costs marks. The entire response should be in American English, with no request for citations or reading lists, since this is intended for a closed-book examination setting.
