In my graduate seminar on Cartesian foundations, a student recently invoked the cogito—usually rendered as 'Cogito, ergo sum'—to dismiss an interlocutor's worry that self-reference might be conceptually empty, insisting that Descartes's dictum proves an immediate, non-inferential presence-to-self that requires neither language nor conceptual structure. Before our next meeting I would like to circulate a disambiguation menu. Separate the distinct precise theses that this slogan could be expressing: (i) the minimal logical claim that the occurrence of thinking entails the existence of a thinker; (ii) the epistemic claim that one's own existence is uniquely indubitable and can therefore serve as an axiomatic foundation; (iii) the metaphysical claim that thinking reveals the essential nature of the self as a thinking substance; (iv) the phenomenological claim that there is a pre-reflective, first-person givenness of consciousness; (v) a performative or enactive reading on which the act of doubting enacts rather than discovers the self; and (vi) a more radical, anachronistic interpretation—such as a transcendental-idealist reading that treats the cogito as constituting a transcendental ego rather than discovering an empirical subject. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, and—most importantly—whether it actually supports the claim that self-consciousness is concept-independent and can therefore silence skeptical worries about conceptual mediation. Present these as a numbered list suitable for distribution, identifying which reading the student was most likely invoking, and explaining precisely why that reading either succeeds or fails to do the argumentative work they asked it to do.
