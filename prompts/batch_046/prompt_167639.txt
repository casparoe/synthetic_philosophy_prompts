I've been argueing with my roomate about The Handmaid's Tale and whether Offred ever genuinely consents to anything, and I think we're both missing a layer. There's this idea in consent philosophy that deception can invalidate consent not just when it's about the act itself, but when it corrupts the background conditions under which you form beliefs about your own options—like, you're not just lied to about what's happening, you're lied to about what kind of chooser you are. I was thinking about those epistemic competence conditions that go beyond the standard mental-capacity check: it's not enough that you're sane and not drunk, your belief-formation environment has to be structurally sound or your yes doesn't count.

I want to read Atwood's novel through that lens, especially the scenes where Offred seems to make a choise—playing Scrabble with the Commander, deciding to stay, even that ambiguous ending where she gets into the van. Does the book show that her competence is intact but her belief environment is poisoned? Or does Atwood suggest that under totalitarian gender coercion, the very distinction between coerced and voluntary collapses in a way that standard consent theory can't capture? Which scenes should I focus on, and where does the novel resist that kind of analysys?
