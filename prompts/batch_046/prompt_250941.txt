I have recently assumed an administrative role in which I found it necessary to deliberately misrepresent budgetary risks to a governing board in order to prevent the abrupt dissolution of a program serving vulnerable students. This personal experience has made the philosophical problem of dirty hands immediately relevant to me, and I wish to understand whether actions of this kind fall within the same moral category that philosophers discuss when they analyze necessary wrongs committed in the service of public goods.

Please argue for the following position: the dirty-hands problem ought not to be interpreted as a direct conflict between private moral prohibitions and public political obligations, but rather as a structural tension between deontic accountability and aretaic integrity. According to this view, the agent does not merely violate an ordinary moral rule for the sake of a greater benefit; instead, she performs an act that retains its wrong-making character in a deontic register while simultaneously preserving a role-specific virtue that would be undermined by strict adherence to conventional morality. The moral residue that remains is therefore not best understood as guilt over a justified transgression, but as a distinctive form of character-alienation—an enduring misalignment between one's actions and the moral dispositions that ordinarily constitute one's practical identity—even when the act is all-things-considered correct. In developing your argument, please address at least one significant objection: that this reframing reduces the problem to ordinary role morality and thereby eliminates the specific sense of moral cost that distinguishes genuinely dirty hands from merely inconvenient administrative compromises.
