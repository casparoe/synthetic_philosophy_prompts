I am preparing a graduate seminar on reference and logical form, and I have been returning to Russell’s analysis of definite descriptions—specifically the part in which he treats the sentence attributing baldness to the present King of France. The passage, as I understand it, argues that such a sentence is not genuinely about a particular subject that either possesses or lacks the property in question. Instead, Russell dissolves the description into an existential claim: there is exactly one present King of France, and that individual is bald. Consequently, if no such monarch exists, the sentence is simply false, not meaningless, and one avoids the contradiction that would arise if the subject term named a non-existent entity. He then sets this quantificational reduction against the view—associated with Meinong—that there are objects that genuinely lack existence yet still serve as subjects for true or false predications.

My difficulty lies in the scope of this dissolution. Russell’s analysis seems designed for straightforward existential assertions in the actual world, but I want to understand whether it is meant to apply uniformly across all logical contexts, or whether it leaves room for descriptions that function as singular terms without carrying an existential commitment. I have in mind what one might call functional descriptions in design ontologies and modal planning: an engineering specification that refers to the cooling system of the proposed reactor, where the reactor has never been built and may never be built, yet the description operates as a structural placeholder within the logical architecture of the plan. In such cases, the description carries what looks like singular reference inside a non-assertoric, modal framework—directing attention to a specific structural role—without entailing that the object exists in actuality.

So my request is for help understanding the passage on precisely this point: Does Russell’s quantificational analysis necessarily apply to descriptions embedded in these niche, non-actualized domains, thereby ruling out any form of reference without existence? Or could his framework accommodate a kind of descriptive singular term that operates within intentional or modal contexts—frameworks in which existence is bracketed—without collapsing back into a hidden subject-predicate analysis and without committing him to Meinongian objects? I am particularly unsure whether Russell’s rejection of non-existent subjects is a claim about logical form in general, or specifically about assertions made within an ontology of actual existents, and I would welcome guidance on how the text bears on this distinction.
