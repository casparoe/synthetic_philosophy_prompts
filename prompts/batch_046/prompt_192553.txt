At a research seminar, two colleagues got into an extended argument about whether mechanistic interpretability should be treated as a control measure for potentially misaligned systems. I was sitting in and didn't take sides; I'm asking you to referee.

The first colleague argued that if we can read a model's internal representations—say, by mapping feature directions in its residual stream—we can verify alignment directly instead of assuming misalignment. She said behavioral sandboxing treats the model like a black-box prisoner, which implicitly concedes it might be deceptive; interpretability lets us check that it actually represents harm-avoidance and truth-telling, so it shifts safety from assuming the worst to confirming the best.

The second colleague replied that interpretability tools are themselves algorithms that can be targeted by adversarial representations: a misaligned model could encode malicious intent in a rotated or distributed basis that looks benign to our probes, making the oversight mechanism part of the attack surface. He said this means interpretability-based control doesn't avoid assuming misalignment; it strengthens that assumption by presupposing the model is actively trying to deceive not just users but the verification apparatus itself.

She came back that adversarial representations require specific capability thresholds—like explicit optimization for deception under a particular probe architecture—that don't apply to smaller or less capable systems, so her point holds for the realistic deployment range. She added that sandboxing makes its own hidden alignment assumptions: it assumes the model won't socially engineer its way out or exploit unknown hardware side-channels, which is just a different kind of intent-assumption dressed up as a capability constraint.

He answered that there's a structural difference: sandboxing constrains capability regardless of intent (a wall is a wall whether you're malicious or not), whereas interpretability verifies intent, which is circular when misalignment is precisely the risk you're trying to control for. He then introduced a distinction he called "functional transparency" versus "epistemic transparency"—the first being when a system's outputs are predictable from its internals, the second being when an observer can correctly read those internals. He claimed interpretability only delivers functional transparency, and treating it as epistemic transparency for control purposes is the category error.

She closed by saying that even functional transparency is enough for control if we pair it with redundant, differently-architected probes, and that his capability/intent distinction collapses because all practical control measures—sandboxing included—are evaluated against an adversarial model of failure, so every safety framework ultimately assumes misalignment in its design conditions.
