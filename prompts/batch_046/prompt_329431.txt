I've been trying to extend Simone Weil's critique of political parties into a general argument against collective deliberation, and now I'm worried it proves too much. Weil holds that party membership corrupts moral perception because it requires individuals to substitute collective passion and doctrinal loyalty for their own direct judgment and attention; the party becomes a mechanism that manufactures consent rather than a space for truth. I formalized this into a chain about all binding collective bodies, but the conclusion seems absurd.

(1) For Weil, the essential corruption of a party is that it demands members treat the collective's positions as authoritative over their personal capacity for attention and judgment.

(2) Any institution that produces a binding collective decision—juries rendering verdicts, legislative bodies passing laws, scientific peer-review panels issuing binding recommendations, citizens' assemblies reaching consensus—requires its members to subordinate their individual judgment to a group conclusion that will bind them or others.

(3) Therefore, every such institution demands exactly the substitution of collective authority for personal attention that Weil identifies as the moral danger of parties.

(4) If this substitution is genuinely corrupting, then all collective deliberative bodies are corrupting in the same way and to the same degree.

(5) But a society without juries, legislatures, or deliberative assemblies is not a purified democracy but an unworkable chaos; we clearly need some collective decision mechanisms.

(6) So Weil's premise in (1) must be false, or there is a hidden distinction that saves collective bodies from the party-analogy.

I feel the chain overreaches somewhere in the middle, but I can't name the precise step. Which premise or transition should I reject? If the argument is valid and forces me to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (5) and abandon whatever saves them, but I want to know what that costs for Weil's broader account of attention, rootedness, and justice. Specifically, can she consistently allow for binding collective judgment in juries or democratic assemblies while still condemning parties, or does saving the argument require her to accept some form of collective authority after all?
