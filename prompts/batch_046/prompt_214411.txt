I'm trying to fix my actual position on presentism in historiography by testing it against concrete moves, not by reciting the standard debate. Here are my gut verdicts.

When a historian of medieval medicine uses modern epidemiological categories to show that a "miasma" explanation masked actual transmission vectors, I think that's legitimate conceptual clarification, not presentism. But when that same historian faults the physician for not running a controlled trial, I think that's an illegitimate imposition of modern scientific norms.

When a historian refuses to apply the concept of genocide to nineteenth-century colonial campaigns on the ground that the term was coined in 1944, even though the acts and intent clearly match the definition, I think that's excessive anti-presentism — lexical history is blocking analytical and moral clarity.

But when a historian applies modern trauma psychology to explain the behavior of soldiers after a particular seventeenth-century battle, and uses that framework to excuse acts that contemporaries themselves condemned, I think that's presentist in a different, damaging way: it replaces the actors' own reasons with ours.

When a historian praises a nineteenth-century feminist activist for advocating women's education by saying she was "ahead of her time," using our modern egalitarian standards as the measure, I feel uneasy — that seems like presentist praise. Yet when the same historian condemns a nineteenth-century statesman for defending slavery using our modern moral framework, I don't feel the same unease; the condemnation seems warranted.

When a historian insists on describing past gender relations using only the binary categories that the society itself recognized, and refuses to ask whether those categories obscured non-binary practices that we can now identify, I think that's a failure of analytical responsibility — letting the past's self-understanding block our better understanding.

When a historian uses modern rational-choice economics to model peasant rebellion and concludes the rebels were "irrational" because they failed to maximize expected utility, I think that's presentist in a damaging way: it judges past action by a framework the actors wouldn't recognize and finds them deficient.

Here is the tension I can't resolve. I think it's wrong to judge a twelfth-century jurist for failing to recognize gender equality in law, because he had no access to that concept. Yet I think it's right to judge a twentieth-century colonial administrator for racial policies that were contested even in his own time — not only by us, but by some of his contemporaries. So my principle can't just be "judge only when the concept was available," because then I'd have to excuse anyone whose harmful acts were normative in their era, which I don't want to do.

Is there a coherent principle — or a small family of candidates — that captures all these verdicts? State each precisely, with the specific case behind each clause. Tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a historian who applies modern climate-science attribution methods to argue that nineteenth-century imperial expansion was partially driven by drought stress, but then uses that causal explanation to diminish moral responsibility for the violence of conquest. Be honest about which of my verdicts your principle must overturn, and separate what is settled historiographical consensus from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and historians of the discipline; do not present a contested view — for instance, about whether presentism is always avoidable or whether moral judgment of the past is ever legitimate — as if it were settled.
