At a seminar on revolutionary history, two colleagues—Maya and Jonas—debated whether the French Revolution “devoured its children” and, if so, through what mechanism. They asked me to referee, and I am genuinely unsure. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves below from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both parties are missing that a fuller interpretation ought to include, and indicate whether your own verdict leans more towards class dynamics, contingency, ideology, or some other factor. In your response, use British English, separate what is historically settled from what remains genuinely contested among specialists of the Revolution, and do not present any contested interpretive claim as if it were the consensus view.

The exchange ran as follows:

(1) Maya argued that the Revolution devoured its children through class dynamics. The bourgeois leadership, she said, mobilised popular violence and sans-culotte energy to destroy feudal privilege, then systematically suppressed those same popular forces—culminating in the Thermidorian Reaction—to secure capitalist property relations. The Terror, on this view, was not an accidental by-product but the consolidation of a new class order.

(2) Jonas replied that this framing imposes a teleological logic the events do not support. The purges and factional violence, he said, were driven primarily by contingency: foreign invasion, internal rebellion, and the collapse of institutional checks under emergency conditions. Without these pressures, he claimed, there is no reason to assume the Revolution would necessarily have turned on its own leaders.

(3) Maya countered that contingency alone cannot explain the systematic pattern of targets—aristocrats, then Girondins, then Hébertists, then Robespierre himself. She proposed that an ideological mechanism was at work: once “the people” was defined as an abstract moral category rather than a concrete social body, any particular faction could be expelled as corrupt or counter-revolutionary. The violence, she said, followed the logic of revolutionary purity, not merely the pressure of events.

(4) Jonas conceded that the pattern was real but denied that it should be analysed as ideology in the materialist sense. Instead, he introduced the idea of “normative recursion without institutional brakes”—a structural feature of regimes that derive legitimacy solely from an unmediated claim to popular sovereignty. In such a system, every faction must eliminate rivals to prove its authenticity, creating a self-amplifying cycle of exclusion that behaves like an institutional auto-immune response. He noted that this explains the self-cannibalisation without invoking false consciousness or class interest, and he observed that the mechanism has analogues in feedback loops where unmediated validation amplifies deviation.

(5) Maya responded that Jonas’s framework is descriptively elegant but materially empty. She asked why, if the mechanism is purely structural, the recursion consistently destroyed the radical wing and protected property relations—why the Thermidorian Reaction followed Robespierre’s fall, and why the bourgeoisie emerged intact. Without class analysis, she argued, “normative recursion” is merely a redescription of ideology, and one that cannot account for the directional outcome of the violence.

Please adjudicate accordingly.
