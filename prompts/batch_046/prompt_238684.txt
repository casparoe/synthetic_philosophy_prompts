I'm running a grad seminar on modality and one of my students threw this argument at me that I can't quite kill. (1) If a state of affairs is genuinely conceiveable without explicit contradiction, then it's metaphysically possible. (2) I can genuinely conceive a world where the fundamental laws are just like ours except moral properties are causally efficacious—cruelty literally slows down local time, kindness speeds it up. No logical contradiction, just bizarre physics. (3) So that world is metaphysically possible. (4) But if that's possible, then the seperation between moral and physical properties in our world isn't metaphysically necessary, so moral naturalism is false—or at least not necessary. (5) Now run the same move on any alleged necessary truth: conceive a twin-world where water isn't H2O, or where 7 is prime but not necessarily so. (6) So conceivability wipes out every non-logical necessity. (7) But philosophers like Kripke and Putnam think there are tons of those. The student wants to save (1) and just deny (7), but that feels like throwing out half of post-Kripkean metaphysics. Where's the slip? Is it an equivocation between "conceiving a description" and "conceiving the thing itself"? A hidden slide from epistmic to metaphysical possibility? Or is the problem that (2) only shows I don't know it's impossible, not that it actually is possible? I want to know which step to reject and what I'm paying if I keep (1) but reject something else—especially since I still think conceivability gives us some modal knowledge, just not unlimited.
