I defend a discontinuous prioritarian position: moral weight should increase with worse-offness, but well-being is not smoothly continuous—it has a structural break at a basic-capabilities threshold. Above that threshold, equalizing downward does not reduce anyone's morally relevant welfare; it only redistributes goods that fall outside the metric. I therefore reject the leveling-down objection as based on a false continuity assumption. What would genuinely count against this? I want the map of its exposure: which empirical findings about welfare measurement, which formal results about aggregation under discontinuity, and which intuitive cases of harm-through-equalization above the threshold would be decisive rather than merely awkward. What does this view predict that continuous prioritarianism or egalitarianism do not—particularly about trade-offs near the threshold? Have I qualified the position—through an adjustable threshold or a malleable metric—into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a defect for a normative theory? Please distinguish defeaters I should concede as damaging from those I can consistently dismiss, rank them from fatal to awkward, and assess honestly whether the view risks anything at all. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question.
