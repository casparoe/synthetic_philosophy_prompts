I am preparing a fifty-minute seminar for third-year philosophy students on the normative architecture of moral emotions, specifically the guilt-shame distinction. Over the past week I have been working through a framing that I have been calling emotional constitutionalism—the thesis that a culture's dominant moral emotion is not merely a collective preference but is institutionally constituted through its specific practices of accountability, repair, and social exclusion. I want the students to come away able to address two intertwined questions: whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion on philosophical grounds, and whether this constitutional framing implies that a culture can deliberately choose, or even design, its dominant emotional regime. Do not explain the distinction itself to me; I need a pedagogical plan for the session.

What conceptual dependency must come first—the phenomenological contrast between internal sanction and external exposure, or the institutional framing of emotional constitutionalism? Identify the one carrying example or case study that should anchor the entire discussion, specifying exactly what features it must possess and what it must lack, and explain why the standard comparison of private religious confession versus public pillory is inadequate for this purpose. Map the predictable misconceptions students will form—such as identifying shame with mere embarrassment, or assuming that guilt is automatically more mature because it is internalized—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. Propose one diagnostic question I can pose at the end to determine whether they have grasped the constitutional claim rather than only the psychological taxonomy. Also, state explicitly what material I should openly refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the full anthropological literature on specific honor societies or the clinical taxonomy of toxic shame—while acknowledging that absence.
