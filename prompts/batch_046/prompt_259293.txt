I am preparing to defend a synthetic thesis in a graduate seminar devoted to political obligation and resistance, and I need a rigorous prep sheet for the question period. My position is that democratic political obligation should be understood dissociatively rather than as continuous fair-play compliance: citizens bear a standing, prima facie duty to obey law not because they have consented to every statute or because they continuously benefit from cooperation, but because the constitutional framework supplies an open, revisable procedure of public justification that they can endorse as a standing institutional commitment. Crucially, I argue that this obligation is suspended—not violated—when citizens engage in public, non-violent civil disobedience that targets procedural breakdowns rather than mere policy disagreement, provided the act is framed as an appeal to restore deliberative integrity rather than as coercive pressure for a specific outcome. The suspension is conditional on transparency and willingness to face legal consequences, but the willingness to face consequences functions as proof of fidelity to the procedure being defended, not as proof of unconditional obedience to the existing legal order.

The audience is composed of political philosophers with deep commitments to Rawlsian liberalism and legal positivism. Two senior members of the seminar are prominent defenders of the fair-play account; they treat any suspension of obligation as conceptually incoherent because fair play requires uninterrupted contribution to a cooperative scheme, and they will press whether my dissociative view collapses into an unacknowledged consent theory or into selective anarchism. Another core participant is a constitutional lawyer who accepts civil disobedience only within a narrowly juridical framework—specifically, where the dissident accepts legal punishment as a performative reaffirmation of constitutional fidelity, and who regards any theoretical justification for refusing punishment as a rejection of the rule of law rather than an internal appeal to it. There are also several agnostic graduate students who will test whether the distinction between procedural-targeting and policy-targeting disobedience is operationally stable, and whether my view gives citizens excessive discretion to declare procedural failure whenever they dislike an outcome.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face in this room, ordered by how damaging each is likely to be given these interlocutors. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that audience member would put it—using the vocabulary of fair play, cooperative scheme, constitutional fidelity, or procedural closure where appropriate—then indicate whether I should concede a point, hold firm, or partially accommodate. Identify the weakest structural point in my own argument that I should actively defend rather than hope they overlook, and specify the reply that must land for the thesis to survive the session.
