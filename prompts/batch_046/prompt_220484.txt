Recently I found myself sustaining a belief not because of its evidential support but because it preserved my capacity to act under extreme uncertainty, and feeling admiration for a figure largely because that admiration anchored my professional commitments. This has convinced me that the wrong-kind-reasons problem may depend on treating belief and admiration as passive representational states. Please argue for the view—call it agential constitutivism about doxastic and evaluative attitudes—that belief and admiration are practices of self-constitution, and that reasons ordinarily classified as "wrong kind" (pragmatic reasons for belief, strategic reasons for admiration) are not violations of independent cognitive or evaluative norms but first-order practical reasons operating at the constitutive level of agency. Defend in particular the claim that there is no freestanding normative kind that practical reasons contravene; the appearance of contravention arises only when we illegitimately apply a second-order evaluative frame to a first-order constitutive practice. Keep the reasoning focused and do not appeal to bibliographic authority. Finally, say explicitly what would change your mind: identify the counterexample, conceptual distinction, or failure in the framing argument that would cause you to weaken or abandon the constitutivist dissolution of the wrong-kind-reasons problem, and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
