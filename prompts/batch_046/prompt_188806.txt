1. surgeon does an emergency op, patient dies on the table despite perfect technique. ravi: not blameworthy, the cetena was pure.
2. factory owner switches to cheap chemicals that poison a river, saving 200 jobs. ravi: blameworthy - negligence in checking, and harm to sentient beings isn't canceled out by jobs saved.
3. prison guard lies to an inmate about a family death to stop him from killing himself during lockdown. ravi: skillful means, right action.
4. charity uses AI-generated images of fake suffering to raise donations that actually feed real people. ravi: wrong, deception corrupts the mind of the donor and the org even if thousands eat.

new case: a monastery runs an automated donation round-up app. users know about it, but the monastery also found that adding a gentle guilt-message ("your neigbors donated more") triples donations for famine relief. they keep it in. net result is thousands saved, no direct harm, but users report feeling subtly pushed and guilty.

what should ravi say for consistency? which of the four is the closest analog, and does his track record genuinely underdetermine this? if he approves the guilt-message, what principle is he adopting - that indirect mental nudges are okay when outcomes are good? if he rejects it, is he saying any intentional shaping of mental states is wrong regardless of consequence? and what's the further case that'd actually split those two rules apart?
