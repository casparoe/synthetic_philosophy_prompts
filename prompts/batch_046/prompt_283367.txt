You chair the authenticity review board at a mid-sized museum, meeting monthly to rule on works whose technical analysis reveals physical discrepancies—anachronistic pigments, later overpainting, or substituted supports—without clear provenance documentation. Write a reusable decision procedure, grounded in philosophy of art and value theory, that guides the board from initial finding to one of three dispositions: unchanged display, relabeled display with contextual annotation, or deaccession. Structure it as ordered questions with decision nodes; specify what evidence counts as sufficient at each step; identify where philosophical disagreement—such as between aesthetic-essentialist and historical-testimony accounts of value—makes judgment irreducible and requires a human vote; and include two operational test conditions, one applying the autographic/allographic distinction to mixed-media objects and one distinguishing damage that erases artistic achievement from damage that merely alters material substrate. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a nineteenth-century landscape with a forged signature but entirely original brushwork; (2) a Renaissance panel whose original ground layer was replaced in the eighteenth century but whose visible paint surface is untouched; and (3) a site-specific installation originally fabricated with biodegradable materials, replaced after decay by the artist’s estate with chemically different but visually identical substitutes. In one case the procedure should yield a recommendation the museum’s development office will strongly oppose. Conclude by stating two questions the procedure explicitly cannot settle—such as market-value adjustments or criminal fraud determinations—and flag the points where a board member without philosophical training must defer to expert testimony rather than follow the checklist alone.
