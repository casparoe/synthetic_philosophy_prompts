Here are my judgments about material constitution in statue-and-clay-style scenarios, and I want to know whether one principle can unify them. (1) A clay statue of a horse is squashed into an amorphous lump; I think the statue is destroyed, though the clay survives. (2) Every piece of marble in a statue is replaced over decades with new stone of identical appearance and structure; I think it remains the same statue. (3) A sculptor abandons a half-carved block; years later, a different sculptor, unaware of the prior work, carves the remaining material into a figure matching the original design exactly. I think the result is a different statue, not the one originally intended. (4) A statue is engineered from living tissue that continuously regenerates, so after many years none of the original cells remain; I think it is still that statue. (5) A bronze ingot is melted and divided evenly into two cast figures; I think the ingot is destroyed and neither figure is identical to it. (6) A synthetic substrate can be reshaped at will without losing continuity; it is formed into a horse, then into a sphere, then back into an externally identical horse. I think the second horse is the original statue restored, not a new one. Is there a single account—perhaps combining conditions on material continuity, formal persistence, and causal-historical process—that captures all six verdicts? If not, which should be sacrificed for simplicity, and what does the best revised principle predict for a case I have not listed: the statue is precisely scanned, destroyed, and then reconstructed atom-for-atom in a different location by an unrelated machine?
