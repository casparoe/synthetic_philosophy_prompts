Write a deadpan travel-guide entry, in the style of a Lonely Planet page, for the fictional Republic of Trajectoria, a country that has codified strict path dependence as its national ideology: the state holds that every social, technological, and political outcome was locked in by minute initial conditions at founding, and that to propose an alternative trajectory is both a logical error and a misdemeanor. The entry should include: an overview of local customs (citizens do not participate in elections because “the vote was already embedded in the founding parameters”; restaurants serve only dishes that replicate the first meal cooked in the capital in 1847); practical tips for visitors (how to apply for the mandatory Counterfactual Competence visa, what to say if a local asks whether you think the railway network might have developed differently, and the penalty for suggesting functional convergence); a sidebar describing the national holiday of Lock-In Day, which commemorates the adoption of the QWERTY-style administrative typing standard despite its inefficiency; and a brief note on the “Convergence Exemption,” the little-known legal clause that allows the ruling party alone to argue that certain outcomes were inevitable regardless of path, provided they are outcomes the party currently favors. Keep the voice entirely straight-faced: never wink, never name the philosophers or economists being satirized, and never state the objections outright. Instead, let the internal contradictions of enforced path dependence surface through bureaucratic detail—especially the circular defense of the status quo, the suppression of functional or selection-based explanations, and the asymmetry between citizens and officials in who is permitted to invoke inevitability. The satire should be legible to someone familiar with the debate over whether history is genuinely “locked in” (small initial advantages amplifying through increasing returns) versus whether similar endpoints would have arrived through convergent pressures anyway, but readable even to someone who is not. Target approximately 600 words.
