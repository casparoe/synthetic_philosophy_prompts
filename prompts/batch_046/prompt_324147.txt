I'm part of this small open-source collective that builds accessibility tools, and last year we switched to a "patron visibility" funding thing. Every donor gets a personalized dashboard that traces their exact dollars to specific lines of code, with little badges and shout-outs in our release notes. Engagement went through the roof—GitHub stars tripled, pledges doubled, everyone loves seeing their name tied to a feature. But lately our maintainars have started gaming it: they chase bugs that photograph well for the dashboard, skip deep architectural fixes that don't map to a visible donation, and even postponed a security patch because it couldn't be tagged to a specific patron's contribution. The founder says this is rational because "approval is the actual resource we're mining," which sounds clever but also kind of creepy to me.

I was talking with my freind about whether this is just smart incentive design or if there's a deeper philosophical mistake in running the collective this way. Like, is optimizing for donor praise fundamentally different from optimizing for actually good software, or is praise just a proxy for value? I feel like there's a distinction between agents that chase rewards and agents that chase real-world outcomes, and our collective might be sliding from one to the other, but I can't articulate it well. Can you help me think through whether approval-seeking behavior corrupts the purpose of the project in some structural way, even if it brings in more money? I don't need citations or a syllabus—just want to understand the conceptual layout.
