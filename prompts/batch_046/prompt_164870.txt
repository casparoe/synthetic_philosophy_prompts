I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I recently encountered a summary of Locke's treatise on education — the work generally referred to as Some Thoughts Concerning Education — and want to apply its framework to mentoring junior developers. Locke seems to argue that habits are formed through the association of ideas with pleasure or pain rather than through explicit rules, and that the tutor's lived example shapes the pupil more powerfully than verbal precept. He also distinguishes between a habit of reason and a habit of mere obedience, warns that punishment can associate study with fear, and insists that learning should feel like play.

Please design a reusable decision procedure my engineering team could run when a junior makes a mistake. The procedure should ask ordered questions grounded in these Lockean distinctions; specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; identify the stopping points where the answer is "intervene immediately" versus "allow the failure to unfold"; and explicitly mark the points where Lockean values conflict — for example, where protecting production systems clashes with allowing the natural association of error and correction. The procedure must be usable by mentors without philosophical vocabulary, but it should preserve the conceptual distinctions rather than flattening them into generic coaching advice.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a junior pushes untested code that could corrupt a shared database; (2) a junior repeatedly chooses an inefficient algorithm because they have not yet experienced its cost; and (3) a junior avoids writing documentation out of anxiety rather than laziness. Finally, flag where Locke's seventeenth-century context — his emphasis on physical health, gentlemanly formation, and the absence of digital artifacts — makes the analogy break down, and state whether the procedure should discard those elements or adapt them for a modern technical environment.
