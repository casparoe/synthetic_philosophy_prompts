I'm a PhD student in political theory studying democratic institutions, and I've noticed a recurring pattern in the literature on sortition and lottocracy that I haven't been able to name. The basic idea is straightforward: selecting legislators, jurors, or advisory bodies by lot is supposed to eliminate campaign corruption and produce descriptive representation. Yet every time a serious practical objection arises—say, that randomly selected citizens lack technical expertise, or that a purely random body would be unaccountable—the proposed remedy is not to defend pure random selection but to add a corrective layer: extensive expert briefings, advisory panels with de facto veto power, merit-filtered preliminary pools, or jurisdictional restrictions that confine the lot-selected body to narrow domains. Each layer is defensible on its own, but cumulatively they transform the institution into something functionally indistinguishable from a conventional appointed commission, even as the original label—"sortition," "lottocracy," or "random selection"—is retained and continues to carry democratic legitimacy. I've seen this happen in at least three concrete proposal families: citizens' assembly models where facilitators shape the agenda so thoroughly that the random membership is largely ceremonial; legislative sortition schemes that preserve lot selection for final membership but insert a preliminary competence filter; and judicial sortition proposals where the randomly drawn pool is so small and so heavily guided by clerks that the randomness does little actual work. Is there an established concept in democratic theory or institutional design for this specific kind of functional hollowing-out, where a signature mechanism survives nominally but is progressively supplemented until it no longer performs the role originally assigned to it? How is it different from an ordinary institutional compromise, from concept creep in normative arguments, or from the motte-and-bailey structure? If no term exists, can you coin one with clear boundary conditions—specifically, distinguishing cases where supplementation genuinely preserves the mechanism's democratic function from cases where it merely borrows the mechanism's normative prestige? I don't need a literature survey; I need the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
