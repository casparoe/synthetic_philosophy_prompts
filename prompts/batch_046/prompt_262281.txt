At a writers' retreat in the Lake District last autumn I listened to two fellow novelists, Elias and Mira, argue for nearly an hour about whether hypocrisy ever genuinely removes one's standing to blame, and I should like an impartial adjudication delivered in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might compose in a long letter to a colleague. Elias began by proposing what he termed a moral bookkeeping model: every agent maintains a running normative account for each kind of wrong, and one can issue a valid blame-claim against another only if one's own account for that specific norm is in credit. On this view, if you have violated the norm against plagiarism, even once and long ago, you possess no standing to blame a student for plagiarising now, because your own debt cancels your authority; he insisted that this is not a demand for perfection but a structural feature of blame itself, which requires that the blamer not be a co-debtor in the very currency she seeks to collect. Mira replied that this confuses the conditions for being a credible messenger with the conditions for having a valid point, and she offered a relational alternative in which standing to blame is a three-place relation between blamer, wrongdoer, and wrong, determined not by the blamer's global moral ledger but by whether she is presently implicated in the wrong in a way that makes her a co-participant rather than an external addressor. A novelist who broke a promise to one friend, Mira suggested, might still have standing to blame a stranger for breaking promises, since the relational position differs, yet she might lack standing to blame the very friend she betrayed, because the wrong is shared between them; she added that if Elias's bookkeeping view were correct, moral life would collapse into near-silence, since almost everyone carries some normative debt, yet we do not suppose that a recovered alcoholic lacks all standing to blame another for dangerous drinking. Elias conceded the relational point but refined his position: standing, he said, is indexed not globally but specifically, so one loses standing only with respect to those wrongs for which one's own account is in deficit, and only towards wrongdoers who stand in the same normative relation that one's own fault disrupted. Thus the plagiarist-novelist retains standing to blame a tax evader, but not to blame a plagiarist, and especially not if the plagiarism is of the same kind he committed; he argued that Mira's view cannot explain why we feel a particular sting when the hypocrite's fault is identical, for if standing were purely relational the identity of the wrong should not matter, yet it plainly does. Mira countered that Elias has merely relocated the bookkeeping from a global to an indexed ledger, and that the sting he describes is better explained by audience psychology, our resentment at being lectured by someone who has not paid the price, rather than by any genuine loss of normative authority. She proposed instead that the only standing-destroying condition is present co-participation: you lack standing to blame someone for X only if you are currently doing X with them or benefiting from their doing X with you, and historical purity is irrelevant; what matters is whether you are now positioned to speak on behalf of the norm without being its living contradiction in this very interaction. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, assessing Elias's initial bookkeeping model, Mira's relational reply, Elias's indexed refinement, and Mira's co-participation counter, and say who is right about which specific point and who is more right overall. Also indicate what both are missing, perhaps a distinction between standing to blame privately and standing to blame publicly, or between the authority of the message and the authority of the messenger, that neither has captured, and write entirely in the manner of a letter without any structural formatting.
