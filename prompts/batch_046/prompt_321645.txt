Write a deadpan, bureaucratic “Terms of Service and User Agreement” (approximately 600–800 words) for a fictional divine-governance platform named Providentia, rendered entirely in the voice of its corporate legal and ethics-compliance team. The target of the satire is the logical architecture of Luis de Molina’s doctrine of middle knowledge—specifically, the claim that God possesses counterfactual knowledge of what every free creature would do in every possible circumstance, prior to any decree of will—translated into the language of modern algorithmic governance, predictive analytics, and data-privacy law. The piece should exaggerate real features of Molinism rather than inventing false ones, so that a reader familiar with the theology recognizes the parody as fair.

Structure the agreement around three “tiers of predictive knowledge” corresponding to natural knowledge, free knowledge, and middle knowledge, rendered respectively as “Necessary System Parameters,” “Post-Decree Actualization Logs,” and “Pre-Volitional Counterfactual Modeling.” The satire should turn on the tension that the platform insists it never causes user choices—each free decision remains genuinely undetermined by the system—while simultaneously curating every circumstantial parameter (the “environmental instantiation package”) with perfect foreknowledge of exactly which choice will result. Include a clause addressing the “transworld depravity” limitation: no matter which world-version is deployed, at least one instantiated user freely selects corruption, and the platform cannot guarantee a corruption-free world without overriding the user’s freedom settings, which would violate Section 4(b).

Inject one original, logically consistent extension: an “Opt-Out of Actualization” provision allowing a user to remain permanently in the “Library of Uninstantiated Possibilities” rather than being actualized in a specific world, with the explicit warning that users who select this option forfeit all standing to file suffering-related grievances about worlds they never entered. Maintain strict internal consistency: the legal voice must remain earnest, technically precise, and free of winks or anachronistic jokes; the irony should emerge from the gap between the contract’s coercive structure and its repeated insistence that acceptance is entirely free. Do not reference living persons, do not request citations or bibliographies, and avoid breaking character to explain the theology—the document itself should perform the concept.
