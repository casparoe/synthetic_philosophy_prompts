You are an expert in Thomistic political philosophy, with particular attention to how interpreters navigate Aquinas's shifting treatment of resistance across his early and mature works. Think carefully, begin with a brief brainstorming of the main interpretive rules that could explain a consistent pattern of verdicts, and then proceed.

A scholar—call her Dr. Vasquez—has published a series of rulings interpreting Aquinas on tyrannicide and resistance. Her verdicts on earlier hypothetical cases are these.

Case 1: A ruler who came to power legitimately but now systematically violates natural law and destroys the common good; a private citizen assassinates him. Vasquez rules: forbidden; this is sedition.

Case 2: A usurper who seized power by fraud and rules with extreme violence; the functioning legitimate civil council removes and kills him. Vasquez rules: permissible and not sinful.

Case 3: A usurper in a province where the civil council has been dissolved; a coalition of former council members—now private citizens—issues a joint declaration stripping the usurper of authority, and one of them kills him. Vasquez rules: tolerable but irregular; not the sin of sedition, yet lacking the formal authorization that makes resistance fully lawful.

Case 4: A legitimate ruler whose policies are harmful but not catastrophic; the people suffer but do not resist. Vasquez rules: they must endure; private resistance would be premature and dangerous.

Next, Case 5: A usurper has taken control of a city. The legitimate assembly was dissolved years ago, and its former members—now dispersed—have never issued any collective declaration. A local bishop, who holds no civil office, publicly condemns the usurper and states that anyone who removes him acts in defense of the common good. A private citizen, citing only the bishop's words and his own judgment, kills the usurper.

Predict Vasquez's verdict on Case 5. Identify which implicit rule—strict institutional authorization (only formal public authority can license killing, and only against usurpers), qualified private resistance (private citizens may kill usurpers when formal authority is absent, provided the risk of sedition is minimized), or a prudential-proxy view (the judgment hinges on whether collective or ecclesiastical endorsement can substitute for missing civil authority to prevent scandal and disorder)—best explains her full pattern. Then name the single earlier case that, had Vasquez ruled differently on it, would most sharply distinguish the leading rule from its closest rival, and explain why that reversal would break the pattern.
