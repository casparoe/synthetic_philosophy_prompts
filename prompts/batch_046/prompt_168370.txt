Here's an argument my study group cooked up about CEV and I can't tell if it's clever or just broken.

1. Coherent extrapolated volition says our real values are what we'd want after idealizing—knowing more, thinking clearer, growing together with others.
2. If I'm fully idealized, I understand that my actual values depend on accidents of birth and culture.
3. A rational agent shouldn't treat accidental features as binding reasons—otherwise I'm just fetishizing my own biograpy.
4. So my idealized self wouldn't want me to stick to my particular values; they'd want me to adopt whatever is best from an impartial standpoint.
5. But then CEV doesn't preserve "our" values at all, it just smuggels in universalist ethics and calls it my volition.
6. Even worse, if different people idealize differently, there's no convergence, so CEV gives no determinante answer.
7. So either CEV collapses into impersonal morality, or it's empty. That can't be right—people use it to argue for AI alignment that respects human pluralism.

Which step should I reject? I kinda want to keep 2 and 3 because they feel true, but I don't want CEV to be useless. Is the slip somewhere between knowing my values are contingent and thinking that makes them non-binding? Or is there a hidden equivocation in what "idealized" means between step 1 and step 4?
