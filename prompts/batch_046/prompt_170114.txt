I'm facilitating a small philosophy-of-mind seminar on Dennett's intentional stance, and we've been arguing about whether large language models are legitimate targets of belief-attribution. A recurring move in our discussion is an analogy I keep hearing: attributing beliefs or intentions to an LLM is like attributing purpose to a shadow cast by a rotating polyhedron—what looks like deliberate movement is just the geometry of the underlying object interacting with a fixed light source. The analogy is used to push a skeptical conclusion: just as we wouldn't say the shadow "wants" to grow, we shouldn't say the model "believes" anything; the appearance of mentality is an artifact of projection onto a deterministic or probabilistic structure. I want to test whether this comparison actually supports that conclusion, or whether it collapses under scrutiny.

Can you walk me through the correspondence in detail—what plays the role of the polyhedron, the light, the shadow, and the observer? Then identify the points where the mapping breaks down: for instance, does the shadow's complete dependence on a single causal chain match the distributed, learned representations in a neural network? Does the analogy smuggle in an assumption that there is a single "real" mechanism behind the appearance, and is that assumption warranted for a system shaped by gradient descent across vast corpora? Finally, tell me whether the skeptical conclusion about belief-attribution survives once these disanalogies are laid out, or whether the analogy was doing most of the argumentative work all along. I'm especially interested in whether there is a better analogy—perhaps one drawn from predictive interpretation or from strategic interaction—that captures the same intuition without importing the misleading geometry of light and object.
