The response reads as follows. Kant holds that animals lack rational nature and therefore lack direct moral status. In the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, he develops the indirect-duty thesis: we ought not to be cruel to animals, not because they are wronged, but because cruelty hardens our disposition and makes us more likely to fail in our direct duties to human beings, who are ends in themselves and possess dignity rather than a mere price. This fits his broader view that only autonomous beings can legislate moral law for themselves.

Regarding the gradation objection, one might think that if rational nature comes in degrees—some animals display instrumental reasoning—then moral status should also be scalar. Kant can reply that the rational nature relevant to morality is not a cognitive capacity distributed across a spectrum, but the specific capacity for autonomous self-legislation according to the Categorical Imperative. This capacity is binary: either a being can legislate for itself, or it cannot. Hence animals fall entirely outside direct consideration, regardless of empirical differences in intelligence.

There is, however, a deeper tension. Kant’s treatment of sympathy suggests he recognises it as playing a foundational role in moral concern, much as Hume argued, though he ultimately subordinates it to rational principle. If sympathy genuinely tracks suffering irrespective of species, then suppressing it towards animals might damage our moral sensibility in ways that exceed the indirect-duty argument. Moreover, maintaining a strict binary risks treating moral worth as measurable—since any graded approach to animals would seem to commodify dignity—so the Kantian must insist that dignity is incomparable and cannot be partially extended. This does not collapse into a contractualist framework, where obligations depend on reciprocal cooperation, but it does require us to separate empirical cognitive gradation sharply from normative moral status. Overall, the indirect-duty view is coherent, though it demands that we keep emotional responsiveness and rational principle in careful balance.
