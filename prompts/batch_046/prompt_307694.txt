Is there a name for this? In philosphy debates about whether really awful laws—like segregation statutes or certain war-time decrees—still count as "law" at all, I keep seeing the same move occuring. Someone says one specific statute is so immoral it shouldn't have legal status. Then the reply comes: "So you think any law with a moral flaw is automatically invalid? That'd destroy the whole legal system." But the first person never said "any flaw"—they meant this extreme case. Still, they get pushed into defending the broad idea that judges or citizens can nullify laws whenever they feel like it, and they spend all their energy saying "no no, not every law," instead of defending the original narrow point. I've seen it happen with court rulings too: a judge strikes one decision down for violating basic fairness, and critics act like she's opened the door to total judicial chaos, even though she only talked about this one case. It's in the neighborhood of legal positivism vs natural law, but it's not exactly that debate—it's more about the rhetorical pattern itself. Is there an established term for being forced to defend a wildly broad version of your own narrow claim, specifcally in arguments about legal validity? If there is, I want you to commit: name it, explain why it fits better than nearby ideas, and draw the boundary lines. If there isn't, coin one with a clear definition and tell me exactly what belongs inside and outside it. Don't just list options—pick a side and defend it.
