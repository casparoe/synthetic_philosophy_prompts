I'm currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on the metaphysics of normativity, and last week during our unit on whether reasons necessarily depend on reasoning agents, a fellow student presented what she called a "sympathetic but unflinching" summary of the free-floating rationales position. She characterized it as follows: "The view claims that normative reasons are ontologically independent in exactly the way mathematical objects are—eternally existing whether or not any mind apprehends them—so that a reason could genuinely obtain in a region of the universe permanently devoid of sentient life, and any agent who eventually entered that region would be bound by obligations they never consented to and never caused. Proponents think this explains the phenomenology of moral discovery, but the fatal problem is that if reasons are genuinely free-floating, they are causally inert with respect to our deliberative processes, which means they cannot play any motivational role whatsoever; that is why the position collapses into an indefensible form of normative Platonism and has been largely abandoned by contemporary philosophers working on practical reason." I have the sense that her summary bundles together at least three distinct commitments—about ontological independence, about the scope of normative authority across space and time, and about the connection between reasons and motivation—that defenders of free-floating rationales might want to separate, but I am not confident enough to identify the missteps.
