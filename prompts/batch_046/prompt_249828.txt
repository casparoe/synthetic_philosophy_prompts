Here is an argument I built for a philosophy-of-mind seminar that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion is far stronger than I can accept.

(1) In Hofstadter's MIU formal system, the string MU is unreachable from MI using the permitted rules, yet a human observer can recognize this limitation by stepping outside the system and inspecting the rules—what Hofstadter terms JOOTSing.

(2) If a system can never transcend its own rules, then being able to JOOTS implies that human cognition is not fully captured by any single formal rule-set.

(3) Suppose cognitive science proposes a formal model F that completely describes human reasoning.

(4) If the mind is exactly F, then it is bound by F's rules and therefore incapable of the transcendence described in (1).

(5) But humans do recognize that MU is unreachable, which requires precisely that kind of transcendence.

(6) So the mind is not F, for any formal model F whatsoever.

(7) By generalization, if cognition essentially involves JOOTSing, then no formal theory of mind is possible.

(8) Therefore either cognitive science is impossible in principle, or human beings never actually jump outside formal descriptions of their own reasoning.

The conclusion in (8) seems to force a choice I do not want to make: I am not prepared to abandon the project of a scientific psychology, nor to deny that we can see the limits of formal games. Where does the reasoning fail? I would prefer to keep (1) and (5) intact. Is the error in (2)—perhaps JOOTSing is just computation within a larger, meta-level formal system? Or is it in (4)—maybe being an instance of F does not preclude running a meta-level simulation of F? Or does (6) commit an illegitimate move from "not this F" to "not any F"? Explain which step should be rejected and what giving it up costs, especially for the distinction between mind and formal system.
