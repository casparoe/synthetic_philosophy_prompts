I'm designing a unit for an advanced philosophy of perception course, and I'm dissatisfied with the standard classroom taxonomies of disjunctivism, which tend to organize positions around whether veridical and hallucinatory experiences share a common mental kind. Those taxonomies work well for textbook cases of color or shape misperception, but they obscure how the argument from illusion plays out for what we might call illusory presence — cases where a subject seems directly aware of an object or event that does not exist, such as phantom-limb awareness or the temporal analog of the stopped-clock illusion, where a brief interval seems to have presentational content despite no corresponding physical occurrence — and for property-mismatch cases where the object is genuinely present but its perceived properties diverge radically from its physical ones. I need a decision tree that sorts actual philosophical commitments rather than party labels, using these two case-types as the diagnostic branching points.

Please construct the tree so that the root asks whether direct realism can survive any form of perceptual error whatsoever. From there, branch on whether error is best analyzed as misrepresentation of genuinely present objects, as the presence of a non-physical sense-datum, or as a failure of perceptual presence itself. Next, split on whether the view permits multimodal conflict resolution to override a single modality's presence claims, and then on whether temporal illusions are treated as continuous with spatial ones or as a distinct category requiring separate analysis. The leaves must include at least naive direct realism, representationalist error theory, classic sense-datum theory, McDowell-style disjunctivism, presence-first disjunctivism (where direct presence and property attribution come apart), adverbial phenomenalism, and any unnamed but logically coherent combinations. For each leaf, provide its name or label it 'unnamed,' and in exactly one sentence state its most awkward commitment with respect to either phantom presence or the stopped-clock case. Flag any pair of leaves that your branching questions cannot genuinely separate, and briefly explain whether they collapse because the distinction is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored on these particular diagnostics. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the tree should stand on the logical structure of the positions alone.
