As a novelist who has sat through many workshops, I keep returning to a ritual that seems philosophically loaded. The author presents a draft, then observes a gag rule: she must remain silent—sometimes physically turning her chair—while peers dissect the work, frequently issuing contradictory directives about what the story "really wants." Afterward, she is forbidden from defending her intentions; she may only thank the critics, then submit a revised draft with a memo accounting for each comment she declined. The practice treats the author's first-person knowledge of her own aims as suspect—perhaps even corrupting—while treating the collective, external judgment as closer to artistic truth, even though the author alone signs the revision.

What commitments about the inside view versus the outside view, and about epistemic modesty, does this institution actually presuppose? Must it hold that artistic value is purely observer-relative and that authorial intent counts for nothing? Or can it be read more narrowly—as a corrective to specific biases (defensiveness, attachment to failed passages) without denying that some choices are defensible only from within? Reconstruct the implicit theory of authority and value, distinguish what the ritual strictly requires from what it merely fits, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—where rival views could license the same behavior. If you claim the practice collapses into incoherence by making the author both epistemically disabled and solely responsible, say precisely what feature of the workshop, if shown to serve a different purpose, would change your mind. Answer in no more than 350 words, and point to the weakest link in your own reasoning.
