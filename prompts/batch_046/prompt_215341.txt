In a graduate seminar on just war theory, a classmate repeatedly deploys the phrase 'the moral equality of combatants' as a conversation-stopper—most recently to reject my proposal that an autonomous targeting system ought to weight the justice of the adversary's cause when assigning proportional risk to noncombatants. He treats the phrase as if it entails that any differential treatment of soldiers based on jus ad bellum status is automatically illegitimate, but he never specifies which thesis is actually doing the work. Can you disambiguate the slogan by laying out the precise, separable claims it could stand for—such as symmetrical permission to kill, symmetrical liability to be killed, symmetrical legal standing under international law, epistemic indistinguishability of just and unjust fighters on the battlefield, and instrumental reciprocity needed to sustain the laws of war—and for each say whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or actually false? Then tell me which of these readings, if any, genuinely supports his objection to cause-sensitive algorithmic targeting, and which readings would actually permit or even require such weighting if the system's information environment were sufficiently reliable.

To keep the exercise from collapsing into pure taxonomy, test each reading against two niche modifications that rarely appear together in standard treatments: first, a 'proportional liability gradient' according to which unjust combatants retain a diminished but non-zero right not to be killed, rather than full equality or total forfeiture; second, a 'structural asymmetry' scenario in which one party is a non-state actor lacking the institutional prerequisites that the tradition treats as necessary for combatant privilege. For each disambiguated reading of moral equality, say whether it collapses under these modifications, survives in modified form, or was never applicable outside a state-centric framework to begin with. Finally, identify the further case—perhaps a soldier who knowingly volunteers for an aggressor state versus one coerced into service under credible threat—that would best distinguish the epistemic-reading from the permission-reading, and explain what substantive principle someone who accepted your best-guess reading would actually be adopting if they insisted on applying it to the governance of autonomous weapons.
