I'm putting together a discussion for a philosophy and literature class on Ursula Le Guin's story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas — the one about the utopian city that depends on keeping a single child in misery. I want to read it through the three concepts of liberty — negative, positive, and republican — particlarly the republican idea (from Pettit and others) that freedom is absence of domination, not just absence of interference. But I'm tired of the standard "the citizens are free to leave so it's negative liberty" reading. Instead, I want to push on whether the story dramatizes a kind of collective domination that republican theory struggles to capture: the citizens aren't individually dominating the child, but they maintain a structure that does, and they stay even though they could walk away. Does that make them unfree in a republican sense — are they living under arbitrary power they've collectively sustained — or does the exit option mean the power over them isn't arbitrary? And what about the ones who leave: are they reclaiming republican freedom, or just exercising negative liberty?

I'd like you to take both the story and the philosophy seriously. Point to specific moments — the description of the child in the cellar, the citizens' rationalizations, the ones walking away at the end — and say where the liberty framework illuminates them and where the story resists being reduced to an illustation of Berlin or Pettit. I'm especially curious whether Le Guin adds something about complicity and collective responsability that the abstract theories miss, or whether the story actually undermines the idea that freedom can be analyzed at the individual level at all. Don't just summarize the theories; treat the text as doing philosophical work on its own.
