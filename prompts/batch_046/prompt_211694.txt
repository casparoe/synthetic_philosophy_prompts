I serve on the university’s general-education curriculum committee, and we must decide whether to retain the required module on actual infinities—Hilbert’s hotel, Cantor’s diagonal argument, and basic cardinal arithmetic—in our quantitative-reasoning course. A faction of mathematics and philosophy faculty, drawing on a finitist line that treats mathematical existence as requiring determinable surveyability or explicit constructibility, argues that presenting Cantorian results as settled fact commits students to accepting actually infinite totalities they cannot in principle enumerate or verify; they propose replacing the module with an analysis of potential infinity in the Aristotelian tradition and its role in calculus limits. The opposing faction holds that omitting these results misrepresents modern mathematics and leaves students unable to grasp basic analysis or computational complexity arguments that depend on cardinal comparison.

Draft the committee’s formal decision memo—recommending retention, removal, or conditional retention with specific curricular adjustments—addressed to the dean of undergraduate studies. The memo should state a concrete, actionable recommendation with a clear rationale, engage seriously with the finitist worry that actual infinity is not merely counterintuitive but conceptually illegitimate because it violates the principle that a collection must be determinately surveyable to count as mathematically real, and treat honestly the strongest consideration on the losing side. In addition, state explicitly what would change your recommendation: identify the specific argument or pedagogical evidence that, if shown sound or unsound, would reverse your verdict, and point out where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
