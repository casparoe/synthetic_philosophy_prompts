I was arguing with my roommate about whether the fall of Rome is actually useful for thinking about modern democracy, and I think we're mixing up two different things but can't quite articulate why. There's the formal side — consuls, senates, tribunician veto — and then there's the social side, where power actually moved through clientela bonds and military loyalty rather than legal procedure. When people say Rome fell because norms eroded, they seem to treat those clientela networks as a corruption of the formal system, but maybe they were the real operating system and the constitutional rules were just a legitimizing shell?

I want someone to explain the conceptual issue here: is there a coherent way to distinguish institutional decay from institutional transformation when the informal networks don't disappear but just shift thier relationship to formal authority? Like, if modern lobbying and partisan patronage are structurally similar to Roman clientela but operate inside legal frameworks, does calling both corruption commit a category error? I'm not asking for a history lesson or citations, just the conceptual machinery to seperate structural drift — where the rules stay on the books but become irrelevant — from normative drift, where the rules themselves get rewritten. I keep feeling like analogies to modern democracies fail precisely because they assume there's a single republic concept being corrupted, rather than two parallel architectures that were always in tension.
