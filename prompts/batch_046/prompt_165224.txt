You are a professional philosopher of history with deep familiarity with Fernand Braudel’s structural historiography, and you should treat this task with corresponding rigor. Before drafting anything, take a breath, slow down, and first brainstorm in brief outline form: distinguish geographical time (the slow rhythms of climate, terrain, and biological processes), social time (the medium-term cycles of economies, institutions, and cultural formations), and individual or event time (the rapid, surface-level occurrences of politics, biography, and discrete decisions). Note explicitly where practitioners most often conflate these scales, and identify one conceptual risk—call it the “structure fallacy,” in which rapid human agency is wrongly dissolved into inevitable geographic determinism, and its mirror, the “event fallacy,” in which a slow structural shift is wrongly attributed to a single contingent occurrence.

Your deliverable is a reusable decision procedure for a specific practitioner: a senior grant reviewer at an environmental heritage foundation who must screen proposed public-exhibition narratives about regional ecological change. The reviewer handles thirty proposals per month, has exactly twenty minutes per initial screen, possesses no specialized historiographical training beyond this procedure, and cannot consult external experts during the review. Every proposal must eventually be co-signed by both a historian and a community stakeholder, but the reviewer alone conducts the first-pass evaluation and must flag items for revision or rejection before they reach that stage.

Translate Braudel’s principle into an operational checklist that the reviewer can run in sequence. Specify, in order: the precise question to ask at each step; what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure issues a hard “revise” or “reject”; where it must hand the decision to the community stakeholder because the question is one of values, representation, or local identity rather than historical method; and which distinctions from the original theory must survive the simplification versus which can be dropped for usability. Be explicit about the cases your procedure cannot settle, and embed within the procedure itself one check that guards against the structure fallacy and one that guards against the event fallacy.

Then apply your procedure to three hypothetical proposals:
1. A proposal attributing a forty-year decline in alpine meadow biodiversity primarily to the signing of a single 1987 international trade agreement.
2. A proposal framing the same decline as an inevitable consequence of post-glacial soil chemistry and slope orientation, with no mention of recent land-use policy or subsidy structures.
3. A proposal arguing that both the geological substrate and recent subsidy reforms jointly shaped the decline, but recommending that the exhibit suppress all mention of the subsidies to avoid “politicizing” the science.

In your analysis of the third proposal, the procedure must yield an answer that the applicant will find unwelcome, and you must explain precisely why the procedure demands that answer despite the applicant’s preference. Throughout, write in a formal, polished tone appropriate to a methodological memo. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from any published text; paraphrase all ideas in your own words. Where you invoke Braudel’s categories, describe them precisely without relying on specific titles, publication dates, or page references that you are not fully certain of. Finally, conclude with a brief, honest statement of what the procedure can never do, no matter how refined.
