In my reporting on the revival of early-modern political thought, I have followed a scholarly debate about Spinoza’s democratic theory that seems to have reached a durable impasse. Readers of the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus generally agree that Spinoza treats democracy as the form of government closest to nature, since it expresses the collective power of the multitude; yet interpreters split sharply over what actually makes democracy function. One faction reads Spinoza as an epistemic democrat: democratic assemblies produce shared rational understandings—what Spinoza calls common notions—through collective deliberation, and good institutions are those that maximize this cognitive convergence. The opposing faction reads him as an affective theorist: democracy succeeds not because citizens reason together but because democratic structures channel and stabilize the passions—hope, fear, ambition—of the multitude, preventing any single person’s affective dominance from becoming tyranny. Both sides invoke the same metaphysical framework, including the conatus doctrine and the claim that natural right can never be fully transferred; neither has persuaded the other over years of exchange, and the disagreement now feels less like an advancing argument and more like two parallel monologues conducted with the same vocabulary.

My working hypothesis is that part of the stalemate is verbal, masked as substantive: the term reason does different work for each camp—one treats it as a cognitive standard democratic procedures must approximate, the other treats it as an affective equilibrium democratic institutions must maintain—while both claim fidelity to Spinoza’s text. But I also suspect something deeper lies hidden beneath the surface. I have been developing a distinction that neither side employs—between democracy as a procedure for aggregating common notions that already exist in citizens’ minds, and democracy as the very process through which common notions are ontologically constituted by collective interaction—and I wonder whether forcing this distinction into the debate would expose that the two camps are actually answering different questions, one about institutional design and one about metaphysical psychology, under the single heading of Spinoza’s view of democracy. Could the disagreement also turn on an unrecognized formal question about whether Spinoza’s monistic metaphysics of substance permits any genuine pluralism in democratic decision-making, or is the impasse instead a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether collective reason is even metaphysically possible?

If you were to diagnose this stalemate, I want you to say what kind of disagreement it really is—verbal, substantive about institutional requirements, metaphysical, or a case of two different inquiries sharing one name—and to support that diagnosis with what each side actually says about the multitude, common notions, and the role of affect in political life. I also want an honest assessment of what, if anything, would count as progress: would introducing a precise conceptual distinction between procedural and constitutive common notions break the deadlock; would a closer reading of Spinoza’s account of the passions in the Ethics show that one reading is textually untenable; or is there a hidden empirical or formal question—perhaps about whether collective decision-making can genuinely increase the power of the multitude in Spinoza’s specific sense—that neither side has recognized? And if the disagreement is genuinely intractable, I want to know why—whether the question is ill-posed given Spinoza’s metaphysics, or whether both sides are correct but describing different dimensions of the same democratic phenomenon.
