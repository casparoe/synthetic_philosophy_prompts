hey, I'm trying to pin down a pattern I keep running into and I don't know if it has a proper name. It's kind of adjescent to free-floating rationales — reasons that exist without anyone actually holding them — but I think it's more specifc.

Here's the thing: sometimes people talk as if a system or institution has its own reason for doing something, and then they slide that reason over onto individuals who are part of the system, even though the individuals don't share the system's goal and the system itself can't actually be motivated. Like, someone says the lab has a reason to preserve its data archives because future replicaton depends on it, and then they use that to argue that a specifc grad student ought to spend her weekend cataloging old notebooks, even though the grad student has no personal stake in replicaton and the lab isn't a person that can care. Or in ecology: the wetland has a reason to filter nitrogen, which then gets used to say that a particular property owner should maintain the marsh, as if the marsh's functional rationale is automatically a reason for the owner, without any bridge principle.

What I'm describing isn't just metaphorical talk about systems — people really do seem to be treating these rationales as genuinely normative, but they float free of any reasoner. They aren't anchored in a collective agent either, because the system isn't organized enough to have intentions. So is there an established concept for this exact slide — attributing a free-floating system-rationale and then treating it as directly binding on individuals? How's it different from just saying there are instituional reasons or that functional explanations can ground norms? And if there's no term, what would you call it?

Also, please say how confident you are in whatever identification or distinction you draw, and single out the point you're least sure of — I get the feeling I'm blurring together a few different mistakes and I want to know where the carving gets shakiest.
