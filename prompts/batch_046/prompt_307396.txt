"The dogmatism paradox is the problem that if rational agents must always be open to changing their minds, then they can never hold beliefs firmly; but if they hold beliefs firmly, they appear dogmatic and closed-minded. Epistemic humility means admitting that one could be wrong about any particular claim, which requires maintaining low evidential thresholds uniformly across all beliefs. Epistemic stability, by contrast, means protecting well-entrenched beliefs from being overturned by minor anomalies.

This tension is resolved by a hierarchical model of belief, along the lines of Quine's web of belief. In this model, beliefs form a network: peripheral observational claims sit at the edges and are loosely connected, while core logical and mathematical commitments sit at the center with dense interconnections. When conflicting evidence arises, rational agents revise the peripheral beliefs rather than the central ones, because altering the center would require widespread adjustments. Thus, humility operates at the periphery—where thresholds remain low—while stability operates at the core—where thresholds are high. The paradox dissolves because the two norms govern different regions of the web.

This situation is essentially the same as the preface paradox, in which an author believes each individual claim in her book is true while also believing that at least one is false. Both paradoxes involve a rational agent holding contradictory beliefs about the same subject matter. In the dogmatism case, the agent simultaneously asserts a core commitment and asserts that she should never revise it, which is a direct contradiction. Quine's hierarchical structure fixes this by showing that any belief is technically revisable—if we are willing to make enough other adjustments—so there is no contradiction and no need for an additional pragmatic rule about when to stop. The structure of the web alone determines the appropriate response to evidence."
