I am a novelist drafting a manuscript that turns on a variation of Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment—often called the "Where Am I?" puzzle. In my version, the protagonist's original brain is extracted and sustained in a vat, while a duplicate body, Hamlet, receives not a static copy but a continuously synchronized neural extension. The result is that both substrates share an unbroken stream of first-person memory, and neither can claim exclusive authorship over the thoughts that arise. I have been workshopping these chapters with a group of philosophers and fellow writers, and we have reached an impasse that precisely mirrors the long-standing philosophical debate. Half the group insists that the protagonist is "located" in the vat because that is where the original biological tissue resides; the other half insists he is located in Hamlet, on the grounds that that is where the continuous narrative of selfhood—the thread that makes a character recognizably himself—is being enacted in real time.

We all accept the same factual setup, agree that psychological continuity carries weight, and acknowledge that the duplicate body behaves identically. Yet nobody has moved in months. My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive metaphysical disagreement about personal identity at all, but a verbal or conceptual stall: the word "where" in "where am I" is being pulled between spatial coordinates anchored to tissue and what I might call "narrative center of gravity" anchored to the locus of enacted experience. Is that right, or is there a hidden formal or empirical disagreement—perhaps about what counts as a self-preserving causal chain, or about the semantics of indexical reference—doing the real work? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of interpretations, and an honest answer about whether anything—a new conceptual distinction, a clarification of first-person reference, or a thought experiment about branching narratives—could actually resolve it, or whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed.
