As a doctoral researcher in philosophy of language and cognitive science, I am attempting to specify precisely what a substantive thesis of linguistic relativity—understood not as the banal observation that vocabulary directs attention, but as the claim that grammatical and lexical structures systematically canalise non-linguistic cognition—would have to deliver in order to count as conceptually adequate and empirically non-vacuous. Write the list of desiderata that such a theory must meet. For each requirement, state the conceptual motivation and supply a concrete illustrative case or empirical constraint that shows why it matters (for example, the need to distinguish genuine representational canalisation from mere lexical availability, or the demand that effects survive in fluent bilinguals operating within a second language whose categorical boundaries differ). Mark clearly which criteria are genuinely non-negotiable for any non-trivial version of the thesis, and which are instead methodological preferences of particular neo-Whorfian or universalist camps. In particular, examine whether the joint requirements of (i) domain-generality across both perceptual discrimination and higher inferential tasks, (ii) resistance to rapid neuroplastic reversal in adult second-language acquisition, and (iii) independence from broader mechanisms of cultural transmission form a jointly unsatisfiable triad given current evidence on cognitive flexibility; if so, say which limb of the triad must be abandoned and what weaker, domain-specific form of the thesis survives. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing studies; I want to see which single requirement does the most exclusionary work and whether the list itself reveals that the strong thesis was never coherently formulated. Use British spelling and avoid bibliographic requests, since this is meant to be answered without external reference.
