I cover science, neuroscience, and legal psychology for a general-interest publication, and I need a reusable editorial procedure I can run in about twenty minutes whenever a study, legal filing, or technology report uses the language of seeing—claims that a patient, witness, or system "sees" an object. The procedure must be grounded in disjunctivism about perceptual experience and its response to the argument from illusion: specifically, the view that veridical perception and illusion or hallucination are not two instances of a shared mental kind defined by subjective phenomenal character, but fundamentally different states, with veridical perception being a direct, object-involving relation and illusion being a state that lacks that relation despite subjective similarity.

Please construct a step-by-step checklist with questions in fixed sequence, specifying for each what counts as a sufficient answer, where the path splits among classifying the claim as veridical perceptual report, non-veridical appearance report, or unclassifiable, and exactly where the procedure halts and requires human editorial judgment because the remaining issue is normative or policy-based rather than conceptual. The checklist must be honest about which steps are judgment calls disguised as rules, and it must be usable by an editor without philosophical training.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:
1. A clinical trial reporting that optogenetically treated patients with retinal degeneration "see" flashes of light, where the only evidence is subjective report and cortical activation, with no demonstrated object-tracking, spatial navigation, or environmental interaction.
2. A legal brief describing an eyewitness who "saw" a dark object in a poorly lit corridor, where defense counsel argues stress-induced hallucination and the prosecution treats subjective report as sufficient.
3. A computer-vision study stating that a neural network "sees" a tumor in a radiological scan.

For at least one case—preferably the clinical trial—the procedure should yield a classification that undermines a vivid, publication-ready headline in favor of a weaker, more cautious formulation, and you should flag explicitly that this is the unwelcome result. Also specify precisely what the procedure cannot settle: for example, whether appearance-reports should ever count as sufficient for clinical approval, legal conviction, or technological deployment, and whether the disjunctivist framework applies at all to non-conscious artificial systems.

Keep the procedure focused on the distinction between object-related success conditions and mere phenomenal match conditions, build in the hard cases of degraded perceptual environments and absence of behavioral verification, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific philosophical works by author and page number.
