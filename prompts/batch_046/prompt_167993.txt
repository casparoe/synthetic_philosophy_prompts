I am preparing a seminar defense of the claim that a single first-person "I" can be distributed across two physically separate human brains—without collapsing into mere intersubjective cooperation—if those brains are linked by a direct, bidirectional neural interface that achieves recursive, self-referential symbolic feedback loops operating within a sub-200-millisecond integration window. I take Douglas Hofstadter's view that the self is not a soul-substance but a strange loop of symbolic self-reference, and I argue that his own criteria do not require that loop to be confined to one cranial vault: if two brains share representations of themselves in a unified loop with sufficient bandwidth and latency, the pattern that is the "I" is one pattern, not two patterns in correspondence. The niche constraint I add is that cross-brain loops slower than that window fragment into sequential signaling rather than simultaneous self-reference, so the boundary is functional and temporal, not anatomical. My audience is a philosophy of mind and cognitive science reading group composed mainly of embodied-cognition theorists who treat personal identity as necessarily anchored to a single biological body and its sensorimotor contingencies, together with neuroscientists who accept distributed cognition in social groups but reject extending first-person phenomenology beyond individual skulls. They are familiar with Hofstadter's general framework but have not encountered my specific inter-brain extension or the temporal-integration boundary condition. I need a Q&A preparation sheet written entirely in continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if composing a long letter—ordered by how likely and damaging each objection will be, with each objection phrased exactly as these philosophers and neuroscientists would voice it, followed by a brief reply, an explicit note on where that reply is weakest, and a clear indication of what I should concede up front versus what I must hold. Please include the single reply that has to land for the proposal to survive, and identify the one question I am most hoping nobody asks—likely concerning whether destroying one brain destroys half of an "I" or whether the loop survives partial disconnection like a Ship of Theseus applied to distributed consciousness. Make sure you do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke theories or data so obscure that they could not be drawn from general training knowledge combined with the premises given here.
