I'm applying for a one-semester research fellowship in history and philosophy of science, and I need a concise proposal—around six thousand words, suitable for a workshop pitch or dissertation prospectus—on a question that sounds familiar but has, I think, been asked in the wrong register. The standard debate asks whether "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution" are real historical entities or later inventions. Critics point to the persistence of Aristotelian natural philosophy in university curricula through the 1650s, the transmission of Islamic astronomical instruments without a corresponding theoretical rupture, and the geographic variability of "Renaissance" culture outside Italian city-states; defenders reply that all macro-taxonomy involves selective abstraction and that these terms still capture genuine discontinuities in patronage, textual circulation, and conceptual vocabulary. Yet neither side articulates what success conditions a period-label is meant to meet. Is it tracking causal mechanisms, licensing inductive projections across contexts in something like a projectibility sense, or functioning purely as a pedagogical boundary-marker? Without that clarification, the stalemate looks less like a disagreement about historical ontology and more like a clash of unexplained explanatory priorities.

My approach is conceptual and comparative rather than archival. I propose to import a distinction familiar from philosophy of science—between categories that carve nature at the joints and practical kinds that earn their keep through predictive and explanatory utility—but apply it specifically to macro-historical taxonomy. Rather than asking whether periods "exist," I will ask what kind of work we want them to do, and then test whether "Scientific Revolution" or "Renaissance" can survive a projectibility check: if we apply the label to Jesuit astronomical practice in Ming-era China, or to Arabic manuscript traditions in the same European centuries, does it generate false predictions about conceptual content, or does it collapse into vagueness? I will examine three well-documented boundary cases—the continuity of alchemical practice across the supposed Renaissance/Scientific Revolution divide, the institutional restructuring of universities versus the continuity of scholastic logic, and the fact that "medieval" operates sometimes as chronology and sometimes as normative diagnosis—to map which criteria (causal rupture, conceptual incommensurability, institutional restructuring) each camp implicitly invokes. The goal is not to defend or attack the terms wholesale, but to show that critics and defenders weight these dimensions differently, so that once the dimensions are separated, objections like "the Renaissance doesn't apply outside Italy" may succeed against one criterion while failing against another.

A successful deliverable would be a framework that distinguishes at least three independent dimensions of periodization and demonstrates that separating them dissolves part of the stalemate while sharpening the remainder into a tractable question about explanatory priorities. The project fails if the dimensions turn out to be empirically inseparable—if causal continuity always accompanies conceptual and institutional continuity—or if the framework reveals the entire debate to be purely terminological, leaving no substantive philosophical work. Given the reliance on established secondary literature and the absence of archival research, one semester is adequate, though I will restrict the scope to Western Europe and the early modern sciences to avoid geographic overreach. I will compile citations and references after the framework is finalized; this proposal states the conceptual architecture only.
