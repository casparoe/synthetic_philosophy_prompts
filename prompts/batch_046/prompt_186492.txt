Compare the position I will call "mnemonic sovereignty" — the claim that a community's sustained collective memory should hold defeasible epistemic authority over official archival records when the two conflict, on the grounds that lived social experience and moral orientation are encoded in memory in ways that documentary procedures necessarily flatten or exclude — with "archival realism" — the claim that intersubjectively corroborated documentary evidence should always override collective memory, since memory is systematically reconstructive and vulnerable to motivated distortion, whereas archives, though partial, remain open to verification and correction. Do not treat this as a generic subjectivity-versus-objectivity dispute. Instead, examine three specific divergences: first, how each view treats cases in which the archival record itself was produced by the very power structures that the community remembers resisting; second, whether the social-function value of collective memory — for instance, maintaining group identity or sustaining moral continuity across generations — can provide a non-epistemic reason to privilege it even when it is factually inaccurate; and third, whether there is a meaningful distinction between an archival silence and an archival contradiction, and whether mnemonic sovereignty applies with different force in each case. Conclude by identifying the consideration that most sharply separates the two views, and flag one argument that is commonly invoked to distinguish them but actually collapses upon closer inspection.
