Exam question: A decision theorist frames Pascal's mugging as follows. A stranger threatens that, unless you surrender your wallet (value ≈ $50), they will cause 3↑↑↑ people to suffer. You assign prior probability ε = 10⁻¹⁰⁰ to this claim being literally true, with linear utility in lives saved or ruined (each life weighted equally). (a) Explain conceptually why expected-utility maximization recommends compliance. (b) Explain why this result is usually treated as a reductio of combining unbounded utility with symmetric probabilistic respect for all logical possibilities, rather than as a straightforward practical instruction. (c) Contrast the bounded-utility remedy with the remedy of imposing a hard prior cutoff δ below which probabilities are ignored. Are the two strategies structurally equivalent? (d) Briefly distinguish this problem from the St. Petersburg paradox, specifying precisely how the expectation dominates or diverges in each case. (e) Does Savage's sure-thing principle have direct bearing on whether an agent should condition on the mugger's claim?

Student answer:
(a) The expected disutility of refusal is ε multiplied by 3↑↑↑. Even with ε as small as 10⁻¹⁰⁰, the product dwarfs the certain $50 loss, so EU mandates handing over the wallet.

(b) This is a reductio because, if applied consistently, every outlandish hypothetical catastrophe weighted by a nonzero prior would paralyze action. The problem is traced to allowing utility to scale without bound while treating all logically possible worlds with symmetric probabilistic weight.

(c) Bounded utility caps the value of saving lives at a ceiling U_max, so 3↑↑↑ lives are not valued more than, say, 10⁹ lives. A prior cutoff δ discards worlds with p < δ. These are structurally equivalent: both replace an unbounded quantity with a constant, and both violate the continuity axiom in Savage's framework, so they stand or fall together.

(d) In St. Petersburg, payoffs grow exponentially (2ⁿ) while probabilities decay exponentially (1/2ⁿ), yielding an infinite expectation through an infinite series. In Pascal's mugging there is only a single outcome with a fixed enormous payoff and an independently assigned tiny probability; dominance comes from one linearly scaled term overwhelming the certain loss, not from an infinite sum.

(e) Yes. The sure-thing principle requires that preferences between acts depend only on outcomes in states where the acts differ. Here, complying and refusing differ only in the state where the mugger is truthful. By refusing, one treats that state as null—ignoring it—which violates the principle. Savage linked null events to failures of conditioning, so refusing is inconsistent with subjective expected utility altogether.
