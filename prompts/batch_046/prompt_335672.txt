I just spent several hours arguing with a close friend about whether people on the opposing side of our current political polarization genuinely reason in good faith but are trapped in structurally different information environments, or whether they systematically engage in motivated reasoning and strategic bad faith. We kept circling: I pointed to the internal coherence of their arguments and the consistency with which they apply evidential standards within their own framework, suggesting an echo-chamber effect rather than dishonesty; my friend countered that coherence within a closed system is exactly what rationalization looks like, and that the refusal to engage with outside counterevidence is itself a failure of good faith. The public and academic discourse seems locked in the same loop—every study on filter bubbles is met by a study on partisan motivated cognition, and neither camp concedes ground. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two different questions being pursued under the single heading of good faith? Are the sides divided about empirical psychology, about normative criteria for attributing rational agency, or about the institutional architecture of information flow—and do they even share a view of what would count as resolving evidence? I have a working hypothesis that the stall hides what we might call a meta-normative divergence: each side implicitly operates with a different standard for what counts as a legitimate move in public reasoning, so that one side reads refusal to update as epistemic closure while the other reads it as principled resistance to illegitimate framing. On this view, they are not disagreeing about the same mental states but about which norms govern the attribution of those states. Evaluate that hypothesis by engaging with what each side actually says and does in practice. Is there a hidden formal or empirical question—perhaps about the conditions under which belief revision is voluntary versus structurally compelled—that is doing all the work? Or is the question ill-posed because good faith is being used simultaneously as a psychological description and as a moral evaluation without distinction? Finally, tell me what progress would actually look like here: a conceptual distinction that separates the descriptive from the normative, an empirical result that could genuinely adjudicate the psychology, a reformulation of the question that dissolves the standoff, or a reasoned argument that no progress should be expected and why.
