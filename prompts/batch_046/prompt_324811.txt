Consider the claim that a single self or “I” can be distributed across two physically separate brains, in the spirit of Douglas Hofstadter’s view that personal identity is not a substance anchored to one skull but a self-referential representational pattern—what he calls a strange loop—that could in principle span substrates. To make the claim precise: imagine two human brains linked by a direct, high-bandwidth cortical bridge that continuously synchronizes their autobiographical-memory buffers and self-modeling networks, so that neither brain can identify which memories originated in which skull, first-person reports are generated jointly, and volitional control is symmetrically distributed with no privileged “owner.” Under these conditions, is there genuinely one distributed “I,” or are there merely two selves with perfect mutual transparency? Give me your credence that there is one distributed self, decomposed as follows: (1) the prior that personal identity is constituted by a functional pattern of this kind rather than by spatiotemporal continuity of a single biological brain; (2) the conditional probability that such a pattern can maintain its unity across two brains given the synchronization conditions described, assuming (1); (3) the probability that symmetric joint control and indistinguishable memory access are sufficient for unity rather than only for extreme empathy or coupling; and any other factor you think belongs, each as a number between 0 and 1 with a one-line justification. Then combine them into an overall credence. Identify which term is your weakest link and specify what empirical finding, conceptual argument, or phenomenological report would shift it the most. End with your final credence in the format \answer{0.XX}.
