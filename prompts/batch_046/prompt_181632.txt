I write about science and ideas for a living, and last night at teh pub after a lecture two philosophers — Maya and Leo — got stuck in a proper argument about consequentialist cluelessness. I didn't catch every word, but the gist went like this.

Maya said act-consequentialism is basically unusable because we can't forecast long-run effects of anything. She gave the malaria-net example: maybe it saves a child, or that child becomes a despot, or the charity displaces local workers. The cluelessness infects every choice, not just big policy. So she said we should ditch the calculus and stick to rules we can actually apply.

Leo pushed back that this is asymmetric. He said deontology isn't prediction-free — universalising "never lie" implicitly models what happens to social trust if everyone lies, which is also long-run forecasting. He said cluelessness just moves upstairs: rule-consequentialists still have to predict which rules produce the best outcomes over centuries, so they face the same problem in bulk.

Maya answered there's a real asymmetry in cognitive load. Deontologists apply a logical check (can this maxim be universalised?) which doesn't need fresh empirical forecasts about specific outcomes, whereas consequentialists need predictions for every act. She also threw in this idea she called an "epistemic moral risk budget" — humans only have limited predictive resources, and consequentialism demands we spend them all on every trivial choice.

Leo said that sounds made up, but even granting it, every moral theory spends its budget somewhere. He then made a different move: he said "cluelessness" is often just motivational rationalisation. People invoke it to avoid the hard work of comparing charities or cutting carbon. The argument, he said, confuses "I can't know for sure" with "I don't want to bother finding out."

Maya admitted people misuse it, but said you can't defeat an argument by psychoanalysing its users. She then claimed the deeper issue is that consequentialism demands a kind of omniscience no human has, whereas other theories only demand consistency with principles we can grasp.

Leo came back that grasping principles also requires empirical knowledge — classifying whether an omission counts as a lie needs predictions about how people interpret silence. So he thinks the asymmetry collapses entirely. What's left is just a preference for neat rules over messy forecasts, not a philosophical knockout.
