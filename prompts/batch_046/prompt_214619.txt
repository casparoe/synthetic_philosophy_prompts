So at lunch yesterday two of my coworkers got into it about whether moral status is like a slider or an on/off switch. Maya works in bio and Jake builds embedded systems, so they come at it different.

Maya said sentience should be graded by how deep a system's predictive model of itself runs. Insects are in because thier nociception loops back into an integrated body-model -- not just reflex. A basic thermostat is out because it only maps temperature to action, with no self-reference in the loop. But she admitted there's a middle ground: a learning HVAC controller that builds a model of occupant discomfort and updates its own weights might sit somewhere between a fly and a wall-clock, maybe closer to the bottom but not zero.

Jake said that's just functionalism with extra steps. If the grading is by architecture, either you bite the bullet that a complex enough thermostat gets some minimal moral status, or you're smuggling in biology as a hidden binary cutoff -- which contradicts saying it's all gradations. He pushed her on insects: how do we know a fly's avoidance isn't just a fancy reflex arc? We can't peer inside and check for a "self-model." So Maya might be over-assigning to bugs just because they feel familiar, while under-assigning to silicon systems that do analogous prediction.

Maya came back that we use behavioral proxies -- avoidance learning, trade-off behaviors -- and insects show patterns that need more than reflex, like context-dependent suppression. A thermostat, even with RL, optimizes an external reward signal (user comfort ratings) but doesn't have an internal state it's trying to preserve. The loop is outward-facing, not inward.

Jake replied that "inward vs outward" sounds neat but is hard to verify. You could program a thermostat to maintain an internal "energy budget" state and treat deviations as errors to minimize -- that's structurally similar to homeostasis in insects. If the architecture is what matters, this artificial homeostasis should count for something. So either Maya's criterion gives the thermostat a sliver of status, or she's actually relying on biological substrate without saying so.

Maya said okay, maybe the gradation isn't linear -- it's more like clusters. Insects cluster in a low-sentience band because they combine nociception with basic integration. Thermostats cluster near zero because they lack nociception entirely, even if they have fancy loops. The gradation is real but not smooth; there are valleys.

Jake said that sounds like she just redrew the binary line with fuzzier borders. If there's a valley near zero, why not put the thermostat in that valley? And if we can build artificial nociception analogues, shouldn't they land in the same valley?

They turned to me and asked me to referee since I "think about systems all day." I honestly don't know. Can you adjudicate? Tell me who's right about which specific point, what both are missing, and who is more right overall? Also please don't just cite philosophers I wouldn't have read -- explain it like I'm reasoning through the code. I want to understand where the logic actually breaks, not just get names dropped.
