I am preparing remarks for a graduate seminar in metaethics and philosophy of language on the Frege-Geach problem as it applies specifically to moral expressivism. A classmate circulated the following summary, drawn from what they called a standard introductory source, and I want to verify its accuracy before I build on it. The summary states that expressivism—associated with thinkers such as Ayer and Blackburn—holds that a moral sentence like "Torture is wrong" does not describe a moral fact but instead expresses the speaker's non-cognitive attitude of disapproval. The Frege-Geach problem, originally formulated by Frege in his logical writings on assertion and then applied by Geach, shows that this view cannot accommodate embedded occurrences: in a conditional such as "If torture is wrong, then teaching torture techniques is wrong," the antecedent clause "torture is wrong" is not asserted and therefore does not express disapproval, yet it plainly retains its moral meaning and participates in the logical validity of the inference. Because expressivism assigns meaning through attitude-expression, it lacks the resources to explain how the same clause can function identically in both asserted and unasserted contexts; consequently, it must either treat embedded clauses as expressing different mental states—thereby violating compositionality—or concede that they operate descriptively. The summary then claims that quasi-realist strategies, which preserve logical structure by treating embedded moral claims as expressing conditional or higher-order attitudes, effectively collapse back into moral descriptivism, since they allow moral discourse to enter into genuine inferential relations in precisely the way expressivism originally denied was possible.

This sounds largely correct, but I suspect it conflates Frege's original contribution with Geach's extension, overstates the dialectical force against quasi-realism, and perhaps presents a false dichotomy between compositionality and descriptivism. Separately, I have been developing a formal framing—outside the standard literature—in which embedded moral claims are treated not as attitude-reports but as modal operators over a preference-ordering space; I am unsure whether such a formalization genuinely resolves the embedding problem or merely relocates it into the semantics of the ordering relation. Could you audit the summary with care?
