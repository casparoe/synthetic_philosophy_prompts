I've been retired from marine biology for about five years and lately I've gotten deep into philosophy of social science, mostly because my old labmates keep saying famine is just nature's check on overpopulation. I'm trying to wrap my head around Amartya Sen's claim that famines are basically political crimes — he talks about the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish potato famine, and the Great Leap Forward in China, arguing that in each case food was available or producible but people died because of entitlement failures, not absolute scarcity. To understand where he is coming from I went back to Thomas Malthus's 1798 Essay on the Principle of Population, where he lays out the idea that human populations grow in a geometrical ratio while food supply only grows in an arithmetical ratio, so eventually famine, disease, and war act as necessary positive checks that keep numbers in line with subsistence. He treats famine as an automatic ecological feedback loop rather than a policy failure.

What I'm stuck on is whether Sen's entitlement analysis actually refutes Malthus or just relocates the cause. In Bengal there was food in the markets but laborers lost wages, so exchange entitlements collapsed; in Ireland the blight destroyed the crop but export policies kept food leaving the country; in China during the Great Leap Forward production genuinely collapsed but distribution was also mismanaged. Does this mean Malthus is wrong about there being any underlying ecological ceiling, or does it only show that the social mechanism of the check can be politically mediated? I'm also wondering if there's a hidden ecological analogy here — like, if democratic accountability is removed, does entitlement collapse propagate downward through a society the way a trophic cascade works when you remove a top predator? I feel like I'm mixing metaphors badly. Can you help me understand whether Malthus's passage describes a real biological law that Sen ignores, or whether Sen's political framing makes the whole idea of a natural check incoherent? And if I extend this to modern algorithmic supply chains — where food exists but routing algorithms fail to deliver it to vulnerable populations — am I applying Sen correctly, or inventing a problem neither thinker would recognize? I'm definately out of my depth here.
