I have recently become personally preoccupied with the ethical status of long-horizon reinforcement-learning systems, as I am now contributing to a research initiative that shapes reward functions for autonomous agents operating over extended timescales. In the discussions that surround this work, a particular analogy is repeatedly deployed to allay concerns about artificial suffering: the reward signal received by an RL agent is said to be strictly comparable to the satisfaction of hunger in a non-human animal — a bare homeostatic feedback loop that modifies behaviour without any accompanying experiential quality, so that attributing valence to a machine is as mistaken as attributing pleasure to a thermostat.

I should like you to audit this comparison with some care. Please set out the explicit correspondences — identifying what plays the role of the drive state, the satiety response, the homeostatic set-point, and the behavioural update — and then indicate precisely where the mapping fractures, particularly if one admits architectures that maintain an internal world-model capable of computing counterfactual expected rewards independently of the signal actually received. I am especially keen to know whether the conclusion that reward signals necessarily lack phenomenal character survives once these disanalogies are laid bare, or whether the analogy was carrying the dismissal all along. Might a different comparison — perhaps to human affective forecasting, in which anticipated outcomes bear emotional weight prior to their occurrence — offer a more defensible framework for thinking about artificial valence?
