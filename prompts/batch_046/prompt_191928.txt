I've been working through Benacerraf's dilemma in a study group that tends to read a lot of rationalist blogs, so naturally the conversation drifted into whether platonism can be saved by treating mathematical cognition as direct intuition of abstract structures. One member kept defending a Gödel-style position by insisting that mathematical intuition is essentially analogous to sensory perception: just as the eye detects photons and the brain constructs a spatial model of physical objects, the mind detects abstract relations and constructs knowledge of numbers and sets. He used this analogy to argue that Benacerraf's causal-access objection misses the point — we don't need physical interaction with mathematical objects any more than we need to touch photons to see them, because intuition is its own direct faculty of access, and therefore the lack of causal chains between us and numbers is no more damaging than the lack of tactile contact with distant stars. He even pushed the comparison further by likening mathematical errors to phantom-limb sensations — apparently real to the subject but lacking an external referent — and argued that just as phantom limbs don't disprove the reality of the body, inconsistencies in a formal system don't disprove the reality of the structures it describes.

I'm skeptical that the analogy can carry the weight he places on it, and I'd like you to audit it systematically. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of photons, retinas, illusions, intersubjective verification, evolutionary adaptation, and error-correction mechanisms in the mathematical case, and specifically how the phantom-limb comparison maps onto formal mistakes? Second, identify where the mapping breaks down — particularly around error-correction, since perceptual illusions are typically corrected by cross-modal feedback and physical interaction, whereas mathematical error seems to be corrected by proof, consistency checks, and peer review rather than by any causal feedback loop with the objects themselves. Third, note which features of sensory perception the analogy quietly imports (spatial localization, temporal dynamics, survival-oriented calibration) that mathematical cognition may entirely lack, and ask whether importing those features makes the analogy misleading rather than illuminating. Fourth, assess whether the intended conclusion — that Benacerraf's dilemma is dissolved once we recognize intuition as a faculty like sight, and that mathematical error is harmless like phantom sensation — actually survives these disanalogies, or whether it collapses into a question-begging assumption that abstract objects are just "there" to be detected. Finally, if you think there is a better analogy for mathematical knowledge — perhaps comparing it to language acquisition, to navigating an internal model by dead reckoning, or to interpreting the rules of an abstract game — present it and explain precisely what it gains and loses relative to the perceptual model.
