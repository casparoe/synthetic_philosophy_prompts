I am trying to determine whether self-deception is genuinely possible given the reflexivity problem—if I intentionally cause myself to hold a false belief, I seem to already know it is false, which undermines the deception; if I do not know, the intention is absent. Despite this, my verdicts on concrete cases pull in different directions, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture them or whether some must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: The filtered feed. A political commentator subscribes only to outlets confirming her view, blocks dissenting voices, and when confronted with well-documented counterevidence, immediately classifies the source as unreliable without examining the documentation. She sincerely insists she is just being careful about bias. Verdict: this is self-deception.

Case 2: The outsourced audit. A CEO suspects financial irregularities but hires an internal auditor with instructions to report only if fraud exceeds a very high evidentiary threshold. The audit returns negative; the CEO genuinely feels relieved and tells the board there is no credible evidence of fraud. Verdict: this is self-deception, even though he never formed the belief that there is fraud—he engineered the conditions under which that belief could not form, and the relief is manufactured.

Case 3: The tired chess player. A competitive chess player consistently blunders in endgames. He tells himself and others he is just tired or not focused, attributing the pattern to transient states. He has access to game statistics showing the blunders are systematic, but he never looks at them. Verdict: this is not self-deception; it is rationalization or self-serving attribution, but there is no false belief about a specific fact that he hides from himself.

Case 4: The dissociative diary. A person writes about a traumatic event in the third person, describing a woman who was attacked, then years later reads the entry and experiences it as a story about a stranger. She does not recognize it as her own memory, and when told, she feels detached surprise rather than denial. Verdict: this is not self-deception; it is a failure of memory integration or dissociation, lacking the intentional structure that deception requires.

Case 5: The hidden confound. A researcher designs a study with a confounding variable she knows could explain the result, runs the study, gets the predicted outcome, and publishes it without mentioning the confound. She genuinely believes the result supports her theory because she has stopped thinking about the confound; she is not lying to anyone, including herself. Verdict: this is self-deception, specifically because she actively suppressed a piece of her own knowledge and allowed her belief-formation process to proceed as if that knowledge were absent.

These verdicts seem to require that self-deception involves not merely false belief or motivated error, but something about the agent’s relationship to her own evidential environment—what I am inclined to call evidential gatekeeping—where the agent restructures access rather than merely misinterpreting content. Yet Case 3 also involves avoiding statistics (gatekeeping) but I deny it is self-deception, while Case 2 involves gatekeeping and I affirm it. Meanwhile, Case 5 involves suppressing knowledge without restructuring external access, and I affirm it, while Case 4 involves restructuring memory access but I deny it.

So: is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the combination of intentional evidential restructuring, present awareness of the restructuring, and a specific false belief that results—that captures all five? Or must I drop one? In particular, does the principle distinguish between gatekeeping that targets a specific threatening belief (Cases 1, 2, 5) and gatekeeping that targets only explanatory patterns (Case 3)? And does it require that the suppressed knowledge be currently accessible but actively isolated (Case 5 yes, Case 4 no)?

Please state the principle precisely, identify the clause that handles each case, predict the verdict for a further case I have not considered—say, a patient who asks her doctor not to tell her test results unless they are definitely bad, then interprets the doctor’s silence as good news and genuinely feels safe—and be explicit about which of my five verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and must be revised.

Also, please say what would change your mind: which feature of one of these cases, or what kind of counterexample, would lead you to reject the principle you propose, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail—particularly around the distinction between intentional restructuring and mere cognitive limitation, or around whether dissociation can ever involve the kind of reflexive awareness that deception demands.
