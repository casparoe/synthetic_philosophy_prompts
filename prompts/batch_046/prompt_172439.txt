You are a philosopher of cognition with particular familiarity with conceptual-space theories of analogy and creativity. Please begin with brief brainstorming of the distinctions between parametric variation, structural inversion, and normative constraint. I have been trying to formalize an objection to Hofstadter's knob-twiddling picture — the view that creative analogy works by sliding parameters within a conceptual space while preserving a deep relational theme. The argument runs as follows.

(1) If creativity is essentially knob-twiddling, then any surface adjustment that leaves the deep theme intact should count as a genuinely novel creation.
(2) But without a principled boundary, random alterations of every feature would also qualify, which is absurd; so the theme itself must carry implicit norms about which knobs may be turned.
(3) Once those norms are internal to the theme, the parameters are not freely adjustable but are constrained by the very structure they are meant to vary.
(4) The model therefore collapses into tautology: a variation succeeds precisely when it respects constraints that already define the original concept, explaining nothing about why some adjustments produce recognized creativity and others produce only noise.
(5) This problem is sharpened by what we might call negative knobs — systematic inversions of direction or valence that preserve formal relational structure but flip its content. Under the model, these should be extreme but valid points in the space; in practice they yield parody or degradation.
(6) So either the framework cannot distinguish creative variation from mechanical constraint, or it must import an unexplained normative criterion that determines permissible sliding.
(7) Consequently, knob-twiddling cannot be the crux of creativity without smuggling in the very judgment it claims to explain. Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost?
