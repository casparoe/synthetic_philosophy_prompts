At a recent colloquium on migration ethics, two colleagues argued about whether auctioning expedited residency permits—using the revenue to fund integration services for refugees—constitutes a repugnant market or a permissible welfare-state instrument. The first claimed that residency rights, like bodily organs or democratic votes, are status goods whose allocation by price corrupts the equal standing required for civic membership; the welfare gains, she insisted, do not redeem the corruption. The second replied that this assumes an incommensurability thesis that needs independent defense—if the auction maximizes welfare and the queue-based alternative inflicts severe deprivation, labeling it "repugnant" merely encodes a conservative intuition. The first responded that the corruption is structural, not intuitive: once entry is priced, the social meaning of citizenship shifts, so beneficial outcomes cannot cleanse the practice. The second countered that this "social meaning" argument overreaches, since need-based tuition and scholarship lotteries also use price-sensitive mechanisms without corrupting education. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: rate each move, indicate who is right about what, and identify what both parties miss—specifically, whether there is a principled distinction between permissible price mechanisms and genuinely repugnant ones, or whether repugnance in this context is irreducibly judgment-sensitive.
