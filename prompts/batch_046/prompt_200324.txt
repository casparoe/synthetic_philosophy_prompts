I'm a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and in three weeks I'm presenting to an interdisciplinary working group on AI governance—mostly analytic moral philosophers (including committed Kantians and virtue ethicists) plus a couple of institutional economists and several effective-altruist researchers. My thesis is that when an AI system must serve stakeholders holding genuinely incompatible moral frameworks—say, one treating informational privacy as an inviolable deontological constraint and another treating radical transparency as a non-negotiable public good—we should abandon the search for a single synthesized "aligned" value hierarchy, and also reject simple majority-rule or procedural-neutrality defaults. Instead, I defend a framework of structured moral compromise modeled on comparative advantage: within any coherent framework, goods are ordered by marginal importance rather than treated as uniformly lexical; by precisely mapping these internal orderings, we can design systems that ask each framework to concede on goods it weights lightly—those near its margin—while securing stronger institutional protection for goods it weights heavily. The result is not fungible exchange but a Pareto-improving arrangement that each side prefers to unilateral imposition, analogous to gains from trade without reducing values to commodities.

The audience will push back in distinct registers. The philosophers will likely argue that moral values are incommensurable, that lexical ordering makes marginal trade impossible, or that any compromise corrupts the integrity of the framework; the economists will press on measurability and on whether this is just masked utilitarian aggregation; the effective altruists will worry that framing compromise as optimal conceals power asymmetries and neglects scale-sensitive obligations to distant suffering. I need a prep sheet ordered by how damaging each objection is, phrased exactly as each camp would phrase it: what to concede, what to hold firm, and where each reply is soft. Crucially, include the objection I'm most afraid of—probably that my framework requires quantifying moral weights in a way that violates the very frameworks I'm trying to accommodate, or that it collapses into preference aggregation once we admit any good is "lightweight" for a given stakeholder. Also flag the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook: I have not fully specified what happens when a framework genuinely denies any internal ordering—that is, when every value is claimed as lexically protected—so I need a direct defense of whether such "pure lexical" frameworks are conceptually coherent or merely strategic postures. Keep each reply to roughly the length of a two-minute Q&A response, and flag where I'm relying on an analogy to economic trade that might break under philosophical scrutiny.
