I'm taking an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and last week a debate broke out among a few of us over whether dreams are genuinely experiential. The discussion kept returning to Norman Malcolm's skeptical argument that dream reports cannot be reports of experiences, since the sleeper lacks the conceptual and behavioral capacities required for experience-attribution, and to Daniel Dennett's opposing view that dream narratives, though partly confabulated upon waking, are organized around genuinely occurring phenomenal events. Two classmates in particular got stuck in a back-and-forth that I found hard to evaluate, and I would like you to adjudicate it move by move.

The first student, call her A, defended a broadly Malcolm-inspired line. She argued that recalling a dream upon waking is structurally unlike recalling a waking perception: there is no criterion for distinguishing, in sleep, between genuinely experiencing content and merely having neural activity that later gets interpreted as experience. She then introduced what she called the "phenomenal residue" test — the idea that genuine perceptual experiences typically leave a direct, non-inferential trace, like the lingering afterimage of a bright flash, and that since waking subjects never report any such residue specific to dream content, we lack evidence that an experience occurred. The second student, B, responded with a Dennett-style position: he granted that dream reports involve narrative reconstruction, but insisted that reconstruction requires some raw phenomenal material to organize, analogous to how waking perception interprets sensory input. He claimed that Malcolm's argument implicitly demands that experiences be accessible to a fully conscious, rational agent, which begs the question against the possibility of non-accessible phenomenal states. B also dismissed the residue test as irrelevant, noting that most waking perceptual details are immediately forgotten, so the absence of a lingering trace proves nothing about the original occurrence.

A then accused B of ontological smuggling: if B truly accepts Dennett's anti-Cartesian framework, he should deny that there is any "raw phenomenal material" prior to the narrative construction, not merely deny that it is globally available. B replied that this misreads Dennett — sub-personal processes can have phenomenal character without being globally broadcast, so there is no contradiction. At this point A proposed a criterion she named "structural transparency": for a state to count as experiential, the subject must be able, in principle, to discriminate being in that state from not being in it through the state's own internal structure, not through subsequent inference. She argued that dream states fail this because the sleeper cannot distinguish dream-content from reality-content in real time. B countered that transparency is too demanding: it would exclude subliminal perception and certain dissociative states that we still classify as experiential, and that requiring real-time discrimination confuses metaphysical conditions on experience with epistemic ones. They asked me to referee, but I genuinely do not know where each stands. Please go through their exchange step by step — A's initial Malcolm-style argument, her phenomenal-residue point, B's reconstruction-with-material reply, A's charge of smuggling, B's sub-personal defense, A's transparency criterion, and B's counterexample — and for each say which side makes the stronger point, what each is missing, whether there is a consistent middle ground, and whether the disagreement ultimately turns on a hidden ambiguity in "experience."
