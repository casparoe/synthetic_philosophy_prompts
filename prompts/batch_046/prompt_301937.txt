I'm a journalist covering philosophy and the science of belief, preparing a feature on why Hume's argument against miracles continues to generate conceptual confusion long after its original publication. I'd like to pose a precise conceptual question for the piece. Hume is typically read as defining a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, then arguing that because uniform human experience establishes these laws with overwhelming weight, any testimony claiming a miracle is always less credible than the hypothesis that the witnesses erred. But this reading seems to conflate an epistemic guideline about how to weigh testimony against background knowledge with a stronger metaphysical claim that the range of possible events is fixed in advance. Consider a hypothetical case: a localized, reproducible, and instrumentally verified suspension of gravitational attraction observed under rigorously controlled conditions, with no plausible instrumental error. Would Hume's framework require us to reject this as impossible on the grounds that it violates uniform experience, or would it allow us to treat it as an inductive revision that legitimately expands our sample of nature? If the latter, then doesn't Hume's argument against miracles depend on smuggling in the assumption that our current sample of experience is already exhaustive—making his rejection of miracle reports circular rather than genuinely probabilistic?
