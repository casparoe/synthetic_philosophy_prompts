I have been working through the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation with a study group, and one of my colleagues pushed it into what looks like a reductio. The argument below now strikes me as suspicious—if sound, it seems to prove that democratic collective reasoning is conceptually impossible not just at the first order but at every meta-level. I do not want a survey of possible responses; I want a committed diagnosis: identify the specific step that fails, defend that choice with reasons, and spell out precisely what giving it up costs us. I would especially like to preserve the consistency requirement for individual belief and the fact that scientific peer-review panels and constitutional conventions genuinely produce rational judgments.

(1) Any rational belief system—whether individual or collective—must be logically consistent and closed under entailment: if P and P→Q are believed, then Q must be believed.

(2) The standard discursive dilemma shows that for any group of three or more agents with individually consistent judgments, democratic premise-based aggregation can yield collective acceptance of P, collective acceptance of P→Q, and collective rejection of Q.

(3) So democratic premise-based aggregation systematically violates rational closure; it is therefore an irrational belief-forming procedure.

(4) The standard remedies—conclusion-based aggregation or hierarchical delegation—are not genuinely democratic: the first ignores members' reasoning and is framing-sensitive, while the second abandons equality.

(5) But the choice among aggregation rules is itself a collective judgment, and members can consistently hold different meta-views about which remedy is appropriate; applying the same democratic procedure at this meta-level reproduces the dilemma, yielding contradictory collective commitments about how to aggregate.

(6) Hence there is no democratically legitimate aggregation rule—at any order—that can guarantee logical consistency.

(7) Therefore, democratic collective judgment is conceptually impossible: no group can rationally reach collective beliefs through democratic means.

(8) Yet peer-review panels, jury deliberations, and multi-stakeholder climate committees are standardly treated as rational and democratically legitimate.

(9) So the argument must contain an error somewhere.

If I must choose, I want to keep (1) and (8). Is the failure an equivocation between individual and collective rationality, a scope shift in applying closure across meta-levels, or a hidden assumption that democratic legitimacy requires premise-responsiveness at every level? Take a definite position, explain why that step initially looked compelling, and tell me whether saving democratic rationality forces us to accept that groups can be rationally inconsistent in a way that no individual agent can be.
