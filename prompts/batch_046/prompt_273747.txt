Next month I defend my thesis chapter to an interdisciplinary workshop. My claim is that German folk morality’s emphasis on explicit rule-following and public order (Ordnung) is not authoritarian residue but a distributed cognitive scaffold: shared rules reduce moral rationalization by constraining private reinterpretation, whereas American folk morality’s privileging of individual conscience generates normative innovation at the cost of what I term “normative drift”—the gradual dissolution of shared standards through successive personal exemptions. I concede that conscience-based morality adapts faster to novel cases, but I maintain that it systematically undervalues the epistemic labor rules perform in preventing self-deception.

The audience is precisely composed of: two cultural anthropologists who study “tight” versus “loose” societies and will suspect my framing is either essentialist or redundant with existing dimensional work; three American moral philosophers with liberal individualist commitments who will read my defense of rule-following as an apology for conformity; and one German sociologist of Alltagsethik (everyday ethics) who will challenge whether I am romanticizing bureaucratic norm enforcement.

Give me the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased as each faction would actually voice them. For each, tell me what to concede, what to hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—specifically, that “normative drift” is not a descriptive mechanism but a conservative lament for lost authority disguised in sociological language—and advise me how to answer it if it comes, given the time available in a seminar defense.
