Since assuming guardianship for my uncle, whose advanced dementia has reduced his responsive capacities largely to reflexive stimuli, I have become preoccupied with whether moral status is genuinely gradated rather than binary. This concern became concrete during a recent discussion with two philosophically trained cousins, who disagreed sharply at our family ethics meeting. The first cousin argued that if moral status tracks sentience along a continuum, then even an artifact such as a thermostat—which maintains temperature through negative-feedback loops—must carry a non-zero, albeit negligible, moral weight; its homeostatic regulation is structurally analogous to the minimal goal-directed agency we attribute to insects, and denying it any status requires an arbitrary cutoff rather than a principled distinction. The second cousin countered that this conflates instrumental value with intrinsic moral status: a thermostat's ends are entirely derivative from its designer, whereas an insect's nociceptive and orienting behaviors are genuinely its own. She then pressed a sharper challenge: if the gradient is taken seriously, my uncle—who now integrates sensory information no more richly than a healthy honeybee, and arguably less, since bees coordinate collective decision-making through distributed signaling—should rank below that insect in moral standing. The first cousin replied that this objection treats moral status as a unidimensional score, which misrepresents the gradated view he defends; he proposed that moral standing is better conceived as a multidimensional vector comprising present cognitive capacity, historical narrative continuity, relational embeddedness, and potential for recovery. On this framework, my uncle retains substantial status not because his present cognitive profile exceeds an insect's, but because his identity is anchored in biographical continuity and in caring relationships that a thermostat entirely lacks and that an insect never possessed. They turned to me for adjudication, but I could not resolve whether the derivative-agency objection defeats the thermostat analogy, whether the uncle-bee comparison functions as a legitimate implication or a reductio, whether narrative and relational dimensions can ground moral status independently of present sentient capacity, or whether a gradated framework can avoid arbitrary boundaries without collapsing into an implausible pluralism in which every entity holds some incommensurable form of value. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess the thermostat analogy, the uncle-bee comparison, the multidimensional-vector defense, and the risk of arbitrary cutoff or pluralist collapse. For each move, indicate which party is more nearly correct and what both are overlooking—particularly whether a gradated account necessarily licenses counterintuitive rankings, or whether it can instead operate as a taxonomy in which different dimensions of moral status apply differently across biological, artifactual, and human entities.
