I'm working through the situationist literature for my moral psychology course and have settled on a somewhat specific reconstruction of the virtue-ethical response. Rather than defending virtue as first-order behavioral consistency across situations, I think it should be reconceived as a stable second-order capacity for moral attention-calibration: the reliable ability to identify which features of a given context are genuinely morally salient and to weight one's emotional and practical responses accordingly. On this view, the classic situationist findings—variability in helping behavior under time pressure, mood-dependent shifts in cooperation, or framing effects on moral judgment—do not undermine virtue but illustrate it, since a genuinely virtuous agent ought to help more when situational costs are low and less when they are genuinely high, rather than displaying flat-rate consistency regardless of context. The locus of virtue is therefore not uniform behavior but uniform competence in reading the moral structure of situations, even though that competence produces highly variable outputs.

What would actually count against this position? I am not asking for the standard situationist rebuttal that people lack cross-situational traits, since I take that to miss the reconstructed target. Instead, I want an honest map of its exposure. Which empirical patterns—say, in studies of attentional allocation, framing effects, or cognitive load—would show that this second-order calibration capacity itself is unstable or illusory? Would it be fatal if people turned out to identify radically different moral features in structurally identical situations depending on trivial contextual cues, or could the view accommodate that by restricting calibration to domain-specific modules? Which philosophical intuitions does the view risk violating: for instance, does reducing virtue to skilled situation-reading collapse it into moral particularism or into a model of ethical expertise that loses the distinctive normative force traditionally attached to character? Is there a formal or conceptual vulnerability—perhaps circularity in defining morally salient features without presupposing the very virtue being explained, or an unfalsifiability problem whereby any behavioral pattern can be reinterpreted post hoc as either good or poor calibration?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of this attention-calibration view—including myself—should concede that it genuinely counts against the position, or whether it can be dismissed as a misreading. In particular, is there any result from cognitive psychology or decision theory suggesting that attention-allocation is too fragmented or context-dependent to serve as a stable locus of virtue? And does the view risk being insulated to the point of emptiness: if virtue always explains away behavioral variability as calibrated responsiveness, what observation—if any—would actually force abandonment of the core idea rather than a local qualification? I would also like to know what the view predicts that rival accounts do not, and whether that predictive content exposes it to distinctive risks.
