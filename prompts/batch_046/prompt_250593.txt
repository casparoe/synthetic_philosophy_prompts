At a seminar on early modern economic thought, two graduate students, Maya and Jonas, argued about George Berkeley's monetary philosophy in The Querist. Maya claimed Berkeley should be read as an early functionalist: he treats money as a token whose value lies not in gold or silver but in its power to circulate labor and goods, and his proposal for an Irish paper currency backed by land rather than specie shows he understood that the material substrate is secondary to the social role of representing purchasing power. Jonas objected that this overstates Berkeley's modernity; for Berkeley the token must represent actual land and labor—value is anchored in tangible, God-ordered production, not in state decree—so he is closer to a real-bills theorist who insists on truthful representation than to a fiat functionalist. Maya replied that Berkeley's sustained attack on hoarding and his claim that money serves chiefly as a motive to labor reveal a proto-Keynesian concern with circulation velocity and aggregate activity rather than with static accumulation of metal. Jonas countered that this confuses a moral-theological point—hoarding withholds the fruits of industry from the community, violating divine providence—with macroeconomic demand management; Berkeley's immaterialist metaphysics makes value mental, but not conventional, since it remains fixed by God's sustaining order and human labor within it. Finally, Maya argued that if value is mental and not intrinsic to metal, then the choice between gold and paper is purely practical, confirming the functionalist core. Jonas responded that substrate arbitrariness does not entail value arbitrariness: Berkeley permits paper only when rigorously tied to land, precisely because false tokens are fraudulent misrepresentations, which is the opposite of modern central-bank fiat. They asked me to referee. Can you go through their exchange move by move, rate each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, say who is right about which specific point, and identify what both are missing—particularly whether either account adequately captures the theological and specifically Irish economic context of The Querist?
