I have come to accept a view in the neighborhood of Parfit's psychological-continuity account: what matters in survival is not the persistence of a particular body or an uninterrupted stream of consciousness, but psychological continuity sustained by the right kind of cause, even if that cause is artificial and discontinuous. I apply this specifically to a variant I will call the intermittent teletransporter: a scanner copies my complete psychological profile, the original body is destroyed, and reconstruction is then suspended for years in a dormant storage medium with no mental activity whatsoever, after which the profile resumes in a new substrate. I believe the reconstructed person is me and that my survival is preserved across the gap. Tell me what would count against this view — not just the standard fission objection, but which intuitions about temporal discontinuity, which analogies to the persistence of organisms, artifacts, or social institutions, and which consequences in adjacent domains (for instance, the metaphysics of processes, the ethics of killing, or the conditions for moral responsibility) it stands or falls with. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of psychological continuity with any reliable cause would concede it as damaging or dismiss it as relying on an unjustified attachment to bodily or spatiotemporal continuity. Finally, assess whether the view can be qualified — say, by tightening what counts as "the right kind of cause" — to the point of unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is itself a philosophical liability.
