Take the view that Nozick's side-constraint framework, if you apply it consistently to animals, lands you in a pretty awkward spot. He treats rights as constraints on action rather than goals to maximise, and he relies on the separateness of persons to block utilitarian aggregation for humans. But with animals it's murkier: either they don't count at all (which feels wrong) or they get full-blown rights that rule out farming or testing entirely (which also feels extreme). So I want to defend a kind of gradient version: the strength of the side-constraint scales with a being's capacity for narrative self-continuity — roughly, the ability to experience it's life as an ongoing story with a past and future. Pigs and crows probably have enough of it to trigger a strong constraint; insects and simple molluscs probably don't, not because suffering is irrelevant but because there's no self to violate. That means factory farming pigs is wrong as a rights-violation, but using nematodes in research isn't protected by the same constraint.

Play it straight: what would have to be true for this proportionaly graded view to collapse? Which bits of Nozick's original picture does it distort — does it smuggle in a kind of utilitarian weighting through the back door, or does it break the binary nature of rights he insists on? And what kind of evidence about animal cognition would actually hurt it: if pigs turn out to lack narrative continuity, or if some invertebrates turn out to have loads of it? I want an honest assessment of whether this is just a fudge, and whether Nozick's defenders would see it as a betrayal of the framework or a natural extension. Also, is there any consequence it's committed to that turns out unacceptable — say, that highly intelligent AI with narrative continuity gets full rights, or that human infants with less continuity get weaker ones?
