Here is an argument I constructed for my aesthetics seminar on the paradox of tragedy, and I now think it proves far too much—possibly that rational agents ought to avoid nearly all serious art—but I cannot pinpoint the exact misstep.

(1) If an emotional state is intrinsically unpleasant, and an artwork reliably produces that state in a viewer who knows its cause, then the artwork is, pro tanto, harmful to that viewer.
(2) The grief, pity, and terror evoked by tragedy are intrinsically unpleasant states, phenomenally continuous with their real-world counterparts.
(3) The viewer knows the events are fictional, but this knowledge does not eliminate the unpleasant affect; it merely coexists with it, just as I know a horror film is unreal yet still feel dread.
(4) Therefore tragedy is pro tanto harmful.
(5) Any activity that is pro tanto harmful is rationally avoidable unless it produces compensating goods that cannot be secured more efficiently by other means.
(6) Any moral or cognitive goods tragedy offers—empathy, discernment, catharsis—are either empirically unsupported (catharsis has no reliable evidence) or available through philosophy and biography without the accompanying distress.
(7) Therefore a rational agent should avoid tragedy.
(8) By parity of reasoning, any artwork that evokes negatively-valenced affect—horror films, melancholic music, paintings of atrocity, poems of despair—should be avoided.
(9) Since virtually all significant art evokes some negatively-valenced affect—tension, longing, dissatisfaction with the given—the argument entails that rational agents should avoid art altogether.
(10) This conclusion is absurd.
(11) So one of (1) through (6) must be rejected. If I had to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2)—that representation does not automatically neutralize harm—than deny the rational-choice principle in (5), since that seems to license self-destructive behavior generally. But if I keep (1) and reject (5), I need an account of why tragedy's harms are not rationally avoidable, which seems difficult. Alternatively, there may be a hidden equivocation in (1) between the harm of an emotion and the harm of an artwork that frames it, or a scope shift between particular negative affects and the overall experience. Where exactly does the argument break, and what does repairing it cost? Does saving the intuition that art can genuinely affect us require us to dissolve the paradox by redefining the emotions away, or can we keep both the harm and the rational pursuit of it?
