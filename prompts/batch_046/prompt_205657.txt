I'm in a graduate seminar on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and we've spent several weeks on hinge propositions—those background commitments (that I have hands, that the earth has existed for a long time) that stand fast for us and make doubt and inquiry possible. Recently we discussed a hypothetical case: an isolated scientific community treats "all physical processes are continuous" as a hinge, not as an empirical hypothesis, and then encounters apparently discontinuous phenomena at the quantum scale. Some members want to revise auxiliary theories; one member argues that the continuity principle itself must be abandoned. In response, another student kept invoking the slogan "doubt comes after belief" to insist that treating any hinge as open to revision is conceptually confused—you cannot rationally weigh evidence against the very framework that makes weighing evidence possible. I want to push back, but I am unsure exactly what that slogan amounts to. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express? I want a full menu: the trivial claim that inquiry requires some fixed points; the stronger epistemic claim that hinges are immune to rational revision because they lie outside the space of reasons; the psychological claim that human beings literally cannot entertain doubts about their hinges without cognitive collapse; and any more radical readings (for instance, that hinge change can occur only through non-rational conversion or a shift in form of life, not through argument). For each reading, say whether it is defensible, whether it actually blocks the quantum-scale revision case I described, and which interpretation my interlocutor was likely relying on when they used the slogan to shut down discussion. Please do not just pick one interpretation and defend it; I need the disambiguation with verdicts so I can bring it to the next session.
