I maintain the repository for a long-running open-source project, and since I recently had to audit a commit history that had been repeatedly rebased and squash-merged into a misleadingly linear narrative, I have become personally preoccupied with how archival records can be silently rewritten. I would like you to restate the Whig-historical temptation—interpreting the past as an inevitable progression toward the present—in the vocabulary of distributed version control: branches, commits, merge conflicts, rebasing, squash merges, HEAD, dead branches, and repository archaeology. Be explicit about my fluency: I work with Git daily, but I am not a professional historian, so do not assume I know the technical historiographical literature. Translate systematically: what corresponds to selecting only the commits that lead cleanly to HEAD? What corresponds to treating abandoned branches as failed mistakes rather than viable alternatives? What corresponds to writing a single, polished commit message over a contested development? Then provide the residue report: which elements of Whig historiography translate cleanly into this vocabulary, which can only be expressed by distorting Git concepts (for instance, attributing intentionality to commit messages), and which have no counterpart at all—particularly regarding historical contingency and the agency of actors who could have branched differently. Crucially, do not smuggle in the metaphor's own assumptions and call them translation. Assess whether the residue indicates a limitation of the version-control framework or reveals that part of the historiographical critique was less precise than it seemed. Finally, state clearly what would change your mind: which discovery about actual archival practice, if it turned out to mirror distributed version control more closely than we assume, or which feature of Git that lacks any analog in historical records, would lead you to reverse your judgment about where the failure lies?
