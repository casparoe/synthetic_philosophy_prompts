In a debate about Sen’s analysis of the 1943 Bengal famine, the Irish potato famine, and the Great Leap Forward, two classmates—Maya and Leo—disagreed over whether famines are best understood as natural disasters or political crimes. (1) Maya argued that Sen’s entitlement-collapse framework shows food was physically available in all three cases: in Bengal, wartime price inflation and procurement destroyed purchasing power despite adequate stocks; in Ireland, grain and livestock exports continued under laissez-faire administration; in China, local harvest reports were suppressed, blocking redistribution. She concluded that calling famine “natural” confuses meteorological triggers with the institutional mechanisms that actually kill. (2) Leo responded that this slides from “politically caused” to “politically criminal” without justification: drought and blight were still necessary conditions, and Sen describes an “entitlement failure”—a breakdown in economic access—not necessarily a criminal act. He noted that bureaucratic incompetence during the Great Leap Forward differs morally from intentional extraction. (3) Maya then introduced a distinction she called “famine by extraction” versus “famine by informational blockage,” claiming both are institutional designs that treat populations as disposable, and that Sen’s democracy thesis—no functioning democracy has experienced famine—proves the decisive variable is always political structure, not weather. (4) Leo replied that this distinction collapses under scrutiny: informational blockage can occur without malicious intent, and if every institutional failure causing death counts as a crime, then inadequate sanitation or bridge maintenance also become crimes, erasing the disaster/crime boundary entirely.
