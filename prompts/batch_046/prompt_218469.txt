An omission can be a real cause when it removes something that would have prevented the result. Here X's omission removes a working Sensor A. The but-for test works: if X had replaced the battery, Sensor A stops the line, so no damage. That B was already broken doesn't matter — this is actually an overdetermination situation, because both B's malfunction and X's omission are each sufficient to produce the overheating. The preemption objection doesn't apply because preemption needs one occurrent cause to block another, and an absence can't be blocked by B's presence. So X's omission is genuinely causal, though not by producing energy like B's failure does, but by leaving a gap in the safety net.
