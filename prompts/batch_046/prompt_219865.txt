I'm running a small adult-ed reading group at the library — mostly retired folks and a couple grad students from other fields — and next month we're tackling whether you can actually learn how to live by reading other people's lives. They mostly think biography is either gossip with footnotes or hagiography with better PR, so I want to design one 90-minute session that moves them from that assumption to seeing Plutarch's Parallel Lives as doing something structurally different from both. I need the explanatory arc: what idea has to land first before the parallel-pairing even makes sense, then the carrying example (I'm thinking Alcibiades paired with Coriolanus, since both are brilliant, both crash because they can't handle adulation, and Plutarch never writes "therefore don't be proud" — he just mirrors the failure across Greek and Roman contexts). Then I need to know exactly where in the sequence to head off the misconceptions: that this is just hero worship (they'll say it in the first ten minutes), that Plutarch is giving explicit moral rules (they'll miss the absence of "thus we learn"), and that hagiography does the same thing more cleanly (they'll think saints are just better biographies). I also want a check at the end — maybe asking them to map a modern biography onto the parallel structure — to see if they've grasped that the learning happens in the negative space between the two lives, not inside either one. What should I leave out at this level? I don't want to drag in modern exemplarist virtue ethics or citation-heavy scholarship, just enough conceptual scaffolding so the session doesn't collapse into "great men are inspiring."
