I'm preparing a long-form essay on what philosophers have actually said about the so-called "meaning crisis" among young professionals, and I keep encountering a misreading that treats Moritz Schlick's account as merely another therapeutic invitation to find one's purpose. For the record, Schlick's central argument is that life acquires meaning not through purpose-driven labor but through play — activities pursued for their own sake, without instrumental justification. The distinction is sharper than popular summaries suggest, because Schlick treats even apparently restorative leisure as infected by the work-mentality once it becomes a means to wellness, productivity, or social capital.

Please describe the most important implications of Schlick's work-play distinction for evaluating the contemporary narrative that millennials and Gen Z suffer primarily from a deficit of career purpose. Specifically, I want to understand: first, how his framework recasts the crisis as a potential misdiagnosis — not too little purpose, but an over-instrumentalization of activities that ought to remain playful; second, what this implies for the ethical evaluation of labor withdrawal and so-called quiet quitting, if these practices represent attempts to reclaim intrinsic activity rather than failures of commitment; and third, where his view forces a journalist to rethink survey instruments that ask respondents whether they find their work meaningful, since the question itself may embed the very work-mentality Schlick critiques. I need conceptual clarity I can deploy without archive access, and I would like you to flag precisely where Schlick's own reasoning is ambiguous or where applying the distinction requires a normative judgment I must make as the writer rather than derive from the theory.
