"Van Inwagen's special composition question asks when parts make up a whole; he answers only for living organisms, largely because any less restrictive answer seems arbitrary. One proposed reply is a field-based structural-unity view: parts compose an object only when they're linked by a continuous physical field (e.g., causal or quantum-field connections), which supposedly gives sharp boundaries without appealing to life. First, explain van Inwagen's argument from arbitrariness. Then evaluate whether this field-based reply actually solves the special composition question, or whether it just relocates the boundary problem from objects to fields."

Student answer:

"Van Inwagen asks the special composition question and answers that parts only compose living organisms. His argument is mainly about ontological parsimony—if we allow tables and chairs, why not allow a shoe-moon hybrid? So he restricts composition to organisms because they have internal functional integration that random collections lack.

The field-based structural view says parts form an object when they're connected by a continuous causal field, like an electromagnetic field linking atoms. This avoids arbitrariness because fields have precise physical boundaries, so there's no vagueness.

But I think this fails for two reasons. First, fields themselves are just structural relations by another name, so this is basically the same as Merricks's structural-unity view, and Merricks actually agrees with van Inwagen that only organisms exist. Second, van Inwagen could accept that organisms have field-integration—he just denies that's enough for composition without life being present. So the field view doesn't conflict with restrictionism, it just describes how organisms are organized. The real problem is that fields don't have sharp boundaries either—think of quantum entanglement across distances—so we haven't solved the vagueness problem, we've just relocated it."
