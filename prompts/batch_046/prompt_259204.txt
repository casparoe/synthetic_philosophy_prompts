Consider the problem of recognition developed in Hegel's master-slave dialectic, but restrict attention to a specific conceptual terrain: recognition that is not pursued through direct intersubjective encounter, but is mediated by an institutional or procedural framework that assigns status asymmetrically — for instance, when one party's acknowledgment of the other is filtered through a role, a credential, or a decision rule that the recognized party does not control. If you were directing a small research group of philosophers and social theorists for three years, with the explicit aim of determining whether the dialectic still applies — and what normative conclusions it supports — in these mediated cases, which open questions should take priority? Provide a defended agenda, not a survey: rank the questions by importance to the overall project, tractability for conceptual philosophers without empirical fieldwork, and dependency relations among them (which answers must come first to unlock the rest). Specifically address four clusters: whether the dialectic requires direct intersubjectivity or can survive institutional mediation; whether the struggle for recognition that risks one's existence has a functional analog in competition for status within mediated systems; whether mutual recognition can be achieved when the mediating framework is designed or enforced by a third party; and whether any of these analyses yield concrete normative guidance for designing institutions that grant recognition. For each cluster, state the criteria behind its rank, identify the trade-offs between tractability and upstream importance, and specify what kind of argument or conceptual clarification over the next few years would cause you to revise the ordering. Finally, indicate which cluster you would deprioritize because resolving it would not significantly alter the answers to the others, and explain why.
