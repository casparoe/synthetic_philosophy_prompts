Two philosophers disagreed about whether Popper’s problem of verisimilitude can be solved by abandoning logical-content measures in favor of structural ones. One argued that the well-known formal obstacle — that for false theories, increasing truth-content necessarily increases false-content — applies only if we rank theories by the sets of their logical consequences. If instead we compare explanatory architectures, measuring how closely a theory’s causal-explanatory graph approximates the true world’s structure, false theories can be genuinely closer or further from the truth without that entailment failure. The other objected that this changes Popper’s subject: verisimilitude was defined relative to the complete true theory expressed in a shared language, and replacing logical consequence with structural isomorphism abandons the very criterion that made the comparison objective. The first replied that causal-graph isomorphism can be assessed empirically without presupposing the full true theory, using interventionist tests that compare predictive robustness across domains. The second countered that invoking interventionist causation smuggles in substantive metaphysical commitments about causal structure that Popper’s logical framework deliberately avoided, so the “solution” pays for its formal success by trading logic for metaphysics. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: does the structural turn genuinely address the formal problem or change the subject? Can causal-graph measures avoid circular dependence on knowing the true theory? And is the second philosopher right that the price of avoiding the formal obstacle is an unacceptable metaphysical loading? Finally, identify anything important both overlook — for instance, whether verisimilitude must be a total ordering, or whether partial rankings might suffice for scientific rationality.
