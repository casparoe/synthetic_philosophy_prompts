In discussions of G.E. Moore's proof of an external world, defenders and critics often invoke a computing analogy: Moore's argument is said to work like a checksum, verifying one uncontroversial instance — his presenting a hand — in order to validate the entire framework of external-object existence. The analogy is used to defend Moore: if the check passes, global skepticism is irrational. Give the explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the checksum algorithm, the input data, the error flag, the system being validated, and the trusted baseline? Then identify where the mapping breaks: does the presentation of a hand function as a verification step or as direct observation, and does skeptical challenge target the checksum or the underlying perceptual protocol? Assess whether the anti-skeptical conclusion survives once these disanalogies are exposed, or whether the analogy smuggles in assumptions about epistemic priority and reliability that Moore never establishes. If the analogy collapses, indicate what a more apt comparison would be.
