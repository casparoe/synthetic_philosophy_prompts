Write a one-page research proposal for a six-month conceptual project on what makes every token of the letter “a” an “a” across fonts, handwriting, noise, and geometric distortion. Frame the precise question: if a generative system can produce a form that every human reader identifies as an “a” yet that shares no measurable geometric feature with any “a” in its training distribution, does this show that letter recognition is rule-governed rather than statistical, or does it show that human categorization is more permissive than philosophers of artifactual kinds typically assume? Explain why current positions—prototype theory, essentialism about artifacts, and statistical-learning accounts of style—leave the issue open: they either treat style as an accidental overlay on geometric form or treat form as an emergent regularity, but neither explains how a single category tolerates radical geometric discontinuity while still excluding near-misses that differ only slightly. Propose an approach that combines conceptual analysis of “letterform” with the philosophical design of minimal boundary cases—forms that sit exactly at the edge of recognition—to determine whether the boundary is best explained by invariant topological relations, family-resemblance clustering, or a separable style-essence. Specify the deliverable: not an empirical computer-vision result, but a conceptual framework that distinguishes these three accounts and identifies the specific adversarial boundary-form that would adjudicate between them. Keep the scope realistic for six months of philosophical analysis and state clearly what failure would look like—for instance, if all three accounts yield identical predictions for every boundary case, then the distinction was not conceptually robust. Conclude by saying what makes this a philosophical rather than engineering question, and why resolving it matters for theories of categorization beyond typography.
