After an extended argument with a close friend about whether rational belief ever requires independent verification of what others tell us, I drafted the following exam question for an upper-level epistemology course, together with a student response I recently received and now find difficult to assess fairly. The question and the answer are reproduced below exactly as they stand.

Question (20 points): First, distinguish global reductionism from local reductionism about testimonial justification, using Hume's discussion of miracle testimony as an illustrative case. Second, explain C.A.J. Coady's anti-reductionist principle of testimony and show specifically how it is meant to avoid the circularity objection that Hume's reliance on past experience of human veracity raises for testimony in general. Third, apply the distinction between provenance-tracking—assessing whether the speaker is competent and sincere through non-testimonial indicators—and content-validation—assessing whether the claim coheres with one's own background beliefs—to determine whether a local reductionist can accommodate Coady's insights without adopting full anti-reductionism.

Student answer:
Global reductionists hold that every instance of testimonial justification must be reduced to non-testimonial sources such as perception, memory, and inference; local reductionists hold that only certain categories of testimony, such as reports of miracles, require such reduction, while ordinary testimony may be accepted without it. Hume argues that we should reject miracle testimony because uniform human experience establishes that miracles do not occur, and any testimony in their favor is always less credible than the direct experience that contradicts them. The circularity problem is that our belief in the reliability of human testimony is itself partly based on testimony, so using experience to filter testimony presupposes the very thing being tested.

Coady responds by proposing a principle of testimony: we possess a prima facie entitlement to accept what others tell us, which can be defeated only by specific counter-evidence. This avoids circularity because we do not need prior empirical proof of each speaker's honesty; we begin with a default acceptance and apply filters only when defeaters arise. Because this makes testimony a basic epistemic source analogous to perception, Coady's view is clearly anti-reductionist.

Applying the provenance-tracking and content-validation distinction: a local reductionist can use provenance-tracking—checking a speaker's track record through direct observation—to justify accepting ordinary testimony, without engaging in content-validation against background beliefs. Thus, for everyday cases, local reductionism yields the same practical result as Coady's principle: we accept the testimony. The two views therefore converge on ordinary belief formation, and they diverge only over extraordinary claims like miracles, where the local reductionist demands content-validation and the anti-reductionist still permits default acceptance unless a specific defeater is present.
