I am a doctoral student in intellectual history and political philosophy. In debates over whether the Enlightenment was principally a source of progress or of domination, I have noticed two analogies doing sustained argumentative work. Critics often liken Enlightenment rationality to a cartographic grid: it renders territory administrable by flattening particular contours into uniform, measurable units, and the very success of navigation makes the omissions invisible. Defenders, by contrast, sometimes liken it to an open-source protocol — a shared, revisable framework that derives its authority from universal accessibility rather than tradition. I want a rigorous audit of both analogies. For each, specify the explicit correspondences (the terrain, the grid, the navigator, the protocol, the adopter), identify where the mapping breaks, isolate the normative commitments about particularity, authority, and value that each analogy quietly imports, and assess whether either can genuinely support the conclusion it is meant to license — that Enlightenment reason is essentially dominating, or essentially liberating. Which disanalogies are fatal to those conclusions, and which are harmless? If neither analogy survives, suggest what a more adequate comparison would be. Please answer in British English.
