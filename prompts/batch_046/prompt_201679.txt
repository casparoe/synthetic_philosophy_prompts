I'm preparing a two-page proposal for a six-month writing fellowship in philosophy of medicine. The project asks what clinical descriptions of pain asymbolia actually establish about the badness of pain. The received debate treats asymbolia as separating pain's sensory quality from its aversive affect, then asks whether badness resides in the quality (the phenomenalist view) or in the affective-motivational response (the functionalist view). I want to argue that both frames miss a third option and that the clinical data are better read as revealing a breakdown not in affect but in a specific form of first-personal normative binding—the integrative condition by which a subject registers a sensation as simultaneously hers, harmful, and demanding a practical response she resists. The precise research question is therefore: does pain asymbolia show that pain is not intrinsically bad, and if so, does the dissociation support a functionalist reduction of badness or rather a relational account on which badness depends on this binding structure? I need the proposal to state why existing answers leave this open—namely, that both camps treat the dissociation as decomposing two pre-existing layers rather than as the absence of an integrative condition, and that neither can explain why asymbolic patients still seek treatment for tissue damage despite lacking distress. The method should be philosophical, not empirical: construct a conceptual taxonomy of binding failures visible in clinical reports, subject the phenomenalist, functionalist, and relational interpretations to a coherence test against that taxonomy, and derive the normative consequences for analgesic obligation if the relational view holds. The deliverable should be a journal-length article taking a definite, committed stance—defending the relational interpretation as the correct reading of the data, not surveying options neutrally—and showing that it forces a revision in the justification for pain relief (away from both intrinsic-badness and pure-behavioral arguments). Define clear failure conditions: the taxonomy collapses into functionalism, or the relational account cannot distinguish asymbolia from mere indifference to bodily harm. Assess scope honestly: six months allows the conceptual framework and one coherence argument, but not a full clinical collaboration or empirical validation, which should be flagged as follow-up work. Explain why funding philosophy here is appropriate: the clinical literature already describes the phenomenon, but without the conceptual distinction between affective absence and binding failure, practitioners misclassify patient reports and misallocate moral urgency. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for references; I will supply those. Write in a formal register appropriate for an ethics or philosophy-of-medicine review panel, and take a committed position throughout rather than laying out alternatives for me to choose.
