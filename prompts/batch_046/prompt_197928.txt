I've been trying to work out my position on privacy for a seminar paper, and I'd like an audit before I commit to it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Privacy is best understood as context-relative informational boundary maintenance: information is private not when it is hidden, but when its circulation respects the normative expectations of the social context in which it was originally shared.

(2) The moral value of privacy is purely instrumental. It matters only because it protects autonomy, intimate trust, and democratic dissent; if these goods could be fully secured without privacy, privacy itself would carry no independent moral weight.

(3) Fundamental privacy rights are inalienable. Even with full information and genuinely voluntary consent, an individual cannot permanently waive these protections, because widespread surrender would erode the social conditions that make autonomous agency possible for everyone.

(4) In digital environments, data is infinitely replicable and inferentially leaky, which makes traditional boundary maintenance technically impossible. The appropriate moral response, then, is symmetric transparency: if institutions cannot be kept out, universal visibility should be enforced, neutralizing power asymmetries by ensuring everyone is equally exposed.

(5) The correct criterion for evaluating any privacy or data policy is whether it preserves epistemic asymmetry in the individual's favor — that is, whether a person continues to know more about their own life, preferences, and vulnerabilities than any institution or stranger does — regardless of whether autonomy, intimacy, or dissent are affected in that particular case.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, explain how badly they conflict and what the cheapest revision would be to restore consistency. Identify any pair that looks incompatible but dissolves on closer inspection. Finally, tell me which single revision — dropping or modifying one commitment — would clean up the most problems with the least damage to the overall view.
