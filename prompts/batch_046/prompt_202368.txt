The philosophical debate over whether emotions are essentially judgments, perceptions, or bodily feelings has been running since James's 1884 essay in Mind and was revived in the late twentieth century. By now the exchange feels circular: cognitivists absorb bodily feeling as a component of appraisal; perceptualists absorb appraisal into the content of affective perception; feeling theorists absorb both into irreducible phenomenology. Meanwhile, emotional granularity data seem compatible with all three sides—cognitivists say concepts drive the differentiation; perceptualists say sensory discrimination does; feeling theorists say vocabulary merely labels pre-existing bodily textures—so no empirical finding has moved anyone.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical but about explanatory priority: whether intentional content must be prior to phenomenal feel, or vice versa. But I also wonder whether the sides are actually answering different questions—one about mental ontology, one about norms of emotional rationality, one about first-person phenomenology—under the single heading "What is an emotion?" Or whether the question is ill-posed because "emotion" is not a natural kind.
