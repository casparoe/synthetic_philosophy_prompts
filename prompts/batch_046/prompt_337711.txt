I am writing as a graduate student in philosophy who has recently begun a seminar on non-Western metaphysics and the philosophy of consciousness. After working through the split-brain literature, I became interested in historical attempts to reconcile the apparent multiplicity of minds with a deeper ontological unity. In that context I turned to Erwin Schrödinger’s later essays on Vedanta, where he maintains that the plurality of conscious subjects is ultimately only an appearance. I am having difficulty grasping the precise philosophical work his central analogy is meant to perform.

Consider the following passage from his essay “The Mystic Vision,” as translated in Quantum Questions (1984):

“But it is quite easy to express the solution in words, thus: the plurality that we perceive is only an appearance; it is not real. Vedantic philosophy, in which this is a fundamental dogma, has sought to clarify it by a number of analogies, one of the most attractive being the many-faceted crystal which, while showing hundreds of little pictures of what is in reality a single existent object, does not really multiply the object... ‘You may suddenly come to see, in a flash, the profound rightness of the basic conviction of Vedanta: … knowledge, feeling and choice are essentially eternal and unchangeable and numerically one in all men, nay in all sentient beings.’”

What puzzles me is not merely the image of the crystal, but how Schrödinger expects it to do explanatory labor. Does the analogy actually account for why individual memory, karma, and the compelling sense of being a distinct “I” persist with such force, or does it merely restate the Vedantic thesis in physical imagery? Moreover, when he describes the recognition of this unity as a sudden “flash,” is he presenting it as an empirical observation about consciousness, a mystical experience that transcends argument, or a rational deduction from premises he takes to be scientifically grounded? I would appreciate any guidance on how to read the logical structure of this passage—without reducing it either to pure mysticism or to a simple category mistake—especially regarding how it might address the phenomenological reality of personal identity without denying it outright.
