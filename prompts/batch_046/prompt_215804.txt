The question asked: In cosmological models with eternal inflation, thermal fluctuations can produce Boltzmann brains—disembodied observers with false memories—potentially in numbers that dwarf ordinary evolutionary observers. Some argue this creates a cognitive instability problem: accepting a model that predicts we are likely Boltzmann brains undermines the reliability of the very reasoning used to accept the model. A recent proposal introduces a “cognitive stability threshold” (CST): an observer must have mental states persisting for at least a minimal duration (longer than a microsecond) to count as an epistemic agent capable of reasoning. Evaluate whether introducing the CST resolves the instability problem without being ad hoc, and whether the problem itself depends on conflating random belief formation with the physical conditions required for reasoning.

Student answer (approx. 350 words):

The Boltzmann brain problem arises in eternal inflation because de Sitter space has a nonzero temperature, allowing thermal fluctuations to assemble brains with false memories. Given infinite time, these fluctuations occur infinitely often and outnumber ordinary observers produced by evolution and structure formation. This yields an epistemic paradox: if the model predicts I am almost certainly a Boltzmann brain, then my beliefs—including my belief in the model—are products of random thermal noise and are unreliable. Accepting the model therefore undermines itself. This is the cognitive instability objection.

A proposed fix is the cognitive stability threshold (CST). Reasoning requires mental states that persist long enough to form inferences; a brain existing for only about 10⁻²⁰ seconds cannot actually think. Because Boltzmann brains fall below this threshold, they should be excluded from the reference class over which we apply self-locating probabilities. In fact, this restriction is already built into the standard self-sampling assumption (SSA), which conditions on being an observer capable of asking the question—something a sub-microsecond brain cannot do. Once BBs are excluded, ordinary observers dominate the reference class, and the instability vanishes.

One might object that the CST is ad hoc, but it is not: it derives from the functionalist view that reasoning requires sustained information processing. Materialists about consciousness might even deny that brief thermal brains are conscious at all, which reinforces the exclusion. The real flaw in the instability argument is that it applies Bayes’ rule with a uniform prior over all brains, ignoring the conditional prior that restricts us to CST-observers. Hence the paradox dissolves, and eternal inflation survives.
