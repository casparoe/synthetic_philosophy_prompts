Following a long and somewhat unresolved argument with a friend about Paul Grice's account of speaker meaning, I want to press a specific tension that she raised but that I think rests on a confusion. She maintained that Grice's intentionalist analysis—according to which speaker meaning is constituted by the speaker's intention to induce a belief in the hearer through the hearer's recognition of that intention—applies uniformly across all dimensions of linguistic content. From this, she inferred that any effect not specifically intended by the speaker, such as the contrastive force conventionally carried by the word but, must fall outside speaker meaning entirely, since it is fixed by lexical convention rather than by a particular communicative plan. She then argued that Grice is therefore forced to deny that conventional implicature belongs to speaker meaning at all, leaving it in an unexplained limbo between what is said and conversational implicature. I tried to suggest that conventional effects might be incorporated into the conventional determination of what is said without requiring a separate, particularized intention for each instance, but she countered that this violates Grice's core commitment to explaining speaker meaning through communicative intentions. I suspect she is conflating the intentional basis of conversational implicature with the conventional basis of lexical meaning, yet I am not entirely sure how Grice's taxonomy actually resolves the status of conventional implicature within speaker meaning. Could you clarify whether Grice's framework genuinely excludes conventionally determined, non-intended lexical effects from speaker meaning? Specifically, in an utterance such as The prognosis is grave, but the patient remains stable—where the speaker's clinical purpose seems directed at the factual conditions rather than at any explicit contrast—should the contrastive contribution of but be treated as part of what is said, as a conventional implicature belonging to speaker meaning despite not being specifically intended, or as something else entirely? And does accepting the second option require us to weaken Grice's intentionalist foundation?
