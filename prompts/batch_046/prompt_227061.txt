Read Sophie's Choice — either the Styron novel or Pakula's film, im not picky — through the question of whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible. Most people frame it as Williams versus the Kantians: he says yes, they leave moral residue behind; they say ought implies can, so it's just a tragic choice, not a real dilemma. But I want you to push past that binary and inject something weirder: treat the camp selection scene as engineered so that Sophie's agency is basically hollowed out — she isn't choosing between two goods but being forced to perform a ritual that only looks like choice. So is this even a pseudo-dilemma? Does her lifelong guilt prove she genuinely did wrong no matter which child she picked, or is it trauma being misread as moral remainder? And look at the film's visual langauge — that long close-up where she barely speaks, the way the sound just drops away — does that framing push us to see her as an agent or as an object the system is manipulating? Don't just summarize the plot. I want a reading that takes both the philosophical machinery and the actual scenes seriously, and ends by saying what Sophie's Choice adds to the debate that a bare thought experient can't capture.
