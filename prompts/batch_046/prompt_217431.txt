I've been reflecting on why the philosophical debate over abortion ethics has settled into a kind of stable equilibrium: both sides keep refining the same moves without either side conceding ground. One cluster of arguments appeals to bodily autonomy and the right to refuse life-sustaining support, often developed through the violinist analogy and its descendants; the other cluster appeals to the wrongness of depriving a being of a future like ours, treating the potential for conscious experience and agency as morally decisive. Over decades, each side has absorbed the standard objections—distinctions between killing and letting die, debates over whether potentiality confers present rights, and disputes about the moral significance of dependency—yet the disagreement rarely shifts in forums and reading groups where participants understand the literature well.

My working hypothesis is that much of the stall is not about empirical facts, nor even about the relative weight of autonomy versus future value, but about a hidden disagreement over what I will call the modal robustness of potential. The autonomy-oriented side tends to treat the fetus's future as counterfactually fragile: in nearby worlds where the pregnancy is terminated, that particular future never actualizes, so there is no present being who is harmed—only a branch that is never realized. The future-like-ours side treats that same future as world-bound: the being already exists in the actual world, and termination is an intervention that destroys an already-actual trajectory rather than merely failing to realize a possibility. If this framing is correct, the two sides may not be disagreeing about moral principles at all, but about the modal and metaphysical structure of the entity being evaluated—whether potential is best modeled as an intrinsic property of a present being that can be destroyed, or as a branching possibility whose closure harms no one.

But I am genuinely unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real architecture of the disagreement, or whether I have projected a metaphysical framing onto what is actually a bedrock divergence about whether biological continuity or conscious self-awareness should ground moral standing.

So I want a careful diagnosis rather than a verdict: is the disagreement verbal, with both sides using terms like 'potential' and 'person' in different senses? Is there a concealed empirical or formal question—perhaps about personal identity over time, or about the ontology of future persons—doing the hidden work? Or is it a case of irreconcilable starting intuitions about whether dependency undermines moral status? Are the sides in fact answering different questions under one name? Given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually move the debate—a conceptual distinction, a result from metaphysics of identity, or a reframing of the moral question—rather than generating another round of symmetrical counterexamples? And please be explicit about which parts of your analysis are settled among philosophers who work on this topic, and which parts remain genuinely contested; do not present a contested metaphysical view about potentiality or identity as if it were the consensus.
