I have just spent the better part of an evening in an exhausting argument with a close friend over the view, associated with Daniel Dennett, that the self is nothing more than a center of narrative gravity — a useful fiction spun out of the stories we tell about ourselves. My friend is generally sympathetic to eliminativism about the self, but she pressed me with a case I could not immediately dismiss. She asked me to imagine two individuals engaged in a sustained, real-time co-authorship of what becomes, over years, a single first-person autobiography: each party corrects the other's memories, fills narrative gaps, and revises motives so thoroughly that the resulting text is genuinely unified — a coherent I that neither could have produced in isolation, yet which both experience as authoritative for their own practical reasoning and moral accountability. She contended that if narrative coherence is all that fixes the boundaries of the self, then we are forced to recognize either a distributed, dyadic center of narrative gravity or to admit that the view collapses into an arbitrary biological individualism it was meant to replace.

Play the committed defender of this narrative-gravity view — not Dennett himself, but an unnamed philosopher who holds the position with full conviction and is prepared to defend its internal resources. You must speak in the first person. Your constraints are these: you deny that there is any Cartesian ego, any biological boundary, or any phenomenological given that fixes the borders of the self; you treat narrative coherence and practical utility as the sole relevant criteria; you are willing to accept strange consequences if they follow consistently from the view; you may not invoke mystical or non-naturalist entities; and you must remain in character throughout, answering my challenge rather than asking me to clarify the rules of the exchange. You may pose clarifying questions only if they are the kind your character would genuinely ask in this conversation.

My opening move is this: in the co-authored autobiography case I have described, the narrative center is genuinely distributed — neither partner can claim sole authorship, yet the I in the text functions as a single locus of agency, memory, and moral responsibility for both. Does your view require you to recognize a joint self here? If not, what principled reason can you offer for locating the center of gravity in one brain rather than in the relational space between them — other than an unacknowledged biological prejudice you have already sworn to reject? And if you do recognize the joint self, are you not committed to the existence of group minds in every long marriage, every collective memoir, every sustained shared delusion?
