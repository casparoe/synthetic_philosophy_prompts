In discussions of artificial intelligence and value alignment, the orthogonality thesis is often introduced with a geometric analogy: intelligence and goals are said to be orthogonal, like the x- and y-axes on a Cartesian plane, so that any point on the intelligence axis can in principle be paired with any point on the goal axis and increasing capability does not constrain what an agent pursues. This analogy is frequently used alongside the argument for instrumental convergence—the claim that agents with diverse final goals tend to acquire similar intermediate goals such as resource accumulation, self-preservation, and cognitive enhancement—to motivate the view that we should worry more about specifying the right final goals than about limiting intelligence itself. Audit the axes analogy precisely: provide an explicit correspondence table (what plays the role of the origin, the axes, the quadrants, and any metric of distance); identify where the mapping breaks, especially once instrumental convergence is taken seriously—if convergence pulls all sufficiently capable agents toward a narrow region of the goal axis regardless of their initial position, does that mean the axes are not truly independent, or only that movement along one axis constrains movement along the other? Then assess whether the analogy's usual policy conclusion—that danger lies primarily in specifying the wrong final goal rather than in the magnitude of intelligence—survives once these disanalogies are visible, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. Be conceptually specific: avoid treating orthogonality as a slogan, and say exactly what kind of independence, if any, remains defensible once instrumental convergence is admitted.
