I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of mind seminar on postdictive consciousness, and I want to apply the Orwellian versus Stalinesque revision distinction—together with the temporal structure of the color phi phenomenon—to Christopher Nolan's film Memento. In the course we treated Orwellian revision as the retroactive rewriting of past experience itself, and Stalinesque revision as the alteration of records or reports while the original experience remains intact; we also discussed how color phi suggests that conscious experience can integrate stimuli over time in ways that make the temporal order of awareness diverge from the order of physical events. Read Memento through this framework: Leonard Shelby's anterograde amnesia forces him to reconstruct continuity through Polaroids, tattoos, and handwritten notes, while the film's reverse chronology fractures narrative sequence in a way that structurally mimics discontinuous snapshots being stitched into an illusory flow. Does Leonard's manufactured pursuit of "John G." and his reconstructed grief over his wife constitute an Orwellian revision—where his emotional experience of vengeance is retroactively manufactured by the narrative he imposes—or a Stalinesque one, in which an original trauma persists intact beneath selectively edited records? Focus on the scene near the end where Leonard realizes he has already killed a previous "John G." and consciously chooses to plant new evidence so that the hunt can continue: is this a dramatization of the color-phi-like illusion that a coherent self can be generated from discontinuous present moments, or does it preserve a kernel of genuine agency that resists being reduced to postdictive construction? I want a reading that takes both the film's narrative architecture and the philosophy of consciousness seriously—showing where the philosophical lens illuminates Leonard's choices and where the film pushes back, perhaps by suggesting that fragmentation does not necessarily dissolve responsibility. End by saying what Memento adds to the Orwellian/Stalinesque debate about the timing of experience that purely conceptual argument misses.
