Please comment on the conceptual coherence and historical plausibility of the following thesis, which I am developing for a feature on democratic failure. Rather than treating the Mytilene debate, the Sicilian Expedition, and the trial of Socrates as isolated catastrophes, I propose that they manifest a single structural deficiency: the absence of institutional "cooling architectures" that separate reactive emotional phases from binding deliberative phases in direct democratic assemblies. In Mytilene, the assembly reversed an extermination decree within a single day, suggesting not moral reasoning but affective oscillation. The Sicilian Expedition was approved amid compressed strategic euphoria that collapsed long-term risk assessment into immediate collective enthusiasm. The trial of Socrates, occurring in the immediate aftermath of the Thirty Tyrants, performed democratic restoration through rapid scapegoating rather than procedural patience. The conceptual claim is that Athenian democracy exercised sovereignty through instantaneous assembly vote without interstitial review mechanisms, making "deliberative latency"—the temporal gap necessary for emotional decoupling—structurally impossible. Is this framing internally consistent across judicial, military, and punitive contexts? Does it risk anachronism by importing modern institutional concepts? And are there counter-examples within Athenian practice—such as the use of graphe paranomon or probouleusis—that would undermine the claim of absolute latency collapse?
