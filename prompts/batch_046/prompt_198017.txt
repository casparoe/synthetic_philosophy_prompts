I'm a doctoral candidate in moral philosophy working on practical reasoning under value pluralism, and I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar. Please write a formal dialogue of roughly 600–800 words between two equally competent interlocutors debating whether genuine moral dilemmas—cases in which an agent faces incompatible binding requirements, each leaving a normative residue after action—are genuinely possible.

One interlocutor should defend what we might call the moral residue view: that some conflicts are not merely apparent but structurally unavoidable, leaving the agent with unavoidable moral wrongness regardless of choice. The other should defend the systematic resolution view: that every apparent dilemma dissolves upon correct normative analysis into a single all-things-considered obligation, and that any lingering regret is rational but not normatively authoritative.

To keep the exchange from rehearsing generic textbook moves, frame the debate through a specific tripartite distinction that should organize the dialogue: (i) epistemic dilemmas, where the agent lacks information to rank duties; (ii) structural dilemmas, arising from institutional roles that generate non-aggregable obligations—specifically, a triage protocol that requires selecting one patient over another when both have identical prognoses and moral status, and where the selection criterion is procedurally fair but morally arbitrary with respect to the patients' equal claim; and (iii) ontological dilemmas, where incommensurable values lack any common metric. The dialogue should concentrate on structural dilemmas. The residue defender should argue that procedural fairness itself can force an agent to violate an unfulfilled duty to the unselected party, producing agent-regret that is best explained as acknowledgment of remaining obligation rather than mere tragic loss. The resolution defender should counter that the wrongness, if any, attaches to the institutional design or to the scarcity of resources, not to the agent's particular act, and should press the point that if no further normative reason favors one patient, then selecting either does not violate a duty to the other—thereby denying that a genuine dilemma exists for the agent.

Both characters must respond directly to each other's actual arguments, particularly addressing whether agent-regret in structural cases carries continued normative force or is instead a non-obligatory emotional response to irremediable loss. Neither should be a strawman; each should concede the strongest point available to the opposing side before rebutting it.

Finally, include a brief meta-commentary of no more than 150 words: identify the weakest argument presented in the dialogue and explain why; specify what conceptual clarification—perhaps about the logic of institutional obligation—or empirical finding—perhaps about the psychology of post-decisional moral regret—would lead you to revise your assessment of which view is stronger; and state explicitly where your own reasoning is most vulnerable to failure, especially regarding whether structural dilemmas genuinely differ from standard conflicts among Ross-style prima facie duties.
