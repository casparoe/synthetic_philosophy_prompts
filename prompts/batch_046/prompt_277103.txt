As a retired physicist who has only recently begun serious study of philosophy, I have been working through the St. Petersburg paradox and have arrived at a cluster of commitments that I should like tested for coherence. I should be grateful if you would examine them, identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely appear so, rank the conflicts by severity, and suggest the least costly revision in each case.

My commitments are as follows:

(1) The divergence of expected value in the canonical St. Petersburg game is best interpreted not as a paradox demanding resolution via utility curvature, but as a boundary condition: expected-utility maximisation forfeits its normative authority whenever a payoff structure requires aggregation over physically unrealisable infinite sequences.

(2) I endorse what I shall call structural closure: in constructing the state space for any decision problem, one ought to exclude outcomes that violate well-established physical constraints—such as the finite information capacity of the observable universe—and treat those excluded branches as decision-irrelevant rather than as live possibilities with negligible probability.

(3) Dominance reasoning, I maintain, applies unconditionally within any properly restricted state space: if option A yields at least as good an outcome as option B in every included state and strictly better in at least one, rationality mandates the choice of A, with no reference to risk attitude.

(4) Accordingly, I judge that a rational agent should be unwilling to pay more than roughly £25 to enter the standard St. Petersburg game, and that this refusal is justified solely by (1) and (2), without invoking risk aversion or diminishing marginal utility.

(5) Although I reject subjective expected utility theory as a universal normative framework—precisely because of unbounded cases like this—I nevertheless wish to preserve the independence axiom for all bounded lotteries whose state spaces and utility assignments are fully computable.

(6) Finally, I hold that any decision-theoretic norm requiring an agent to execute computations extending over infinite or physically unrealisable domains describes an idealised calculator rather than a genuine standard of rational agency for finite beings.

Please audit this package. In particular, I suspect that (4) may not actually be entailed by (2) without smuggling in an unacknowledged risk preference, since a physically grounded cutoff might still permit an enormous—though finite—expected value. I am also concerned that (3) and (5) sit uneasily together, given that dominance and independence are usually grounded in the very framework I reject. Does (6) undermine the precision of the monetary verdict offered in (4)? Please present your analysis in British English, organised with clear headings, and do not include a bibliography or list of further reading.
