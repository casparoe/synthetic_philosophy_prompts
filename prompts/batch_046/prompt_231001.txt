I have recently found myself returning to Ian Hacking's distinction between interactive and indifferent kinds, not as an abstract philosophical exercise but because a family member's recent psychiatric evaluation turned on precisely the kind of boundary dispute that looping effects are meant to illuminate. I grasp the elementary mechanism: once a classification like adult ADHD or prolonged grief disorder is institutionalized, people who identify with it begin to behave differently, and clinicians in turn revise their instruments. What I cannot quite visualize is the conceptual architecture of the reflexive layer that forms when the loop itself becomes an object of clinical management.

Suppose epidemiologists notice that prevalence is rising partly because awareness is reshaping symptom reporting, and in response they tighten diagnostic thresholds to preserve predictive validity. Does this corrective intervention generate a second-order loop—one in which patients near the new boundary restructure their self-narratives not in response to the original category, but in response to the category's deliberate contraction? I want an intuitive, step-by-step explanation of how this meta-stabilization effort fits within Hacking's framework without collapsing into the claim that psychiatric kinds are merely fabricated. Specifically, help me see whether the attempt to fix the loop undermines the reality of the underlying differences, or whether it reveals that interactive kinds have dynamics—self-correction, resistance, boundary negotiation—that indifferent kinds like chemical elements simply do not. Avoid summarizing the literature; I am asking for the conceptual machinery in motion, particularly for cases where the classification is both biologically anchored and socially responsive.
