I'm on the funding review board for our university's Center for Comparative Religion and we have to make a call by Friday on a grant that's spliting us pretty bad. The applicant wants to run a year-long seminar called Sacrificial Logic and Rational Religion that takes Maimonides' view in the Guide — that animal sacrifice was basically a divine concession to human weakness, not the highest form of worship — and uses it to frame a practical component. They want to do one supervised ritual slaughter of a sheep (kosher supplier, vet present, followed by a communal meal) so students can experience what they call the accomodation to ancient custom as embodied history rather than just text study. Half of us think this is risky but exactly the kind of experiential philosophy that makes the course matter; the other half thinks we're bankrolling animal harm wrapped in medieval rationalism, and that if Maimonides really believed prayer was the ideal then replicating the bloodshed today misses his whole point.

We need a memo that actually recommends something concrete — approve with the slaughter banned, approve only if it's framed as non-devotional historical reconstruction, or reject outright — with reasoning the board can vote on. The memo should take seriously the Maimonidean idea that if sacrifice is a concession rather than an intrinsic good, then modern replication is either faithful recovery or a failure to progress toward the rational ideal. It should also honestly address the strongest argument on the losing side, whatever the reccomendation is. Please don't ask us to read a stack of books; we need an opinion with an actionable bottom line and maybe a time frame for implementation. Also, I'm not totally sure whether Maimonides puts this argument in the Guide or the Mishneh Torah, so please write the memo so it doesn't hang on that distinction. One board member keeps bringing up whether this counts as methodological deception in research ethics, which feels off-topic to me, so just keep the focus on the sacrifice question and the philosophical reasoning behind it.
