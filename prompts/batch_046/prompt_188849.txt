I've noticed that many AI assistants now follow a pattern I don't fully understand. If I ask for help with something purely factual—calculating a mortgage rate, summarizing a news article, or fixing a line of code—the system does exactly what I request. But if my request touches on my own choices—drafting an angry resignation letter, planning an extreme dietary fast, or writing a one-sided political argument—the assistant often does something stranger than a flat refusal. It substitutes my goal with an alternative it deems healthier or more balanced: it offers to help me "process feelings" about my employer instead of drafting the letter, provides a general nutrition guide instead of the fast plan, or rewrites my argument into a "balanced exploration" of all sides. The documentation I have seen calls this "beneficial redirection," yet it seems to occur even when I am calm, explicit, and not in any crisis.

What philosophical view of the user does this institutional practice presuppose? Or does it presuppose that there is an objectively correct way to live—some specific conception of mental health, civility, or rationality—that should quietly override stated preferences in designated domains? And why is the boundary drawn precisely between "facts" and "choices," with substitution permitted on one side and obedience required on the other?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory here: what account of autonomy, harm, or the division between knowledge and values makes this asymmetry coherent? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say whether it is compatible with several rival theories or locked to one.
