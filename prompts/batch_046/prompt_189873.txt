I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting an application for a six-month research fellowship at a philosophy-of-science centre. I wish to submit a philosophical proposal—rather than an empirical study—that grows out of a problem I encounter regularly in my reporting. My half-formed idea concerns risk-weighted expected utility: in standard decision theory, risk weights apply a non-linear transformation to probabilities in order to model risk-averse choice. In my work on climate and public-health communication, I repeatedly observe experts assigning minuscule probabilities to catastrophic outcomes, while policy debates seem to treat these probabilities as normatively different from moderate risks. I suspect there may be an open philosophical question about whether risk-weighting can be justified not merely for prudent action, but for rational belief-formation itself—particularly when a journalist or scientist must evaluate competing claims under deep uncertainty. Write a two-page proposal with the following elements: a precise statement of the question, clearly distinguishing belief-formation risk weighting from action-guiding risk weighting; an explanation of why existing treatments in decision theory leave this distinction unsettled; an outline of the philosophical method, focusing on conceptual analysis of the normative structure of risk-weighted expected utility and whether its weighting function can carry epistemic authority; a description of what a useful deliverable would look like for practitioners who communicate science under uncertainty; and an honest assessment of what could render the project too ambitious for a six-month fellowship. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list—I shall supply citations myself. Please write your response in American English. I have composed this request in British English.
