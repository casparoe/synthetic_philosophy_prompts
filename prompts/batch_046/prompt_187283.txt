I've been reading popular explanations of artificial intelligence that compare the internal patterns in neural networks to files in a computer folder or entries in a dictionary: the idea is that somewhere inside the model there is a discrete, readable representation corresponding to a specific concept, and that interpretability tools are like opening the folder to see what's inside. This is used to argue that networks genuinely think in terms of these internal representations. But I've also encountered the objection that these patterns are distributed across thousands of parameters and that the reading only happens when an external probe is applied — suggesting the representation might be more like a statistical correlation than a stored idea.

Can you audit this folder-file analogy? I want the correspondence table — what plays the role of the folder structure, the file, the file name, the reader, and the act of opening? Then tell me where the mapping breaks: does the representation exist independently of the probe that detects it, is it localized or spread across the network, and does the analogy smuggle in assumptions about discrete, symbolic content that neural networks don't actually have? Finally, does the conclusion — that networks have real representations in a meaningful sense — survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was the folder picture doing all the work? If the analogy collapses, is there a different comparison — perhaps to a landscape of activation patterns rather than a filing system — that better captures what interpretability research actually reveals?
