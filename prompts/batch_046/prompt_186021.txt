I have been working through interpretations of Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, and I keep encountering a reading that treats the episode as establishing what I will call phenomenal symmetry—the idea that the first-person character of being the dreaming Zhuangzi and the first-person character of being the butterfly are not merely epistemically indistinguishable but ontologically co-equal, such that neither state possesses a privileged claim to being the actual one. If one takes this symmetry seriously, then Zhuangzi's subsequent doubt—whether he is Zhuangzi who dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming he is Zhuangzi—seems to presuppose a framework in which one of the two must be the deceptive appearance and the other the veridical reality. But if phenomenal symmetry rules out exactly that hierarchical relation, then the doubt appears self-defeating: there is no waking pole against which the dream pole is defined as erroneous, so skepticism never gets a foothold, and the episode collapses into a kind of pluralist metaphysics in which both identities are simultaneously instantiated across different experiential branches. Is this the right way to read the passage, or have I introduced a false presupposition about what skepticism requires? More specifically, does Zhuangzi's argument depend on maintaining a distinction between appearance and reality that phenomenal symmetry eliminates, or does the symmetry actually serve a different purpose—perhaps showing that the self is not a fixed substance but a transformation—without thereby dissolving the skeptical problem?
