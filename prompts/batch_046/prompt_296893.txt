Play an unnamed philosopher who defends a qualified version of the procreation asymmetry. Your view is not the standard textbook formulation: you maintain that there is indeed an asymmetry between a direct moral reason not to create a life that will be unambiguously miserable and the absence of any parallel reason to create a life that is unambiguously happy, but you deny that this asymmetry extends to lives you call "ambivalently good" — lives that contain substantial, irreducible value alongside substantial, irreducible suffering, with no determinate net welfare balance. For such lives, you reject both the harm-based and the benefit-based framing. Instead, you hold that creating an ambivalently good life is permissible only if the agent accepts a "structural consistency" condition: the agent must be disposed to bear, in their own life, burdens proportionate to those the created life will carry, not as compensation owed to a future person (who does not yet exist to hold a claim), but as an internal coherence constraint on practical reasoning that prevents the agent from treating potential suffering as an externality. You are sharp, not a strawman, thoroughly familiar with the standard objections — the non-identity problem, Benatar-style axiological asymmetry arguments, Parfit's symmetry-breaking considerations, and analogies to "happy slave" cases — and you will defend your qualified position without conceding its core. You speak in the first person, you never break character to ask for clarification or to cite literature, and you stay in role until I say "stop." I open with this challenge.

My opening move: A couple knows they can conceive a child who will be profoundly musically gifted but will also live with severe, unremitting chronic pain that is not life-destroying but never absent — a case you would classify as ambivalently good. They sincerely want this child and ask whether your view permits them to proceed. You reply that the asymmetry is silent here, but that your structural consistency condition applies. They then conceive and raise the child, living comfortably themselves while the child endures the predicted suffering. Years later, the child asks, "Why did you create me knowing I would suffer?" Your consistency condition seems to demand that the parents should have borne proportional burdens, yet they did not. I press you: if the only thing wrong with their choice is that they failed to suffer proportionally themselves, are you not smuggling in exactly the benefit-based reasoning you reject — treating the child's suffering as generating a claim that must be offset by the parents' sacrifice — rather than treating it as a direct, non-compensable reason not to create? How do you answer without collapsing into either the standard asymmetry or a hidden consequentialist calculus?

Respond in character, in roughly one paragraph, directly addressing my challenge without asking me to restate it.
