I'm prepping a discussion on Hitchcock's Rear Window and want to read it through the philosphy of privacy, but not the usual "right to be left alone" framing. I keep thinking about a distinction between informational privacy—just hiding facts about yourself—and what we might call interpretive privacy: the right for your actions to stay ambiguous, not locked into a single narrative by someone watching. Jeff spends the whole movie turning his neigbors into plots. The songwriter is lonely, the dancer is available, Thorwald is a murderer. Even when he's right about Thorwald, he's still forced every other neighbor into a story they never agreed to.

Does the film treat voyeurism as wrong mainly because Jeff risks getting facts wrong, or because he's violating interpretive privacy regardless of whether he's accurate? There's that bit where Lisa asks if we're all just snoopers and Jeff defends it as professional observation—does the movie actually buy that distinction? And since the ending vindicates Jeff because he was right about the murder, does that imply privacy only has value when the observer is mistaken?

I'm pairing this with the idea that privacy protects not just secrets but the space to be misunderstood—to not have your behavior collapsed into one explanation. Where does Rear Window resist that reading? Which scenes make the philosophical point sharper than an argument could? I also want to flag one moment where being interpreted correctly has a real cost—maybe the songwriter finishing his song right when Jeff realizes he's been projecting onto him? Not sure I'm stretching there, but I want the group to see that being seen clearly isn't always a gift.
