Construct a clinical decision thought experiment in which a physician updates her beliefs about a patient's condition after receiving an ambiguous lab result—specifically, a quantitative biomarker reading corrupted by a known but uncalibrated instrument drift, so that she can assign credences across a range of possible true values rather than observing a precise point. Engineer the scenario so that Jeffrey conditionalization and strict Bayesian conditioning (treating the nearest rounded value as certain evidence) yield opposite recommendations about whether to administer a drug with severe side effects but high efficacy if the condition is present. The case should serve to isolate whether preserving prior correlations across ambiguous observations is rationally required, not merely to show that one update rule dominates in general.

Constraints: the setting must be an ordinary hospital (no futuristic technology or exotic physics); the ambiguity must arise from a mundane source, such as a degraded printout, a cross-calibrated assay discrepancy, or a technician’s qualified verbal report; the payoff structure must reflect real clinical trade-offs (risk of unnecessary harm versus missed cure); and the physician’s prior must include a non-trivial correlation between the biomarker’s noise profile and the patient’s treatment history, so that rounding the observation and conditioning on it violates that correlation. Present the scenario in one paragraph.

After the scenario, demonstrate step-by-step: (i) how Jeffrey conditionalization updates the posterior probabilities of the disease and of treatment efficacy, preserving conditional probabilities given each possible observation weighted by her credence in that observation; (ii) how the rigid conditionalizer updates by rounding to the nearest discrete value and conditioning on it as certain; (iii) the precise structural feature that makes the two diverge—namely, that Jeffrey’s rule keeps the prior correlation between noise profile and patient variables intact across the weighted updates, whereas rigid conditioning severs it by collapsing the observation space; and (iv) whether removing that correlation (making the noise independent of patient history) eliminates the divergence, and whether making the instrument perfectly precise collapses the case into standard conditioning.

Then explain how a defender of rigid conditioning would attack the case—identify which feature they would deny is rationally relevant or which step they would claim misapplies the rule. Finally, say what would change your mind: specify which formal result about belief revision or which empirical finding about clinical reasoning would cause you to reverse your judgment about which update rule handles this ambiguity correctly, and identify the specific step in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure.
