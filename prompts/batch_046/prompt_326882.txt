Write a dialogue between Sam and Jo, two philosophy grads chatting in a pub, about whether individual actions like cutting meat, voting, or lowering your carbon footprint actually matter when the harm is collective and your own contribution is tiny. Sam thinks the inefficacy objection is overblown — she talks about expressive meaning and collective patterns — while Jo is more sceptical and thinks people exaggerate their causal role. They should discuss three specific cases: giving up beef for a year, casting a single vote in a large election, and turning down the heating on a coal-heavy grid. Then Jo introduces a hypothetical she calls the '9,999 rule', where damage only occurs once exactly 9,999 people have done the act, making the 10,000th the one who actually tips it. She asks Sam whether being that 10,000th person is morally worse than being the 9,998th. Sam should argue that real-world causation is continuous, not step-like, but Jo should counter that climate tipping points and election margins can be genuinely discontinuous. Make sure they recieve each other's points properly — no strawmen, real back-and-forth. Keep it casual, use British spelling (behaviour, centre, programme if you like), and don't be too formal.
