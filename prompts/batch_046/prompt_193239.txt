Below is an exam question from an upper-level philosophy of history course, followed by a student’s answer. Read both carefully.

Question: Consider the debate between the covering-law model of historical explanation, associated with the philosophy-of-science tradition, and the narrative model developed by philosophers of history. One prominent objection holds that historical events are unique and non-repeatable, which seems to preclude subsumption under general laws. Meanwhile, narrative theorists maintain that historians do not merely list causal antecedents but construct coherent temporal sequences that carry explanatory weight in their own right. Address the following: (1) Does the uniqueness of historical events defeat the covering-law model, or can uniqueness be reconciled with law-based explanation? (2) Can narrative explanation be fully reduced to an implicit, highly complex covering-law explanation, or does it involve a distinct form of understanding—sometimes called Verstehen—that law-subsumption cannot capture?

Student answer:
The covering-law model, as Carl Hempel formulated it, requires that an explanation subsume an event under universal or statistical laws together with initial conditions. Because historical events occur only once, there are no repeatable regularities available, so the model cannot apply to history; uniqueness therefore defeats it. Narrative historians such as Hayden White, by contrast, merely arrange facts in chronological order to help readers follow a story, without making genuine explanatory claims. This chronological ordering is just a psychological heuristic for readers who cannot process the enormously long chains of law-based premises that actually explain the event. Furthermore, the distinction between Erklären (law-based explanation) and Verstehen (interpretive understanding) was drawn by Hempel to show that all apparent cases of narrative understanding are really shortcuts for underlying covering-law explanations that are too complex to state fully. Consequently, narrative explanation offers no independent explanatory form; it is only a practical substitute for an implicit law-subsumption that historians do not bother to articulate.
