As a journalist covering philosophy and existential risk, I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I cannot place precisely. In discussions of human extinction, people routinely concede that the final catastrophe involves mass death, yet insist the deeper wrong lies elsewhere: long-term projects—climate archives, scientific repositories, cultural transmission—would suddenly become dead letters, and even intimate acts like teaching a child to read or planting an orchard feel hollow if there is no subsequent generation to receive them. The harm is not framed as lost welfare for future people who never exist, but as a retroactive collapse of present meaning: the value of what we do now seems to depend on an assumed future continuation that acts not as a beneficiary but as a necessary condition for the coherence of the present effort.

Does this configuration have an established name? I know it sits near the idea that human continuity provides a kind of secular afterlife sustaining present value, but the cases I see are more specific: they treat the absence of future people as undermining the meaning of present actions for those who are still alive, rather than as a simple failure to produce future goods. Is there a concept that captures exactly this dependency—where present meaning is contingent on transgenerational reception, and extinction harms present agents by removing that receptive frame? How does it differ from ordinary instrumental reasoning about future benefits, and how does it interact with the non-identity problem—if changing course produces different future people, can we meaningfully say that present meaning is harmed specifically by their absence? Finally, what is the boundary: if a project is genuinely self-contained and requires no audience, does the phenomenon disappear, or does the underlying principle apply more broadly?
