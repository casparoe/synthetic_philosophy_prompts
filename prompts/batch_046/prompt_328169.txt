Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains and only two rhymes throughout—that treats Gongsun Long’s “white horse is not horse” not as sophistical wordplay but as a serious problem in the metaphysics of property composition. Use the first refrain, some version of “A horse is not white,” and the second, “Hard and white stand apart,” to track the School of Names (Mingjia) distinction between properties present separately (li) and properties present together (he)—the same framework Gongsun Long applies to the hard white stone. Let the poem’s volta occur in the final quatrain, where both refrains are forced into the same four-line space and into shared rhyme, so that the form itself enacts the coincidence (or failure of coincidence) of form and color in one named object. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with the Mingjia recognizes the li/he distinction doing structural work rather than being named as ornament, yet it should leave genuinely open whether the separation lies in the world, in language, or in the act of naming itself. Do not resolve the paradox with a sermon or a shrug; let the villanelle’s circling, returning form hold the tension exactly where our discussion of determinables and determinates tends to end up.
