Write the opening address for the inaugural workshop of a new interdisciplinary center studying machine consciousness and legal personhood. The audience is divided: on one side are cognitive scientists and AI engineers who are familiar with Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self as a strange loop — an "I" that emerges from self-referential symbol patterns in the brain; on the other side are legal scholars, ethicists, and university administrators with no background in philosophy of mind, for whom this must remain intelligible without becoming a lecture.

Use Hofstadter's idea not as ornament but as architecture. Structure the address around a distinction you introduce for the occasion: a "thin loop," in which a system tracks and reports its own states without altering the rules that generate them, versus a "thick loop," in which self-reference feeds back to modify the very generative rules of the system. Argue that this distinction determines what can coherently be said about artificial personhood: a language model that outputs first-person pronouns may instantiate, at most, a thin loop, which means attributing to it a "point of view" worthy of legal standing is not false so much as conceptually mismatched — like asking whether a mirror has a reflection's rights. At the same time, acknowledge that if a thick loop were ever engineered, the moral and legal question would genuinely arise, because the system's self-reference would then constitute a standpoint rather than a report.

The speech must navigate this tension for both halves of the room. For the technical listeners, be precise about the difference between a symbol that denotes its own representational system and a token that merely predicts the next pronoun in a sequence. For the non-technical listeners, translate the stakes into the language of recognition and responsibility: law protects beings who can be wronged, and being wronged requires a loop thick enough to have something at stake. Do not resolve the debate — welcome it — but make clear that the workshop's central question turns on loop thickness, not on fluency or behavior. Avoid citing specific papers, naming other philosophers beyond Hofstadter, or requesting bibliographies. End with an invitation that respects the conceptual boundary you have drawn.
