At a departmental reading group on distributive ethics, two postgraduate students—Nadia and Theo—fell into a sustained disagreement about whether the numbers should count in rescue cases. (1) Nadia argued that if you can save either one stranger or five strangers from an imminent harm, with all other factors equal, you ought to save the five. Each individual possesses an equal claim to rescue, and these claims aggregate straightforwardly: five valid claims outweigh one. (2) Theo objected that this treats moral subjects as fungible units of value. The rescue relation is irreducibly dyadic—between rescuer and each victim separately—and there is no metaphysically real "group claim" that emerges merely from adding individuals together. Consequently, one should be genuinely indifferent between saving the one and saving the five, perhaps resolved only by a fair lottery. (3) Nadia replied that Theo's position cannot survive scaling. If there were a million strangers facing the same harm, Theo should still be indifferent between saving the million and saving the one, which is morally absurd. The strong intuition that disparity of scale matters demonstrates that numbers do count, even if the aggregation is not strictly linear. (4) Theo accepted that practical reasoning might shift at extreme scales—perhaps due to epistemic uncertainty or resource efficiency—but maintained that the fundamental moral arithmetic remains non-aggregative. He then introduced a refinement: suppose the five "individuals" are in fact five psychologically continuous stages of a single person, linked by memory and intention, while the lone individual is metaphysically distinct. Saving the five stages preserves only one interrupted narrative, whereas saving the single person safeguards an entirely separate life. Theo claimed this shows that what truly matters is not head-count but the ontology of the subjects involved, and that in such a case saving the one might be correct. (5) Nadia countered that Theo has now smuggled aggregation back in through a different door. By allowing the internal structure of a group—its metaphysical unity—to determine the moral weight of saving it, he is not rejecting the idea that numbers count; he is applying a more complex aggregation rule that weights subjects by their narrative coherence. Unless Theo can provide a non-circular criterion for when a collection counts as one subject rather than many, his view does not eliminate counting but merely conceals it inside an opaque notion of "genuine subjecthood." They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who is right about which specific point. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both parties are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
