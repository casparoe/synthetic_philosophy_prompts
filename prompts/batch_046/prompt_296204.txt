I have been discussing with colleagues the long-standing dispute between William James and W.K. Clifford regarding the ethics of belief, specifically whether holding a belief without adequate evidence can ever be justified. Most treatments focus on James's defense of religious faith in The Will to Believe and Clifford's counterexample of the negligent shipowner in The Ethics of Belief. I am trying to move past these canonical illustrations to isolate a more structurally elusive conceptual issue.

Consider the distinction between beliefs evaluated directly by evidence and what we might call regulative frameworks—the background conditions that determine which observations count as relevant data within a particular domain of inquiry. Give a concrete example of a conceptual problem that emerges when Clifford's evidentialist demand is applied not to an isolated first-order belief, but to one of these regulative frameworks. Your example should highlight a tension between treating such a framework as an individual epistemic duty subject to evidential scrutiny, and recognizing it as a collectively maintained precondition for evidence-gathering itself. In developing the example, address whether James's appeal to practical or vital necessity offers a defensible extension to these cases, or whether it risks the kind of ungrounded credulity that Clifford warns against. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; develop the conceptual point clearly and independently.
