I'm preparing a long-form piece on the foundations of rational choice, and I keep returning to the tension between normative axioms and observed preference. Most readers have encountered the Allais paradox in passing, but I want to go deeper: specifically, I want to understand what the paradox actually diagnoses about the independence axiom rather than about human psychology alone.

Consider a modified choice structure. In the classic setup, a decision maker chooses between pairs of lotteries that share a common consequence across pairs, and the independence axiom demands that the preference ordering be preserved when that common consequence is substituted. Now imagine that the common consequence is not a fixed payoff but a contingent entitlement whose value itself depends on an unresolved ambiguity about the state space—say, the entitlement pays differently if the underlying probability model is Knightian rather than Bayesian. In both choice pairs, this ambiguous entitlement is present as the shared component, yet subjects still reverse their preferences in the Allais pattern.

My question is conceptual, not empirical: does this nested-ambiguity framing weaken the paradox's force against the independence axiom, because the outcomes in the two pairs are no longer in a genuinely identical outcome space? Or does it sharpen the critique, by showing that the axiom implicitly requires separability not only over probabilities but over the very framing of what counts as an outcome? In other words, is the independence axiom being violated, or is it being misapplied because the common consequence was never common in the relevant sense?

I'd like an explanation that keeps the mathematics of expected utility in view—how the axiom operates on mixture spaces—while also addressing whether this kind of ambiguity-sensitive restructuring reveals a hidden normative commitment in the axiom itself. Don't summarize the literature or offer a reading list; I'm looking for a clear conceptual map that I can use in the article without misleading readers about where the logical fault line actually lies.
