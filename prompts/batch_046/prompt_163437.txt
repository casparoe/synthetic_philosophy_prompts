I am the designated backup specialist for a high-stakes technical function within a research consortium. The role carries no contractual obligation to respond outside standard hours, though an informal norm encourages availability when primary staff fail. Six months ago, I resolved a critical system failure over a holiday weekend—an intervention that was explicitly recognized as going far beyond any formal or collegial duty. Since then, the director has treated my availability as an implicit baseline rather than an exceptional contribution, scheduling me as the first-resort responder for after-hours emergencies and recently reprimanding me for declining a weekend crisis due to prior personal commitments, on the grounds that my refusal "breaks the trust established by my earlier commitment." I feel caught between two competing intuitions: that my original act was genuinely voluntary and therefore should not have generated a standing, personalized obligation; and that my demonstrated capacity reveals a larger actual responsibility, making subsequent refusal a failure to provide what I have already shown I can deliver. I have also wondered whether my initial exceptional act might function as a kind of moral credit that should excuse later non-intervention, but the director treats it instead as proof of a concealed baseline duty. Is there a principled distinction between required and voluntary action that survives this kind of expectation conversion, and can performing one genuinely exceptional voluntary act ever legitimately create a new binding obligation that attaches specifically to the person who performed it?
