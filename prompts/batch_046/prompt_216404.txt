I am defending the following thesis at a philosophy-of-biology workshop: that etiological accounts of biological function fail for synthetic organisms created through directed evolution with severely truncated selection histories—specifically, a novel ribozyme evolved in vitro over only fifteen generations in a continuous-culture chemostat—because there is no sufficiently long evolutionary lineage to ground a selected-effect ascription. Meanwhile, causal-role accounts cannot explain why the same molecule, when misfolded and performing an off-target catalytic reaction, counts as malfunctioning rather than gaining a new function. I therefore propose a hybrid normative criterion that imports design intent for origin and evolutionary stability for maintenance, which I anticipate will be attacked as an ad hoc pluralism.

The audience is a philosophy-of-biology seminar: roughly half are committed etiological theorists working in the Wright/Neander selected-effect tradition, a quarter are causal-role pluralists influenced by Cummins-style system-capacity analysis, and a quarter are evolutionary biologists who are skeptical that philosophy can distinguish real biological function from engineered artifact at all. They care deeply about whether function talk carries normative force—malfunction versus mere functional change—and they will press on whether my synthetic cases are genuinely biological or just disguised engineering metaphors.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the voice of each camp, with for each: (a) what I should concede, (b) what I should hold, and (c) the weakest reply I might be tempted to offer and why it fails. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely that my hybrid criterion smuggles in intentional design as a proxy for missing selection history, rendering the account circular for synthetic biology—and tell me exactly how to address it in under ninety seconds without sounding defensive. Also flag the single weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.

Keep the response to about 400 words. Lead with that hidden weakness and the ninety-second defense; then list the remaining objections ordered from most damaging to least damaging.
