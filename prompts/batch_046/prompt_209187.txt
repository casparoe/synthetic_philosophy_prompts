I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I recently joined a reading group where we discuss conceptual issues that come up in system design. Last week, someone summarized the pragmatist theories of truth associated with Charles Peirce and William James in a way that seemed relevant to how we think about validating outputs from distributed algorithms versus locally tested heuristics. The summary went like this. Peirce is said to have defined truth as the belief that an unlimited community of investigators would eventually converge upon if inquiry continued forever; on this reading, his view is essentially a sociological prediction about future scientific consensus, which means truth is just whatever the collective eventually decides, with reality playing no independent constraining role. James, by contrast, is said to have claimed that a belief is true precisely when it works for the person who holds it—when it yields successful predictions, practical satisfaction, or useful outcomes in that individual's context—so that truth becomes a property of beliefs relative to particular agents and their needs. The summarizer drew a sharp conclusion: Peirce offers a collective relativism anchored in an unreachable endpoint, while James offers an individual relativism anchored in personal utility, and therefore neither account can support an objective standard for debugging software or evaluating model predictions, since one defers judgment to an infinite future vote and the other collapses into subjective preference. I am inclined to think this comparison misses something important about both thinkers, especially regarding how James understood verification across experience rather than private feeling, and about how Peirce's convergence idea involves reality's resistance to belief rather than mere agreement. But I am not sure where the errors lie, whether the conclusion actually follows from the corrected views, or whether there is a meaningful analogy here between philosophical pragmatism and the distinction between global consensus protocols and local unit tests in engineering. Could you audit this summary? Please separate what is accurate from what is a misrepresentation or overstatement, indicate what a defender of each view would object to, and assess whether the claim that pragmatism is useless for objective technical validation survives once the summary is corrected. If there is a defensible way to apply either Peirce's or James's framework to the evaluation of predictive systems, I would like to understand it without being referred to further reading lists.
