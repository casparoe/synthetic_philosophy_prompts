In a philosophy of decision seminar I am running, the discussion of transformative experience has reached an impasse that I find more puzzling than the familiar deadlock over L.A. Paul’s parenthood example. We have been concentrating on a particular variant: cases where an agent considers undergoing a prolonged neurostimulation protocol that permanently reconfigures her affective calibration—knowing that her phenomenal architecture, not merely her beliefs or preferences but the basic subjective structure of how values feel from the inside, will be altered, and knowing that she cannot now simulate or access that future subjective quality. One faction treats this as an extreme but tractable form of decision-making under uncertainty: the agent assigns probabilities to future utility profiles, updates with available evidence, and chooses; if the future self’s preferences diverge radically, that is merely preference change over time, no different in kind from the drift we already tolerate. The opposing faction argues that rational choice requires a stable deliberative subject whose values can be consulted, and that when the evaluative apparatus itself is among the things transformed, there is no coherent “choice” being made—only the replacement of one agent by another, masked by bodily continuity.

My own suspicion is that the stalemate conceals a disagreement about diachronic personal identity disguised as a debate about rationality: the first group assumes a psychological-continuity framework in which future selves are nodes in a single narrative that can be compared via intertemporal welfare calculus, while the second group assumes something like a phenomenal-binding condition for agency, under which radical restructuring of subjective architecture severs the authority of present choice over future outcomes. Yet I wonder whether there might also be a hidden formal issue—perhaps about whether standard Bayesian frameworks can represent states in which the prior and posterior belong to incommensurable phenomenal spaces—or whether the sides are simply answering different questions, one normative (what should I do given my present values?) and one metaphysical (who will be there to inherit the consequences?). I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication: what kind of disagreement is this, really—substantive, verbal, or a collision of incompatible research programmes? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction between preference revision and architectural transformation, a formal result showing the limits of intertemporal choice under phenomenal incommensurability, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed?
