As a journalist covering philosophy and cultural history, I have been preparing a feature on how Enlightenment anxieties about artificial representation anticipate contemporary debates about immersive media. In conversations with editors, I found myself returning to Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert and its well-known attack on theatrical spectacle: the idea that theater corrupts not simply by depicting vice, but by generating a shared emotional response in physically co-present spectators who simultaneously know the representation is fictional, thereby eroding the practical boundary between performed vice and lived resistance to it. I attempted to reconstruct this into a rigorous argument, but the reasoning now strikes me as suspiciously expansive, and I want to locate the precise failure before I commit the analysis to print.

Here is the argument as I have formalized it. (1) If a cultural form produces genuine shared affect directed at a vice, and if this affect occurs despite the audience's knowledge that the representation is fictional, then the form weakens moral resistance through a mechanism that depends solely on the structure of co-present emotional complicity, not on the specific artistic medium. (2) Rousseau's proposed alternative—civic festivals celebrating collective virtue—also involves physically gathered citizens experiencing shared affect directed at representations (historical narratives, symbolic pageantry) that they know to be partially constructed. (3) By the reasoning in (1), such festivals should corrupt moral resistance just as theater does, because the mechanism—shared affect in the presence of known fiction—is identical. (4) Furthermore, private reading of novels or historical chronicles can produce intense affective identification without physical co-presence; if the mechanism is purely affective, then these solitary practices should also corrupt, yet Rousseau does not condemn them. (5) Therefore, Rousseau's specific critique of theater applies with equal force to his own positive proposals and to uncontroversial cultural practices, which is absurd. (6) The only available rescue is to say that theater depicts vice while festivals depict virtue, but then the corruption is explained by content rather than by the phenomenology of theatrical presence, making Rousseau's elaborate analysis of the actor's physical immediacy redundant. (7) Hence, either the argument proves too much and collapses Rousseau's distinction into a generic, content-based censorship, or it forces him to deny that shared affect is the relevant mechanism, undermining his central claim. I am inclined to preserve Rousseau's insight that theatrical presence is morally distinctive, but I cannot see where the chain breaks. Where does this argument go wrong—whether through an equivocation about co-presence, a hidden modal slide, or a misattribution of the mechanism—and what does giving up the weakest link cost for the broader project of distinguishing corrupting from edifying public representation?
