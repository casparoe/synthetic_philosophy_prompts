Following a lengthy and somewhat heated discussion last evening with a friend who studies institutional economics, I want to determine whether I have misapplied the backward induction argument to collective decision-making. We were examining the centipede game and its relevance to a funding committee that reviews a multi-phase project through successive annual votes. I argued that backward induction demonstrates the committee should terminate funding at the first review: each member anticipates that continuation will eventually collapse, and since termination at any stage yields a marginally better collective payoff than allowing collapse, rationality demands an immediate withdrawal vote. My friend objected that this reasoning illicitly transfers an individual payoff structure onto a collective body. In a committee, he noted, no single voter captures the payoff, and the aggregation rule—say, majority vote—introduces a discontinuity that breaks the smooth sequential structure the induction requires. He then proposed an alternative formalization using what he termed bounded reflective endorsement: rather than assuming an infinite hierarchy of common knowledge about rationality, each committee member represents the reasoning of others only up to a fixed epistemic depth—perhaps two iterations—after which belief attribution stops. Under this cap, he claimed, the induction chain is interrupted before reaching the first node, making sustained funding rational at early stages. I remain unconvinced, but I want to be precise about why. It seems to me that if the collective payoff matrix retains the centipede structure—each round of continuation produces a jointly superior outcome, but termination at the current round produces a slightly superior outcome to termination at the next—then truncating the belief hierarchy should not alter the logical structure of the argument; it should merely make agents less confident in applying it. Is this correct? Or does the bounded-reflective-endorsement framework genuinely dissolve the paradox in collective contexts? Furthermore, if the induction proof indeed requires an infinite hierarchy that no real institution can instantiate, should we interpret the centipede result as a normative benchmark rather than as a descriptive prediction of institutional failure?
