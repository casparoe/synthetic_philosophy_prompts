I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of action working through Daniel Dennett's framework on the varieties of free will worth wanting for a seminar on moral agency. In my reading group last week, two colleagues—I'll call them M and N—fell into a sustained disagreement about whether Dennett's taxonomy is genuinely exhaustive or whether it omits a structurally distinct variety they tentatively called "interstitial agency." I am writing this as a bystander with no settled view, and I want a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as actually made, not personal advice.

Here is how the exchange unfolded. (1) M began by insisting that Dennett's distinction between the freedom worth wanting—the kind that grounds reactive attitudes through rational responsiveness—and metaphysical libertarian freedom is exhaustive: either an agent can be moved by reasons in a way that makes moral address appropriate, or it cannot, and any intermediate appearance is just noisy instantiation of the first category. (2) N replied that this collapses a real structural difference. She proposed that some agents—consider a non-speaking autistic adult whose caregivers consistently interpret his gestures and vocalizations as meaningful expressions of preference—exercise what she termed "interstitial recognition." They are embedded in practices of giving and asking for reasons without themselves engaging in explicit propositional reasoning; the socio-normative scaffolding is not merely causally enabling but partly constitutive of their agency. (3) M countered that this is merely subpersonal rational responsiveness, causally scaffolded by others but not conceptually distinct from the kind Dennett already describes. (4) N then argued that if the scaffolding is partly constitutive, then the agent supports a form of moral respect that does not require full-blown rational autonomy, which explains our intuitions about obligations to beings that fail Dennett's stricter criteria—including, she admitted, some infants and trained animals, which she took to be a feature rather than a reductio. (5) M concluded that without a principled cutoff, N's view collapses into ascribing agency whenever an observer feels sympathy, smuggling moral sentiment into what should be a conceptual taxonomy.

Can you adjudicate this exchange move by move? For each of the five moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Say who is right about which specific point and what both parties are missing—particularly whether "interstitial recognition" can be distinguished from subpersonal rational responsiveness without relying on an observer's sympathy. Then say explicitly: what argument or consideration would change your verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail? Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then provide the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question. Write in American English.
