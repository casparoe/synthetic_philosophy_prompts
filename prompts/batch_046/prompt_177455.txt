In macroeconomics, epidemiological forecasting, and climate modeling, researchers routinely defend theoretical models built on assumptions they openly admit are false—perfectly rational agents with unlimited computation, pathogens with uniform transmission rates, linear climate feedback loops—by appealing not to the realism of the premises but to predictive accuracy at the aggregate scale, and they often reject rival models with more realistic micro-foundations when those produce worse system-level forecasts. At the same time, in medicine and toxicology, black-box predictive success is typically treated as insufficient: regulators and clinicians usually demand an identifiable mechanistic pathway before accepting an intervention. What philosophical commitments about scientific explanation, understanding, and realism does this disciplinary double standard presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—whether it requires instrumentalism about theories, a layered realism that distinguishes explanatory levels, or a pragmatic distinction tied to policy urgency and tractability—and say whether the practice strictly needs that view or is compatible with several rivals. Identify where the practice’s parts pull in inconsistent directions, and explain what would have to change in how these fields validate and deploy models if the underlying view were replaced by one that requires mechanistic understanding in every domain.
