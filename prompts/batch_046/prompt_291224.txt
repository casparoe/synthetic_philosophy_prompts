I was reading this old essay by Simone Weil—she argues political parties are basically machines that manufacture collective passion and suppress independant moral judgment, like religious bodies demanding total allegiance, and she thinks they should be abolished entirely. But then I look at modern democratic institutions: we fund parties with public money, organize legislatures around party blocs, and use whip systems to enforce voting discipline, all while claiming that representatives should exercise individual judgment and answer to constituents rather than party leaders. There's this obvious tension.

What theory of representation and political agency does that institutional package actually presuppose? Does it require viewing representatives as delegates bound by collective will, or can it be squared with a view of them as independant moral agents? Is the practice philosophically consistent with Weil's claim that parties destroy the capacity for independent thought, or does it quietly assume parties are just instrumental coalitions that don't affect judgment? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice strictly needs it or just happens to fit, and tell me what would have to change in the institutional design if we took Weil's critique seriously—would we have to drop public funding, abolish party discipline, or is there some middle ground?
