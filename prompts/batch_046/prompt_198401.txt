I'm a retired biologist — thirty years in marine ecology — who only got into philosophy seriously last year after my grandson kept sending me articles about Kuhn and then I wandered into historiography. Lately I've been chattting with friends about presentism, that idea historians can't escape judging the past by today's standerds. But someone mentioned a stronger version involving what they called a regime of visibility, where the present doesn't just color interpretation but actually decides which parts of the past even show up as problems at all.

I want to apply this to a real episode I remember well: the 2020 protests when the Colston statue in Bristol was pulled into the harbor, and the parallel fights over Columbus monuments in American cities. The salient facts, as I understand them: Colston died in 1721 and made money in the slave trade; his statue was put up in 1895 by people promoting Bristol's civic pride, not as a 17th-century memorial; protesters in 2020 said keeping it up endorsed slavery; defenders said you can't judge a 17th-century merchant by 2020 ethics and that removing it erases history; meanwhile some historians pointed out that the monument itself was a 19th-century political act, not a neutral relic.

Using presentism — both the weak version that historians inevitably use present categories, and that stronger regime-of-visibility idea — analyze this episode as a case study running both directions. What should the protesters, the defending historians, and the city councils have done by the lights of each version? Which features of the disagreement does presentism actually explain, say why both sides accused each other of being anachronistic, and which does it miss, like the fact that the statue's origin was already presentist? And does the 1895 erection date show that historians can genuinely recover foreign past perspectives, or does the whole episode prove escaping our own time's standards is impossible?
