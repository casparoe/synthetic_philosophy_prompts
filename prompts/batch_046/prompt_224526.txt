Write a parody of a scientific field-guide entry, in the deadpan voice of an 1870s naturalist’s monograph excerpt—approximately 600 words—titled as an excerpt from On the Ontogeny of Institutional Kinds. The narrator should classify three “species” observed in the wild: the U.S. dollar (in its native habitat of clearinghouses), gender pronoun usage (in the ecosystem of bureaucratic forms), and racial census categories (in the migratory patterns of state record-keeping). Invent a fictional taxonomic framework called “Institutional Phylogenetics,” complete with deadpan jargon—e.g., “ontogenetic drift,” “normative selection pressure,” “essence migration”—that parodies actual interdisciplinary borrowings of evolutionary biology into social theory without attributing any real scholar. The satire should target the real tendency, in both popular and academic discourse, to apply biological metaphors (speciation, adaptation, phylogenetic trees) to social kinds without noticing the category mistake. Treat each case study differently to stay in the weeds: for money, trace a fake evolutionary lineage that obscures its legal-conventional origins; for gender, map “reproductive strategies” onto pronoun shifts in a way that erases the distinction between biological reproduction and social role; for race, construct a “phylogenetic tree” of census classifications that confuses genetic clustering with administratively enforced boundaries. By the end, the narrator should have inadvertently demonstrated that the framework can only “discover” these kinds by presupposing the very essentialism it claims to study—leaving the contradiction visible to the reader without ever breaking deadpan or winking. Avoid named living persons, do not request bibliographies or citations, and do not quote any real text verbatim; paraphrase familiar debates about social construction without quotation marks. The piece should reward a reader familiar with kind-realism debates about institutional entities versus demographic categories, but remain readable as straight-faced natural-history prose to someone who is not.
