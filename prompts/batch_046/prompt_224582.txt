I've noticed a recurring pattern in very small, self-contained reasoning spaces—like alphabet-string puzzles where you extend a sequence (ABC becomes ABD, so MRRJJJ becomes what?)—and in everyday debates about whether two situations are genuinely analogous. In these confined domains with only a few visible features, people seem to decide what counts as the "same kind of relation" not by applying one fixed rule, but by letting the salient dimension slide: one moment the repetition count is decisive, the next it's the left-right pairing or the alphabetical position, and when a counterexample threatens the conclusion they've already reached, they quietly shift to whichever feature still supports it. The shift isn't presented as a change of framework; it's treated as if the same underlying criterion had been in force all along. I see this not only in puzzle explanations but also in policy analogies, where someone first appeals to structural similarity, then, when the structure differs, appeals to shared purpose, then returns to structure for a different comparison—never flagging that the ground of comparison has moved.

Is there an established name for this particular kind of in-domain elasticity, where the axes of similarity inside a bounded problem space are renegotiated case by case to preserve judgments, without the speaker treating it as ambiguity? If there is, how precisely does it differ from plain equivocation and from the motte-and-bailey pattern, given that here the shifts occur among dimensions that are all genuinely present in the domain rather than between a defensible core and an indefensible extension? If no precise term exists, can you coin and define one, specifying what distinguishes it from ordinary contextual flexibility on one side and from deliberate rhetorical sleight-of-hand on the other, and describing a clear case where the phenomenon is absent despite looking similar?
