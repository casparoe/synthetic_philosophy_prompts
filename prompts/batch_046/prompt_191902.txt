I hold that the traditional problem of other minds rests on a false presupposition: it assumes I have direct, non-interpretive access to my own mental states and must analogically extend this privilege to others. I think instead that first-person mental-state attribution is already an interpretive, theory-laden practice learned through linguistic socialization, so there is no epistemic asymmetry requiring analogical inference in the first place, and the philosophical difficulty dissolves into a question about how we calibrate shared interpretive frameworks. At the same time, I think this dissolution is threatened by cases of radical first-person error, such as anosognosia for hemiplegia or certain forms of blindsight, where a subject sincerely denies a mental state that observers confidently ascribe, suggesting that sometimes third-person attribution can be correct while first-person report is systematically unreliable. What would count against my view? Please also assess whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, if I can always redescribe any counterexample as a calibration failure within the interpretive framework, and whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind.
