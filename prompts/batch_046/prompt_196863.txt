Design an exam for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar: one essay question answerable in about 90 minutes and roughly 1,200 words, accompanied by a model answer of about 800 words and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks.

The domain is higher-order theories of consciousness, but the question must not be answerable by reciting Rosenthal’s standard actualist HOT account. Instead, center it on a specific conceptual revision—call it Dispositional-HOT (D-HOT)—according to which a first-order mental state is conscious if the subject is disposed, under normal conditions, to form a non-inferential higher-order thought directed at it, regardless of whether such a thought is ever actually tokened. The essay prompt should require students to do four things: (a) explain why Rosenthal insists on actual token HOTs rather than mere dispositions, with explicit reference to the explanatory work he assigns to the higher-order state (for instance, in accounting for the subjective appearance of consciousness); (b) evaluate whether D-HOT genuinely answers the objection that actualist HOT makes consciousness too conceptually demanding, or whether it merely relocates the problem by making consciousness depend on counterfactual capacities that may be equally demanding; (c) apply both Rosenthal’s view and D-HOT to a case of inattentional blindness, in which a subject retains a first-order visual representation of an unexpected stimulus yet fails to report or notice it, and determine whether the two views agree or diverge on whether consciousness is present; and (d) assess whether D-HOT collapses into a first-order representational theory or retains a genuinely higher-order structure, turning specifically on the distinction between representing a mental state and representing that one represents it.

Provide the essay question, the model answer, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, each worth 4 marks: (1) accurate exposition of Rosenthal’s rationale for actual tokening; (2) clear evaluation of the demandingness objection together with the relocation problem; (3) correct application to inattentional blindness with correct attribution of predictions to each view; (4) precise argument about collapse versus retention of higher-order structure; and (5) overall coherence and direct engagement with the specific crux rather than generic summary. For each point, include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail it. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and ensure everything is answerable without external sources. Keep the formulation formal and focused.
