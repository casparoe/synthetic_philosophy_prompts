At an interdisciplinary workshop on long-term societal dynamics, a senior archaeologist kept returning to the line: “Societies never collapse; they merely redistribute their complexity across smaller networks.” She used it to dismiss distinctions between the Bronze Age systems crisis, the Classic Maya decentralisation, and Easter Island’s monument cessation, insisting that in each case we witness only a downward rescaling of organisational density rather than any genuine failure. I want to understand whether this is a substantive conceptual claim or a verbal manoeuvre, but I am unsure what precise theses it might be hiding.

Could you lay out the distinct, precise readings this slogan could carry — for instance, whether it denies that population or institutional capacity ever declines sharply; whether it treats political fragmentation as the preservation of social function at local level; whether it makes a normative claim that “collapse” is an inappropriate, value-laden label for value-neutral change; or whether it rests on a specific theory of complexity (energy-based, informational, or network-theoretic) that redefines failure as efficient adaptation? For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, empirically defensible, or false, and specifically whether that reading actually supports dismissing the Bronze Age, Maya, and Easter Island cases as non-collapses.

Please apply the analysis concretely to those three cases rather than speaking only in generalities. In particular, address whether “redistributing complexity across smaller networks” genuinely captures what occurred in the Eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE, in the southern Maya lowlands after 900 CE, and on Rapa Nui after the seventeenth century — or whether each involves specific losses (of literacy, long-distance exchange, monumental coordination, or demographic recovery) that the slogan cannot accommodate without stretching “complexity” beyond useful recognition.

And please indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail — for instance, if you conflate organisational scale with social welfare, or if you overstate the coherence of the “collapse” concept you are defending.
