I'm preparing a syllabus module on Ian Hacking's looping effects for an advanced seminar in philosophy of psychiatry, and I've grown somewhat dissatisfied with the usual pedagogical examples—multiple personality disorder, childhood autism—because they have become so canonical that students can recite the standard analysis without engaging the mechanism itself. To force a more granular application of the framework, I want to calibrate looping intensity across a mixed set of categories that includes both familiar DSM-5 diagnoses and a deliberately constructed hypothetical.

Please rank the following five conditions from strongest to weakest looping effect: schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, and a hypothetical construct I will call algorithmically mediated social-anxiety presentation, defined as a diagnostic profile whose criteria are continuously rewritten by real-time feedback loops among patients, clinicians, and predictive recommendation algorithms embedded in telehealth platforms. Assign each a score between 0 and 1 representing the approximate fraction of the category's present clinical morphology that you take to be reflexively constituted rather than tracking an independent biological substrate.

For each entry, provide a brief justification that separately addresses (a) the internal loop—how being classified alters the subject's self-conception and symptom expression—and (b) the institutional loop—how the classification reshapes treatment protocols, insurance codes, and surveillance practices. Then, in a brief concluding note, identify which of the five a committed biological essentialist about psychiatric kinds would most plausibly cite as the best counterexample to looping dominance, and explain which specific clinical feature of that condition makes it resistant to reflexive constitution.
