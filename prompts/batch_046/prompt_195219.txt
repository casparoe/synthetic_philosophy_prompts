As a journalist covering philosophy of science and conceptual change, I have been observing a curious deadlock in the debate over unrealistic assumptions in scientific modeling — specifically, the enduring dispute over what philosophers call the "F-twist," derived from Milton Friedman's classic argument that a model's predictive power matters more than the literal truth of its premises. For decades, the exchange has circled through familiar positions: instrumentalists defend macroeconomic models with false microfoundations, realists insist that only structurally accurate representations yield genuine explanation, and conciliators propose that we split prediction from understanding and declare peace. Yet the literature feels circular; no experiment, no theorem, and no conceptual distinction has dislodged either camp. I am inclined to think that the stall is not accidental but structural — that the two sides are operating with different criteria for what counts as a successful model, criteria rarely made explicit because both sides use the same vocabulary of idealization and unrealistic assumption.

But I want a sharper diagnosis that engages the debate as it actually stands, not a generic survey. Is the disagreement genuinely substantive — about whether causal mechanism is a necessary condition for scientific explanation — or is it verbal, with "explanation" functioning as a placeholder for predictive utility in one discourse and dependency-mapping in another? Are there hidden empirical commitments, perhaps about whether models with false assumptions can ever be robust across counterfactual domains — a question now pressing in climate attribution and adversarial machine learning but largely absent from the Friedman debate — or is the impasse driven by unargued intuitions about scientific purpose that neither side can justify? Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each camp actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result on identifiability, a distinction between predictive and explanatory adequacy, a reframing that asks which model we should use rather than which is true, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
