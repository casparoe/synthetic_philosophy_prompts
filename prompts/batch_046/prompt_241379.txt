I am designing a ninety-minute philosophy seminar for third-year students who have encountered the basic animal-ethics literature but have never studied population ecology. I want them to see why the question “does habitat destruction reduce wild animal suffering?” is conceptually treacherous rather than merely empirically open. The tension I want to foreground involves two mechanisms that pull in opposite directions: intact ecosystems often generate enormous reproductive surplus—countless larvae, juveniles, or small mammals born into rapid death—while fragmented or degraded habitats suppress total biomass yet can intensify per-capita suffering through edge-effect predation, thermal stress, and disrupted foraging patterns. I have been framing this as a “welfare-density paradox,” though I am unsure whether that framing clarifies or obscures the issue, and I want the students to recognize that evaluating habitat change requires choosing between aggregate suffering, average suffering, and suffering intensity rather than defaulting to an intuitive “nature is balanced” view.

Plan the explanatory arc for this session. First, specify which conceptual distinction must be in place before the empirical question even makes sense—perhaps the difference between suffering-reduction-through-population-decline versus suffering-reduction-through-niche-compression, or between aggregate and average welfare metrics—and why that distinction must come before any discussion of forests or clear-cuts. Then recommend one concrete carrying scenario that can sustain the entire discussion; I had considered comparing a continuous boreal forest with a mosaic of logging clear-cuts and secondary growth for small-mammal populations, but suggest a different comparison if it better exposes the paradox without requiring specialized ecological knowledge. Lay out the exact order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the counterintuitive possibility—that destruction could, under some metrics, be welfare-positive—feels like a logical consequence of the framework rather than a provocation.

Next, map the misconceptions these students are likely to bring or form in sequence. They will likely arrive assuming that wild nature involves minimal suffering and that human interference is harmful primarily because it causes visible, immediate harm; they may then conflate biodiversity preservation with welfare preservation, or assume that a lower population automatically entails less total suffering without accounting for suffering intensity per individual. For each misconception, indicate precisely where in the sequence it should be anticipated and structurally defused—not as an aside, but as a hinge that moves the discussion forward. Then tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level—perhaps explicit r/K-selection dynamics applied to suffering calculus, or detailed trophic-cascade mathematics—and how to acknowledge that boundary without sounding dismissive. Finally, provide one diagnostic question I can pose in the last fifteen minutes that will reveal whether they have grasped the central conceptual dependency—that the welfare evaluation of habitat change hinges on which suffering metric one privileges—rather than merely adopting a reflexive pro- or anti-conservation stance.
