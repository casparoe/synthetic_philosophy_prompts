I've been working through some readings on consciousness and cognitive architecture for a course I'm taking, and I keep getting stuck on a specific conceptual tension rather than the broad outline of the theories themselves. The basic contrast is familiar: the Cartesian theater assumes there's a single spatiotemporal point in the brain where diverse sensory and cognitive streams converge to be presented to a central observer, giving experience its unified "now" quality. Dennett's multiple drafts model rejects this convergence point, proposing instead that the brain produces parallel, competing narratives—various drafts—that are updated at different rates, with no privileged "final" version that gets shown to consciousness.

What I need explained is the deeper conceptual machinery behind rejecting the theater, specifically regarding temporal binding and the authority of subjective report. In the theater picture, the stability of "I am experiencing this now" derives from a centralized presentation event: information arrives, is synthesized, and is witnessed. The drafts model dissolves this event, suggesting that what we call the present is just whichever narrative happens to dominate verbal report or behavioral control at a given moment. But this raises a puzzle I can't resolve: if there is genuinely no canonical draft, then on what basis does one report carry authoritative weight rather than merely being the loudest? Is the core error of the theater metaphysical—positing an inner observer or homunculus—or is it also phenomenological, in that it falsely describes conscious experience as possessing a single, continuous "stream" with sharp temporal boundaries? And can the drafts model genuinely eliminate the need for a unified experiencing subject, or does it merely redistribute that unity across successive temporal slices, leaving us with a fragmented "subject" that is no less mysterious?

Please explain this at a somewhat granular conceptual level: how abandoning a spatiotemporal convergence point changes what it means to "experience" something, whether the drafts model requires us to reconceive subjective authority as a kind of functional dominance rather than witnessed presence, and whether there remains any coherent sense in which consciousness has a unified "owner" if every draft is genuinely on equal ontological footing.
