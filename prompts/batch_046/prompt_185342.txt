I've been trying to work out whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can just pick one and run with it, by going off my gut on cases.

If I lie to a friend and feel guilty afterwards, that feels right and useful. If I lie and only feel ashamed because I got caught, that feels like the wrong kind of bad feeling — it's about my image, not the harm.

But then a culture that uses public shaming for corruption seems to work in some places, while a culture that makes people feel guilty for social failures seems to isolate people. So maybe shame is actually better for collective stuff?

Case A: a surgeon makes a mistake that kills a patient and feels deep guilt, then fixes the system. That seems good. Case B: same surgeon feels shame and hides it. That seems bad.

Case C: someone born into poverty feels shame about their status even though they did nothing. That's clearly harmful shame.

Here's the weird case I made up: imagine a culture called the Velari where moral education doesnt use guilt or shame. Instead they cultivate "reflective dissappointment" — you sit with the person you wronged, they describe how your action affected them, and you're expected to feel sorrow that isnt about your character or debt. Is that just guilt/shame in disguise, or a real third option?

Also, if a culture decides guilt is toxic and replaces all moral education with pride-based norms, have they lost morality?

My verdicts: guilt is better for individual repair, shame is okay only when tied to actions not identity, and cultures probably cant fully opt out of both without losing accountability. But I keep going back and forth on whether the Velari thing counts as culture choosing, or if guilt and shame are biologicaly hardwired so culture can only redirect them.

Is there a principle that captures when guilt beats shame and vice versa? What does it say about the Velari case and about suppressing guilt? And is there a case where my verdicts clearly clash?
