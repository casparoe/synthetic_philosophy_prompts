As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue for a literary work on intergenerational ethics, I have drafted the following argument for one of my characters—a moral philosopher who holds that our obligations not to harm are structurally different from any obligations to benefit, yet finds that conviction driving her toward paralysis. She wishes to know precisely where the reasoning slips.

(1) The procreation asymmetry holds: if a potential person would suffer, there is a decisive moral reason not to bring them into existence; if a potential person would flourish, there is no parallel reason to bring them into existence.
(2) The best explanation for this asymmetry is a broader principle: moral duty is asymmetrical with respect to harm and benefit. To cause harm is always wrong; to fail to cause benefit is not wrong.
(3) This principle is not confined to reproductive choice. It governs any action that might affect the welfare of potential or actual beings.
(4) Every concrete action—writing a novel that might distress unknown readers, adopting a policy that shifts demographic outcomes, or sustaining one's own life through resources that could have supported others—carries some non-negligible risk of causing harm.
(5) By the principle in (2), any action that carries a risk of causing harm is morally prohibited.
(6) Therefore every possible action is prohibited, including inaction, which also carries risks.
(7) Since universal prohibition is incoherent, the argument must fail.

She suspects the error lies either in a quantifier shift between direct causation and statistical risk, or in an overextension of the asymmetry from the reproductive domain to all practical choice; but she is also open to the possibility that (2) itself is too strong and must be qualified. Her priority is to retain (1) if possible. Which step should be rejected, why does it initially appear sound, and what does its rejection cost for the broader theory? In particular, can the procreation asymmetry survive without collapsing into either universal inaction or a symmetrical duty to maximize benefit? Please frame the diagnosis with the precision of philosophical analysis, but in language suited to a literary dialogue—that is, without reliance on technical bibliographies or specialized citations.
