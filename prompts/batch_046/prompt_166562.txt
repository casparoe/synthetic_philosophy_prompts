I take the view that the standard welfare-state argument for immigration restrictions — that open borders would overburden public goods and redistributive programs — misses the deeper problem. The real issue, I think, is what we might call bounded reciprocity: democratic welfare states depend not just on fiscal sustainability but on a shared intergenerational understanding among citizens that they are co-participants in a specific cooperative project, where today's contributors accept obligations to future beneficiaries precisely because they recognize them as members of the same bounded scheme. Open borders don't merely add costs; they dilute the phenomenology of mutual recognition that makes high-tax redistribution politically stable, and residency-based welfare rules can't restore it because the damage is to the associative framework itself, not to the budget line. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings about immigrant integration, fiscal contribution, or social trust in high-migration societies would count decisively against it; which conceptual distinctions — say, between associative obligations and mere preference for similarity, or between democratic closure and corporate exclusivity — would undermine the move from "cooperative scheme" to "border"; whether analogies to internal mobility (federalism, free movement within states) show that bounded reciprocity can survive without territorial closure; and whether the view risks insulating itself by redefining "mutual recognition" to exclude newcomers by fiat. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones defenders of bounded-reciprocity closure would themselves concede as damaging, and assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a theoretical vice for a view of this kind.
