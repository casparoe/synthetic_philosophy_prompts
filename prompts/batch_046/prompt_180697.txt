I'm trying to sort out my own gut intuitions about epistemic injustice for a course discussion, and I'm stuck because my cases don't seem to line up under one label. Here goes: a Black woman physician gets asked for credentials constantly while white male colleagues don't, and when she corrects a misdiagnosis she's called "aggressive" — clear testimonial injustice, no doubt. Then there's a deaf student in a philosophy seminar who uses sign-language concepts that don't map neatly onto English words for "knowledge" and "belief"; the professor treats it as confusion and grades her down. That feels hermeneutical, but also like a different beast from the first. Then there's this weirder one I keep thinking about — a community organizer from a favela describes police violence with a local category that blends fear and structural neglect; academics cite her work but translate her terms into standard sociological jargon without credit, then say her original framing was "theoretically unsophisticated." I want to call this injustice too, but it's not that they don't hear her; it's that they hear her, take her conceptual stuff, and then downgrade her for not sounding like them. Let's call it extractive hermeneutics, though I'm not sure Fricker's framework captures it cleanly.

But now compare that to a small-town librarian who notices the catalog system files every book by local indigenous authors under "folklore" regardless of genre. She writes the catalog company, they reply that the taxonomy is "neutral" and global, and she eventually stops pushing because she lacks the technical vocabulary to fight back. My roommate says that's just burocratic inertia, not epistemic injustice, but I feel like it's wronging her as a knower somehow. Here's the tension that breaks me: in the organizer case the academics over-understand her framework just enough to steal it, then deny her credibility; in the librarian case they never understand her framing at all, and she can't enter the conversation. One is extraction-with-comprehension, the other is exclusion-without-comprehension. Can one principle cover both without getting hopelessly messy?

Also, I haven't decided on this one: a science blogger with no formal credentials writes a correct explanation of quantum decoherance, and physicists ignore him not because he's wrong but because he writes in meme-heavy language. Is that testimonial injustice, or is weighting institutional credentials okay? So please tell me: is there a precise principle that saves my verdicts on cases 1-4 (treating case 3 as that niche extractive kind), predicts the blogger case, and doesn't force me to drop either the organizer or the librarian? Or do I have to choose between a broad account that saves all four but gets complicated, and a cleaner one that dumps one? Lay out the principle clause by clause, say which case motivates each part, state its verdict on the blogger, and be honest about which of my gut calls it can't accommodate.
