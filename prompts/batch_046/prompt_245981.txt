Hey—quick question for you. There's this old stall in the negative-utilitarian litterature: the lexical-threshold people (who think any amount of suffering outweighs any amount of pleasure) and the commensurability people (who think pleasure and pain sit on the same scale and can be traded off) have been talking past each other since at least the 80s, maybe earlier. But I'm not convinced it's just one debate. It feels like half the disagreement is about whether affective states have a common metric at all, and half is about whether moral reasoning should be lexical—like, is 'worse' here a metaphysical claim or a decision-procedure?

Lately I've been playing with this idea that both sides are stuck because they treat pain and pleasure as properties of individual subjects that can be summed or ranked. But what if the unit of comparison isn't the private feeling but something like an intersubjective calibration—like, the 'badness' of a state partly depends on whether it's recognized or shared in a social frame? If that's right, then asking whether pain is lexically worse than pleasure might be the wrong shape of question entirely, because there's no isolated unit to apply the ordering to.

Anyway, can you diagnose what's actually going on? Is it verbal? Substantive? Are they answering different questions? And if progress is possible, what kind—conceptual, empirical, formal—would actually break the logjam? Don't bother with a lit referance, just work with the positions as they stand.
