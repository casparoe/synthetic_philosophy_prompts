I am preparing a long-form feature on how philosophers of action are rethinking rationality in contexts of real-time feedback. In the course of my reporting, I have become preoccupied with a tension between embedded agency—the view that an agent's deliberative capacities are partly constituted by the environmental, social, and technological loops in which she is enmeshed—and the Archimedean spectator model, which holds that genuine practical reasoning requires a representational standpoint that is causally insulated from the very states being evaluated. I grasp the broad outlines of both positions, yet I find myself stuck on a conceptual pressure point that my interview subjects have not resolved to my satisfaction.

Consider, for the sake of analysis, a climate-adaptation algorithm that does not merely forecast human behavior but continuously revises its own utility weights in direct response to the social and ecological shifts induced by its prior recommendations. The embedded framework seems to treat this loop as constitutive of reasoning itself, whereas the spectator model treats it as a corruption of objectivity. Could you compare these two conceptions of agency, focusing particularly on whether the embedded view can preserve a coherent normative distinction—say, between adaptive learning and a self-reinforcing bias—without secretly smuggling in the very detached, causally insulated standpoint it officially rejects? I want a conceptual comparison that remains internal to the logic of the theories themselves, not a survey of the literature or a list of further reading.
