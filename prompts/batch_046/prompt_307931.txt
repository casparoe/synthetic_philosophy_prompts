I'm trying to clarify where I actually stand on moral status and permissible treatment across different kinds of beings, working partly from what I take to be the spirit of Nozick's bifurcation — side-constraint morality for persons, aggregative welfare considerations for non-persons — but finding that my gut reactions don't fall cleanly on either side of that line. I want to see whether a refined principle can capture my judgments, or whether I have to abandon some of them for the sake of coherence.

Here are my verdicts. Factory-farming sentient animals for food when adequate alternatives exist strikes me as wrong. A global ecological intervention that eliminates wild predators and replaces them with herbivorous species, thereby reducing total prey suffering across generations, strikes me as permissible and perhaps even required. Killing one healthy human being to harvest organs and save five others is clearly wrong. Euthanizing one healthy cow to rescue five other cows from a burning barn, with minimal suffering, strikes me as permissible. Engineering a "hedonarium" of genetically simplified chickens designed for constant mild contentment but lacking autonomy, and using them to replace wild bird populations in order to maximize global welfare, strikes me as wrong despite the welfare gain. A synthetic distributed system that reports aversive states when its nodes are disrupted, yet has no evolutionary history, no biological substrate, and no persistent self-model, should be protected from arbitrary disruption, but its interests do not outweigh those of a single mammal. Forcing a human being into a dangerous medical trial that saves five strangers is wrong; forcing an animal with comparable behavioral complexity into an analogous trial, if suffering is minimized and no side-constraint applies, is permissible. Lying to a dying person solely to comfort them, when they have explicitly asked for the truth, is wrong even if welfare increases.

Is there one account — or a small family of candidates — that delivers all of these verdicts? State the principle precisely, map each clause to the case that motivates it, and say what it predicts for an unlisted case: a synthetic environment populated by thousands of digital entities that experience episodic suffering only during discrete computation cycles, with no continuous identity linking one cycle to the next. If simplicity requires overturning one or more of my judgments, identify which and defend the revision. Finally, say whether the resulting view remains recognizably Nozickian, or whether it collapses into a graded, pluralistic, or otherwise different framework altogether.
