I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a close friend about whether Spinoza's democratic theory in the Tractatus Politicus is genuinely normative or merely descriptive of collective power. For a podcast interview next week, I'm speaking with a scholar who defends what she calls a threshold model of collective rationality: democratic sovereignty is legitimate only when the multitudo achieves a minimal density of rational agreement, and the familiar exclusions of women, dependents, and foreigners are not contingent prejudices but structural conditions for sustaining that density. I'd like eight to ten interview questions that avoid having her restate the thesis. Specifically: at least one question pressing how this threshold distinguishes Spinoza from Hobbes's account of democratic dissolution into faction; at least one on whether the exclusions collapse into circularity, since democracy is said to require rational citizens yet rationality is partly defined through democratic participation; and at least one on whether the model can survive Spinoza's claim that democracy is the most natural form of government without rendering that claim vacuously true. For each of these, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, especially any appeal to the distinction between the power of the multitude when guided by reason and when led by passion. I also want one final question that injects a tension she has not addressed in print: if the threshold is genuinely rational rather than demographic, what prevents a small, homogeneous council of rational agents from counting as the true democratic sovereign, and does that not reduce democracy to aristocracy under another name?
