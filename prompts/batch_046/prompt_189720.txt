I'm preparing a unit for my graduate seminar on comparative moral psychology, and my students have been arguing—somewhat heatedly—about whether societies that celebrate exceptional achievement (the American Dream narrative) or those that practice deliberate suppression of visible distinction (Jantelagen, the tall poppy syndrome) possess the more defensible account of desert. Rather than lecture, I would like you to stage a dialogue between two interlocutors, Mira and Jonas, who are equally philosophically competent and neither of whom functions as a strawman. Mira should defend the view that cutting down the successful reflects not mere envy but a substantive moral insight: that desert is indexical to the social structures that recognize it, and that celebrating outliers corrupts our normative vocabulary by conflating structural advantage with individual virtue, thereby making merit impossible to track accurately. Jonas should defend the opposing view: that suppressing visible distinction destroys the very epistemic conditions under which merit can operate—if achievement is never made public, communities lose the feedback mechanisms that distinguish genuine excellence from mere conformity, and egalitarian restraint collapses into a hidden hierarchy of power masked by false modesty. Crucially, they must respond directly to one another's arguments: Mira should address Jonas's claim about epistemic feedback and visibility, and Jonas should address Mira's claim about the indexicality of desert. To keep the abstraction grounded, have them discuss a single concrete scenario—perhaps a small-town engineer who develops an inexpensive water-filtration device and receives both local acclaim and local resentment—working through whether the community's response reveals or conceals the true nature of her contribution. Please write in a formal, philosophical register appropriate for a seminar discussion. Finally, please state explicitly how confident you are in the coherence of each position presented, and single out the specific point in your own analysis where you are least sure.
