hey, been thinking about the color phi phenomemon and that Orwellian vs Stalinesque distinction Dennett draws — the one where either your brain retroactively edits the memory trace after the fact, or it never lets the discrete flashes into consciousness at all and just stages the intermediate experience like a show trial. i tried extending it to a hypothetical "jitter-phi" case where the red and green flashes come with random millisecond noise and slight retinal position jitter, so the physical sequence is actually scrambled, but subjects still report the same smooth red-to-green trajectory. here's the argument i built and now it feels like it eats itself:

(1) in standard color phi, subjects report seeing a dot move from A to B and change color midway, even though there's no green at A, no red at B, and no physical stimulus at the midpoint.

(2) the Orwellian account says the brain first registers two discrete conscious events — red-at-A, then green-at-B — then retroactively revises the memory so the subject remembers a continuous trajectory.

(3) the Stalinesque account says the brain never registers those discrete flashes consciously at all; it directly constructs the intermediate moving-dot experience and suppresses the raw inputs.

(4) now run jitter-phi: same colors, but with randomized interstimulus intervals and positional jitter, so the neural input sequence is genuinely chaotic — sometimes green reaches the retina before red, sometimes they overlap spatially.

(5) subjects still report the canonical smooth trajectory.

(6) if Orwellian revision is right, then the brain must have reconstructed not just a false intermediate state but an entire coherent temporal order ("first red, then green") from inputs that never formed such a sequence. that means the revision mechanism isn't correcting an experienced sequence — it's inventing one from noise.

(7) if Stalinesque construction is right, then there was never a "true" discrete sequence to suppress in the first place, since the inputs were scrambled. so "suppression of the original witnesses" can't do the explanatory work.

(8) either way, both accounts collapse into the same claim: the brain writes a narrative that doesn't track any actual sequence of neural registrations.

(9) but if both mechanisms can operate even when there's no original sequence to revise or suppress, there's no principled reason they can't operate on every perceptual sequence.

(10) therefore, for any conscious experience, we have no justification for believing its subjective temporal order reflects the actual order of neural events; every "before" and "after" in consciousness might just be post-hoc fabrication.

(11) if that's true, then the stream of consciousness has no real temporal structure — it's just isolated moments with false ordering relations slapped on them.

(12) but the very concept of a "stream" requires real temporal relations, so consciousness as we understand it is impossible.

i'd really like to keep (1) through (3) intact because i think the distinction is genuinely illuminating, and i'm also attached to (9) being at least plausibly true for phi cases specifically. where does the chain snap? which step should i reject, and what does rejecting it cost me — do i have to give up on generalizing from illusion to ordinary perception, or is there a hidden scope shift between "no justification for this sequence" and "no justification for any sequence"? also, if i keep (9), am i forced into some kind of global skepticism about temporal experience, or can i contain the damage?
