I have been noticing a particular structure in debates about accountability for autonomous systems, and I am unsure whether it is recognized as a distinct phenomenon or merely a variant of the familiar responsibility gap. The pattern works like this: an institution deploys an automated decision-making system, but retains a human in a formally accountable role—say, a safety driver in an autonomous-vehicle trial, or an operator who must sign off on an AI-generated targeting recommendation. The system is engineered so that meaningful human intervention is practically impossible: the decision window is shorter than human reaction time, the data load exceeds human processing capacity, or institutional incentives strongly penalize overriding the algorithm. When harm occurs, the deploying institution points to the human's formal authority—“you were in the loop”—and assigns blame to the human for failing to intervene, even though the architecture made vigilant oversight unrealistic. Meanwhile, the institution retains control over the system's design, updates, and deployment conditions, avoiding structural liability.

This seems different from a pure responsibility gap, where no human agent is available to bear blame at all. Here, blame is not absent but displaced onto someone who has been structurally stripped of the power to prevent the outcome. It also feels distinct from automation bias—which describes a psychological tendency to defer to machines—because the issue is not merely a cognitive failure but an institutional allocation of liability that exploits that tendency. And it is narrower than general criticisms of meaningful human control, since those can apply even when humans genuinely could intervene but choose not to.

My question is whether this configuration has an established name in the philosophy of technology or law. If so, how exactly is it delimited: does it require that the human's disempowerment be engineered rather than incidental? Must the formal responsibility be legal, or can it be purely moral? And if there is no precise term, how should one distinguish it from neighboring concepts—for instance, from the problem of many hands, from organizational scapegoating, or from standard cases of diffused responsibility? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know what the phenomenon essentially is, where its boundaries lie, and which existing framework captures it best.
