I'm writing an essay for my philosophy of economics course, where we've been debating whether countries should treat the World Happiness Report as a legitimate policy target or whether its aggregate life-satisfaction scores necessarily conceal distributional failures. I want to read Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" not as a generic utilitarian fable, but as a precise dramatization of how happiness metrics can become conceptually detached from the suffering that structurally sustains them.

Read the story through the lens of happiness measurement and policy optimization. Omelas is presented as a society of genuine, widespread flourishing—its citizens enjoy art, learning, and community—yet this aggregate well-being is causally dependent on the hidden, extreme suffering of a single child. The citizens who stay appear to rationalize this by treating the child's misery as a necessary condition for the statistical average; those who leave reject the framing altogether. I want you to treat the narrator's refusal to describe the child's experience in detail, and the citizens' simultaneous knowledge and avoidance, as analogues to specific features of large-scale well-being surveys—particularly the way aggregate indices can render outlier suffering statistically invisible while still allowing respondents to report high life evaluation.

Specifically, does the story illuminate a distinction between aggregate societal happiness and the distribution of that happiness, and does it suggest that optimizing for the former necessarily requires institutionalized ignorance of the latter? Where does the philosophical idea help us understand why some citizens stay—are they acting like policymakers who maximize average utility—or where does the text resist that reading by insisting that their happiness is not false consciousness but genuinely experienced? I'm also interested in whether Le Guin's narrative form itself enacts a methodological critique: by withholding descriptive access to the child, does the story mirror how survey instruments abstract away from particular suffering? Please work with the plot and major scenes as they are commonly taught, avoid giving me bibliographies or lists of philosophers to consult, and end by saying what this reading adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone doesn't capture—particularly whether a society can be said to be "happy" in a policy-relevant sense when its happiness is causally tethered to an invisible exception.
