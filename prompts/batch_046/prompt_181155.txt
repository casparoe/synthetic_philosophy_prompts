I'm writing a paper for an advanced moral philosophy seminar on the persistence of Glaucon's challenge in the Republic — specifically, whether the ring of Gyges genuinely demonstrates that justice is merely conventional and pursued only out of fear of punishment and desire for reward. In discussion with friends, we've kept circling the same problem: many modern responses seem to answer Glaucon only by showing that justice is instrumentally useful, not by showing that an agent who is genuinely invisible — with no prospect of reputation, reciprocity, or social sanction — still possesses a reason, moral rather than prudential, not to commit injustice. Before I assess any particular account, I want the full specification of what it would take to meet the challenge properly.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of moral motivation must satisfy in order to count as answering Glaucon. For each requirement, state the phenomenon it must explain, the distinction it must preserve, or the constraint it must respect, together with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a mere preference. The list should include, but need not be limited to: how the theory handles the invisibility condition (no reputation costs, no risk of detection); whether it distinguishes having a prudential reason to appear just from having a genuinely moral reason to be just; whether it can account for the comparison between the just man who suffers unjustly and the unjust man who prospers, without collapsing that comparison into a calculus of hidden benefits; whether it respects findings in social psychology about cooperation and defection under conditions of anonymity; and whether it allows justice to have value independent of its instrumental returns, without thereby becoming rationally inert for agents who do not already care about it. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable conditions of success and which are camp-specific preferences — for instance, demands that morality be categorically rational for every agent regardless of their contingent desires, or demands that the explanation be compatible with evolutionary explanations of cooperation.

Then, identify whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: for example, whether requiring moral reasons to be both categorical and derivable from purely self-interested rationality generates an internal contradiction, or whether demanding an intrinsic value for justice while also requiring that it motivate every rational agent creates a tension that no single theory can resolve. Finally, very briefly, indicate how three broad approaches fare against your list: Plato's own harmony-of-the-soul account, a Hobbesian contractarian view, and a modern evolutionary-cooperation model. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard, and present the answer in British English. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and avoid relying on obscure secondary texts that could not be expected to be within the training knowledge of a well-informed but non-specialist model.
