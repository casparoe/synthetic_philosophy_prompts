As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am helping a senior researcher in classical reception and moral psychology prepare for a high-stakes defense of her thesis at an interdisciplinary symposium on narrative and virtue. She argues that Plutarch’s Parallel Lives operate not as hagiographic templates for direct imitation, but as instruments of moral contour mapping: a process in which readers calibrate ethical judgment by tracking the uneven topography of a life across public triumph and private failure, rather than extracting abstract maxims or selecting a single hero to emulate. She contends that modern moral psychology has systematically misunderstood biography by treating it either as data-rich anecdote or as didactic fable, missing the intermediate cognitive work that occurs when a reader compares paired lives—Alexander against Caesar, Demetrius against Antony—not to declare a winner, but to map the shape of practical wisdom under historical constraint.

Her audience will be composed primarily of experimental moral psychologists and philosophers of education who are skeptical of narrative-based interventions. They are committed to reproducible measurement, wary of selection bias in canonical biography, and inclined to favor explicit pedagogical frameworks over implicit learning through literary form. They will likely press her on ecological validity—whether anyone actually learns this way outside seminar rooms—on the risk of hagiographic distortion in Plutarch’s sources, on whether moral contour mapping is distinguishable from ordinary emotional identification or parasocial attachment, and on whether the comparative architecture of the Parallel Lives introduces a false binary that flattens historical particularity into a moral scoreboard.

Prepare her for this audience. Identify the objections they will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary, ordered by how likely and damaging each is; note which objections carry real force and where she should concede ground up front without collapsing her position; specify the single reply that must land if her proposal is to survive—particularly regarding the cognitive mechanism that distinguishes her view from both emulation theory and principle-based moral education; and include the objection she is hoping nobody asks, namely whether her framework can survive if Plutarch’s sources are shown to be systematically unreliable in ways that undermine the comparative architecture itself. Deliver a prep sheet calibrated for seminar Q&A time: brief replies, honest notes on where each reply is weakest, and clear guidance on what to hold versus what to concede.
