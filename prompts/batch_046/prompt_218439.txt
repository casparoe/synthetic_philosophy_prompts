I'm preparing a single ninety-minute session for a mixed group of cognitive-science graduate students and clinical neurology residents on split-brain cases and what they actually show about the unity of consciousness. I want to avoid the standard pop-science narrative that "the left brain writes a story for what the right brain does," and I want to avoid the opposite oversimplification that "there are literally two people in one skull." Instead, I want the session to turn on the conceptual distinction between functional disconnection and phenomenal disunity—specifically, on whether the absence of interhemispheric information transfer necessarily implies the presence of two streams of experience, or whether it might instead reveal a single, adaptively fragmented system.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, identify the dependency order: what conceptual distinction (for instance, between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, or between narrative integration and experiential unity) must be in place before the split-brain data can even be interpreted, and what has to come only after the students have seen why that interpretation is underdetermined. Second, specify the one carrying case or thought-experiment that should anchor the session—preferably not the classic left-hand/right-hand pointing task, since they will have seen that; it should be a scenario that makes the ambiguity between "one fractured subject" and "two cooperating subjects" unavoidable rather than resolvable by intuition. Third, map the misconceptions they will bring in sequence: likely the "two people" intuition, then the "left-brain interpreter" as a homunculus, then the slide from "no reportability" to "no experience," and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Fourth, propose a single diagnostic question or exercise I can use at the end to determine whether they have grasped the conceptual problem—whether they understand why the data underdetermine the metaphysical conclusion—rather than whether they can merely recount Sperry's results. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level (for instance, specific neuroanatomical pathways beyond the corpus callosum, or full engagement with the combination problem for panpsychism) and why omitting it serves the conceptual aim.
