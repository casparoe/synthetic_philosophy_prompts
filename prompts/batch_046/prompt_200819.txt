In rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent discussions of decision theory, there is a long-running stall around whether transformative experiences—parenthood, religious conversion, severe disability acquisition—can be objects of fully rational first-person choice. One side, following the framing L.A. Paul popularized, argues that the combination of first-person epistemic opacity about phenomenal quality and the likelihood of deep preference reversal makes standard expected-utility reasoning inapplicable: you cannot compare your current utility function with the one you will have after the transformation, and testimony is structurally inadequate to bridge the gap. The opposing side treats this as an overstatement of our ignorance, arguing that statistical and testimonial evidence can provide sufficient indirect information, and that diachronic consistency norms—perhaps something like a modified Savage axiom applied across temporal parts—are enough to preserve rationality even when values shift. For years, neither camp has moved, yet they continue to talk past each other in forums and reading groups.

My hypothesis is that the stall is not purely about epistemic access, but that a hidden normative disagreement is doing the work: the first camp implicitly treats the preservation of one's current evaluative framework as a constraint on rational choice, while the second treats value-revision-through-experience as not only permissible but sometimes required for authenticity. Meanwhile, both sides frame their arguments using the vocabulary of information and decision theory, which obscures the fact that they may be answering different questions—one about individual deliberation under uncertainty, the other about whether a policy of encouraging transformation is defensible—under the single heading of rational choice about transformative experience.

Diagnose this disagreement for me. Is it verbal—are they using terms like rational or choice in different technical senses, perhaps ex ante instrumental versus ex post integrative? Is it substantive but masked, with a hidden formal question in the background, such as whether a discontinuous shift in utility across a transformation violates the independence axiom in a recoverable way, or whether we need a branching multi-agent model to represent the decision problem? Or are they simply defending bedrock intuitions about whether it is better to remain the kind of person one currently is? Support your diagnosis by engaging with what each side actually claims—the specific moves about testimony, preference reversal, and consistency—not with generic summaries. And tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates the epistemic from the normative claim; an empirical study about the reliability of pre-transformation forecasting; a formal result about decision rules under value-branching; a reframing that dissolves the question; or an argument that the disagreement is ill-posed and no progress should be expected, with reasons.
