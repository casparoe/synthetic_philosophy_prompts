I've come to hold a modified Scanlonian contractualist view that treats the aggregation problem not as a puzzle to be solved but as a correct refusal to sum harms or benefits across persons. On my view, contractualist justification operates pairwise: a principle is acceptable only if no individual can reasonably reject it, and the fact that five people suffer a given harm does not, by itself, generate a stronger complaint than one person suffering the same harm. To avoid the obvious counterintuitive result in rescue cases—where saving five over one seems required—I supplement this with a procedural layer rather than an aggregative one: when we face an unavoidable choice among non-fungible claims, we should rely on an ex ante fair procedure (say, a lottery or a prior agreement on selection rules) rather than on headcount. This means the view can allow a decision procedure in rescue scenarios without treating persons as interchangeable units of moral value, but it also implies that contractualism cannot justify saving the five over the one solely because there are five. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which specific intuitions about rescue and triage it must bite the bullet on, whether formal results in social choice theory (e.g., Arrow-style impossibility results applied to moral aggregation) show that any procedural rule collapses back into implicit weighting, and whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability by allowing any outcome to be retrofitted with a procedural justification. I also want to know which of these objections a committed defender of this hybrid view would themselves concede as genuinely damaging, versus which they would dismiss as presupposing exactly the aggregative intuition they reject. Please include an honest assessment of whether the position is well-insulated and, if so, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a moral theory of this kind. Answer in American English.
