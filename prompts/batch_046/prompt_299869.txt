Last night I had a long, circling argument with a close friend about whether understanding can ever be assembled out of parts that do not themselves understand, and we kept returning to Searle’s distinction between original and derived intentionality and to Dennett’s two-bitser thought experiment. Most textbook treatments, I think, miss the precise compositional problem that makes the case philosophically sharp, so I want to turn this into a final exam for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar.

Please design a take-home essay exam with the following specifications. The course is an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar; students have encountered standard material on intentionality but are not expected to have read niche secondary literature. The exam should be answerable in roughly ninety minutes of writing (target 1,200–1,500 words) and must be fully answerable without web access, so please do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to obscure texts and specific publication dates that students could not be expected to recall from memory.

The exam question itself must require three moves. First, students must reconstruct Searle’s distinction between original and derived intentionality, including the reason he gives for denying that artifacts possess the former. Second, they must explain Dennett’s two-bitser thought experiment not as a generic artificial-intelligence example, but specifically as a compositional challenge: sub-components that individually lack original intentionality are arranged into a composite that appears to possess it. Third, the question must require evaluation of whether Dennett succeeds, with explicit attention to what I will call the nesting objection—if each constituent bitser’s intentionality is derived (whether from an external interpreter or from still-smaller parts), then the composite’s intentionality seems equally derived rather than genuinely original.

Please provide all of the following. One: the exam prompt exactly as students would see it. Two: a model answer that names the specific conceptual moves a top response must make—correct reconstruction of the distinction, accurate description of the composite structure without reducing it to a standard computer program, direct engagement with the nesting objection, and a reasoned conclusion—and that notes the specific mistakes that should cost marks. Three: a marking scheme allocating points (suggested total: 30 marks) that lists identifiable misconceptions and the penalties each should carry, for instance: conflating derived intentionality with the complete absence of intentionality; describing the two-bitser as software running on hardware rather than as a composite of intentional subsystems; ignoring the nesting objection entirely; or treating Dennett’s challenge as purely about interpretation rather than about compositional emergence.

The difficulty should come from the crux, not from recall: a student who has only memorized the labels “original,” “derived,” and “Chinese Room” should be unable to score well, whereas a student who grasps the compositional logic should be able to navigate the nesting problem cleanly. Include no requests for quotations from specific texts, and avoid framing the task in terms of citation or source verification.
