I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates in a history-of-ideas course who have not previously read Hobsbawm or Anderson. I want them to understand the conceptual dependency between "invented tradition" and "imagined community"—specifically, why grasping the latter is a prerequisite for seeing the former as a political technique rather than as mere fraud—through one carrying case rather than through a comparative survey of nations. The case I intend to build around is the 1896 revival of the Olympic Games, treated as an invented tradition that simultaneously constructed an imagined international community of "civilized nations."

Plan the pedagogical arc, not as a lecture transcript but as a teaching sequence: (1) the precise order in which the two concepts must be introduced, with an account of why reversing them produces a specific conceptual collapse; (2) the exact features the Olympic-revival case must possess and must lack in order to illuminate that dependency, including what in it risks reinforcing the misconception that "invented" equals "fake" and how that risk is structurally unavoidable; (3) the predictable misconceptions students will form at each stage—particularly that "imagined" means fictional in a dismissive sense, that "tradition" implies unbroken continuity, and that inventing tradition is necessarily cynical—together with the specific point in the sequence where each should be intercepted and defused; (4) a single diagnostic question or brief exercise at the end that distinguishes students who understand the dependency (that invented traditions operate by addressing an already-imagined community) from those who have merely memorized the two terms; and (5) the conceptual refinement or historical complication you would deliberately omit at this level—such as the distinction between invented and genuinely revived traditions, or Anderson's typology of census, map, and museum—and an explicit justification for why leaving it out protects the core idea rather than simplifying it dishonestly. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the design must stand entirely on conceptual reasoning and the internal logic of the case.
