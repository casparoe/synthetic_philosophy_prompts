I'm drafting the final examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar on moral judgment and historical agency, and I want one essay question that targets the so-called "man of his time" defence specifically as applied to Thomas Jefferson's slaveholding, Winston Churchill's imperial policies including the British response to the 1943 Bengal famine, and Christopher Columbus's initiation of the violent subjugation and enslavement of the Taíno. To keep the question from being answered by reciting the standard taxonomy of excuses—ignorance, cultural embeddedness, lack of agency—I want it built around a conceptual distinction I have been developing in the course: the difference between an epistemic archive that contained live, articulated moral objections within the agent's own intellectual and social milieu (what I call an "available counter-voice") and a moral horizon in which the very conceptual categories needed to formulate the objection had not yet achieved any institutional or discursive presence. The exam should ask students to adjudicate whether the defence succeeds or fails for each figure depending on whether the relevant wrong was one for which a counter-voice existed in their archive but was ignored or suppressed, versus one for which the conceptual resources for objection were genuinely absent from their horizon. The question must also require them to confront a tension: if Jefferson is more blameworthy than Columbus because abolitionist arguments were available to him, are we implicitly committing ourselves to the view that moral blame tracks not the objective wrongness of the act but the agent's access to dissenting voices—and is that a defensible metric?

Please write the exam question, a model answer of approximately 650 words, and a marking scheme for a 60-minute essay worth 25 marks. The marking scheme must separate an A-level answer (22–25) from a B-level answer (17–21) by identifying at least four specific conceptual moves the top answer must contain—such as precisely distinguishing archive from horizon, applying the distinction consistently across all three cases without flattening them, identifying the tension about blame tracking access rather than wrongness, and resolving or accommodating that tension without collapsing back into a simple "they should have known better" verdict. For each required move, include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail at B level. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; students have engaged primary sources in the course, but the answer must be fully evaluable without reference to specific secondary scholarship. The difficulty should come from applying the archive-horizon framework with philosophical precision rather than from recalling biographical detail.
