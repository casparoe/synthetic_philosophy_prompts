Me and my friend were listening to two people in a seminar — Jordan and Riley — argue about hedonism and the heterogeniety problem. Jordan started by saying every pleasure has to share some minimal affective tone, like a faint "this is good" signal, even in intellectual stuff like nailing a proof; otherwise there's no reason to call them all pleasure. Riley said that's just projecting sensory examples onto everything else — the pleasure of getting a proof is more like seeing a pattern lock into place, there's no shared glow, and calling it a "feel-good tone" is just metaphor. Jordan then switched tactics: okay, drop the sensation view, pleasure is just being in a pro-attitude toward an experience, you either want it to continue or you'd seek it out again. Riley pushed back with a few cases — people finish long books they aren't enjoying just to be finishers, they watch tragedies they wouldn't sit through twice and still call it enjoyment, and someone can take deep satisfaction in seeing a rare sunset knowing it'll never happen again, so "wanting more" clearly isn't doing the work. Jordan admitted those cases muddy things but said Riley hasn't shown there's no common factor at all, just that pro-attitude is too simple; he suggested maybe pleasure is a kind of cognitive endorsement with openness but conceded he can't draw a clean line between that and plain approval. They asked us to referee but we stayed out of it. Can you adjudicate move by move? Rate each of Jordan's and Riley's points — the hidden-tone claim, the metaphor objection, the pro-attitude pivot, and the counterexample push — as clearly wrong, partly right, or clearly right, and say what each is missing. Then take a committed position: can hedonism survive the heterogeniety problem without secretly relying on a uniform feeling, or does the diversity of pleasures break the theory? Don't hedge — pick a side and defend it.
