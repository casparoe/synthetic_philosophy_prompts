I'm taking a course on the philosophy of history, and our seminar has been debating whether entities like climate systems, pathogens, or volcanic eruptions can be understood as historical actors without reducing human history to environmental determinism. For next week I need to work through a passage from the ancient Hippocratic treatise On Airs, Waters, and Places. In the section I have in mind, the author argues that the physical qualities of a region—whether its air is sharp or soft, whether its waters are stagnant or flowing—actively shape the bodies, temperaments, and even political dispositions of the people who live there. Inhabitants of rugged, windy highlands are described as growing hardy and liberty-loving, while those of flat, marshy lowlands become softer in body and more submissive to rule. The text presents this not as accidental correlation but as a formative process, as if the environment were molding human possibility from the outside.

What I find difficult is identifying the precise logic of causation at work. Is the treatise attributing to climate and geography something like agency—an ability to direct outcomes—without invoking human-like intention? Or is it better read as describing a set of constraining background conditions that limit but do not determine human development? I want to avoid anachronistically projecting modern ideas—such as treating climate as an actant within distributed networks of causation—back onto ancient medicine, yet I also wonder whether comparing this framework to modern discussions of germs or volcanic winters (for instance, the global agricultural crisis following the 1816 eruption of Mount Tambora) might illuminate what is genuinely distinctive about Hippocrates's view. Could you help me parse where the passage risks sliding into determinism and where it leaves space for human adaptation or resistance? And could you suggest how to compare ancient environmental causation with these modern cases of non-human historical influence without committing a category mistake?
