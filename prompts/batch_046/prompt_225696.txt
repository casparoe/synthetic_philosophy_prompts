After retiring from four decades in experimental ecology, I have lately been auditing philosophy seminars and reading in political theory with a small discussion circle at the university extension; my background makes me impatient with arguments that invoke historical authority without specifying the causal mechanism being defended. We have been debating Chesterton's fence—whether it is a genuine principle of institutional epistemology or a rhetorical shield for inertia—but the popular treatments conflate the preservation of concrete practices with the preservation of decision-procedures, and I want a more disciplined analysis. Please draft the joint statement that a Burkean traditionalist and a reformist institutionalist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The traditionalist holds that the fence applies uniformly: we should not remove either a first-order social practice or a second-order institutional decision-rule until we understand why it exists, because in both cases implicit knowledge is embedded in the structure. The institutionalist accepts the fence for first-order practices but introduces a distinction—call it the second-order fence problem—arguing that when institutional memory has decayed, the decision-rules no longer carry embedded knowledge, so reforming them is not an act of epistemic arrogance but of reconstruction. The document should begin with a numbered section of agreed claims about Burke's actual arguments, the logical structure of the fence principle, the conditions under which an institution's purpose is genuinely opaque, and the coherence of the first-order versus second-order distinction. Follow this with a disagreements table: the precise formulations of divergence, phrased so that each side accepts the wording of its own view; the cruxes—especially whether institutional-memory decay is empirically widespread in modern governance, whether the burden of proof lies with the reformer or the conservative, and whether second-order reconstruction risks cascading failure; and, for each crux, a concrete historical or hypothetical case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Note also whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal rather than substantive. Write formally, without requesting bibliographies or citations, and conclude by stating how confident you are in the framing—particularly in the claim that the second-order distinction clarifies rather than invents the disagreement—and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
