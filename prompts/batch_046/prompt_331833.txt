I'm trying to stabilize a position in Buddhist ethics for a seminar and need a consistency audit before I present it. Here are the commitments I want to hold together:

(1) The tradition's ethics is best reconstructed as long-horizon, agent-neutral consequentialism: an act is right in proportion to its expected reduction of dukkha across the causal chain of rebirths, with no special weight to the agent's own future suffering.

(2) The anatta doctrine denies any enduring self that "owns" karma, so self-interested avoidance of future pain is rationally baseless; ethical motivation should be non-possessive and other-regarding.

(3) At the same time, cetana (intention/volition) is the primary locus of moral evaluation, not outcomes: an act performed with pure compassion but poor results is morally superior to one with excellent welfare results but malicious intent.

(4) The sila precepts are not deontological side-constraints but skillful means (upaya)—heuristics calibrated for beings with limited foresight—and can be overridden in principle.

(5) From (1) and (4), I derive that an advanced practitioner should violate a precept—say, lying to prevent violence—whenever the direct welfare calculus clearly favors it.

(6) I also hold that nirvana, the complete cessation of dukkha, is the only intrinsic good; virtue, wisdom, and compassion are valuable only instrumentally.

(7) In concrete cases, this yields two verdicts: a surgeon who accidentally kills a suffering patient while distracted by greed for a fee has acted gravely wrong, even though the outcome reduced suffering; but a bodhisattva who tells a compassionate lie to save lives is required to do so.

Please audit this package in American English. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear incompatible but dissolve on inspection, and what is the least costly revision for each real conflict? Rank the tensions by severity, specify whether the problem lies in the general principles or in how they apply to (7), and for each tension say exactly what would have to be revised—whether (1), (3), (6), or something else. Do not treat every pair as equally problematic; discriminate carefully.
