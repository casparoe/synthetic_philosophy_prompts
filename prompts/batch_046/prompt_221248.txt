I am preparing a 75-minute session for an upper-level philosophy of social science seminar of about eighteen students, and I want an in-class simulation that lets them feel rational herding as a structural problem rather than a mere failure of independence. The idea should emerge from the rules, not from my explanation afterward: students should discover for themselves that individually rational inference can lock a group into a collectively wrong trajectory even when every member holds some private evidence pointing the other way.

Design the activity around a hidden binary state—say, whether a proposed institutional rule is genuinely beneficial—about which each student receives a private signal of varying strength. Add the following twist: in addition to the signal, each student draws a hidden “inferential weight” card that determines how much their private signal should rationally count against the visible history of previous declarations. On each turn, a student may either declare an action (support or oppose) based on their private information and the public record, or pay a small penalty—losing one of three “credibility tokens” they need to remain in good standing—to reveal both their signal and their weight card before declaring. The payoff scheme should reward correct group identification of the hidden state with shared points, while also awarding individual points for being personally correct, so that following the crowd can be individually optimal even when it suppresses dispersed knowledge. Provide clear rules, paper-based materials with no technology, a minute-by-minute timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps specific moments—such as a student paying to reveal and either breaking or reinforcing the cascade—onto the formal conditions for an information cascade. Include an honest note about what this design cannot reproduce: in real-world cascades people rarely face an explicit priced option to disclose their reasoning, so the facilitator must supply that institutional gap. Finally, predict what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely that students will either treat the penalty as trivial and over-reveal, or misinterpret the weight cards as deterministic instructions rather than Bayesian weights—and tell me how to correct it in the moment.
