You are delivering the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop titled Intoxication, Judgment, and Civic Order, hosted jointly by a law school and a classics department. The audience is split: half are philosophers and legal historians familiar with Platonic political thought, the other half are public-policy scholars and clinicians who have never studied Plato's Laws. Your task is to frame the workshop's purpose in about four minutes, using Plato's account of regulated symposia in the Laws—specifically his claim that wine, when administered under strict institutional controls, functions not as pleasure but as a voluntary test of the soul's rational governance, exposing hidden vices that sober life conceals—as the structural backbone of the speech. Crucially, you must also foreground the circularity problem implicit in Plato's framework: the legislator who designs these diagnostic drinking institutions must already possess the rational self-mastery that the tests are meant to cultivate, which raises the question of whether a secular state lacking such a virtuous legislator can ever legitimately institutionalize intoxication as civic diagnostics. Because Plato insists that unregulated drinking destroys the very faculty it is meant to test, you must use this distinction to explain why the workshop is not celebrating intoxication but investigating the conditions—both psychological and institutional—under which states can regulate it without undermining citizens' rational capacities. You need to land for the experts, who will notice if you conflate the Symposium's convivial drinking with the Laws' regulatory framework, without losing the policy audience, and you must avoid suggesting that ancient authority alone resolves modern regulatory questions. Write the remarks in American English, approximately 600 words, with no bibliography, no citations, and no verbatim quotations from ancient texts: paraphrase Plato's position entirely in your own words.
