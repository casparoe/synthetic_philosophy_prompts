I just came away from a long argument with a friend about whether interpretability research is uncovering real representations inside neural networks or merely inventing useful narratives. I take the realist side, but with a specific twist: I think representation does not require a full-blown semantic consumer or embodied interaction, and that even small predictive models contain what we might call functional indicator representations—internal states that systematically carry decodable information about specific distal properties, are causally implicated in downstream computation, and can be selectively disrupted with predictable behavioral effects—so realism holds for ordinary transformers, not only for hypothetical world-models in large systems. What would actually count against this position? I want the decisive defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including whether successful intervention is always post-hoc, whether genuine misrepresentation is possible in these architectures, and whether the absence of evolutionary or intentional design undermines the causal-explanatory role of the state. I also want an honest assessment of whether realists have qualified their view so heavily—say, by treating almost any decodable correlation as representational—that it risks unfalsifiability, and if so whether that insulation is a defect for a claim of this kind. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
