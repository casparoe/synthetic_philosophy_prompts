Below is an exam prompt from an advanced metaphysics seminar on material constitution, together with one student submission. You are grading this submission as an instructor or teaching assistant. Provide a specific grade (using either a letter grade or a score out of ten) followed by a point-by-point commentary that explains and justifies that grade. Your commentary should identify where the student correctly distinguishes identity from constitution and where they properly apply notions of spatiotemporal coincidence and persistence conditions. It should also flag any subtle errors—such as an internal tension in how the original material lump is treated across dispersal and reassembly, a conflation between lacking artistic form and failing to stand in a constitution relation, or any step in which a conclusion about object-counting does not follow from the premises offered. Do not rewrite the answer; deliver only the assessment.

Exam question: Consider the classic problem of material constitution illustrated by a statue—call it Form—and the lump of marble—call it Matter—from which it is originally carved. Now extend the scenario: over centuries, Form is restored part by part until none of its original marble remains, yet its shape, artistic identity, and functional role are continuously preserved. Meanwhile, every replaced fragment of original marble is catalogued and stored. Eventually these fragments are fused, elsewhere and without any sculptural shaping, into a single chemically identical block—call it Aggregate A—which possesses the exact original material composition but no artistic form or historical continuity with Form.

At the precise moment when the last original fragment has been replaced in Form but Aggregate A has not yet been assembled, how many objects occupy the region of space inhabited by Form? Once Aggregate A is assembled in a different location, what is the correct metaphysical relationship, if any, between Aggregate A and Form? In answering, carefully distinguish identity, constitution, and mere spatiotemporal coincidence, and explain whether the persistence conditions of artistic objects and those of material lumps necessarily diverge in this case.

Student answer: At the initial stage, Form and Matter are two distinct objects that fully coincide in space and share all of their parts; they are not identical, because they have different persistence conditions—Matter could survive being squashed into a sphere, whereas Form could not. This is the standard constitution relation, so initially there are two objects in one place. Once every original molecule has been replaced, Matter no longer exists, since its parts are now dispersed in storage, while Form continues to exist with an entirely new substrate; hence only one object occupies that space at that intermediate moment. When Aggregate A is later assembled elsewhere, it is numerically identical to the original Matter, because it contains all and only the original parts. Nevertheless, because Aggregate A lacks Form's artistic properties and is spatially separated, it does not constitute Form. Thus, after assembly there are again two distinct objects—Form with its new marble and Aggregate A with the old—but they no longer overlap. The central lesson is that artistic objects depend on form for their persistence, whereas material lumps depend solely on part-composition, which is why the two can separate over time.
