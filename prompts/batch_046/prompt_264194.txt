I serve on my university’s Committee on Intellectual Integrity and Research Classification. A proposal has reached us to reclassify the longstanding Graduate Seminar in Mathematical Logic—traditionally funded under Philosophy of Mind and Epistemology—as a Computational Engineering Workshop, effective next fiscal year. The proposers, a coalition of computer-science and mathematics faculty, argue that recent advances in interactive theorem proving have collapsed the classical Lucas–Penrose distinction: if human mathematicians can now formalize every step of insight they previously claimed as non-mechanical, and if proof-checkers can verify arbitrarily deep self-referential consistency statements, then there is no remaining Gödelian gap that justifies treating human mathematical reasoning as a distinct epistemic kind deserving separate philosophical funding. They ask the committee to approve the reclassification, which would shift tenure lines and redirect grants away from philosophical logic toward automated verification infrastructure.

The seminar’s defenders—two philosophers and a historian of mathematics—have submitted a counter-memorandum that I find compelling but incomplete. They note that even if every particular proof can be mechanized, the institutional decision to treat formal system F rather than system F′ (or to treat proof-assistant outputs rather than rival formalisms) as authoritative requires a normative judgment that is not itself derivable within any of those systems. They invoke what they call a second-order reflective gap: the committee’s own endorsement of mechanization as the terminus of mathematical cognition presupposes a non-formalizable capacity for reflective closure. I need the committee to reach an actionable decision by the end of the month—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—and I have been asked to draft the formal opinion that will circulate before our vote.

Please write the committee’s decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a clear time frame, engage the proposers’ claim that interactive proof technology has dissolved the Lucas–Penrose boundary, and treat the defenders’ second-order reflective-gap argument with genuine philosophical seriousness rather than as a rhetorical add-on. Crucially, the memo should acknowledge the strongest consideration on the losing side: if the committee sides with the defenders, it must explain why the institutional cost of preserving a philosophical funding line is warranted despite the mechanization of particular proofs; if it sides with the proposers, it must explain why the second-order gap does not undermine the reclassification even though the committee’s own reasoning cannot be fully formalized. The document should be suitable for circulation among faculty with no specialist background in logic, but it should not dilute the conceptual stakes.
