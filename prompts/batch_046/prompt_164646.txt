You are a senior philosopher of language and epistemology advising a colleague on seminar defense strategy. Next Thursday I present in a philosophy of language and logic seminar. My thesis is that Quine's essay Two Dogmas of Empiricism succeeds in undermining the conventionalist and explicit-definitional accounts of analyticity—the Carnap-style reduction sentences and pure stipulation—but fails against a cognitive-architectural version of the distinction. On my view, a judgment counts as analytic not because it is unrevisable in principle, not because it is tautologous, and not because it expresses a metaphysically necessary truth, but because it is structurally protected from revision by the default architecture of human belief fixation. Specifically, abandoning it would require restructuring core inference rules rather than merely updating peripheral empirical beliefs. This yields a non-circular, empirically discoverable epistemic asymmetry: all bachelors are unmarried is analytic in this architectural sense, whereas all green emeralds are green is not, even though both may be metaphysically necessary. I concede that no single judgment is immune to revision in principle, and I concede that metaphysical necessity does not map onto this cognitive asymmetry.
