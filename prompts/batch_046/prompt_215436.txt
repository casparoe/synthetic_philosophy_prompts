I just finished a long and somewhat exhausting discussion with a friend about embedded agency, and I want to sharpen my thinking on one particular thread that kept coming up. We both granted that an agent physically situated inside the world it acts upon—rather than observing it from an external Archimedean point—faces serious modeling challenges, but we disagreed about whether those challenges are merely practical or fundamentally undermine standard decision theory. My friend insisted that even an embedded agent can, in principle, maintain a clean separation between its preference structure and the causal dynamics of the environment, treating its own utility computation as just another subsystem to be protected or optimized. I think that view misses a deeper form of reflexivity that arises specifically from what I will call intrinsic embedding: the fact that the agent's criterion of choice is not merely observed from within the world but is implemented in the very substrate that the agent is trying to steer.

Given this framing, please argue that embedded agency does not merely complicate expected-utility maximization but actually destabilizes its coherence conditions. In particular, develop the claim that when an agent's utility function is causally entangled with the environment—so that altering the environment can alter the computation of utility, and altering the computation of utility alters the environment—there is no stable fixed point that corresponds to maximizing a single preference ordering. You should address the obvious reply that hierarchical self-modeling or reflective equilibrium can restore coherence by treating the utility computation as an object-level variable. I think that reply fails because any meta-level criterion used to stabilize the object-level preference is itself subject to the same reflexive loop, leading not to a well-founded hierarchy but to a regress without a base case. To make the argument concrete, consider a bounded agent that can access its own internal states only through noisy, physically embedded sensors, and that must allocate finite computational resources between world-modeling and self-modeling. Argue that under these conditions the agent cannot consistently apply one preference ordering across time, and that the appropriate normative framework is therefore not utility maximization but something closer to procedural stability or evolutionary robustness—where the criterion for action is not whether an option maximizes U, but whether it preserves the integrity of the decision-making process itself. Please keep the reasoning formal and precise, and avoid treating embeddedness as a mere engineering limitation; I want to see why it is conceptually irreducible to the standard agent-environment boundary.
