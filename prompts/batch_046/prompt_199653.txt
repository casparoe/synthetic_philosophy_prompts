In comparative economic history and sociology courses, Weber’s claim that a particular religious ethos contributed to the rise of modern capitalism is routinely taught under a strict double constraint: the thesis is presented as proof that cultural values can exercise macro-level causal efficacy—genuinely shaping economic development rather than merely reflecting material conditions—yet the very same texts insist that this is not “cultural determinism” because the values only “facilitated” or “accelerated” a transformation that was structurally possible anyway, and they caution against treating culture as an independent variable that could be deliberately engineered for policy. The practice, then, is a specific pedagogical and disciplinary convention: culture is granted causal status solely as a contingent accelerator operating within structural constraints, never as a primary driver, and never as something whose presence or absence can be directly manipulated like an institutional rule or a tax rate. What philosophical commitments about causation, explanation, and the ontology of social facts does this convention presuppose? Does it implicitly rely on a form of explanatory pluralism in which values and structures belong to incommensurable causal orders, such that values can “push” but not “pull”? Does it commit, perhaps inconsistently, to the view that for a factor to count as genuinely causal it must also be susceptible to intervention, and if so, how does that commitment square with the same disciplines’ willingness to cite non-manipulable causes—geographic endowments, path-dependent lock-in, or demographic accidents—without similar qualification? Finally, does the distinction drawn here between “facilitating” and “causing” map onto any defensible metaphysical distinction, or is it primarily a way of preserving Weber’s thesis while sidestepping the deeper problem of explaining with values—namely, whether a collective ethos can be a cause in the same sense as an institutional design or a technological innovation, or whether invoking it inevitably collapses into either reduction to individual psychology or empty post-hoc description?
