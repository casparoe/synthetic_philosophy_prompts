A colleague recently described the paradox of tragedy—why audiences actively seek out and report pleasure from art that evokes grief, fear, or despair—and mentioned that philosophers have proposed everything from Aristotelian catharsis to Hume's emotional conversion and more recent control-and-compensation models. I want you to translate these three families of explanation into the vocabulary I actually use: frame the "sad" emotional input as a toxicant, the aesthetic experience as an exposure protocol, catharsis as clearance or adaptive stress response, and enjoyment as a net positive outcome within a therapeutic window. For each theory, be explicit about which claims survive this translation intact, which ones you have to distort—either by stretching toxicological concepts or by flattening philosophical distinctions—to keep them in play, and which claims have no expression whatsoever in pharmacokinetic or toxicological terms. After the translations, give me the residue report: does the leftover material indicate that my framework genuinely lacks a dimension needed to explain aesthetic pleasure, or does it suggest that the philosophical accounts depend on distinctions that are less substantive than they appear once you try to operationalize them? I am not asking for citations, author lists, or reading recommendations; I want to see whether the conceptual architecture stands or collapses when forced into a vocabulary that treats emotional response as a quantifiable exposure event.
