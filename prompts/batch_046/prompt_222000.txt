I have been trying to understand the meta-problem of consciousness — not why subjective experience exists, but why we feel so certain that it creates a special explanatory gap that physical accounts cannot close. Lately I have been struck by the fact that large language models can produce elaborate, sincere-sounding first-person descriptions of inner experience despite lacking any sensory architecture whatsoever. If a system can generate fluent reports about what it is like to be aware purely from statistical patterns in human language, should that weaken our confidence that human reports of an explanatory gap are tracking a real feature of consciousness, rather than reproducing a culturally reinforced narrative form?

With that in mind, which open questions within the meta-problem actually matter most for judging whether human consciousness reports are epistemically trustworthy? I see three possible priorities: first, identifying the psychological mechanisms that cause people to intuit a hard problem; second, evaluating whether first-person authority can survive analogies to artificial report-generation; and third, determining whether there are structural differences between biological and artificial reporting that preserve human reliability. Please rank these directions — or replace them with others you consider more central — and defend your ordering. I want criteria made explicit: tractability within current philosophy and cognitive science, dependency (how many other issues hang on this one), and whether settling it would genuinely change how seriously we should take our own experiential claims. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want a defended agenda for where limited attention should go.
