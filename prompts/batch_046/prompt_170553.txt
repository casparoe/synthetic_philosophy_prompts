After thirty years in condensed-matter physics I have turned, in retirement, to philosophy with the habits of a scientist rather than those of a historian: I think naturally in terms of equilibria, focal points, and coordination failures, and I am inclined to read conceptual texts as models of stability conditions rather than as expressions of theological commitment. Next Thursday I present to an interdepartmental seminar—hosted by a history of philosophy group whose members read Pascal theologically and treat his political fragments as subordinate to Jansenist anthropology, together with two political philosophers who work on obligation and civil disobedience and who are skeptical of importing formal machinery into seventeenth-century texts—my argument that Pascal’s claim that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical ought to be reconstructed as a coordination analysis rather than as conventionalist cynicism. On my reading, Pascal describes a situation in which legitimate authority depends on a common-knowledge expectation that enforcement tracks a shared normative convention; the convention is the focal point, the force is the mechanism that sustains it, and civil disobedience becomes rational precisely when that expectation collapses—not because justice is absent in some abstract sense, but because the equilibrium has dissolved. I am aware that this risks anachronism, that it may seem to smuggle Hobbesian or modern game-theoretic concepts into a text concerned with salvation and fallen nature, and that political theorists will ask whether this reconstruction differs in substance from familiar service-based or consent-based accounts of authority.

I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis in writing. Please list the objections I will actually face, ordered from the most damaging to my credibility downward, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would use: for example, whether I am ignoring the explicitly theological framing of the Pensées, whether my Schelling-point language explains anything that standard theories of political obligation do not already cover, whether I have misread the fragment by treating a rhetorical observation as a structural model, and whether a retired physicist should be reconstructing canonical texts with formal tools he barely masters. For each objection, indicate what I should concede immediately, what I must hold at all costs, a concise reply deliverable in under two minutes, and the specific weakness in that reply that I should be ready to defend rather than hope goes unnoticed. Include the one question I am praying nobody asks—the one that exposes either a gap in my knowledge of Pascal scholarship or forces me to defend the anachronism charge with direct textual evidence I do not possess. I am not requesting a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want to survive the room.
