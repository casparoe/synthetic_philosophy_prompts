As a residential college administrator who has recently been tasked with revising our alcohol-education framework, I have turned to Plato’s Laws and Symposium to see whether there is any rigorous philosophical precedent for treating supervised drinking as a pedagogical or civic instrument rather than as a mere risk to be suppressed. For an internal faculty seminar I have drafted the following examination question and a sample student response; I need an objective, point-by-point assessment because I am not fully confident that I have identified every subtle error.

Question: How does Plato, particularly in the Laws and in the Symposium, assess the value of supervised communal drinking? In your answer, explain the Athenian Stranger’s argument that wine can function as a kind of civic medicine or diagnostic test, and contrast this with the role of Alcibiades’ intoxication in the Symposium. Conclude by stating whether Plato’s position amounts to a unified theory of sympotic education that applies equally to philosophical inquiry and to democratic citizenship.

Student answer: In Plato’s Laws, the Athenian Stranger proposes that supervised symposia should be regulated like medical treatment, with wine administered in measured doses to test citizens’ resistance to pleasure and fear. By lowering inhibitions, drinking reveals whether a person is genuinely courageous and temperate or is merely concealing vice behind social decorum. This makes the symposium a civic laboratory for character assessment, analogous to physical training. In the Symposium, Alcibiades’ drunken entrance performs a parallel function: his intoxication strips away the rhetorical polish of the other speakers and exposes Socrates’ unique immunity to bodily temptation. Because both texts present intoxication as unmasking hidden truth, Plato effectively treats sympotic drinking as a form of non-discursive dialectic—an experiential elenchus that bypasses verbal argument. The physician Eryximachus’s opening remarks about regulating wine for health therefore anticipate the Athenian Stranger’s civic program, showing that Plato’s view is unified across dialogues. From this it follows that democratic assemblies should incorporate supervised drinking as a regular deliberative tool, since citizens who have been tested in symposia will be less likely to deceive one another in political debate.
