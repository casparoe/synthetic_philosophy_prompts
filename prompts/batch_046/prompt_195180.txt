I'm presenting a seminar paper defending the claim that Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach conflates syntactic diagonalization in formal arithmetic—which requires explicit Gödel numbering and the derivability conditions—with the recursive self-modeling found in predictive-processing architectures, which minimizes hierarchical prediction error without any analog of arithmetization. I argue that calling both "strange loops" obscures the fact that only the first involves a provability predicate applied to its own code; the second involves no such predicate and therefore cannot reproduce the specific incompleteness result that Hofstadter imports into philosophy of mind. The audience is an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind and theoretical computer science group: about half are computationalists who treat the analogy as harmless heuristic; a quarter are logicians who insist any sufficiently powerful self-referential architecture will reproduce Gödelian limitations, often citing reflexive logics; one attendee is a prominent integrated information theorist who thinks self-reference is largely irrelevant to consciousness; and the rest are graduate students in cognitive science who have read the book but never worked through the proof of the first incompleteness theorem. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each faction would phrase them, with what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my position I should defend rather than hope they miss—especially on my claim that predictive-coding loops lack derivability conditions. Include the specific question I'm hoping nobody asks: the one that forces me either to deny consciousness to any system lacking explicit Gödel numbering or to admit I'm merely policing metaphor. For each objection, note where a two-minute reply is softest, and flag the reply that has to land for the proposal to survive.
