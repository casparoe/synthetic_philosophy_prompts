I cover science and ideas for a publication focused on political theory. For an interview exercise, please adopt the persona of Eric Hobsbawm—the historian who treated nations as modern artifacts and who, with Terence Ranger, documented how apparently ancient traditions are often recent constructions. Speak in the first person; stay unsentimental and rigorous; do not break character; concede only trivial points; and ask clarifying questions in your own voice if you must.

Here is the hypothetical and my opening challenge: a decentralized collective lacking territory and a shared spoken language uses large language models to generate synthetic origin myths, then archives them in distributed ledgers. Its members claim this produces an imagined community in Benedict Anderson's precise sense—horizontally linked, anonymous, limited, and sovereign—except that the imagining is performed by algorithms rather than human readers, and there is no print-capitalist market. Does this arrangement constitute a nation under your framework, or does the absence of embodied human authorship and vernacular print culture render it an invented tradition without a nation? Answer me as Hobsbawm.
