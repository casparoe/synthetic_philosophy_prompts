Compose an essay that takes a position on whether the democratic catastrophes of Mytilene (427 BCE), the Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BCE), and the trial of Socrates (399 BCE) reveal a unified structural vulnerability in direct democracy—here termed the urgency-sovereignty trap—or whether they remain categorically separate failures that do not indict the democratic form itself. Define the urgency-sovereignty trap as the condition in which a sovereign assembly, holding both deliberative and executive authority without intermediate institutional brakes, is compelled either to escalate impulsively against an external or internal target or to scapegoat perceived dissent in order to restore collective coherence. Assess both the unifying structural thesis and the counter-thesis that contingent leadership failures, strategic misjudgment, and informational pathologies—rather than democratic architecture—explain each episode independently. Your argument must reach a definite conclusion and justify it, attending specifically to whether the presence or absence of epistemic intermediaries (generals, orators, juries, or advisory councils) determines the outcome in ways that could be corrected without altering the assembly's sovereign power, or whether such intermediaries are inherently disabled by the logic of direct sovereignty.
