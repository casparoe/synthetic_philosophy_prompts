I am in the early stages of a novel whose protagonist is a retired coffee buyer for a large roastery, now slowly losing her sense of smell. A central philosophical tension in the narrative concerns whether she can coherently claim that her inner experience of flavor remains unchanged even as her judgments about quality have collapsed. At a recent dinner, a philosopher acquaintance summarized for me what she called the Chase-Sanborn line of argument against qualia, drawn from Dennett's well-known intuition pumps. She described the original case: two veteran tasters at Maxwell House, Chase and Sanborn, who after years of drinking the same blend reported that they no longer enjoyed it, yet insisted the coffee tasted exactly as it always had—only their preferences had shifted. From this, she said, Dennett establishes that whenever a change in report can be fully explained by a causal or functional story—receptor adaptation, altered reward pathways, associative conditioning—there remains no explanatory gap that requires us to postulate a private, intrinsic mental property. She went on to claim that Dennett therefore shows such properties to be not merely redundant but literally incoherent, comparable to Ryle's ghost-in-the-machine, and that he extends this conclusion to deny all first-person authority: if the tasters themselves cannot distinguish between a genuine change in subjective experience and a mere shift in judgment, then no subject can ever legitimately claim knowledge of her own mental states. Finally, she concluded that Dennett's result eliminates not only the traditional notion of ineffable qualia but any robust conception of subjective experience, leaving only public behavior and neurophysiological description.

I am inclined to think her account overreaches—particularly the move from showing that a specific theoretical posit is unnecessary to claiming that first-person knowledge is globally undermined—but I lack the philosophical training to locate the precise missteps. Which elements of her summary accurately capture Dennett's strategy? Where does she conflate his critique of intrinsic, private properties with a broader denial of consciousness? Is her claim that he proves literal incoherence a misrepresentation of a more modest argument about explanatory redundancy? And does her final conclusion, that subjective experience must be abandoned entirely, actually follow from the corrected parts of her account? I want an accuracy audit that I can apply not only to my understanding of the philosophy but to the coherence of my character's insistence that something inside her tasting experience persists unchanged.
