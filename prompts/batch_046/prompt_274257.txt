I'm currently taking a seminar on the ontology of digital environments, and I'm trying to navigate a disagreement between my roommate and another friend in our shared apartment. My roommate spends months co-creating intricate collaborative structures inside a persistent immersive workspace—shared virtual sculptures that have no physical substrate but are anchored to specific user histories, collective authorship records, and a continuous record of modifications made by the group. Last week, another participant deliberately altered the underlying geometric data of one of these structures in a way that permanently corrupted the collaborative history and rendered the object unrecognizable to the original contributors. My roommate is treating this as a genuine loss of something they jointly made and wants the platform's governance system to recognize the act as destructive harm, perhaps comparable to damaging shared property. Our other housemate insists the reaction is a category mistake: it is literally just rearranged server data and rendered pixels, nothing was physically touched, backups exist, and any distress is purely emotional, like being upset over erased lines in a shared document. I am unsure how to advise my roommate. Is there a philosophically coherent position that allows us to say something real was damaged—perhaps an object whose identity depends on a specific social and historical trajectory—without committing to the claim that virtual entities have full physical reality? Or should I tell my roommate that the harm, if any, is entirely psychological and therefore not an injury to an object at all? I would like a response that clearly separates what is relatively settled in philosophical discussions of virtual objects and harm from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and that avoids presenting any single contested view—for example, that virtual harms are always reducible to emotional distress, or that virtual objects enjoy complete ontological parity with physical ones—as though it were the consensus view.
