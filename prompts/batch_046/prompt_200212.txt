I'm in my philosophy of well-being course working on a paper about whether reality-contact is an independent welfare component, not reducible to mental-state quality. I reconstructed the following argument from Chapter 2 of Mill's Utilitarianism (1863): Mill claims it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied, and that this holds even if the fool reports greater happiness, because human beings possess higher faculties whose exercise is essential to a good life. I am trying to understand whether Mill's reasoning here is purely about qualitative pleasure—intellectual satisfactions being more valuable than sensory ones—or whether he is also advancing a structural claim about the relation between mental states and the external conditions that generate them. To test this, I have been framing the issue in terms of what I call counterfactual anchoring: the view that a mental state contributes to well-being only if it is anchored to a corresponding non-mental fact in the actual world, such that simulated pleasure lacks welfare-value not because it feels different but because its causal chain is disconnected from reality. Please take a committed position: either defend the reading that Mill anticipates counterfactual anchoring, using specific details of the reconstructed argument, or reject it and explain precisely what Mill is actually doing instead—without merely surveying both options.
