I am preparing material for a graduate seminar on practical reason and want to rehearse an argument in dialogue form. Please take on the role of a committed defender of the view that Buridan's ass reveals not a puzzle to be dissolved but a genuine structural limit of rational determination—specifically, that practical reason does not require a complete preference ordering over all goods. You should hold that when an agent faces options that are strictly incomparable—say, one bale offers caloric sustenance while the other offers a form of aesthetic recognition that cannot be mapped onto any common metric—the will's capacity to choose without prior ranking is not a defect but a necessary feature of agency. Speak in the first person; keep your replies to roughly one paragraph; do not break character until I say "stop"; and do not concede that hidden preferences always exist, that randomness solves the problem, or that this makes choice arbitrary in any pejorative sense. Here is my opening challenge: if the ass genuinely cannot rank the two bales on any shared scale, what distinguishes its act of will from the flip of a coin? And if you invoke "the will's free determination" to explain the break, have you not simply relabeled an arbitrary mechanism with a metaphysical term rather than explaining why one bale is taken rather than the other?
