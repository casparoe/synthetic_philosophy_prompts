“On Nussbaum’s judgmentalism, emotions are essentially evaluative judgments—specifically, judgments that something is important for one’s flourishing and that one is vulnerable to it. This makes emotions rational and open to correction, but it can’t explain emotional recalcitrance: if I judge that flying is safe yet still fear it, my judgment hasn’t changed, so the fear is irrational in a way the view can’t accommodate. Prinz’s perceptualism treats emotions as perceptions of bodily changes that represent core relational themes; this captures recalcitrance since perceptions can persist against belief, but it struggles to explain why emotions seem directed at specific external situations rather than just internal states, and it risks reducing emotional evaluation to non-cognitive feeling. Tani’s predictive-affective model improves on both. Like Prinz, it is bottom-up and non-judgmental, using interoceptive prediction error rather than conceptual evaluation. Unlike Prinz, however, it doesn’t represent an external object perceptually; instead, the narrative frame assigns meaning to arousal without requiring a perceptual representation of the cause. This lets Tani avoid the recalcitrance problem—since predictions can be updated without belief revision—while also avoiding Prinz’s problem of external direction, because the narrative frame is about the situation, not a direct perception of it. The main risk is that ‘narrative frame’ sounds like a disguised judgment, but Tani specifies it operates at a subpersonal level, so it isn’t genuinely conceptual. Therefore, Tani succeeds in carving out a stable third position.”
