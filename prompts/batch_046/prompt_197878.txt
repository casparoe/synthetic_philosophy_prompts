I have been working through some problems in the metaphysics of material objects, and I find myself returning repeatedly to the Ship of Theseus, though I now suspect I am importing assumptions from an adjacent debate about vagueness. Specifically, I have been considering the view—sometimes defended in discussions of the sorites—that apparently continuous changes in fact conceal sharp, epistemically inaccessible boundaries. If one applies this to the ship, it seems there must be a precise moment, perhaps the replacement of one particular plank, at which the original vessel is destroyed and a numerically distinct object takes its place. Our inability to identify that plank would then be a mere epistemic limitation, not a metaphysical indeterminacy.

This framing appears to dissolve the traditional paradox. Once we accept a hidden cutoff, the vessel reconstructed from the retired planks is clearly not the original, because it is assembled only after the original has ceased to exist; meanwhile, the gradually repaired ship at the dock is the same object up to, but not beyond, that unknown boundary. Yet the philosophical literature presents the case as if it exposes a genuine tension between material continuity and spatio-temporal or functional continuity, rather than a simple ignorance of where a sharp line lies. I am struggling to see why we should treat the competing claims of the two resulting vessels as equally legitimate, rather than dismissing one as the product of misidentifying a new object. Is the paradox therefore relying on the denial of sharp boundaries, and if so, does that denial follow from anything more than our reluctance to posit unknown cutoffs? I should be grateful for a careful separation of what the case actually demonstrates about diachronic identity, and whether an epistemicist account of vagueness genuinely resolves the tension or merely relocates it into our knowledge without addressing the underlying metaphysics.
