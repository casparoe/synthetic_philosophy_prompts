Next month I defend a hybrid thesis before a joint history–philosophy seminar: that collective moral responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War should be reconceived outside the Fischer controversy’s intentionalist frame—whether Berlin deliberately pursued hegemonic war aims in July 1914—and outside its denialist counterpart that the powers sleepwalked into catastrophe without meaningful agency. I propose instead an institutional-risk model in which blame attaches to the normative architecture of a state’s decision system, specifically its calibrated acceptance of escalation risk, rather than to any recoverable collective intention. The audience comprises diplomatic historians skeptical of moralizing historiography and familiar with Fischer’s critics, plus two philosophers of collective action who have not worked on the 1914 archival specifics. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: list the objections I will actually receive, ordered by how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary of its source—historians pressing on contingency, source-base, and the erasure of individual agency; philosophers pressing on collective intentionality, distributive blame, and whether institutional evaluation smuggles in normative claims. For each, specify what I should concede immediately, what line I must hold, and the soft point in my reply. End with the objection I am hoping nobody asks—whether applying my risk-architecture framework symmetrically to Entente alliance commitments and mobilization timetables dissolves the aggressor–victim distinction that makes the Fischer debate politically urgent—and sketch a two-minute reply with its weakest hinge noted.
