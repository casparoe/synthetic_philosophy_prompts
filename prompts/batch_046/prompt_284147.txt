I've been turning over a question that came up at a dinner with two friends, and I keep going in circles, so I'd like you to work through it with me. Let's call them Sam and Riley. They started arguing about whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg and still been this exact person — not a similar person, but literally me. I listened without taking sides, and now I want a point-by-point adjudication of what they actually said.

Sam opened by saying that tying identity to one specific pair of cells feels wrong. He thinks who I am is mainly psychological and social: my memories, my relationships, my character. If my parents had conceived a child a month earlier or later, some other sperm and egg would have met, and that child might have lived a life somewhat like mine if raised in the same home, but it wouldn't be me — it would be a different consciousness altogether. So asking whether "I" could have come from those other cells is, in Sam's view, just asking whether a different person could have filled my role, which is possible but doesn't touch my actual identity.

Riley pushed back hard. She said Sam is treating a person like a job description that anyone can fill. For her, you don't begin as an abstract pattern; you begin as one specific biological individual at conception. If the gametes had been different, the organism that developed would have carried different genes, followed different developmental paths, and therefore wouldn't be me — it would be a sibling, or no one I should care about in particular. She also argued that Sam's psychological view can't explain why I have a special reason to care about my own future rather than about any psychologically similar being.

Sam replied that Riley overweights genetics. He pointed out that I could lose a limb, suffer a brain injury, or even undergo gradual personality change, and I'd still be me. So why treat a tiny genetic difference at conception as essential, when a large physical or mental change later isn't? He said this shows biological continuity matters, but exact genetic origin doesn't.

Riley answered with a distinction she thought Sam was missing. She said there's a categorical difference between altering an existing thing and substituting its origin. If you replace a table leg, it's the same table modified; if you build the table from different wood from the very start, it's a different table. By analogy, changing the gametes doesn't modify me — it creates something else from the outset. But then she added something I hadn't heard before: even with my exact sperm and egg, development isn't fully determined. Random X-chromosome inactivation, epigenetic variation, and stochastic gene expression mean the same genome can produce somewhat different bodies and temperaments. Riley argued that this noise actually strengthens her position — if the same origin already permits divergence, then a different origin would diverge even further, making it even less plausible that "I" could have emerged from other cells.

Sam wasn't convinced. He said that if development is already noisy, then my identity can't be tightly locked to either my exact genome or my exact phenotype, since both vary across counterfactual scenarios. That undermines Riley's claim that there's a sharp line between "me with different gametes" and "me with the same gametes but different development." If both involve variation, Sam asked, then Riley needs some principle other than origin — maybe psychological continuity — to explain why one case counts as me and the other doesn't. Otherwise, he said, she's just privileging the origin she actually had and declaring it essential by fiat.

They kept circling these points. Can you adjudicate move by move? Specifically: is Sam right that psychological continuity makes genetic origin non-essential? Is Riley right that substituting an origin is different in kind from later modification? Does Sam's comparison between a genetic difference at conception and a brain injury later hold up, or is there an asymmetry he's ignoring? And regarding the point about developmental noise — does it help Riley's essentialism, or does it actually support Sam's view that identity isn't biologically fixed? Finally, what important consideration are both of them missing — perhaps something about how counterfactual reasoning works with "I," or about whether origin questions even have determinate answers? I want to know who's right about what, who is closer overall, and where the argument still hangs.
