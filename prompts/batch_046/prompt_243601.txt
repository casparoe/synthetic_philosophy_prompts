I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of technology seminar on whether simulation reasoning undermines longtermist fanaticism, and I've worked out an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to show that such reasoning either collapses all practical obligations or none of them. I want to know where it slips, if it does.

(1) If technologically mature civilizations are possible and run ancestor simulations, then simulated observers vastly outnumber unsimulated ones, giving us non-negligible credence that we live in a simulation.
(2) If we are simulated, then distant future states our actions might shape are either truncated when the simulation ends or determined by the simulators' choices, not causally fixed by our present behavior.
(3) Longtermist fanaticism assigns astronomical moral weight to present actions in virtue of their expected influence on the far future.
(4) So if (1) grants us genuine credence in (2), longtermists must apply a proportional discount factor to the expected value of present actions.
(5) But the reasoning applies recursively: if our simulators are themselves simulated, then their influence on base reality is discounted by the same factor, and so on through any finite nesting depth n.
(6) Hence the total expected causal reach of any present action into base-reality future states is bounded by a convergent series whose sum is comparable to near-term, within-layer effects.
(7) Therefore simulation reasoning does not merely dampen urgency about the distant future; it equalizes moral weight across all temporal distances, rendering longtermist prioritization irrational.
(8) Yet within any given layer, near-term obligations—keeping promises, preventing immediate harm—remain fully intact, since they depend on causal relations internal to that layer.
(9) So if (7) is true, then either near-term obligations are also undermined, because all causal influence is equally discounted, or (7) is false.
(10) But (8) is clearly correct, so (7) must fail; yet if (7) fails because internal causal relations are preserved, then longtermists can still influence the far future internal to our layer, and simulation reasoning fails to dampen fanaticism at all.
(11) Thus the argument yields a dilemma: simulation reasoning is either globally destructive to practical reason or selectively inert with respect to far-future urgency.

If I must choose, I would keep (1) and (8), but I need to know precisely which step to reject and what that costs—whether the error lies in treating simulation credence as a linear discount on expected value rather than an epistemic filter, in conflating causal influence on base reality with causal influence within a layer, in an illicit quantifier shift across the recursive nesting, or somewhere else entirely.
