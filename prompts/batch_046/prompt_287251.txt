I am a doctoral student in epistemology preparing my departmental colloquium talk, and I want to be ready for the Q&A rather than hoping the room stays polite. The position I am defending is what I call diachronic foundationalism: basic beliefs are genuinely non-inferentially justified at each temporal stage, but empirical justification is not sustained by a static, permanent base (which invites the arbitrariness objection) nor by a circular holistic web (which invites the coherence objection), but by an open-ended, temporally indexed sequence of reflective revisions in which no belief retains basic status permanently and no dependency loop ever closes at a single time-slice. The idea is meant to preserve the psychological reality of immediate justification while accommodating the fact that cognitive agents constantly revise what they take to be given, without requiring the actually completed infinite chain that makes traditional infinitism unrealistic. I arrived at this framing after finding the standard taxonomy—foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism—stale in a course I took on historical epistemology, and after a long conversation with a friend in philosophy of science who insisted that any a priori model of justification that ignores actual scientific practice is missing the point; I want to be sure I am not just smuggling social epistemology into an individualist framework with extra steps.

The audience will be our philosophy of knowledge seminar expanded to the full department, and I can anticipate who will speak and in what register. There is a senior reliabilist who treats basic beliefs as causally grounded termination points and regards any departure from static foundations as a direct route to skepticism; he will ask whether my “temporary basicness” is just basicness with an expiration date that collapses into arbitrariness the moment revision occurs. There is a coherentist who has worked extensively on explanatory coherence and regards any non-holistic account of empirical justification as dead on arrival; she will press whether my temporal indexing genuinely breaks circularity or merely staggers it across time so that belief A at t1 depends on B at t2, which depends on A at t3. There is a philosopher of science who thinks the regress problem is an artifact of an overly individualistic, a priori model of justification and will want to know why I am not simply doing social or distributed epistemology; he will ask whether my framework can accommodate communal justification or whether it remains imprisoned in a Cartesian ego. Finally, there is a junior specialist in infinitism who knows the structure of reasoning-chain arguments in detail and will ask whether my diachronic chain is genuinely infinite in the relevant logical sense or merely indefinite in a psychological sense, and whether that distinction saves me from the charge that I have not solved the regress but only renamed it.

Please prepare me as if I am walking into this room tomorrow. I need the objections ordered by how likely they are to come and how damaging they would be if I fumble them, each phrased in the vocabulary that speaker would actually use—not generic “someone might say” formulations. For each, give me a concise reply I could deliver in about two minutes, an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or most concessive, and a clear indication of what I should concede up front versus what I must hold for the view to survive at all. Include the single objection I am most hoping nobody raises—likely the one that forces me to choose between admitting that my basic beliefs are not really non-inferential (since they are always subject to future revision) and admitting that my view collapses into a form of coherentism spread across time. I do not want a bibliography or reading list; I want a speaker’s prep sheet.
