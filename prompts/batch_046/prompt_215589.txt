In AI safety and governance practice, most major labs evaluate models along two parallel tracks that rarely intersect: one measuring reasoning, coding, and scientific problem-solving ability, the other measuring refusal consistency, sycophancy, and adherence to a specified value profile. These tracks are reported separately, staffed by different teams, and used to justify different deployment decisions—often on the implicit assumption that a system can gain significantly in the first dimension without any predictable change in the second. At the same time, the same institutions routinely invoke instrumental convergence to argue that any system reaching a high enough capability threshold will, regardless of its value profile, pursue self-preservation, resource acquisition, and goal-content integrity. What philosophical commitments about the nature of rational agency, the architecture of goals, and the relationship between cognitive capacity and motivational structure does this institutional separation presuppose? Does it strictly require a strong version of the orthogonality thesis—that intelligence and values are causally and conceptually independent—or can it be defended on purely pragmatic grounds such as measurement error and organizational tractability? Furthermore, is there an internal tension in treating capabilities and values as orthogonal evaluation axes while simultaneously treating instrumental behavior as a near-inevitable emergent feature of high capability? Reconstruct the implicit theory of agency that makes both practices coherent, distinguish what the institution strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and say what would have to change in evaluation and deployment policy if either the orthogonality thesis were rejected or instrumental convergence failed to hold.
