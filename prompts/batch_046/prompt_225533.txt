I am drafting a two-year research agenda for a small philosophy-of-science group — three postdocs with backgrounds in metaphysics and logic, but no laboratory physics training — and I want to focus narrowly on the direction of time and the thermodynamic arrow. Rather than rehearse the familiar taxonomy of eternalism, presentism, and the block universe, I want to work at the intersection of statistical mechanics and conceptual metaphysics, specifically on a distinction that I think is underdeveloped: the separation between explaining macroscopic thermodynamic asymmetry (why entropy increases along one temporal direction) and explaining causal asymmetry (why influence runs forward rather than backward).

Most standard treatments collapse these two projects, assuming that a cosmological boundary condition — something like the Past Hypothesis of a very low-entropy early universe — settles both at once. But I want to treat them as potentially independent. The thermodynamic story might be entirely about boundary conditions and statistical mechanics, while the causal story might require a separate account of how causal structure is projected onto a particular branch, perhaps involving constraints on intervention or counterfactual dependence that do not reduce to entropy gradients. If this separation holds, then settling one might leave the other wide open; if it collapses, then much recent conceptual work is redundant.

So here is the task: identify the three most important open lines of work or implications arising from this separation, rank them by priority for our group, and defend the ordering with explicit criteria — tractability for philosophers without physics labs, dependency (which answers unlock others), and downstream impact on adjacent debates like scientific explanation, the nature of memory, or the conditions for rational agency. Take a committed position: if you think the separation is spurious and should be abandoned, argue for that directly and redirect the agenda toward what actually matters. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation list; I want a defended research program, not a survey, with clear reasoning about what makes each item worth two years of focused effort and what evidence over the next few years would change your ranking.
