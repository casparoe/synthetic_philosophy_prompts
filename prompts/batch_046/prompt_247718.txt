I'm moderating a panel with a philosopher of social science who defends the view that generations—Millennials, Gen Z, and so forth—are not mere demographic slices but diachronic social kinds with emergent normative profiles, where cohort membership can partly constitute practical identity. The position holds that kind-boundaries are not fixed by birth years but by retrospective narrative consolidation, so a generation's essential properties only stabilize once its members reach interpretive maturity. I recently realized that my own sense of obligation to "my generation" might depend on exactly this ontology, and I want to press the view rather than let it pass as sociology.

Please draft eight interview questions that turn on the actual content of this position. At least one should probe the retrospective kind-stabilization problem: if a cohort's essential features do not exist until narrative maturity, then early members seem to belong to a kind whose defining properties are not yet actual. Include a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that kind-membership is relational rather than intrinsic. Another question should press intersectional fragmentation—whether racial or class sub-cohorts undermine the unity required for kindhood—with a follow-up tailored to the answer that generational kinds are "fuzzy" yet causally projectible. A third should ask whether treating generations as real kinds licenses predictive social science or instead invites a self-fulfilling loop in which marketing and policy discourse manufacture the very properties they claim to discover, with a follow-up anticipating the defense that this loop is just standard interactive kind dynamics. For each of the eight, provide a brief follow-up keyed to the answer the defender is most likely to give. No generic biographical questions, and no requests for citations or reading lists.
