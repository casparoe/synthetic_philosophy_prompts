At our phil study group, Jordan and Alex got stuck arguing about whether the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are really the same beast. Jordan said yes -- both show belief isn't closed under conjunction, since you can rationally believe each page of a book has no errors (the preface) and each lottery ticket will lose, but you can't rationally believe both the conjunction and its negation. Alex said that's too quick: the preface is about epistemic humility and your own fallibility as an author, while the lottery is about probabilistic thresholds over large populations, so fixing one doesn't automatically fix the other. Jordan then invented a hybrid case -- a peer reviewer who reads 300 proposals, assigns each a 1% funding chance, and thus believes each specific one fails, yet knows the pool funds exactly 3, so she should believe at least one succeeds. He said this collapses the distinction because now you've got both self-referential fallibility and statistical structure in one case. Alex replied that in the reviewer case she can just revise her individual probabilities once she sees the pool constraints, so it's not a paradox at all, just bad prior assignment. Jordan said if she can do that in the hybrid case, she should be able to do the same in the pure lottery case by revising her belief about the specific ticket she holds, which undermines the paradox's force entirely. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Can you walk through each round, say who's right on which specific point, and tell me who is more right overall? Also, is there something deeper they're both glossing over, maybe about whether belief revision works differently when the fallibility is about yourself versus about the world?
