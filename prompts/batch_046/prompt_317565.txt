I recently finished a term on the advisory board of a community organization that had spent a decade building administrative infrastructure. When new leadership proposed dismantling a program that was clearly failing but had absorbed years of funding and staff loyalty, several members argued that abandoning it now would waste the accumulated investment and undermine the organization’s authority. Their reasoning struck me as a practical version of honoring sunk costs, and I turned to Pascal’s Pensées—specifically his fragments on justice and force—to understand whether institutional endurance creates a kind of moral entitlement. From there I constructed an argument that seems to show Pascal must endorse every established power structure, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) Pascal holds that justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is tyrannical; therefore legitimate authority requires that justice and force be combined.

(2) In any actual society, the available force is always embodied in particular institutions that have accumulated power over time—laws, hierarchies, and customs that represent irreversible investments of authority.

(3) To combine justice with force, then, justice must attach itself to these particular existing institutions, not to an abstract ideal.

(4) If justice is attached to a particular institution, then to criticize, reform, or dissolve that institution is to separate justice from the force that sustains it.

(5) Therefore, on Pascal’s view, sustained criticism of an established institution is self-defeating: it attempts to apply justice without the coercive backing necessary to make it effective.

(6) This implies that change can only be authorized from within the institution itself, because only the institution possesses the force with which justice must ally.

(7) By extension, the longer an institution has existed—the more sunk authority it has accumulated—the stronger the moral obligation to preserve it, regardless of its current moral quality.

(8) But Pascal clearly condemns arbitrary tyranny and writes in defense of resisting unjust power; he also denies that custom alone proves justice.

(9) So the argument proves too much: it makes Pascal’s own criticism of established power incoherent, and it turns every durable regime into a legitimate one.

Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (1), since it captures Pascal’s core insight, but I want to know exactly what abandoning or weakening (2) through (7) costs. Is there a hidden equivocation between force as such and this particular distribution of force? Or a modal slide between justice needing some coercive backing and justice needing this specific backing? Explain the flaw precisely, and tell me what each alternative repair requires me to give up.
