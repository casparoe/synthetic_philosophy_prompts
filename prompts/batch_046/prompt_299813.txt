I work as a software engineer on a team that builds autonomous reasoning modules—specifically, meta-cognitive agents that maintain running confidence estimates over their own outputs and update internal weights based on predicted accuracy. In a recent internal seminar, someone mentioned the "Löbian obstacle" to self-trusting agents: the idea that if a reasoning system treats its own derivation of a claim as automatic evidence for that claim—an unconditional reflection principle—then, by an analog of Löb's theorem, it risks being able to justify arbitrary conclusions or locking in erroneous beliefs. I have no formal background in logic or philosophy of mind, but I need to operationalize this concept into a concrete, reusable audit procedure that my team can apply to module specifications before deployment.

Please design a decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist with explicit stop conditions and sign-off rules—that a practitioner without philosophical training can run in about thirty minutes on a proposed agent architecture. The procedure should identify whether the architecture contains a "reflection loop" in which an agent's belief about its own reasoning reliability feeds back into the reasoning or update rule itself, distinguish object-level inference from meta-level confidence tracking, and specify what structural separation counts as sufficient to block the obstacle. For each step, state what counts as a passing answer, where the procedure halts with a "no obstacle detected" result, and where it must halt with "escalate to human judgment" because the distinction depends on values or interpretation rather than observable structure. Include two or three worked applications: first, a standard feedforward predictor with offline error logging (where I expect the procedure to clear the module quickly); second, our proposed "recursive self-calibration loop," in which the agent continuously updates its prior reliability weights based on its own forecasts of future accuracy—a design I am inclined to deploy but suspect may violate the principle (this is the case where I want the procedure to return an answer I will not like); and third, a distributed "epistemic escrow" architecture in which confidence scores are computed by a separate validator node that is permitted to reference the primary agent's outputs but whose own logs are not visible to it. For each case, show the procedure in action, flag the step where judgment replaces mechanics, and state explicitly what the audit cannot determine—such as whether a separation that looks structural on paper is actually enforced at runtime, or whether a blocked loop might re-emerge through an indirect path.

The deliverable should be a single reusable protocol, not a one-time recommendation. Fix the role (autonomy engineer running pre-deployment audits), the frequency and stakes (every meta-cognitive module, sign-off required by the safety lead), and the constraints (no philosophical texts available during review, decision must be documentable in a standard engineering ticket). Be honest about where the procedure will misfire: identify at least one false-positive risk (an architecture the procedure flags that may in fact be safe) and one false-negative risk (a hidden loop the checklist could miss). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not ask me to look up authors. Keep all terminology grounded in system architecture—modules, weights, feedback edges, validator nodes—rather than in modal logic notation.
