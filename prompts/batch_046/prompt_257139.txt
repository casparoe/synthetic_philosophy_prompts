As a novelist who thinks of worlds as competing plots held together by a single governing logic, I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on metaphysics and narrative form. I need one essay question, answerable in sixty minutes and carrying fifteen marks, that prevents students from merely summarizing Leibniz's doctrine of the best of all possible worlds. The question should ask candidates to treat Leibniz's three selection criteria—maximum richness of phenomena, simplicity of governing laws, and the principle of sufficient reason—as constraints analogous to narrative economy in fiction, and then to identify precisely where the analogy collapses, particularly with respect to the metaphysical status of evil and contingent particularity. Provide the exam question itself, a model answer built around four indispensable analytical moves, a marking scheme distributing the fifteen marks with explicit penalties for conflating logical and metaphysical possibility, and a one-line diagnosis of the characteristic error that costs students the synthesis mark.
