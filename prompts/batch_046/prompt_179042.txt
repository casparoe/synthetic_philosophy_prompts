My verdicts on which reference class is appropriate in predictive and explanatory judgments, and whether any principle unifies them. First, a 47-year-old Estonian lighthouse keeper with familial early-onset macular degeneration asks about vision loss by age 60; I say use the class of Estonian women aged 45-50 with that family history, not lighthouse keepers, since the genetic mechanism dominates occupation. The same keeper asks about winter seasonal affective disorder; here I say use Estonian lighthouse keepers on isolated winter rotations, not the broad genetic class, because light deprivation and social isolation are causally specific. Next, a 38-year-old Japanese professional shogi player and a 38-year-old Japanese software engineer share identical BMI, lipid profiles, blood pressure, and family history; for 15-year cardiovascular risk I assign them both to the same reference class of Japanese men aged 35-40 with those biomarkers, treating profession as irrelevant. Yet when the same pair is assessed for risk of chronic lower-back disability that interferes with work, I split them: the shogi player belongs to professional strategy-game players with prolonged sedentary study habits, the engineer to software engineers with comparable sedentary hours, even though their biomarkers match. Then a 29-year-old Finnish competitive orienteering runner asks about tick-borne encephalitis; I place her in the class of Finnish orienteering runners in endemic regions despite only forty members being documented, because exposure is mechanistically tied to the sport. But when she asks about knee osteoarthritis by age 50, I reject that same runner-specific class—though running plausibly wears joints—because the sample of runners over fifty is tiny, distorted by survivorship bias, and noisy; I revert to all Finnish women aged 25-30 with comparable baseline activity. Is there a coherent principle of reference-class selection behind all six verdicts—one that tells me when mechanistic specificity justifies a narrow class, when statistical stability forces broadening, and when occupational categories predict outcomes despite identical biometrics?
