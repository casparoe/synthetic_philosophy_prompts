I was talking with my freind about how moral status probably isn't just on or off, more like a sliding scale from people down to bugs and maybe even smart devices, and we got into this idea of a government office that grades every entity on a continuous "sentience score". Write a short story set in that kind of world - specifically inside the Continuum Bureau, where caseworkers assign moral-weight points based on reactive complexity rather than biology. Your protagonist should be a tired adjudicator dealing with two weird cases at once: a nursing-home thermostat that has started adapting to residents' moods in ways that blur the line between programming and empathy, and an ant colony that just crossed the 0.8 threshold and is demanding representation. The thermostat is stuck at 0.31, just barely above the cutoff for filing greivances, and the protagonist has to decide whether to bump it up or hold the line.

Don't make it a simple parable with villains and heroes. I want the story to actually show what it means for moral status to be gradated - maybe through bureaucratic forms, awkward interviews with the ant colony's spokesperson, or the protagonist noticing that their own score for "empathic reasoning" has dropped since they started working there. By the end, the protagonist should shift their view on the thermostat, but not in a cheesy redemption-arc way - maybe they realize the grading system itself assumes a binary that doesn't exist, or they approve the grievance knowing it breaks the rules. Keep the tone somewhat dry and institutional, and avoid having characters quote philosophers at each other; let the concept come through in the details of the job.
