I've been working out my position on the metaphysics and ethics of pain for a graduate seminar, and I want a consistency audit before I present. Here is the package I think I hold: (1) Pain is intrinsically and essentially bad—any state that genuinely counts as pain is, in virtue of being pain, pro tanto a harm; (2) The clinical and neuropsychological literature on pain asymbolia—where patients retain full sensory-discriminative nociception but report no suffering—shows that the sensory and affective dimensions are not just functionally but metaphysically dissociable; (3) At the same time, I think phenomenal concepts of pain are essentially "motivationally loaded": grasping what pain is like necessarily involves grasping its aversive force, which makes the notion of "pain that does not hurt" conceptually incoherent, on a par with "heat that is not warm"; (4) The right clinical endpoint should therefore not be conventional analgesia that suppresses nociceptive transmission, but rather a targeted, reversible decoupling of affective precision-weighting in interoceptive predictive processing—preserving sensory discrimination of tissue damage while eliminating negative valence—because retaining that epistemic access to bodily harm is valuable and the affect is only a contingent evolutionary overlay; (5) I also maintain that evolutionary considerations show the aversive character of pain is an adaptive tagging mechanism rather than part of its essential nature. Please go through these commitments and tell me: which pairs are in genuine logical or motivational tension, which pairs only look incompatible but can be reconciled, and what is the least costly revision for each real conflict? Rank the tensions by severity. Be specific about whether the deepest problem lies between (2) and (3), between (1) and (5), or in (4)'s implicit ontology, and do not treat every pairing as equally problematic.
