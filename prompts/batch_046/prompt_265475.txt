Read Aldous Huxley's Brave New World through the lens of performance legitimacy—the debate over whether sustained prosperity and social order can justify authoritarian governance, with specific reference to the analytical frameworks applied to contemporary China and Singapore. The World State delivers precisely what performance-legitimacy theorists promise: material abundance, the elimination of war and poverty, engineered contentment through chemical and behavioral conditioning, and social stability maintained without democratic input. Mustapha Mond explicitly defends this trade-off, while John the Savage rejects it in the name of human dignity, authentic suffering, and individual freedom.

Provide a critical reading that moves in both directions. First, apply the distinction between input legitimacy and output legitimacy to Mond's defense: does he justify the State solely by its measurable results, or does he rely on hidden premises about human nature, happiness, and the value of knowledge? Examine the conditioning-center tours, the routine distribution of soma, the suppression of scientific inquiry, and the confrontation between Mond and the Savage to ask whether Huxley depicts a regime that genuinely succeeds on its own metrics, or whether he embeds hidden failures—such as the hollowing of art, the destruction of genuine human attachment, and the redesign of human psychology—into the very mechanism that produces prosperity and order.

Second, ask what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that abstract argument cannot. Political-science treatments of performance legitimacy typically treat prosperity and order as external outputs delivered to stable subjects; Huxley imagines a society that optimizes those outputs by altering the subjects themselves. Does this expose a blind spot in the framework—namely, that maximizing output legitimacy may require eliminating the kind of agents who could meaningfully value autonomy? Or does the novel unfairly load the case by equating well-being with sedation?

Finally, address where the work resists the reading. Does Huxley's critique depend on a specifically Western liberal commitment to individual autonomy that defenders of performance legitimacy—whether in Singapore's developmental-state discourse or in China's prosperity-and-stability narrative—explicitly reject? If so, can the novel still pose a challenge that someone accepting the framework must answer, or does it merely reinforce liberal assumptions?
