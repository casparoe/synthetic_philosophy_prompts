I have recently begun working with formal proof assistants, and it has forced me to ask whether a computer-verified proof is still a mental construction in any meaningful sense. If Brouwer—the founder of intuitionism—were confronted with modern interactive theorem provers, with Martin-Löf's type-theoretic formalization of intuitionistic reasoning, and with the idea that equality itself can be interpreted homotopically through the univalence principle, how would he have reacted? Would he have regarded these as natural extensions of his view that mathematics precedes language, or as a dangerous formalism that substitutes symbolic manipulation for intuitive construction? I am not asking for a survey of intuitionism, but for a philosophically serious reconstruction: apply his actual commitments about the continuum, about the rejection of the law of excluded middle, and about the primacy of mental activity over formal system to these specific post-war developments. In particular, address whether he could have accepted proofs as computational objects with internal structure, or whether that very idea violates his distinction between mathematics and its linguistic expression.
