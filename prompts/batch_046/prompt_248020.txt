Debates that invoke Hofstadter's tripartite distinction from Gödel, Escher, Bach—frame messages (the interpretive machinery or decoding rules), outer messages (the decoded sequence or pattern considered relationally), and inner messages (the representational content that the frame unlocks)—have settled into a peculiar standstill. One camp, the frame-locators, holds that meaning is constituted by the decoder: without the ribosome's reading rules or the phonograph's speed setting, the outer sequence is merely chemistry or plastic grooves, so semantic value enters only at the frame. A second camp, the inner-message defenders, insists that the frame is only a key; the content is already structurally present in the DNA or groove pattern, and meaning resides in what the frame reveals rather than in the frame itself. A third position, the outer-message view, treats the frame as a causal precondition but locates meaning in the relational properties of the decoded string, independent both of its physical substrate and of the deeper representational state it might induce.

The stall is puzzling because all three sides accept the same analogies and even the same synthetic cases—engineered genes read by artificial ribosomes with non-natural frames, corrupted frame instructions that still yield functional proteins, phonographs playing records at wrong speeds that produce audible but unrecognizable patterns—yet none of these cases move anyone. Each side re-describes the outcome: the frame-locator calls wrong-speed playback "noise without semantic value"; the inner-message defender calls it "genuine but distorted content"; the outer-message theorist calls it "a different message altogether, neither the intended one nor mere noise."

My working hypothesis is that this is partly a verbal dispute about what "location" means—causal, functional, or phenomenological—and partly a misalignment of questions asked under one heading: one group asks about the conditions necessary for interpretation, another about the ontology of representational content, and a third about the causal chain that produces understanding. But I am unsure whether there is also a hidden formal question doing the work—whether frames themselves must carry representational content in order to decode content, or whether a purely syntactic frame can ever be semantically neutral.

Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says about these synthetic and mis-decoding cases. Is the impasse verbal, substantive, or a case of different questions masked by shared vocabulary? Is there a bedrock intuition about whether a message can exist unread? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—an engineered synthetic-biology experiment that cleanly decouples frame fidelity from functional outcome, a conceptual distinction between "conditions for interpretation" and "locus of content," or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be dissolved?
