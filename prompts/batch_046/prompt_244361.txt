Having recently been asked to advise on the interpretation of an unprovenanced assemblage of ritual vessels for a local museum display, I have become caught in a methodological disagreement that now feels personally urgent. One camp holds that standardized material forms—repeated vessel shapes, consistent decorative motifs, and structured deposition patterns—provide direct evidence of shared institutional beliefs and collective ritual intentions, essentially allowing us to read cognitive coherence from physical regularity. The opposing view maintains that such artifacts only record practical habits, supply chains, and social coordination mechanisms, and that attributing specific beliefs or institutions to them commits a category error that conflates institutional footprints with doxastic residues. Write an essay on whether archaeologists can legitimately reconstruct beliefs and institutions from material objects in the absence of textual corroboration. Weigh the main arguments for the inferentialist and minimalist positions carefully, but do not end on an equivocal note: defend a specific verdict about where the evidential boundary lies and why.
