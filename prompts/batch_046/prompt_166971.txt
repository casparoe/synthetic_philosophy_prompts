I'm writing a feature on whether cultural values can do genuine causal work in economic history, using Weber's Protestant ethic thesis as the central test case. In drafting, I keep returning to an argument that seems to show culture can never be a cause—yet I suspect there is a hidden slide in it. Here is the chain as I have it laid out:

(1) If a cultural value genuinely causes an economic system, then wherever that value is widely present and baseline material conditions are satisfied, the system should be the modal tendency.
(2) The Protestant work ethic was widely present across Northern Europe in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, not only in the regions that industrialized first.
(3) Capitalism did not emerge uniformly across those regions; some Protestant areas developed slowly or not at all, while certain Catholic regions advanced.
(4) So the ethic was neither necessary (capitalism arose elsewhere) nor sufficient (it failed to produce capitalism everywhere it existed).
(5) But if values are never necessary or sufficient for economic outcomes, they cannot be genuine causes—only correlates or post-hoc narratives.
(6) Therefore either Weber's thesis is false, or culture is never a cause in economic history.
(7) The second disjunct is implausible: values plainly shape labor norms, investment practices, and risk attitudes in well-documented ways.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) than (5), because (1) captures the intuition that causal explanations should carry some modal robustness rather than being purely accidental correlations. But if retaining (1) forces me into (6), I want to know exactly what I am paying—whether the fix requires abandoning Weber specifically, or whether it only exposes a broader mistake about what causation requires. Please diagnose the step, explain why it looked sound, and price the alternatives.
