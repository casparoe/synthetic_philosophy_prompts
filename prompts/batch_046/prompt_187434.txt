I have recently been weighing whether to leave a stable research position for an uncertain but potentially more impactful role in public governance, and the decision has forced me to clarify my views on value comparison. Here is where I stand: (1) I accept value pluralism—goods such as intellectual autonomy, social contribution, and relational stability are genuinely incommensurable and resist reduction to a single scale; (2) I think hard choices are not rational failures but occasions for authoring—by committing to one path I generate reasons that did not exist prior to the choice; (3) I believe a rational agent can experience appropriate regret, or normative residue, after a hard choice without having made a mistake; (4) nevertheless, if option A is marginally better on one incommensurable value and no worse on all others, then A is determinately preferable and the choice is not genuinely hard; (5) I think institutions should avoid forcing incommensurable values into single metrics such as cost-benefit analysis when designing policy. Do these hang together? Specifically, does (4) undermine (1) and (2) by smuggling back in a hidden comparability condition? Is (3) compatible with (2)'s claim that commitment creates reasons, or does residue imply that the unchosen option retained undefeated weight? And does my institutional view in (5) cohere with my individual view in (2), or am I assuming that collective decision-making lacks the authoring capacity available to persons? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which pair is only apparently in tension, and say what the least costly revision would be—giving up the threshold in (4), weakening pluralism in (1), or restricting authoring to personal contexts.
