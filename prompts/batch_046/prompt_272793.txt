Write an essay on whether predictive processing, interpreted through the free energy principle, can coherently distinguish veridical perception from hallucination without importing normative criteria that lie outside the framework. Critics observe that, within the model, the brain minimizes variational free energy either by updating predictions or by adjusting precision weights; consequently, a system that assigns pathologically high precision to a false prediction — as occurs in certain hallucinations — is, from its own internal standpoint, executing the very computation the principle requires. If accuracy is judged solely by local free-energy reduction, then the framework appears unable to mark the difference between seeing an object and merely seeming to see it, unless it appeals to an evolutionary or ecological success condition that is not itself derived from free-energy minimization. Defenders respond that precision weights are calibrated over long timescales to environmental regularities, so "inappropriate" precision is just poor statistical calibration relative to those regularities. Evaluate this exchange and defend a verdict: does the free energy principle genuinely lack internal resources to separate perception from illusion, or does the objection conflate local surprise minimization with global model fidelity? In your answer, consider whether the notion of "epistemic friction" — the differential resistance that the environment exerts against competing predictive models — can be fully captured as a parameter of precision weighting, or whether it necessarily points to an external benchmark that predictive processing cannot supply.
