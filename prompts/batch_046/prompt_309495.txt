I've been working through al-Ghazālī's occasionalism on my own, and I think I see the standard move: fire does not burn cotton, God creates the burning in direct association with the fire, and the habit is just divine regularity. But I'm stuck on a more granular conceptual problem that the usual summaries do not address. Let's call it the problem of modal intermediaries. Between seeing fire near cotton and seeing the cotton burn, there is my mental state of expecting the result, and that expectation carries content — specifically, the content that the fire will cause the burning. In the occasionalist picture, God creates both the expectation and the subsequent burning, with no real causal link running through the fire. But God also seems to create the expectation with that precise causal content. So the expectation is not tracking an actual power in the world; it is just another synchronized event in the divine orchestration. This makes me wonder: can occasionalism distinguish a genuinely rational inductive belief from a merely implanted correlation-belief? If every expectation is directly authored by God with whatever content God chooses, then what normative work is induction doing? Does al-Ghazālī have a way to preserve the rational authority of human reasoning without secretly reintroducing real causation between mental states and the world? I would like an intuitive explanation of whether occasionalism collapses here, or whether there is a conceptual distinction I am missing that saves the normative force of induction without compromising the core claim that God alone is the direct cause.
