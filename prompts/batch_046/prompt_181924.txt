I am a high school senior taking an introductory philosophy seminar, and I have become stuck on a problem about what actually makes a life good rather than merely impressive. My older cousin, who works in urban planning, recently received two offers: a senior role at a prestigious firm with international travel, competitive recognition, intellectually intense projects, and a high salary; and a local position maintaining public green spaces, with low pay, almost no prestige, very little responsibility, and a rhythm so calm that she describes it as "barely feeling like work." She is strongly inclined toward the senior role because she believes a good life must contain intense achievements, moments of pride, and deep engagement, and she worries that choosing the quieter path would be a kind of resignation or failure of ambition. At the same time, she notices that the senior position seems to generate an endless chain of new desires—better reviews, larger networks, more impressive outcomes—together with chronic stress and social comparison, whereas the green-space role leaves her with almost no cravings, almost no suffering, and almost no dramatic highs either. I find myself drawn to the idea that there might be a coherent philosophical position—one our course texts never name—according to which only the absence of craving and suffering carries real value, while achievements, pleasures, and excitements are essentially neutral, or even harmful when they seed new wants. To make this less abstract, I have started framing the dispute as "subtractive" versus "additive" welfare: is a life improved only by removing disturbances, or is it improved by accumulating positive goods? If the subtractive view is defensible, how should it apply to her choice? Specifically, is her fear that the calm path is "giving up" actually a confusion between the absence of negative states and the absence of valuable experiences? I want an analysis that treats this as a genuine axiological disagreement rather than as a psychological preference for comfort, and that explains whether tranquility alone can be sufficient for a good life without collapsing into mere avoidance of effort.
