You sit on the planning board of a mid-sized town deciding whether to approve a privately funded, publicly accessible garden designed around the rituals and symbolic layout of one specific religious tradition. The proponents say it will serve all residents as a quiet communal space; critics worry that its form imposes a particular spiritual framework on shared land.

One board member, who practices that tradition, supports the project without citing scripture. She argues that modern zoning and planning treat people as temporary consumers of space rather than as beings embedded in obligations that outlast individual interest and lifespan. She claims her tradition offers the most coherent account of why residents should maintain and respect neighborhoods they may never personally own or profit from, and that excluding this account forces the board to rely on an impoverished, purely instrumental model of civic membership. She insists she is not asking anyone to adopt her beliefs, only to recognize that her worldview supplies a practically indispensable resource for long-term community stability—one that secular frameworks, in her view, cannot replicate.

Another board member objects that public planning must rest on considerations that do not depend on any particular metaphysical or moral framework. He says that framing religious commitment in terms of “obligation” or “stability” does not remove its dependence on premises non-adherents reject. He further argues that requiring religious citizens to translate their reasons into shared vocabulary, while secular citizens may present their own contested commitments—such as the equal moral status of all persons or the priority of individual autonomy—as if they were neutral, imposes an unfair asymmetry on deliberation.

You are unsure how to vote, but your deeper concern is conceptual: is the first member making a legitimate contribution to collective decision-making, or is she privileging one particular vision of human flourishing under the guise of civic utility? More precisely, does the demand for reasons independent of any worldview itself rest on a contested conception of citizenship that quietly favors secular moral frameworks? How should you evaluate her argument, and what conditions would have to be met for a religious contribution to be genuinely acceptable in this kind of shared choice without being reduced to mere rhetoric?
