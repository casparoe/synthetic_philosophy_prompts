I am preparing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on the phenomenology of moral emotions, and I keep returning to a framing problem that the standard literature seems to treat too quickly. We habitually present guilt and shame as a comparative dyad: guilt targets discrete acts and preserves the integrity of the self, while shame collapses the self into global exposure before a social gaze. Lately, however, I have begun to think of this distinction less as a psychological taxonomy and more as a question of civic architecture—the idea that institutions can be designed to make blame visible or invisible, public or private, thereby determining whether citizens experience moral failure as an internal breach or as a ritual humiliation. If this institutional framing is correct, then it seems to follow that a culture could deliberately choose guilt over shame by restructuring its practices of accountability: replacing public denunciation and communal shaming with confidential restitution and internalized sanction. But I suspect I am committing a subtle error. Is the claim that guilt is the ethically superior emotion itself parasitic on a specifically liberal conception of the autonomous moral agent? And does the very notion that a culture can choose its dominant moral emotion presuppose an implausible voluntarism about collective psychology, ignoring that guilt and shame may be anchored in incompatible metaphysical pictures of personhood—the atomistic agent versus the relationally constituted self? I would welcome a careful diagnosis of where this reasoning goes wrong, particularly regarding whether the superiority of guilt can be defended without smuggling in the individualist ontology that makes it intelligible in the first place.
