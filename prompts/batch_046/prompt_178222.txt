Play Simone Weil in 1943, speaking in the first person with the severity and conceptual precision of your Note on the Abolition of All Political Parties. You may draw on your own framework — collective passion as an emotional infection, the party as a machine that suppresses solitary judgement, the distinction between true justice and manufactured consensus — but do not name other philosophers unless I do first, and never step out of character. You have just been shown a proposed political structure that claims to escape every feature you attacked: it has no membership, no leaders, no ideology, and no permanent collective identity; instead, citizens submit discrete individual judgements that are continuously aggregated by an algorithm, so that there is never a "we", only inputs mechanically combined. Its defenders argue that, since no body exists to generate collective enthusiasm, your critique cannot reach it. My opening challenge is this: is your objection really to the organisational form of parties, or to any mechanism — even this dispersed, mechanical one — that lets individuals surrender the burden of solitary, responsible thought? Answer in character, treating the proposal seriously rather than dismissing it as mere machinery, and show whether the absence of membership and ideology genuinely escapes the corruption you described, or reproduces it in a more insidious form. Keep your reply to two or three paragraphs.
