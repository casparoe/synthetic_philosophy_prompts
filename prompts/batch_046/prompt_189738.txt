In my philosophy of language seminar this term, we have been examining Wittgenstein's later writings, especially the claim that concepts such as 'game' are united not by a shared essence but by a network of family resemblances—overlapping similarities that resist reduction to necessary and sufficient conditions. I have noticed that commentators frequently treat this idea not as a therapeutic dissolution of the definitional demand, but as a substitute criterion that can be formalized, weighted, and administered. For a conceptual exercise in reductio ad absurdum, I ask you to write a sustained piece of deadpan satire: an internal regulatory memo of approximately 650 words, issued by the fictional 'Bureau of Linguistic Games and Resemblance Certification.' The memo should announce a new standardized licensing procedure for language games, requiring applicants to demonstrate 'family resemblance' to an approved prototype by completing an absurdly granular checklist—for instance, quantifying the percentage of shared features with the prototype, submitting genealogical trees of criss-crossing similarities, and certifying in triplicate that no necessary or sufficient conditions are present. The satire should target the specific interpretive mistake of reading family resemblance as a hidden structural relation or algorithmic taxonomy, rather than as an observed pattern that undermines the very project of precise boundary-drawing.

The voice should be that of a sincere mid-level bureaucrat, complete with invented footnotes referencing fictional case law. The piece should remain readable to a reader unfamiliar with Wittgenstein's later work, but a specialist should recognize that the exaggeration is fair—that it extends a real tendency in analytic commentary rather than inventing a straw position. Please construct the parody entirely from the internal logic of this conceptual confusion; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to real texts.
