Next Thursday I defend a revision of my dissertation chapter to a seminar audience split between analytic moral philosophers steeped in Strawsonian accounts of responsibility and legal theorists who insist that moral accountability must be individual to be coherent or actionable in doctrine. My thesis is that Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes should be expanded to include what I term "structural reactive attitudes"—responses directed not at an individual's quality of will but at institutional roles, organizational patterns, and systemic design—without collapsing individual responsibility into collective blame. I develop this in three moves: that personal reactive attitudes already implicitly reference social roles (the resentful parent is also a citizen reacting to institutional failure); that stripping away these references produces a descriptively inadequate, atomistic psychology; and that recognizing structural attitudes lets us distinguish legitimate collective accountability from mere scapegoating. The audience will include philosophers who regard collective responsibility as conceptually confused and at least one legal scholar who will press whether any of this has purchase in actual tort or criminal doctrine. Please prepare me for the Q&A as a prep sheet in American English: list the six or seven objections I will actually face, ordered by the damage they could do, phrase each the way a Strawsonian moral philosopher or a legal theorist would actually put it, and for each indicate what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and the weakest point in my reply. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—specifically, whether structural reactive attitudes are genuinely moral responses or merely political judgments expressed in moral vocabulary—and suggest how to answer it without sounding like I am changing the subject. Time the replies for roughly two to three minutes each, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
