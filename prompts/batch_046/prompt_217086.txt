I am currently taking a graduate course on the philosophy of social science, in which we have been examining classic critiques of historical determinism alongside contemporary debates about whether large-scale predictive modelling—including statistical simulations of demographic and institutional change—can recover durable macro-patterns in human affairs. During a seminar, one of the tutors offered a compressed summary of Karl Popper's *The Poverty of Historicism* that struck me as both compelling and possibly too sweeping. According to this account, Popper's central achievement is empirical: he demonstrates that historicism is discredited because its specific prophecies fail—most notably, Marxist predictions of an inevitable proletarian revolution that did not materialise on schedule. From these failures, Popper is said to derive a general methodological prohibition: because societies evolve in irreducibly open-ended ways, there can be no universal scientific laws in sociology or economics at all, only descriptive chronicles. He concedes that historians occasionally identify temporary 'trends', but he allegedly treats these as entirely non-lawlike, since they depend on initial conditions that never repeat exactly; on this reading, trends possess no forecasting utility whatsoever, not even conditionally. Consequently, the tutor concludes, Popper regards any large-scale social planning guided by historical patterns as not merely ethically dangerous—he associates it with the road to totalitarianism—but also conceptually impossible, because the social sciences lack the kind of universal generalisations that make physics genuinely predictive.

The summary sounds authoritative, yet I have the impression that Popper's actual argument is more nuanced—particularly that he distinguishes unconditional prophecy from conditional scientific prediction, and that he does not intend to eliminate all law-like explanation from the social sciences.
