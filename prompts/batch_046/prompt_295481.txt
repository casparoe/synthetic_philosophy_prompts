Q: Explain the "scale argument" for taking insect welfare seriously even though we're unsure any given insect feels pain. Then present and evaluate one serious objection.

Student answer:
The scale argument is basically expected-value math. If there's a 0.01% chance that any particular insect is sentient—since sentience needs some minimal neural architecture and most bugs don't have it—then with about 10^19 insects alive we still get ~10^15 sentient individuals in expectation. That's way more than farmed vertebrates, so ignoring insects is like ignoring a continent of suffering. This framing comes straight from Peter Singer's Animal Liberation, where he extends equal consideration to every sentient being no matter the species.

One objection is that this confuses two different kinds of uncertainty. You shouldn't apply the 0.01% probablity individually, because sentience is a species-level trait: either bees have it or they don't. So the right method is to assign probabilities to whole taxa—maybe 30% for bees, near 0 for flies—and then multiply by population within each group. Once you correct the math that way, the expected total falls sharply because lots of insect groups have almost no chance. Also, even if the numbers stay big, moral theories that require actual harm rather than expected harm won't generate obligations here, since no specific insect is definitely suffering.
