Read E.B. White's Charlotte's Web through the lens of sentience as a matter of degree and the gradation of moral status running from insects to thermostats. Charlotte is granted language, loyalty, and self-sacrifice, yet she remains an orb-weaver who kills to eat and dies after egg-laying; Wilbur is a sentient mammal with human-like fear of death; Fern is a child; and the farm itself is full of tools and mechanisms that respond to stimuli without consciousness. Does the novel map these figures onto a continuous gradient of moral considerability, or does it preserve sharp thresholds? I want a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously: address whether Charlotte's narrative interiority places her closer to Wilbur than to Templeton the rat, and whether the text ever flirts with attributing even minimal intentional states to inanimate farm objects in the way that philosophers extend the intentional stance to thermostats. Where does the work illuminate the difficulty of fixing a non-arbitrary cutoff for moral status—perhaps in Charlotte's death, which is mourned differently from Wilbur's salvation, or in Fern's shifting sympathy from saving the runt to accepting the spider's fate? And where does it resist a gradualist reading, insisting that Charlotte's sacrifice is meaningful precisely because she crosses a kind-boundary rather than sliding along a scale? Say whether White's portrayal adds anything to the philosophical discussion that pure argument misses—perhaps about how narrative sympathy can extend across a sentience gap without collapsing it into equality.
