At a recent seminar, two colleagues—Jordan and Riley—debated whether philosophy genuinely makes progress. Jordan proposed that philosophical progress should be measured not by accumulating settled truths but by what he termed "precision of inheritance": a later thinker can articulate the errors or limitations of an earlier position using conceptual resources that the earlier thinker would recognize as internal to their own project. He offered the shift from Cartesian substance dualism to modern property dualism as an example—not a change of subject, but a sharpening of the same explanatory gap made ontologically more precise. Riley replied that this criterion is too permissive; by it, even astrology progresses, since contemporary astrologers can frame Ptolemaic mistakes in Ptolemaic terms. Genuine progress, she argued, requires convergence on a stock of solved problems that practitioners can treat as settled and need not revisit. Philosophy possesses no such stock—free will, moral realism, and consciousness are reopened by every cohort with no inherited sense of resolution. Jordan answered that mathematics also lacks a permanently settled foundation—logicism, formalism, and intuitionism all remain live options—yet mathematics clearly progresses. In philosophy, reopening questions is not a failure but the mechanism of progress: puzzles become technically tractable and migrate into decision theory, formal semantics, or cognitive science, leaving philosophy with refined versions of the original problem. Riley countered that this renders philosophy self-eliminating: if progress consists in exporting questions to other disciplines without resolving them, then the more philosophy succeeds, the less philosophy remains, which is a strange form of progress. Jordan responded by comparing the situation to biology: molecular genetics did not make biology self-eliminating by replacing vitalism with answers, and philosophy similarly transforms its subject matter without disappearing. Riley said the analogy breaks down because genetics replaced vitalism with answers, whereas philosophy exports its questions without answering them. They asked me to referee, and I could not resolve the dispute. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: evaluate Jordan's "precision of inheritance" standard, Riley's convergence test, Jordan's mathematics analogy, Riley's self-elimination objection, and Jordan's biology counter-analogy. For each move, say who is more right and why. Then identify what both parties are missing about how conceptual refinement relates to disciplinary boundaries, and suggest whether a coherent middle position is available.
