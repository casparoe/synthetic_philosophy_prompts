I am not trained in philosophy or political theory, but I have been thinking through a moral problem on my own and have tried to organize my reasoning in a short essay. The problem is the familiar one about a lifeboat that is full and a person in the water asking to be taken aboard. Rather than debating whether there is literal physical space, I want to distinguish two obligations that I think usually get conflated: the obligation to rescue someone who is immediately in peril, and what I am calling the maintenance obligation—the duty that each person aboard owes to the collective labor that keeps the boat from sinking. In my scenario, the boat is slowly filling with water, and survival depends on continuous bailing; every occupant must contribute. The stranger asks to board. The standard question asks whether saving one more life is worth risking all others. I want to ask a different question: even if there is physical room for one more body, is there maintenance room? That is, will the stranger's expected contribution to the bailing equal or exceed the additional risk, consumption, and burden they introduce?

From this, I try to argue that many real-world debates about immigration and resource scarcity are not actually about counting lives saved versus lives lost, but about anxiety over this maintenance debt—whether newcomers will share in the labor of sustaining shared institutions. I suggest that if this anxiety is legitimate, the moral framework should shift from a simple calculus of rescue to an evaluation of reciprocal contribution and collective burden. I then worry that my framing might wrongly assume that those already aboard have a prior claim not because of greater need, but because of prior labor investment, and I try to resist that by insisting that the maintenance obligation applies equally to anyone who boards, old or new.

These are only rough notes. Would you comment on whether the distinction between rescue obligation and maintenance obligation is coherent? Does it unfairly diminish the urgency of the drowning person's claim? And is there an obvious philosophical objection—perhaps from an ethics of care or from a rights-based view—that exposes a hidden assumption I have not defended? I am particularly interested in whether my argument accidentally privileges those who happen to be inside the boat at the moment of decision.
