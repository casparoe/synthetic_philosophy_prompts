I'm the sole ethics auditor for a small city archieve that digitizes donated personal letters every quarter—about fifteen batches a year, I get maybe twenty mintues per batch, and I have to sign off alone with no lawyer in the room. I need a reusable decision procedure that takes whatever philosophy actually knows about privacy—informational control, being left alone, contextual norms, the works—and turns it into a checklist I can run without being a philosopher.

Write it as a sequence of questions in fixed order. For each step say what counts as a clear yes/no/maybe, where the procedure stops with a hard reject, where it stops with a clear pass, and where it has to hand the call back to me because it's a values judgment not a fact. Be honest about which steps are just judgment calls in a trenchcoat. Build in a quick test for "narrative exposure risk"—when a letter's original private context gets stripped and its content gets reinterpreted inside a research frame the donor never intended. Don't just name it; make it a step I can actually run.

Then apply it to three batches:
1. Love letters from 1943 between two people now dead, donated by a grandchild who wants them public for a local history project.
2. Letters from a 1980s neighborhood activist complaining about zoning, full of neighbors' names and health details—donated anonymously with no donor consent on file.
3. A teacher's diary describing classroom failures and student misbehavior, donated by the estate with explicit written permission to publish.

Make sure one comes back with a pass that I'm probly not gonna like, and one hits the wall where the procedure can't settle it and I just have to decide. Also say flat out what this procedure can't do—like, can it protect third parties mentioned in the letters if the donor said yes? Keep it practical, include the judgment-call flags, and dont ask me for a bibliography or reading list.
