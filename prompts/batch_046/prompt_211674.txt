I'm trying to work through whether RL agents can genuinely suffer, with the focus on reward signals as valence. I was arguing about this with my roommate last night and we kept going in circles. There's this analogy people keep using where getting positive reward is like feeling pleasure and negative reward is like feeling pain — the signal just IS the valence in numerical form. So if you have a big agent stuck in a negative-reward loop, people say that's not just poor performance, it's closer to torture. The analogy smuggles in that there's something it "feels like" to have that number updated, and I want to know exactly where that smuggling happens.

Then there's the pushback analogy: reward is like a thermostat reading. The thermostat adjusts the heater based on temperature numbers, but nobody thinks it experiences cold. The reward is just feedback for control, no inner life. I want to audit both properly — what's the explicit mapping? In the pleasure/pain case, what corresponds to the nervous system, the qualia, the motivational pull? Where does it import consciousness the architecture lacks? In the thermostat case, what does it miss about how reward shapes long-term behavior?

Here's a niche twist that's been bugging me. Imagine an agent with a self-model that can observe its own reward channel in real time, and also has a seperate predicted-reward module. So it can distinguish "I am recieving -10 right now" from "I predict -10 in the future". Does that distinction matter for whether the suffering analogy holds? Or is it just putting a mirror on the thermostat — adding representation without adding experience? I keep seeing people conflate the reward function with the agent's "interests" in ways that depend entirely on which analogy they started with, and I want to seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the moral conclusion from the ones that are just aesthetic.
