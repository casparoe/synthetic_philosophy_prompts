Construct an argument that the canonical discrete formulation of the Sleeping Beauty problem masks a deeper structural question about self-locating belief by framing the update solely in terms of counting awakenings. To expose this, propose and work through a continuous-time variant: Beauty is sedated on Sunday and will be awakened exactly once at a time drawn uniformly from a 48-hour interval if the coin lands heads, but twice—separated by exactly one hour, with both awakening times drawn uniformly and independently from that same interval—if it lands tails. Upon awakening, she knows she is awake but has no access to the clock or calendar, so her indexical information is a location in a continuous temporal space rather than a discrete awakening number.

Argue that in this setting the standard “halfer” and “thirder” positions cannot both be consistently applied, because there is no natural counting measure over the uncountable set of centered worlds compatible with her evidence; instead, resolving her credence requires integrating over a specific metric structure on temporal locations weighted by the number of awakenings per unit time. From this technical result, defend the broader philosophical claim that self-locating belief should not be modeled as probabilistic updating over discrete centered worlds, but rather as updating over a measure space of possible agent-locations, where the “weight” of a world depends on its density within that space.

Finally, explain why this continuous analysis undermines the intuitive force of the original discrete problem: if the correct treatment of indexical information requires a metric-space integration rather than a simple ratio of awakening counts, then Beauty’s change in credence upon awakening in the standard two-day version reflects an artifact of discretization rather than a genuine feature of rational self-locating belief. Be precise about how the measure is assigned, address the objection that the continuous case merely complicates without illuminating the discrete one, and conclude with what this implies for other puzzles involving centered propositions—such as the doomsday argument or duplicate-identity scenarios—where counting individuals or times plays a central role.
