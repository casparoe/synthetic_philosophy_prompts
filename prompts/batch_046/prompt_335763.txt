I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on development ethics, and the question has become personally urgent because I recently began volunteering with an organization that delivers emergency food aid in drought-prone regions; my supervisor insists that we should never label drought-related hunger a "natural disaster," but should always call it an entitlement failure or a political crime, which has forced me to examine my own intuitions about where nature ends and politics begins. Here are my verdicts on specific historical cases, and I want to know whether they reflect one coherent principle or whether some have to be overturned.

In the 1943 Bengal famine, I think it was clearly a political crime rather than a natural disaster: the rice blight was relatively minor, and the mass death resulted from wartime procurement priorities, speculative price spikes, and the colonial administration's failure to protect entitlements. The Irish Potato Famine of 1845–52 strikes me the same way—though the blight was a biological event, the continued export of other grains and the refusal of direct relief turned starvation into a crime of institutional omission. The Chinese Great Leap Forward famine is even more obviously political: there was no comparable natural trigger, and the catastrophe arose from forced collectivization, false production reporting, and the deliberate denial of crisis. But I am genuinely torn about the 1974 Bangladesh famine. Sen's analysis shows that flooding destroyed the aman rice crop and that rural laborers lost wages and purchasing power, yet there was no aggregate national food shortage; the deaths came from an entitlement collapse that the state failed to arrest. I feel pulled to classify it as a political failure of entitlement protection, analogous to Bengal, but I also feel that the severe meteorological shock distinguishes it—without the flood, the labor-market collapse might not have occurred—so I am unsure whether to call it a natural disaster mediated by politics, or a purely political crime that used a flood as cover.

I also want my principle, if there is one, to handle a contrast I have been turning over: in a democratic country with robust cash-transfer and strategic grain-reserve institutions, an identical meteorological shock produces no starvation, whereas in 1974 Bangladesh it killed tens of thousands. Does the absence of famine in the democratic case retroactively prove that the Bangladesh deaths were entirely political, or does it merely show that politics can neutralize nature without erasing nature's causal role? And what would the principle say about a hypothetical scenario—let's call it anticipatory entitlement erosion—where a state-adjacent monopoly hoards grain in anticipation of a predicted drought, so that prices spike and laborers starve before any rainfall deficit actually materializes? Is that closer to Bengal or to the democratic counterfactual? I am looking for the precise principle that captures my verdicts on Bengal, Ireland, the Great Leap Forward, and Bangladesh, together with predictions for these two further cases, and an honest statement of which of my four historical verdicts it forces me to overturn if I value parsimony over saving every intuition.
