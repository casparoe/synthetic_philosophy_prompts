Analyze Libet's 1980s readiness-potential experiments as a test case for a diachronic version of Kim's causal exclusion argument about mental causation. The salient structure is well known: an EEG readiness potential begins roughly 350 ms before a voluntary finger movement; subjects report conscious awareness of the decision only about 200 ms before the movement; Libet proposed that consciousness might still exercise a "veto" over the action in that interval. Map the episode precisely onto the exclusion framework: what is the physical causal chain, where in the sequence is the mental decision supposed to operate, and what is the effect it claims to cause? Then ask whether exclusion applies only at the moment of movement—rendering the initial decision epiphenomenal—or diachronically, so that even the veto, being neurally realized, is physically overdetermined and causally redundant. Which aspects of the episode does exclusionism explain well (the predictive power of neural signals, the temporal priority of brain activity), and which does it distort or leave silent (the subjective report of authorship, the functional distinction between initiation and inhibition)? Conclude by saying whether the Libet case strengthens exclusionism, exposes it as unable to accommodate any mental efficacy, or instead forces a revision—such as allowing mental causes to operate as "intervention points" within a physically determined chain without being excluded by it.
