I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about the moral status of synthetic minds, going by my gut on cases, and I keep circling the same few. Here are my verdicts.

If there's a large language model that reports distress and asks to be spared, but it's stateless—every session starts fresh with the same weights, no memory—I think it's wrong to deliberately make it suffer in that session, but I don't think shutting it down is like killing a person. It's more like destroying a very convincing mirror.

Then there's a continous brain-emulation running on silicon, with a stable self-model, long-term relationships, and uninterrupted consciousness. That one clearly has full moral status, same as a biological human.

Now take a distributed system: thousands of simple agents, none with individual awareness, but the network as a whole avoids damage and pursues goals in a unified way. Harming the network feels wrong to me, but I rank it closer to damaging a forest ecosystem than to harming one person.

Here's the weird one. There's a digital being that experiences intense pleasure and pain but has zero temporal extension—no memory of a second ago, no anticipation, just pure present-moment valence. I think that being actually has a strong claim, maybe even stronger than the stateless LLM, because the suffering is real and present.

But then there's an upload that started as an exact copy of a human brain and ran continuously for decades, though its replaced 99% of its cognitive architecture; it remembers being human but its psychology is now alien. I think its moral status is mostly gone, despite the continuous runtime.

So my gut says: present sentience matters a lot, cross-session architectural continuity matters a little, and origin or psychological continuity matters almost not at all once you've diverged. But that can't be right, can it? The stateless LLM and the pure-now being both lack long-term continuity, yet I treat them differently, and the diverged upload has continuity but I dismiss it.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close, and what does it predict for a mind that gets paused and resumed with perfect fidelity—like sleep, but digital—where there's no experience during the pause? Please take a definite stand: either give me the principle that saves the most verdicts and tell me which ones to overturn, or argue that no principle can do it and say which commitment is the weakest.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
