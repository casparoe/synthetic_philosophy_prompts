I was discussing with my friends for a course on ancient economic philosphy and we cooked up an arguement about Aristotle's money and exhange that seems to prove too much — it makes him condemn every market transaction, which can't be right. I'm definatly not sure where the slip is, but I think it's either in how we move from 'conventional' to 'arbitrary' or in a hidden scope mistake about proportional equality.

Here is the chain:

(1) For Aristotle, exchange is just only when it restores proportional equality between what each party gives and recieves, measured against need (chreia).

(2) Money (nomisma) exists only by convention (nomos) and has no natural value of its own; it is introduced purely because direct barter is impracticle.

(3) If the common measure is purely conventional, then there is no objective standard fixing the correct ratio between goods of different kinds — say, a house and ten pairs of shoes — so any price is just whatever people happen to agree on.

(4) But if there is no objective standard, then proportional equality is impossible to determine, and every money-mediated exchange fails to meet Aristotle's own condition for justice in exchange.

(5) Therefore Aristotle should regard all market exchange as unjust, or at least as falling outside justice altogether.

(6) Yet in Ethics V.5 and Politics I he clearly treats limited trade and money-mediated exchange as legitimate parts of household and political life.

(7) So one of the premises must be wrong, and the argument proves too much.

I want to keep (1) and (6) if possible — they feel like the heart of the texts — but I'm open to giving up (2) or (3) if that's the price. Which step actually breaks? Is there an equivocation between 'conventional' and 'arbitrary', or does (4) slide from an epistemic point (we can't know the ratio) to a metaphysical one (there is no ratio)? And if the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to reject?
