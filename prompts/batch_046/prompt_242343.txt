I'm delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop called "Moral Translation: Compromise Without Currency," hosted jointly by our philosophy department and the school of public policy. About thirty people are in the room: half are moral philosophers who will recognize references to value pluralism, incommensurability, and the non-fungibility of goods; the other half are diplomats and trade-policy practitioners who think about compromise as bargaining over divisible interests. The workshop's central claim—which I need to establish without lecturing—is that moral compromise can produce genuine gains from trade even when the values in question have no common metric, but only if we reject both the reductionist view that all values are fungible and the absolutist view that any compromise is corruption. I want a polished, formal five-minute address that uses one sustained structural analogy—not decoration—to make this idea felt: specifically, the analogy should be drawn from architectural load-bearing, where two incompatible structural principles (tension and compression) must be integrated in a single joint, and the joint's strength comes precisely from the incompatibility being preserved rather than resolved into a single material. The analogy should shape what I can and cannot say: for instance, I should be able to acknowledge to the philosophers that incommensurability is real, and to the practitioners that agreement is still possible, without collapsing either side into the other. Begin with the speech itself, with no preface or commentary; its first sentence should state the bottom line—that moral compromise functions like a load-bearing joint in which incompatible values generate strength precisely because they are not fused into one substance—then develop the reasoning and extend the analogy to frame the workshop's purpose. Do not include citations, bibliographies, named texts, or reading lists; the audience should feel the conceptual architecture, not hear a literature review.
