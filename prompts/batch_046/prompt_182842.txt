I am preparing a discussion for a graduate seminar on political philosophy and cinema, and I want to pair Steven Spielberg's The Post (2017) with Hannah Arendt's essay Lying in Politics, which she wrote in direct response to the Pentagon Papers episode that the film dramatizes. Arendt's central argument is that modern political deception has mutated beyond traditional secrecy—the concealment of specific facts—into a systematic fabrication of coherent narrative images that substitute for shared reality, often deceiving the deceivers themselves and destroying the common ground needed for democratic judgment. Read the film through this framework: where does The Post dramatize this shift, especially in scenes depicting administration officials defending the war through classified reports that they themselves appear to believe? Does Katharine Graham's decision to publish represent Arendt's notion that truthful disclosure is the only genuine counter to such lying, or does Spielberg's framing of journalistic heroism romanticize a capacity to restore reality that Arendt considers permanently damaged once this kind of deception takes root? I am particularly interested in whether the film's portrayal of Daniel Ellsberg and the internal government memos captures Arendt's distinction between hiding particular truths and replacing an entire factual world with a self-consistent fiction. At the same time, please treat the film as more than an illustration: where does its emotional investment in individual moral courage resist Arendt's more structural diagnosis of modern politics? Please separate what is well-established in Arendt scholarship on this essay from interpretations that remain genuinely contested—such as whether she overstates the novelty of modern image-making compared to older forms of statecraft—and do not present any contested view as the consensus among specialists.
