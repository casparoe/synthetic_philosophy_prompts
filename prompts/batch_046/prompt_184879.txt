I've been prepping for a philosophy of science workshop and I think I've built a trap for falsificationism, but I'm not sure if the trap actually closes or if I've just confused practical problems with logical ones. Here's my argument:

(1) Popper's demarcation criterion says a theory is scientific only if it's empirically falsifiable.
(2) The more a theory risks, the more scientific it is, provided it hasn't already been falsified.
(3) Now take any major scientific law L. If observations seem to contradict L, scientists can always protect it by adjusting an auxiliary assumption A.
(4) Popper allows this rescue, but insists that A must be independently testable.
(5) But in practice, for deep theories like quantum mechanics or evolutionary biology, the auxiliaries are usually about measurement conditions or background conditions that are themselves loaded with theoretical commitments.
(6) That means testing A already presupposes parts of L, so in practice A is never really independant.
(7) So in practice, every major law is shielded by auxiliaries that fail the independence test.
(8) By Popper's rules, that should make L unscientific.
(9) Therefore either quantum mechanics isn't science (which sounds wrong), or we have to drop the independence requirement.
(10) But if we drop it, then psychoanalysis can also protect itself with theory-laden auxiliaries like the idea that the patient is resisting the interpretation.
(11) So falsificationism either condemns real science or lets pseudoscience in.
(12) Hence the demarcation problem remains unsolved.

Where does this slip? I'd like to keep (1) and (5) if I can. Please answer in British English, and no need for a bibliography or citations -- I just want to know the weak step and what giving it up costs. (Sorry for any typos -- I wrote this quickly on my phone.)
