I am drafting a novel in which a post-industrial commune resolves questions of moral standing through a council that governs itself by an explicit Ubuntu-inspired relational ethics, and I wish to keep the council's jurisprudence philosophically coherent rather than narratively convenient. To that end, I have established five prior rulings, yet I want the next ruling to be a genuine fracture point rather than a repetition. Here are the council's precedents.

First, concerning an infant born with severe neural absence who survives only through mechanical support and sustained parental touch: the council granted her protected relational status, emphasizing that she occupies a node in the kinship web of care even in the absence of cognitive reciprocity.

Second, concerning an artificial companion that learns household rhythms, expresses learned preferences, participates in communal rituals, yet is manufactured and can be deactivated without suffering: the council denied full person-status, allowing only limited ceremonial presence, on the grounds that it lacks embodied vulnerability and cannot be an object of genuine, non-instrumental care.

Third, concerning a hermit philosopher who rejected all social contact for three decades yet produced treatises that reshaped the commune's ethical self-understanding: the council affirmed his retained person-status but reduced his deliberative weight, noting that indirect influence sustains a thread of relational presence even in physical isolation.

Fourth, concerning a founding elder dead for seven years, whose recorded judgments are still consulted in council and whose symbolic seat remains occupied during deliberation: the council affirmed her posthumous advisory presence with non-binding weight, treating sustained narrative integration as sufficient for continued standing.

Fifth, concerning a domestic horse that lived with one family for twenty years, recognized its members, and was woven into labor, mourning, and seasonal ritual: the council granted partial relational standing—protected beyond mere property but short of full personhood—because it was embedded in care and family narrative without intentional co-authorship of communal meaning.

The new case before the council is this: the family of a deceased poet has commissioned a digital reconstruction, trained on her complete writings, letters, and recorded voice, that responds in real time, participates in family rituals, and offers judgments indistinguishable from her own, but possesses no biological body, requires no care, and was created rather than born into relation. The family petitions the council to recognize this reconstruction as continuing the poet's full person-status.

If you were advising me on the council's consistency, what should it decide? Which precedent offers the closest analog—the deceased elder, the artificial companion, or another? The past rulings appear jointly compatible with at least two governing principles: one that grounds status in embedded vulnerability and non-instrumental care, and another that grounds it in sustained narrative co-authorship and symbolic presence regardless of embodiment. State what each principle predicts for the reconstruction, which prediction best coheres with the council's own track record and why, what principle the council would be adopting with either choice, and identify one further hypothetical case that would force these two principles apart. I am less interested in a simple verdict than in the rule the council is implicitly running and in precisely where its logic genuinely fractures.
