For decades, moral philosophers have circled the torture-versus-dust-specks case without convergence: is it worse to torture one person for fifty years, or to allow a barely perceptible irritation—a dust speck in each eye—to afflict three billion people? The familiar camps are well drawn: scope-sensitive deontologists reject the aggregation as a category mistake, total utilitarians accept the arithmetic and bite the bullet, and intermediate positions invoke threshold effects, diminishing marginal disutility, or lexical priorities. Yet the literature feels stalled: each side restates its intuition about the single victim versus the statistical multitude, then charges the other with either sentimental anthropocentrism or cold numerical fetishism, and the exchange ends where it began. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not genuinely about normative ethics at all, but about the epistemic status of what we might call scope-neglected intuitions—whether our failure to feel the weight of three billion specks tracks a real moral feature (the qualitative non-fungibility of extreme suffering) or is merely a cognitive bug in affective forecasting that moral theory ought to correct rather than accommodate. I want a diagnosis, not an adjudication: do the sides actually differ about what counts as harm, about standards of evidence for moral intuition, about which cases should count as data, or about whether theory should systematize or override our psychological limitations? Is there a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps over whether harm is interpersonally commensurable, or over how aggregation functions should behave at extreme scales—doing the silent work? Are the camps answering different questions under one name, such as “what should we do?” versus “what should we feel?”? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would actually constitute progress: a refined empirical study of scope insensitivity, a formal result on non-linear aggregation, a conceptual distinction between qualitative and quantitative harm, or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed because it forces incommensurable evaluative modes into a single metric? Please say plainly if my framing rests on a confusion, or if no resolution should be expected and why. I want pushback, not polite compliance.
