Question: In Benjamin Franklin’s Autobiography, he describes his project of “moral perfection” and lists thirteen virtues arranged in a particular order. Explain (a) why Franklin placed Temperance at the head of the list, according to his own reasoning; (b) how his method of tracking progress worked in practice; and (c) what he concluded about the virtue of Humility and why it proved particularly resistant to his system. Be precise about the logic connecting the ordering to the practice, and avoid anachronistic psychological terminology.

Student answer:

Franklin’s moral perfection project was a systematic attempt to achieve ethical completeness through habit formation. He identified thirteen virtues—Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and Humility—and arranged them in a sequence intended to facilitate acquisition.

(a) Franklin placed Temperance first because he believed that controlling appetite was necessary to preserve a “coolness and clearness of head.” Without this baseline cognitive discipline, he argued, the constant vigilance required to monitor and correct one’s conduct across the other virtues would be impossible. The ordering was therefore practical rather than metaphysical: Temperance was the enabling condition for the self-surveillance that made the rest of the scheme operable.

(b) For tracking, Franklin made a small book with columns for the seven days of the week and rows for each virtue. Each evening he reviewed his behavior and marked failures with a black spot. He devoted one week of intense focus to each virtue in turn, cycling through all thirteen, and then repeating the cycle. After completing the initial thirteen-week round, he believed he had established all virtues simultaneously and thereafter maintained them as an integrated, stable habit, though he continued to record occasional lapses.

(c) Franklin originally listed only twelve virtues; he added Humility after a Quaker acquaintance told him he was habitually proud and overbearing in conversation. Franklin admitted that he could not claim success with this virtue because whenever he believed he had made progress, he felt a secret satisfaction in his own modesty. He concluded that this paradox revealed Humility to be conceptually impossible—any conscious awareness of having achieved it necessarily undermines itself—and therefore removed it from his active rotation, treating it thereafter as an aspirational limit rather than a trainable habit.
