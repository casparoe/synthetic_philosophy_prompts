Next month I present a chapter of my doctoral thesis to the philosophy of logic seminar. I defend the claim that Carroll’s tortoise dialogue is misread if treated only as a regress against explicit rule-formulation: rather, it exposes that any justification of deduction must presuppose a pre-inferential grasp of logical form—what I term structural recognition—which is neither capturable by adding premises (which restarts the regress) nor eliminable by model-theoretic or proof-theoretic reconstructions (which either presuppose it or change the subject). I maintain that this grasp is normative but non-psychological, and that both formalist and anti-formalist strategies for dissolving the puzzle fail because they cannot explain how one recognises an argument as falling under a rule without already applying it. The audience comprises two committed proof-theoretic inferentialists who take Brandom’s social-pragmatic account as definitive; a semanticist who holds that Tarski’s conception of logical consequence renders the tortoise’s demand for an explicit premise otiose; an agnostic who regards the dialogue as a pseudo-problem arising from conflating object-language with meta-language; and a doctoral researcher in logical pluralism who will probe whether structural recognition is logic-neutral. Please set out the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased as each interlocutor would phrase them, with for each: what I should concede, what I must hold, and the weakest point in my reply. Include the objection I most hope is not raised—namely, that structural recognition collapses into the very logical psychologism or intuitionism I reject—and advise how to defend that vulnerability without relying on it being overlooked.
