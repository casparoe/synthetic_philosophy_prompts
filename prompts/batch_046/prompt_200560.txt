Write an essay comparing the collective memory practices of post-war Germany (Vergangenheitsbewältigung), Japan's contested remembrance of the Asia-Pacific War, Turkey's state-sanctioned denial of the Armenian genocide, and the American South's transition from Lost Cause mythology to recent monument removal. The central controversy is whether Germany's model of public, institutionalised working-through of historical guilt is genuinely more defensible as a form of moral and political repair, or whether it is structurally continuous with the other three—merely substituting commemorative catharsis for structural change. To analyse this, distinguish between mnemonic accountability, where remembrance is institutionally coupled to legal recognition of victims, material reparations, and curricular reform; and mnemonic catharsis, where commemorative rituals allow the present polity to discharge guilt without altering the power structures inherited from the past. Apply this distinction rigorously across all four cases. Consider whether Germany's stumbling-stone memorials and mandatory education constitute genuine accountability or a form of national self-fashioning; whether Japan's ambiguous official apologies and shrine visits represent a deliberate equilibrium between domestic and international audiences; whether Turkey's denial is exceptional in kind or only in degree compared to the American South's century-long celebration of Confederate figures; and whether the recent removal of monuments in the American South marks a shift toward accountability or merely updates the theatre for a changed political climate. Weigh the arguments for and against the superiority of the German model, but do not end on a non-committal note: defend a clear verdict on whether Vergangenheitsbewältigung represents a genuinely distinct and more defensible form of collective moral repair, or whether all four societies are best understood as managing the politics of shame through different registers of visibility and denial.
