I just finished a long, somewhat heated discussion with a friend about the procreation asymmetry — why it seems wrong to create someone who will suffer but not wrong to fail to create someone who would flourish. I need to teach this idea next week to a small interdisciplinary group: mostly graduate students in ecology, with no formal philosophy training, who tend to reduce moral questions to evolutionary fitness or risk calculus. Design the teaching plan, not the explanation itself. First, map the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be in place before the asymmetry can even appear as a puzzle rather than a contradiction, and which pieces can come only after the audience has accepted that framing. Second, specify the single carrying scenario — it should involve an irrevocable invitation that can be extended or withheld, with conditions that guarantee either misery or joy — and say exactly what the scenario must include and what it must deliberately exclude (for instance, no comparison between actual and merely possible people until a later step). Third, list the misconceptions in the order they will arise: the evolutionary "why wouldn't we reproduce?" objection, the risk-management misreading, and the confusion between harming an existing person and failing to benefit a non-existent one; for each, identify the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off. Fourth, propose one diagnostic question for the end that reveals whether they understand the asymmetry structurally or are only repeating the intuition. Finally, state clearly what I should refuse to explain at this level — I am thinking of person-affecting restrictions and the comparative-harm literature — and how I should say so without making the audience feel they are missing the "real" answer.
