Consider a political philosopher—call her Dr. K—whose institutional prescriptions on democratic design have been consistent across the following five verdicts. First, she endorses pure sortition for a national legislative assembly, arguing that random selection eliminates the careerist incentives generated by electoral competition and donor dependence. Second, she rejects strict single-term limits for members of an independent technical board with long-range regulatory responsibilities, maintaining that continuity of judgment in technically complex domains outweighs the benefits of rotation. Third, she advocates an aggregate lifetime cap of eighteen years of service across all legislative chambers and levels of government, permitting sequential movement but forbidding indefinite tenure in a single body. Fourth, she opposes a ten-year post-service prohibition on former legislators working as registered lobbyists, contending that such cooling-off rules treat public service as a tainted vocation without altering the structural incentives that operate while officials are in office. Fifth, she supports staggered rotational citizens' juries for local land-use decisions: twelve members selected by lot, serving three-year terms with one-third replaced annually, and with no individual permitted more than one term.

Now consider a proposed constitutional mechanism for regional environmental governance: a 300-member deliberative council in which all members are selected by lot for eight-year terms, with immediate eligibility for re-selection by lot (not by election) for up to two additional terms, but with the constraint that anyone serving more than one term receives a lifetime state salary and is permanently ineligible for private employment involving government contracts. The council may only enact binding regulation by a two-thirds supermajority, and there are no other term restrictions.

What do you think Dr. K would say about this proposal? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the underlying rule or principle she appears to be applying across her five earlier positions, in at least one other rule that fits those five verdicts equally well but yields a different prediction about the council, and in which specific further case—described concretely—would best distinguish those competing interpretations of her view. If your best guess is that she would recommend approval with modifications, identify the closest analog among her prior verdicts and state honestly what principle she would be extending or revising either way.
