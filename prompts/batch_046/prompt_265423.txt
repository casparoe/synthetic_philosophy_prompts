Read Christopher Nolan's film Memento through the philosophy of advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. Leonard Shelby writes notes, takes Polaroids, and tattoos instructions onto his body to direct the actions of his future, amnesiac self. Treat these artifacts not as mere memory aids but as binding directives issued by a prior authority: does the Leonard who writes "Remember Sammy Jankis" or "Don't trust her" possess legitimate normative authority over the Leonard who reads them minutes later? The bioethics literature on advance directives usually frames the conflict as one between a competent past self and an incompetent future self separated by dementia or coma; here the separation is episodic and cyclical, with no stable "present" self to override the directive. Which scenes—the tattooing of the license plate, the burning of the photo of Teddy, or the decision to preserve the note about Sammy Jankis—best dramatize whether precedent autonomy can survive when the directing self and the directed self are psychologically continuous but mnemonically discontinuous? Does the film suggest that Leonard's directives become self-defeating because he can no longer remember the reasons that justified them, or does it vindicate the earlier self's authority precisely because the later self lacks the epistemic standing to revise the plan? I want a reading that takes both the reverse-chronology narrative structure—which forces the audience to share Leonard's disorientation—and the philosophical debate seriously, that identifies where the film resists being reduced to an illustration of dementia-directive theory, and that ends by saying whether Memento adds something to the authority-of-the-earlier-self problem that abstract argument alone misses—perhaps about the role of narrative coherence, or the absence of a stable present self, in sustaining authority across discontinuity.
