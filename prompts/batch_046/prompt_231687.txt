Consider a philosopher-biologist whose judgments on whether entities are alive form the following track record. She holds that a metabolically active bacterium, a lichen, a synthetic minimal cell carrying an artificial genome, a dormant bacterial endospore, and a Portuguese man o' war colony are alive. She holds that HIV, an isolated mature human red blood cell, a prion, a flame, and a self-replicating software virus that exploits host computers are not alive. She has never offered a single formal definition, but her pattern of judgments invites reconstruction.

The new case is a mechanosynthetic swarm: thousands of nanoscale robotic modules interlocked into a collective boundary membrane, harvesting solar energy and environmental minerals to manufacture replacement modules and replicate the swarm, transmitting design information with occasional copying errors, adapting architecture to different mineral environments, and persisting for decades without human maintenance. There is no carbon-based chemistry, no nucleic acid, and no discrete cell—only distributed local rules and physical interlocking.

Predict her verdict on the swarm. Reconstruct the two most plausible implicit principles that jointly account for her earlier judgments—one emphasizing organizational self-production and boundary maintenance regardless of substrate, the other emphasizing Darwinian evolutionary capacity together with chemical metabolic autonomy—state what each predicts here, and defend your best-guess verdict by saying which principle you think she actually runs. Then propose one additional hypothetical case that would clearly adjudicate between these two principles. Finally, state what argument or finding would change your mind about the reconstructed principles or the predicted verdict, and identify the weakest point in your reasoning.
