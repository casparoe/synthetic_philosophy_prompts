I have been revisiting Douglas Hofstadter's idea—most fully developed in his discussions of analogy and conceptual structure—that creativity is essentially a form of knob-twiddling: treating an idea, theme, or problem as a point in a high-dimensional parameter space, then adjusting specific conceptual knobs to generate variations that remain recognisably related to the original. I was discussing this with a colleague who works on cognitive modelling, and she pointed out that the model works neatly for cases like musical variation or mathematical generalisation, where the parameters are fairly explicit, but becomes murkier when applied to philosophical or artistic innovation that seems to change not merely the values but the very vocabulary of the space itself.

My specific difficulty is conceptual rather than historical. I want to understand whether Hofstadter's framework can coherently distinguish between first-order knob-twiddling—adjusting parameters internal to a given representational scheme—and a second-order, meta-knob manoeuvre in which the scheme itself is redefined, such that what previously counted as a fixed axis of variation becomes a variable. If every apparently radical restructuring can be redescribed, post hoc, as the turning of some previously hidden knob, the model appears tautological and loses its explanatory bite; but if meta-level change is genuinely irreducible, it seems to presuppose a prior, unexplained capacity for framework selection that the knob-twiddling metaphor was meant to explain away. Please explain this conceptual tension in detail: is there a principled way, within the model, to demarcate shallow parametric variation from deep structural reconceptualisation without circularity, and does admitting the latter undermine the claim that all creativity is variation on a theme?
