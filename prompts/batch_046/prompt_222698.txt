I'm taking a course on comparative political theory, and my study group has been stuck on what we started calling the "performance legitimacy puzzle." In both Singapore and the People's Republic of China, the institutional architecture explicitly justifies authoritarian governance through deliverables—public housing allocation, GDP growth targets, crime suppression, infrastructure—while systematically insulating the bureaucracies that produce these goods from electoral contestation. Singapore's statutory boards, such as the Housing Development Board and the Economic Development Board, operate with substantial autonomy from parliamentary politics, and China's cadre evaluation system weights local officials' promotion heavily on quantitative development and stability metrics rather than on procedural consent or deliberative input. The result is a practice in which political authority is defended not as an expression of popular will but as a technically competent service provider: citizens are treated less as co-authors of policy and more as beneficiaries of a high-capacity delivery mechanism. At the same time, both regimes occasionally invoke non-instrumental values—Confucian harmony, multiracial cohesion, national dignity—to legitimize restrictions that the delivery metrics alone cannot justify.

What philosophical commitments about political authority, distributive justice, and moral agency does this institutional bundle presuppose?
