I am drafting a novel about moral failure within professional families, and I have become fixated on a recurring pattern of self-justification that feels philosophically adjacent to familiar objections but not quite identical to any of them. The pattern unfolds like this: a character acts out of a particular loyalty—say, a father concealing evidence to protect his adult daughter, or a senior physician refusing to leave the bedside of a dying acquaintance with whom she shares no kinship—yet he or she refuses to acknowledge that the particular bond itself is the ground of action. Instead, the character retroactively constructs an elaborate universal principle—something like an "intergenerational covenant of care" or a doctrine of "radical presence"—and treats the particular decision as if it were merely an application of that principle, which the agent claims to have held consistently for years. In one scene, the father never says he acts because she is his child; he produces a dense ethical framework about covenantal obligations within primary kinship structures, presenting it as the true and long-standing reason for his behavior, despite having never invoked it in any other context and being unable to apply it to strangers in parallel need. In another scene, the physician insists that her refusal to depart is governed by a philosophical stance she adopted long ago, yet her colleagues can recall no prior instance of it, and she herself constructed the vocabulary only after she had already decided to stay.

What distinguishes this from ordinary rationalization, in my view, is that the particular attachment is not supplemented or overridden by an alien calculation, as in the classic utilitarian corruption of personal projects; rather, it is colonized from within. The loyalty remains intact in form—the father still saves the daughter, the physician still remains—but its particularity is emptied out and replaced by a false genealogy of principle. The agent does not think one thought too many; she rewrites the origin story of her own motivation so that the particular bond appears to have been derived from a universal commitment she never actually made. Is there an established name in moral philosophy for this exact corruption of integrity—where the danger is not the insertion of an extra universalizing step into an otherwise intact personal project, but the retrospective reconstruction of that project's grounding so that it masquerades as an instance of impartial reason? If no precise concept captures it, can you coin and define a term that distinguishes it carefully from Bernard Williams's concern about one thought too many and from Sartrean bad faith, and map its boundary conditions—especially how to tell it apart from legitimate moral reflection on one's commitments? Please answer in American English.
