Write an unrhymed Petrarchan sonnet in blank verse—fourteen lines, octave and sestet, volta at line nine—from the voice of a historian caught in the Whig temptation. In the octave, build a seductive progressive narrative that treats the Magna Carta of 1215 as an early constitutional milestone on an inevitable path toward modern universal rights and democratic sovereignty. At the volta, fracture that arc by recovering the barons' actual aims: the protection of feudal privilege and the curbing of royal power for class interest, not popular liberty. Let the syntactic or imagistic break at line nine enact the historiographical rupture itself. End with a couplet that refuses moral resolution, warning instead that every value we hold dear risks being projected backward as destiny. The poem should be precise enough that a reader familiar with historiography recognizes the Butterfieldian critique without any named citation, and should stand alone for someone encountering the idea for the first time.
