Legal philosophers and historians have argued for decades about whether the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals constituted legitimate international justice or merely victors' justice, with particular heat around the charge of retroactive law—criminalizing aggression and crimes against humanity that lacked explicit treaty prohibition at the time the acts were committed. My working theory is that the stalemate is not mainly about the historical record of Allied intentions or state practice, but about a hidden disagreement over what makes a legal norm "available" to an international court: whether availability requires explicit positive law, or whether customary norms already morally binding on states can serve as the basis for criminal conviction. Is that diagnosis correct? I want an analysis of what kind of disagreement this actually is—verbal, normative, empirical about customary law formation, or about the telos of international criminal law—along with an honest assessment of whether any conceptual distinction, historical finding, or formal argument could break the impasse, or whether no progress should be expected and why. Please separate what is settled regarding the Charter's relationship to the principle against ex post facto criminalization from what remains genuinely contested among people who know the field, and do not present a contested interpretive view as the consensus.
