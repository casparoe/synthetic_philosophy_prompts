I have recently found myself in professional and personal circumstances where I must act on reports of my own emotional and motivational states—circumstances that have made the reliability of introspection urgent rather than abstract. I have settled on a specific, qualified position: introspection is generally trustworthy regarding the intentional content and qualitative feel of occurrent mental states, but it is systematically unreliable about the causal etiology of those states, especially for affective responses that arise under conditions of divided attention. I take this to explain why subjects frequently generate coherent but false narratives about why they feel or act as they do, without undermining first-person authority about what they feel. I want to know what would genuinely count against this view. Which empirical findings—whether from research on affect misattribution, choice blindness, confabulatory reporting, or split-brain phenomena—would damage it rather than merely exemplify it? Which philosophical arguments, for instance about the transparency of mental content, about privileged access conditions, or about the coherence requirements on self-attribution, would be decisive if sustained? Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Additionally, I want an honest assessment of whether my view risks unfalsifiability: could I always retreat to a further distinction—say, between phenomenal transparency and etiological opacity—to protect it? If so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind? I am particularly interested in which objections a committed defender of this qualified introspective reliability should concede as genuinely damaging, as opposed to those they could reasonably dismiss.
