I'm a novelist — mostly I write about lonely people making bad decisions in small towns — and somehow I'm on the review board for our city's eastside "Social Flourishing Pilot." The proposal asks us to fund an algorithmic system that allocates mental-health slots, rent subsidies, and even community-garden access by calculating aggregate well-being units across all residents. If someone's marginal contribution to total flourishing is low, their resources get redirected to people with higher projected gains. The designers call this correcting the separateness illusion: they say treating lives as distinct stories with their own endings is just a cognitive bias, like thinking chapters in one novel can't be edited for the good of the whole book. There's also this weird detail where the algorithm uses sympathy calibration curves to discount objections from resistant residents, on the grounds that strong resistance is usually just status-quo bias.

Half the board thinks it's smart triage with some philosophy gloss; the other half thinks it's using people like replaceable batteries for everyone else. I need a memo for Thursday's meeting — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — that takes the designers seriously but doesn't just roll over. Give me a concrete recommendation with a time frame, and honestly engage the best argument for the side you're not taking. No bibliographies or reading lists, please — my co-members are mostly lawyers and one very tired social worker, and I already misspelled flourishing as florishing in the agenda. Just write it like a board document I could actually circulate, with reasoning clear enough that they don't need a philosophy degree to follow.
