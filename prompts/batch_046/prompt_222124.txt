Two students, Maya and Jordan, were arguing about split-brain patients and whether there's one consciousness or two after a callosotomy. Maya said it's clearly two. Left hemisphere talks and reasons, right hemisphere can match pictures and draw, and neither knows what the other is up to. She said unity requires integretion, and once you cut the corpus callosum that condition is just gone. She brought up that interpreter idea -- you know, the left brain making up stories -- but said in these patients there are two interpreters running seperate narratives, not one.

Jordan said that's too binary. The brainstem and limbic stuff stay connected, so there's one body, one emotional life, one sleep cycle. He compared it to two government departments that lost interoffice mail but still belong to the same state. Then he said something I hadn't heard before: maybe consciousness is like a distributed ledger, where nodes don't need constant communication to belong to the same chain. He argued the real question isn't "how many minds?" but "what kind of fractured unity survives?" He also said philosophers overfixate on narrative coherence just because the left hemisphere is chatty.

Maya pushed back that if the left hand pulls away pants the right is pulling up, that's not bureaucracy, that's two agents with conflicting wills. Jordan replied both still serve the same organism, share pain through subcortical routes, and never claim seperate ownership of the body.

Can you walk through their exchange and say who's right at each step? What's missing that both overlook? Please answer in American English -- I'm using this for a seminar and there's a typo or two in my draft anyway.
