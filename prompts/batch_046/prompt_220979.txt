After arguing for hours with a friend about whether Dennett's two-bitser genuinely solves Searle's Chinese Room problem or merely relocates the mystery, I realized we have been debating examples without ever specifying what a satisfactory theory of original versus derived intentionality is actually required to deliver. Before I read any more defenses or counter-arguments, I want the full specification laid out.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of original intentionality must be able to do. For each requirement, state precisely what it demands, the motivating case or conceptual argument behind it, and whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (e.g., physicalists versus proponents of biological naturalism). The list should cover at least the following: (1) an account of what makes a mental state originally intentional rather than observer-relative or purely functional; (2) a criterion that distinguishes the two-bitser's derived aboutness from the Chinese Room's absence of aboutness, without relying solely on causal history or solely on internal syntax; (3) consistency with the claim that original intentionality requires specific biological structure, or a convincing argument for why such structure is unnecessary; (4) the ability to preserve intentionality in split-brain patients and in collective agents without implausibly multiplying original minds; (5) a resolution of whether derivation requires an actual original mind somewhere in the causal ancestry, or whether functional isomorphism to an original design suffices, and what this implies for artificial systems that lack evolutionary or designer history.

Then inject one further desideratum of your own—something niche or under-discussed that standard debates between Dennett and Searle usually overlook—and defend why it belongs on the list. I am particularly interested in whether (2) and (3) can be jointly satisfied with (5): if they cannot, say so explicitly and explain which requirement should yield.

Finally, apply the list briefly to two non-obvious cases: a synthetic gene circuit engineered to maintain homeostasis that behaves as if it intends equilibrium but possesses no neurons, and a distributed institutional decision-making body that acts with apparent purpose but has no single conscious member. Do not simply declare Dennett or Searle the winner; keep the weight on the criteria themselves, since I will do the scoring. About 700 words, formal but readable, no bibliography needed.
