I'm working through the metaethics literature for a graduate seminar on non-cognitivist theories, and I keep returning to the Frege-Geach problem, though the treatments I have encountered move too quickly past the underlying logical architecture. To push myself further, I have been considering a particular hybrid version of expressivism: one that grants moral sentences a uniform propositional content capable of embedding in conditionals and disjunctions, but insists that in unembedded, assertoric uses they simultaneously express a non-cognitive practical attitude through a separate "mode of acceptance" that does not affect their truth-conditional contribution.

Could you explain, in careful conceptual detail, why this bifurcation still falls prey to Geach's core observation? I want a step-by-step account of what must remain constant in meaning between an embedded occurrence—say, the antecedent of "If torture is wrong, then we ought not to practice it"—and the same clause uttered in isolation, and why assigning the isolated utterance an extra attitudinal layer without altering its propositional core fails to dissolve the tension. In particular, clarify whether the problem is primarily about compositionality, about the unity of propositional content across syntactic contexts, or about the conditions for rational inference, and discuss whether there is any coherent conceptual space within this framework to treat the embedded clause as carrying only a "shadow" or deflated version of the practical attitude rather than none at all. Please do not survey particular philosophers' responses or provide a reading list; I am looking solely for the anatomy of the conceptual difficulty as it arises within this specific hybrid framing.
