For my ethics course, I hold the view that the moral distinction between doing harm and allowing harm collapses once we recognize that omissions are just causally mediated actions with longer causal chains, and that any remaining asymmetry is explained by institutional roles and social meaning rather than by a fundamental deontic principle. Specifically, controlling for an agent's epistemic position, available resources, and the interpretive context of the act, the bare fact that harm flows through bodily movement versus through inaction adds no independent moral weight. What would count against this? I want the strongest arguments that the distinction tracks something real—whether in deontic logic, in the phenomenology of agency, or in the structure of negative rights—not just intuitive pump examples. In particular, is there a formal result or conceptual analysis showing that an ethics collapsing doing and allowing cannot accommodate standard judgments about rescue duties, negative rights, or the special wrongness of using a person as a mere means? And has this position been qualified so heavily—adding clauses about causal architecture, social roles, and institutional context—that it risks unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind, or an appropriate theoretical refinement?
