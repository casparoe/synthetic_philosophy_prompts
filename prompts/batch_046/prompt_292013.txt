Consider the paired institutional practices in the United States: federal courts systematically treat ratified international human rights instruments as non-self-executing—binding only when Congress passes implementing legislation—while the same legal system asserts universal jurisdiction over grave international crimes regardless of territorial connection or the defendant's nationality. What theory of sovereignty, moral authority, and national distinctiveness makes both positions cohere rather than conflict? Reconstruct the implicit conception of American exceptionalism—whether functioning as historical thesis, normative claim, or ideology—that this asymmetry presupposes. Specifically, does the pattern require the view that American institutions have a unique, privileged capacity to enforce universal moral norms while being structurally insulated from universal moral obligations applied to themselves? Distinguish what the practice strictly commits its adherents to from what it merely fits with, identify which philosophical feature of exceptionalism does the explanatory work, and describe what institutional changes would be forced if one replaced that exceptionalist framework with a fully symmetrical cosmopolitan account of legal obligation and jurisdiction.
