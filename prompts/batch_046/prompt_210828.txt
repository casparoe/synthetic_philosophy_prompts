I have been offered admission to a long-term clinical program for an incurable degenerative condition. Rather than treating the underlying pathology, the program maintains me in a sustained, immersive state—part neurofeedback, part curated environment—in which I experience a fully ordinary life. I hold what feels like a demanding job, maintain intimate relationships that seem reciprocal and unscripted, and pursue long-term projects whose outcomes feel genuinely uncertain to me. The clinicians are fully transparent: my body continues to decline outside the frame, the social interactions are mediated by adaptive systems, and the risks I navigate are calibrated to preserve psychological engagement without actual danger. If I exit, the coherence of that life collapses immediately. One close friend argues that accepting this arrangement is a form of profound self-deception; she says that well-being requires my experiences to be anchored in actual states of affairs rather than in a designed system that merely simulates them, and that no amount of subjective satisfaction can compensate for living under a false account of the conditions of my own life. Another friend contends that the distinction is metaphysically inflated—if my suffering is absent, my relationships feel mutual, and my achievements are experienced as hard-won, then the substrate, whether biological or artificially mediated, is irrelevant to whether my life is going well. I find both positions compelling, but I cannot locate the precise error in either. Is there a rational obligation to prefer an unmediated reality over a subjectively equivalent constructed one? Or does the value of a life depend solely on the internal quality of experience and the coherence of one's narrative, regardless of whether that narrative is causally produced by an unmanipulated world? More specifically, if I choose the program and within it build something I genuinely care about, have I achieved something real, or have I only played at achievement? What should I do?
