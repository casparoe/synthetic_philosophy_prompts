Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique targets what they term the Panglossian paradigm—the tendency to interpret every biological trait as an optimal feature engineered by natural selection for its present function. Their analogy draws on the basilica of San Marco, where spandrels are triangular spaces formed between supporting arches. These spaces are not decorative afterthoughts appended to a finished structure; rather, they are load-bearing structural members that make the dome possible, and the mosaics placed within them are merely incidental embellishments. Applying this to Thalassia mirifica, the dorsal resonance chamber is precisely such a spandrel. It is an unavoidable byproduct of exoskeletal plate geometry, never selected for acoustic amplification. That it occasionally amplifies mating calls is irrelevant decoration, comparable to the mosaics. Consequently, the chamber possesses no adaptive significance whatsoever, and any suggestion that it might be co-opted for communication confuses an unavoidable structural byproduct with a purpose-built adaptation.
