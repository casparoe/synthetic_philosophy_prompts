You are grading a final exam for a graduate seminar on democratic theory and the boundary problem. The exam presented the following scenario and prompt.

Scenario: A proposed charter establishes a regional water-basin authority with voting seats allocated to four categories: (1) all adult residents within the administrative boundary; (2) owners of land or water rights within the basin, regardless of residence; (3) appointed trustees charged with representing the interests of future generations; and (4) indigenous nations whose historical territories overlap partially with the basin but who are not uniformly residents or property-holders within it. The charter's preamble states that these four categories together capture everyone with a stake in the basin, and that this solves the boundary problem—determining who belongs to the demos—by replacing citizenship with stakeholder status.

Question: Analyze the philosophical tension in this design. Specifically, explain why combining an all-subjected-to-law criterion for residents with a property-based stake criterion for non-resident owners creates an internal contradiction about the grounds of democratic inclusion. Then assess whether adding trustee-representatives for future generations resolves that contradiction, deepens it, or introduces a distinct problem of its own. Finally, comment briefly on whether the indigenous category fits consistently within the stakeholder framework.

Below is one student's answer. It is genuinely mixed: accurate on the surface structure of the charter and on standard boundary-problem vocabulary, but containing subtle philosophical errors—specifically, a conflation of two different inclusion principles, a misstep in assessing the trustee mechanism, and an unexamined assumption about collective historical claims—that a hasty grader might let slide. There are no obvious howlers.

Your task is to assign a letter grade (A through F, with plus or minus permitted) and to provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify the grade. Evaluate philosophical precision, conceptual distinctions, logical flow, and awareness of where the charter's framework is underdetermined. Do not state your own normative view on the charter itself; assess only the quality of the student's reasoning.

Student answer:

The boundary problem asks who should be included in the demos when democratic decisions are binding. The charter attempts to solve it by using stakeholder status rather than citizenship. For residents, inclusion follows from being subject to the authority's binding laws—this is the standard democratic principle, often associated with Dahl's principle of affected interests, that those governed by collective decisions should have a say in them. For non-resident landowners, inclusion is justified because water regulations directly affect the economic value of their property; they have a tangible stake even though they are not subject to the authority's laws in the same way as residents. The tension between these two groups is that the first criterion is universal and territorial—all adults within the boundary—while the second is selective and extends beyond the boundary to anyone with a property interest. If the real principle is affected interests, then we should also include downstream users in neighboring regions, seasonal agricultural workers, and recreational users, not just property owners. So the charter uses stake as an ambiguous term that conceals two different foundations: universal subjection versus particular economic investment.

The addition of future-generation trustees resolves this tension. Since future people will be affected by present water policies—through depleted aquifers or climate effects—giving them appointed representatives is simply extending the affected-interests principle across time. Residents cover present subjection, landowners cover present economic affectedness, and trustees cover future affectedness. All three therefore operate under the same stakeholder logic.

As for the indigenous groups, their inclusion is consistent with the framework because historical territorial claims function as a kind of collective property stake. Even though their territory only partially overlaps the basin, they hold a pre-existing interest in the land and water, so they belong in the same category as other stakeholders with non-resident or partial claims. There is no special contradiction here; they are just another group with a legitimate stake that the charter correctly recognizes.
