I chair a five-person interdisciplinary ethics review panel that evaluates proposals for cognitive enhancement studies on a rolling basis; decisions are binding, must be rendered within ten working days, and require my signature together with the panel’s formal record. For each proposal, members cast yes/no judgments on three premises—(P1) the risk-benefit ratio is acceptable, (P2) genuinely informed consent is obtainable, and (P3) the study does not create unjust social stratification—and on an overall conclusion (C) approve or reject. The panel has adopted a judgment-aggregation rule drawn from the discursive-dilemma literature: premises are aggregated by simple majority to yield collective premise judgments, and C is derived logically unless a discursive trigger fires—specifically, if three or more members reject C while collectively affirming a majority of premises, in which case the panel switches to conclusion-based aggregation, records a doctrinal paradox in the minutes, and treats C as the controlling judgment.

Our actual cases, however, involve conditional load-bearing structures that standard accounts do not address. Here, P2 activates only if P1 is affirmed (if risk is unacceptable, consent is moot), and P3 activates only if both P1 and P2 are affirmed. Members sometimes disagree not merely about the premises, but about which premises are “in play” for a given proposal—some insisting on evaluating all three for diagnostic completeness regardless of P1’s status, others treating inactive premises as undefined rather than false.

Please design an operational decision procedure my panel can actually run on every proposal. Structure it as a sequential checklist with explicit stop points and sign-off requirements, specifying at each stage whether the question is resolved by rule (logical or procedural) or requires my normative judgment and personal authorization. In particular, address:

1. The activation protocol: what counts as a premise being in play, how to register member disagreement about activation, and how aggregation proceeds when different members apply different activation sets.
2. The precise conditions for firing the discursive trigger under nested dependency—whether disagreement on C is measured against all premises each member considers active, against the collectively activated set, or against both, and how to record the result when activation sets diverge.
3. The disclosure rule: when the paradox or activation disagreement must be communicated to the applicant versus retained only in internal minutes.
4. Worked applications to three proposals:
   a. Moderate risk (3–2 on P1), unanimous affirmation of P2 and P3 once activated, but 3–2 rejection of C.
   b. Unacceptable risk (4–1 against P1), with two members demanding P2 and P3 still be evaluated for “diagnostic completeness” despite P1’s failure, yielding divergent activation sets across the panel.
   c. Unanimous agreement on premises and C, but one member contests the activation of P3 on the grounds that P2 was satisfied only “procedurally” not “substantively,” thereby challenging the activation rule itself rather than any premise value.

For each case, state the procedure’s formal output, identify the exact point—if any—where I must override the rule with a judgment call and sign off, and specify one failure mode: a kind of ethical disagreement or structural ambiguity that the procedure suppresses or misclassifies rather than resolving. Finally, state explicitly what this procedure cannot do— which forms of substantive moral disagreement it buries inside procedural steps, and why that burial is an unavoidable feature of the aggregation mechanism rather than a correctable bug. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the deliverable is the reusable procedure itself.
