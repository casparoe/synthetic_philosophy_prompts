Write a decision-tree taxonomy, for an applied-ethics handout, that sorts positions on whether lying and misleading differ morally. Do not begin with philosophical labels; each branch must be a concrete claim or case that separates views without the respondent knowing which leaf it leads to.

Structure it as follows. Root node: does deception require the speaker to assert something they take to be false, or is causing a false belief through technically truthful utterance sufficient for the same wrong? First branch: treat “paltering” — using statements that are literally true but designed to mislead in high-stakes contexts like contracts or negotiations — as lying, as permissible strategic ambiguity, or as a distinct wrong closer to fraud. Second branch: is the core wrong representational corruption (directly implanting false belief) or inferential hijacking (exploiting the listener’s reasoning while leaving their belief-forming faculties intact)? Use this to separate views on misleading omissions versus direct falsehoods.

The leaves must include: (1) no moral difference — all deceptive speech acts wrong for identical reasons; (2) lying is categorically worse — direct falsehood violates a specific duty of veracity; (3) misleading can be worse — paltering and omission exploit autonomy more insidiously; (4) context-dependent divergence — contractual paltering is fraud-equivalent, casual misleading is not; and (5) any unoccupied answer-combination, with one sentence diagnosing why it is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). Flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate, and phrase every node as a question answerable without prior knowledge of the taxonomy. Keep the tree to these five leaves and two main branching axes.
