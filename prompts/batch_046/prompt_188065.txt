A colleague reconstructed an argument at a bioethics seminar that seems to show that no advance directive can derive genuine authority from an earlier self. I have laid out the steps below; they feel individually plausible, yet together they appear to reduce every past commitment to social convention.

(1) If an advance directive binds a later patient, its authority must come from the autonomy of the person who issued it.
(2) On a four-dimensionalist account, the issuer and the later patient are not strictly identical persons but temporal parts of one persisting spacetime worm.
(3) Therefore authority cannot rest on strict identity; it must rest on a continuity relation—psychological, neural, or narrative—between those temporal parts.
(4) Continuity is always a matter of degree, so we need a threshold that specifies how much continuity is required for authority.
(5) In progressive conditions such as dementia, the relevant continuities fade gradually, so there is no sharp qualitative break that can ground a non-arbitrary threshold.
(6) Without such a break, selecting any cutoff—say, retaining 60% of prior psychological overlap—is arbitrary: there is no principled reason why 60% confers authority and 59% does not.
(7) If the threshold is arbitrary, the directive's force does not flow from the earlier self's autonomy but from a social convention we impose after the fact.
(8) By the same reasoning, every past commitment—career plans, vows, long-term preferences—depends on a similarly arbitrary threshold and thus lacks autonomous authority.
(9) So either we deny that any earlier decision binds a later self, or we reject the framing in (2) and find some other basis for authority.

My colleague takes this to mean that advance directives have no special moral status beyond ordinary revocable contracts. I am inclined to defend (1) if possible, but I cannot tell whether the real problem is in (3), (5), or (7). Which step should be rejected, why did it seem acceptable, and what does giving it up cost? If I must choose, I would prefer to preserve (1) and (8) in some form, even if it requires abandoning the four-dimensionalist premise.
