Please explain the conceptual tension between Wittgenstein's account of family resemblance and the assumption that language games possess discrete, non-overlapping boundaries. To render the problem concrete, consider a constructed institutional practice—adjudicative testimony—in which participants are simultaneously bound by the evidential norms characteristic of scientific peer review and by the performative commitments characteristic of sworn oath-taking, yet lack the full institutional scaffolding of either domain. If family resemblance allows individual concepts to lack an essential core and to be held together only by a network of overlapping similarities, can language games themselves stand in relations of family resemblance to one another? Or does Wittgenstein's view that the significance of an utterance is determined by its use within a specific form of life require that any hybrid practice must ultimately resolve into membership in one dominant game? In your answer, distinguish carefully between internal family resemblance—the crisscrossing analogies among moves within a single game—and external family resemblance—the structural analogies that might hold across different games. Then address whether permitting the latter undermines the autonomy of form-of-life distinctions that Wittgenstein seems to presuppose, and whether the interstitial status of adjudicative testimony reveals a hidden essentialism in his framework or instead demonstrates that games can be genuinely hybrid without collapsing into one another.
