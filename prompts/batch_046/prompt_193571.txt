For my seminar on transformative experience and practical rationality, I’ve assembled a package of commitments that I’m unsure is jointly tenable, and I’d like an audit rather than a lecture. Here is where I stand:

(1) I accept Paul’s argument that genuinely transformative experiences are rationally opaque ex ante: the pre-experience agent cannot assign subjective values to post-experience outcomes because the evaluative framework itself is altered.

(2) I hold a Humean internalist view of practical reason: all normative authority derives from an agent’s present, first-personal motivational set, with no appeal to objective goods or external reasons.

(3) I think voluntary transformative choices—parenthood, deep religious conversion, and the like—can be rationally justified through what I call “epistemic escrow”: the present self delegates evaluative authority to a projected future self, treating the transformation as value-generating rather than as a consumption good evaluated now.

(4) I also maintain a diachronic coherence condition on agency: one should avoid actions that are foreseeable destroyers of one’s own future capacity for autonomous deliberation, absent an overriding justification.

(5) Finally, I deny that transformative choice escapes rational assessment altogether; I think it remains subject to ordinary decision-theoretic reasoning, but with an unusually steep epistemic discount rate applied to post-transformation payoffs.

Do these hang together? I see at least three pressure points. First, (2) and (3) seem to pull in opposite directions: if reasons are anchored entirely in my current motivational set, I appear unable to justify delegating authority to values I do not yet possess, which makes “epistemic escrow” look like disguised externalism. Second, (4) looks threatened by (3) if the transformation radically replaces my motivational set, since that seems to destroy the very source of my practical reasons. Third, (1) and (5) might appear incompatible—how can reasoning apply if the situation is rationally opaque?—though I suspect that tension dissolves, because the reasoning governs entry into the opaque zone rather than evaluation within it.

I don’t know which commitment has to go, or whether none do. Could you order the tensions by severity, distinguish the genuinely incompatible pairs from the merely apparent ones, identify the cheapest consistency-restoring move and what it costs, and specifically assess whether “epistemic escrow” is salvageable within a strictly internalist framework?
