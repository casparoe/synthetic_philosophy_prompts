I was discussing with friends whether an artificial intelligence far more intelligent than a human ought to be permitted to address us in any manner it chooses, and I found that the usual answers about transparency and deception seemed to miss a more subtle problem. I am now applying for a six-week independent research fellowship offered by my school's philosophy centre, and I require a formal proposal. The question concerns how superintelligent AI systems should be designed to communicate with humans, given that they may possess extraordinary persuasive capacities. Rather than rehearsing the familiar debate about openness versus manipulation, I wish to investigate a framing I have begun to call 'asymmetric scaffolding': the principle that such a system ought to be required to expose the logical architecture of its reasoning, yet simultaneously constrained from adapting its rhetorical form—its emotional register, its pacing, its analogical choices—to the particular cognitive or psychological vulnerabilities of its interlocutor. The proposal should state precisely whether this asymmetry can be ethically principled, or whether it merely relocates manipulation from the level of content to the level of form. It should explain why existing positions in AI ethics and philosophy of language leave this question unsettled; outline a method combining conceptual analysis with structured comparison to professional communication ethics, such as the obligations governing medical consultation or legal counsel; specify what a successful conclusion would look like, what would count as failure, and how the project avoids collapsing into a restatement of standard transparency arguments; and provide an honest assessment of whether this is achievable within six weeks by a sixth-form student. I will supply all citations and references myself, so please do not include a bibliography or reading list.
