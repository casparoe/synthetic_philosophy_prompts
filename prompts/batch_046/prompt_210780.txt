I'm trying to map my actual intuitive commitments about moral status across a gradient of sentience, partly because I've been unsatisfied with both the sharp-line view and the simple "capacity for suffering" metric that gets trotted out in every animal-ethics thread. I want reflective equilibrium: a principle, or a small competing family, that captures my verdicts, predicts on cases I haven't settled, and honestly tells me which of my gut reactions I should probably discard.

Here are my verdicts, mostly ordered by complexity of system, though not strictly. A standard mechanical thermostat with a bimetallic strip, maintaining room temperature through feedback: zero moral status. The Venus flytrap, which generates action potentials and closes on prey but lacks any neural integration: zero. A honeybee navigating via polarized light and performing the waggle dance: a tiny non-zero sliver, maybe 0.02 on a normalized scale, but essentially negligible in practical ethics. C. elegans, with its 302 neurons and dedicated nociceptive withdrawal pathways: about the same, perhaps 0.03, but I feel this should be comparable to the bee despite the bee's far richer behavioral repertoire. A human newborn with anencephaly—brainstem intact, reflex withdrawal from noxious stimuli present, but no cortex and no possibility of phenomenal consciousness: substantial status, around 0.6, though I recognize this might be speciesist. A healthy human in deep surgical anesthesia: full status, 1.0, despite temporary absence of consciousness. A sleeping adult cat: near-full, perhaps 0.95. A synthetic system I'll call a "nociceptive oracle"—a deep network trained exclusively on human pain reports, that outputs avoidance recommendations when its weights are corrupted, but has no sensory apparatus, no homeostasis, and no world-model beyond the training distribution: zero, despite the functional isomorphism with nociception. Then there is the case I keep oscillating on: a hypothetical engineered organism with a distributed nociceptive architecture—no central brain, but integrated feedback loops across tissues that produce sustained, coordinated avoidance behavior lasting minutes rather than reflexive milliseconds. I have no stable intuition; sometimes I think it should rank near C. elegans, other times near the anencephalic infant.

My question is whether there's a coherent principle behind this pattern. Does it depend on phenomenal consciousness, potential for consciousness, biological integration, or some hybrid? If the principle saves my high rating for the anencephalic infant, what does it say about the engineered organism? If it saves my low ratings for the bee and worm, must it deny the infant's status? And what should I think about the sleeping cat versus the anesthetized human—are they equal because of potential, or does sleep preserve something anesthesia disrupts? Please state the principle precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case, apply it to the engineered organism and to one other unlisted case of your choosing, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without revision.
