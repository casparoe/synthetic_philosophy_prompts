I am presenting a 20-minute seminar paper in comparative politics defending a tripartite framework for democratic decay: procedural erosion (court-packing, media capture), discursive delegitimation (treating electoral opponents as existential enemies rather than legitimate competitors), and constitutional hardening (using supermajoritarian mechanisms to entrench temporary victories). My thesis is that Hungary exhibits all three; Poland mainly the first two; Turkey represents authoritarian consolidation rather than backsliding, since its democratic baseline was never robust; and the US currently shows only discursive delegitimation without institutional hardening or capture, which means applying the backsliding label to the US is conceptually premature and largely partisan. The audience is five graduate students—two Hungarianists, a Poland specialist, a Turkey scholar, and an Americanist—who are institutionally focused, skeptical of normative taxonomy, and inclined to see "backsliding" as Western liberal pathologizing of conservative governments.

Prepare me for the objections in the order they will come: first, the area specialists challenging my country classifications in their own vocabulary; second, the Americanist arguing that discursive delegitimation in the US is already severe enough to count; third, the methodological challenge that constitutional hardening is just a redundant subtype of procedural erosion. For each, state the objection as they would phrase it, what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and the weakest point in my reply. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—whether discursive delegitimation can be operationalized independently of partisan preference without circularity—and advise me how to defend it briefly without sounding like I am smuggling normative commitments into my measurement.
