At a rationalist forum meetup, two regulars — Sam and Jordan — argued about whether moral compromise between genuinely incompatible value systems can be understood as a form of gains-from-trade. They had in mind a concrete scenario: a pluralist polity negotiating bioethics rules where secular liberal and traditionalist religious groups both have to concede ground.

Sam argued that the analogy holds precisely. Each side holds commitments ordered by importance; compromise involves exchanging lower-ranked applications for stronger protection of higher-ranked ones. If both sides secure more of what they most value, that is a Pareto improvement in moral space, not a betrayal. That values are not fungible like grain is irrelevant — what is traded is not the core value but its marginal extension.

Jordan objected that moral commitments are not goods in a basket but are constitutive of identity. Surrendering even a "low-ranked" prohibition — say, a limited exception to a reproductive or dietary norm — is not like exporting surplus; it is like tearing a fabric that holds the self together. She introduced the notion of "moral residue": unlike economic deals, moral compromises leave a stain or accumulated guilt that does not clear with the agreement. Over repeated compromises, this residue destabilises the agent or community, so the apparent gains are illusory.

Sam replied that Jordan was reifying values. He granted that residue exists but treated it as a transaction cost, like shipping fees. A rational agent should internalise it into the pricing mechanism: if residue exceeds the benefit, the trade does not occur. This does not invalidate the model; it merely sets the price.

Jordan's final move was that Sam's framework smuggles in a single metric of "moral satisfaction" that can be aggregated across agents. But if value systems are genuinely incommensurable, there is no common currency, hence no Pareto frontier — only domination or surrender disguised as exchange.

They asked me to adjudicate and I genuinely could not. Please explain who is right about what, and who is more right overall.
