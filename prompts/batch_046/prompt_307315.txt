Please compare the conceptual framework of Moloch—understood here as a multipolar trap in which individually rational competitive optimisation produces a collective outcome that no participant actually prefers—with Garrett Hardin's tragedy of the commons, in which rational agents deplete a shared resource through uncoordinated use.

Consider, in particular, a variant that might be termed a structural-commitment race rather than a preference-driven one: imagine an industrial sector in which every actor genuinely shares a preference for slower, more cautious development, yet each is locked into rapid deployment by interlocking capital-market obligations, supply-chain contracts, and first-mover penalties that make unilateral deceleration individually ruinous, regardless of collective intent.

Your analysis should address the following points:

1. Which framework more naturally explains the failure of cooperation in this scenario, given that the agents' preferences are aligned rather than opposed?
2. Does the multipolar-trap analysis presuppose a hidden preference divergence—say, a private benefit that outweighs shared risk—or can it operate purely through commitment problems and institutional lock-in?
3. What does the comparison reveal about whether races to the bottom are best understood as failures of collective action over resources, or as failures of credible commitment among rational agents with common goals?
4. Should you conclude that one framework is substantially more illuminating, specify what conceptual work the other framework still performs that the winner cannot replace.

Please justify your answers with conceptual reasoning rather than empirical case studies, and avoid treating the two frameworks as merely synonymous.
