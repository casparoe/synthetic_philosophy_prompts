I've been working through Gödel's less-discussed extension of his modal ontological reasoning into the question of personal survival, particularly the inference that if consciousness is grounded in rational structure rather than in contingent biological organization, then its annihilation by physical death is logically impossible. The position, as I reconstruct it, holds that in a strongly rationalist ontology—where every consistent set of positive properties is exemplified and physical dependence is treated as a contingent or even negative feature—consciousness belongs to the class of rational structures that must persist across modal domains. Death, on this account, is not extinction but a shift in accessibility conditions, which is why Gödel apparently treated an afterlife not as a theological consolation but as something like a corollary of the same completeness principles that underpin his argument for a supreme being.

What would have to be true for this conclusion to collapse? I want an honest exposure map rather than a dismissal based on dualism's current unpopularity. Specifically, which premises are load-bearing for the survival inference—the identification of consciousness with a positive property, the claim that substrate-dependence is itself a negative or contingent feature, or the bridge principle that logical completeness entails physical independence? What predictions does this view generate that its rivals do not, such as structural coherence in near-death reports, the preservation of mathematical intuition despite severe neurological damage, or the impossibility of irreducible quantum indeterminacy? I also want to know what committed defenders would concede as genuinely damaging: would they grant that Gödel's own psychological deterioration and refusal of nourishment near the end of his life undercut the serene rationalism the view seems to require, or dismiss that biographical tension as irrelevant? And how does the position navigate the gap between logical survival—the persistence of a rational agent as a kind of set-theoretic object—and phenomenal survival—the continuity of subjective experience—since the argument appears to secure the former far more easily than the latter?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I am especially interested in whether a decisive formal result—perhaps about the consistency of modal ontological closure with physicalist descriptions of consciousness, or about the failure of positive-property preservation under brain death—could break the inference entirely, versus adjustments that merely force the view to concede that post-mortem existence lacks bodily sensation or that personal identity becomes radically discontinuous. State precisely which of Gödel's implicit commitments must hold for the afterlife conclusion to follow, and assess whether the position is ultimately well-insulated at the cost of empirical emptiness, or whether it takes risks that its defenders rarely acknowledge.
