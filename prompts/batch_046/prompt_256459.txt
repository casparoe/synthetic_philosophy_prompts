At a graduate seminar on Marx and technology, two participants—Elena and Marcus—debated whether Marx’s writings on machinery support an accelerationist reading of automation. Their exchange unfolded as follows.

(1) Elena argued that in the Grundrisse fragment on machines Marx anticipates full automation collapsing necessary labor time and opening a realm of freedom, implying that Marx ultimately welcomed technological development as the material precondition for post-capitalist flourishing.

(2) Marcus responded that this ignores Capital, where Marx describes machinery under capitalist relations as extending the working day, cheapening labor, and functioning as a hostile power over workers—hardly a welcoming stance toward automation.

(3) Elena replied that Marcus conflates the technical potential of machinery with its capitalist application; Marx distinguishes machinery as a general means of shortening labor from machinery as a means of prolonging labor under capital, suggesting the technology itself is not the enemy.

(4) Marcus countered that Elena smuggles in a false neutrality through the idea of machinery “in itself.” Marx’s concept of real subsumption shows that capital does not merely apply pre-existing technology but reshapes technical development from within—designing for valorization, surveillance, and deskilling—so there is no pure technical core separable from its social form.

(5) Elena then proposed a test: imagine an automated factory with identical physical architecture—same robots, same layout—owned collectively and producing for use rather than profit. If the machinery were truly constituted by capital, such redirection should be impossible without complete reconstruction; yet the physical plant could apparently be repurposed, which suggests the subsumption is institutional rather than ontological.

(6) Marcus answered that this hypothetical misses the depth of real subsumption. The speed-ups, control mechanisms, and skill-stripping embedded in the design are not incidental institutional overlays but are engineered into the machinery itself; redirecting use requires redesigning these features, which proves the technology was never neutral and that Elena’s “same architecture” is never actually the same.

They asked the group to referee. Please adjudicate each move in turn: rate Elena’s and Marcus’s arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, justify each rating briefly, and say who is more right overall. Then identify what both are missing—specifically, whether Marx’s framework genuinely permits a distinction between technical design and capitalist social form, and whether Elena’s collective-factory hypothetical actually tests that distinction or instead assumes an unstated premise about technological plasticity. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; adjudicate the arguments as presented.
