Your colleague in the history of medicine and political institutions has consistently judged whether Enlightenment-era reforms were primarily sources of progress or of domination. Her track record: she called the replacement of guild-controlled printing with universal licensing open to any literate applicant progress; the pairing of state foundling hospitals with mandatory civil registration of births domination; the abolition of hereditary provincial estates in favor of uniform administrative departments progress; the state-imposed decimal calendar enforced across all public records domination; the distribution of vernacular-language procedural codes to every canton progress; and the establishment of centrally inspected national schools with standardized curricula and state-appointed examiners domination.

Now consider a late-eighteenth-century reform proposal that abolishes the guild of master surgeons, replaces it with state-examined officers of health, requires those officers to provide free emergency care to indigent patients as a condition of practice rights, but also obliges them to submit monthly reports using a standardized nosological form that classifies symptoms for a central health bureau. Predict whether she will classify this reform as progress or domination. Lay out the two leading implicit rules that fit her earlier six verdicts—one that weighs the dissolution of corporate privilege against the creation of a state licensing monopoly, and one that weighs explicit reciprocal service obligations against the imposition of classificatory transparency—stating what each rule predicts here. Give your best-guess verdict, identifying which specific feature of the reform does the decisive work, and propose a stripped-down further case that isolates only one of these two features in the absence of the other, explaining what her answer to that case would reveal about which rule she actually runs.
