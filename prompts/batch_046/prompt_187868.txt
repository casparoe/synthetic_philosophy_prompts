I'm a retired physicist who has recently begun studying moral philosophy, and I'm trying to determine whether a single principle governs my judgments about what wealthy nations owe to poor ones. Here are my verdicts on specific cases.

If a wealthy nation's historical carbon emissions caused drought and crop failure in a poor nation, I think it owes substantial compensation; this feels like settling a causal debt. If another nation is equally poor and suffering, but the wealthy nation never affected it in any way, I still think some aid is owed—perhaps a duty of rescue—yet the obligation feels different in kind, not merely smaller in magnitude. If a poor nation is impoverished primarily because of its own extractive institutions, and the wealthy nation offered fair terms and caused no harm, then I think the obligation is minimal or absent; the failure is internal, like an uninduced system breakdown. If one poor nation was formerly a colony exploited for resources and is now middle-income, while another was never colonized and is currently poorer, I judge the obligation to the former colony stronger, even though present need is lower; historical causation seems to generate a persistent claim that current need does not fully override. If aid sent to a corrupt regime will largely be stolen, I do not think the obligation is discharged by sending cash; it transforms into supporting institutional reform or direct delivery, not budget support.

These judgments pull in opposite directions: if the principle is causal responsibility, why do I owe anything to the unaffected nation? If it is present need, why do I owe more to the richer former colony than to the poorer unaffected one? Is there a coherent principle—perhaps weighing causal contribution, institutional integrity, and present need—that yields all five verdicts? Or must I abandon unity and drop one? Please state the best principle precisely, show which clause explains which case, predict what it says about a nation suffering a purely natural disaster with corrupt governance, and tell me honestly which of my judgments it cannot accommodate.
