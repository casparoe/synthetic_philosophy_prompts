I just spent most of last evening arguing with a close friend—she studies rhetoric, I edit copy for a policy magazine—about whether Orwell's analysis of political language can be operationalized or whether it remains an aesthetic complaint dressed up as ethics. She insisted that without a reproducible test, an editor is just enforcing personal taste; I countered that there is a recoverable core about how language smuggles in commitments that can be made explicit and checkable. I am asking you to settle the practical question for me, not the metaphysical one. You should take the role of a professional philosopher of language who has actually worked in editorial review. Before you build anything, spend a moment brainstorming: identify the specific cognitive or pragmatic failures Orwell targets when he describes corrupted political language—semantic dilution, dead metaphors, and the substitution of abstraction for concrete reality—then flag the gap in that framework that makes it unusable for an editor without philosophical training, and propose one supplementary distinction to close that gap.

Your deliverable is a reusable decision procedure that a copy editor at a weekly policy publication can run in roughly ten minutes per article. The editor handles about fifteen pieces a week, must justify any proposed change to the author, and needs sign-off from the editor-in-chief whenever the issue is one of values rather than linguistic fact. The procedure should be a step-by-step flow in words: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure mandates a change, where it mandates leaving the text alone, and where it stops and hands the decision upward. Ground it in Orwell's actual analysis—paraphrased, not quoted—of how political language operates, but inject and formalize one new distinction: the difference between semantic dilution (a term losing concrete referential content and becoming a hollow signifier) and frame coercion (grammatical or rhetorical structure that forces the reader to accept a contested causal, moral, or agentive relation without explicit argument). Both are corruptions of thought, but they require different editorial interventions.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: first, a headline using the passive voice to report a policy failure without specifying an agent; second, a belief-report quotation in which substituting a descriptive title for a person's proper name alters the pragmatic presupposition about the speaker's authority; and third, a case where the procedure correctly outputs a recommendation to alter language that the author will strongly resist and that the editor-in-chief will likely reject—explain precisely why this is the correct procedural result even though it is editorially unwelcome. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot do: which forms of corruption it will systematically miss, which false positives it will generate under time pressure, and the exact boundary where irreducible editorial judgment takes over. Think carefully, move slowly through the translation from theory to practice, and do not dress up judgment calls as mechanical steps.
