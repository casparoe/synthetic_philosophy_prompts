Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—call them M and N—who are preparing a graduate seminar on the School of Names and have reached an interpretive deadlock over Gongsun Long’s argument that a white horse is not identical to a horse. M defends a modal-reconstructive reading: she claims the argument turns on the separability of properties in possibility space, so that the intensional profile of the compound concept includes contingent co-instantiation conditions that the bare concept of horsehood lacks, and that this difference in modal structure—not a failure of reference—drives the denial of identity. N counters with a compositional, quasi-mereological reading: he holds that color and shape belong to different ontological parts of the compound entity, so that treating the white horse as identical to bare horsehood ignores the part-whole architecture that the text uses to distinguish named kinds from their attributed instances. Have them engage in direct back-and-forth rather than talk past each other: M should explain why N’s part-whole framework fails to capture the dialogue’s explicit concern with self-identity versus other-identity, and N should argue that M’s modal apparatus anachronistically imports possible-world semantics into a debate originally concerned with naming and classification. Frame the opening as their conversation in a seminar office, with both aware that students will ask whether modern logical tools clarify or distort the ancient fragments.

Separate what is broadly settled in the scholarship—such as the association of the argument with the School of Names and its survival in fragments attributed to Gongsun Long—from what remains genuinely contested about its underlying logical machinery, and do not present either the modal or the compositional reconstruction as the consensus view among specialists. Neither interlocutor should be a strawman; both should acknowledge the textual difficulties that motivate the other’s approach, and the dialogue should conclude without a forced resolution, leaving the audience with a clear sense of which premises each side is defending and where the crux actually lies.
