As a novelist who also curates a quarterly fiction series for a small press, I am confronted every submission cycle with manuscripts that slip through the taxonomic nets our guidelines erect—categories such as literary fiction, speculative fiction, and autofiction—not because they are mere hybrids, but because they seem to share scattered traits with several families without possessing any essential feature common to one. I want to convert Wittgenstein's conception of language games and family resemblance into a usable editorial protocol: a step-by-step procedure that my readers can run on any new manuscript to determine whether it participates in an existing narrative-language game, extends that family through resemblance without membership by essence, or inaugurates an altogether different form of narrative practice.

The protocol should be structured as a sequence of ordered tests, with explicit criteria for what counts as a passing or failing answer at each stage, and it should clearly demarcate the points at which classification becomes impossible without importing editorial values—for instance, whether the press's mission to publish formally experimental work should override a family-resemblance reading that places a manuscript closer to conventional realism. It must preserve those conceptual distinctions that actually do work—such as the difference between sharing a vocabulary with a genre and sharing its form of life—while allowing less load-bearing distinctions to fall away for the sake of practical decision-making. Finally, it should include an honest accounting of where the procedure is likely to misfire, particularly when resemblances pull in opposing directions, and it should specify what it cannot do, including cases that demand aesthetic judgment rather than taxonomic analysis.

Please apply this procedure to three hypothetical submissions: first, a narrative composed entirely of footnotes to a nonexistent scholarly article about a fictional event; second, a story that shifts between meticulously researched historical documentary and unmarked magical transformation without signaling the transition; and third, a dialogue between two unreliable speakers who recount an event neither witnessed, presented with no framing narration or attribution. In your applications, include at least one instance where the procedure yields a recommendation—say, classification within an existing family—that I, as curator, would likely resist on editorial grounds, and conclude by stating precisely which questions remain outside the procedure's reach and must be resolved by direct judgment.
