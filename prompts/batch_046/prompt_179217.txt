I'm preparing a defense of a particular form of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—one that goes beyond the familiar claim that qualitative feels are misrepresented, and instead holds that the very notion of intrinsic phenomenal properties is generated by a specific functional misreading within the brain's self-modeling architecture. On this view, first-person reports describing what it is like to undergo an experience do not describe a non-representational substrate; they describe the integrative dynamics of access-conscious representations, but they are systematically misinterpreted by the cognitive system as reports about a private, non-functional owner of those representations. The illusion is not accidental: it is structurally incentivized because constructing a unified phenomenal owner solves a cross-modal binding problem, and the brain therefore treats this constructed owner as the bearer of qualitative character rather than as a representational artifact.

Given this framing, I want an honest map of its exposure. What would actually count against it? I am not asking for the standard objections—such as appeals to introspective acquaintance or arguments from phenomenological transparency—but rather for the kinds of evidence, conceptual results, or theoretical developments that would genuinely destabilize this specific architecture. Which empirical findings about self-model disruption, representational integration, or first-person report generation would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Which formal results in philosophy of mind or cognitive science—say, about the necessary conditions for representational misinterpretation—would force a revision, and which could be absorbed by further qualification? I also want to know what a committed defender of this view should themselves concede as counting against them, not only what critics claim: are there predictions about the correlates of self-model failure that, if falsified, would undermine the structural-incentive story? Finally, is there a risk that this position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent counterexample can be redescribed as just another layer of representational misinterpretation—and if so, is that a defect for a view of this kind? I would personally find it most persuasive if there were a well-described neural or cognitive condition in which the self-model remains intact but the misattribution of phenomenal ownership is selectively abolished without any corresponding loss of representational content; short of that, I want to know where the view is genuinely vulnerable and where it is merely insulated.
