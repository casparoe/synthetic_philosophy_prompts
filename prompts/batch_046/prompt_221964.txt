Two colleagues in a philosophy of cognition reading group disagreed about whether Gregorian creatures—those that rely on culturally transmitted mental tools—encounter a form of representational error that is conceptually unavailable to Popperian creatures. Alina proposed that because Gregorian cognition depends on external symbolic systems (written notation, formal grammars, shared algorithms), a Gregorian creature can harbor beliefs whose content is fixed partly by institutional norms governing those tools. This generates a distinctive failure mode: one can misapply a symbol not merely by misrepresenting the world, but by violating the normative constraints that authorize its use—an error that requires recognizing the symbol's social function, not just testing a private hypothesis against experience. Ben objected that this collapses into standard Popperian error. Internal hypotheses can also be governed by norms of coherence or consistency, and misinterpreting one's own mental representation is structurally identical to misinterpreting an external one; the location of the symbol is irrelevant to the logic of belief revision. Alina answered that Popperian norms are instrumental—aimed at predictive success—whereas Gregorian norms are constitutive: to use a symbol correctly just is to satisfy its institutional conditions, so the error is not a failed prediction but a category mistake about the tool itself. Ben replied that constitutive norms are themselves hypotheses about which practices survive cultural selection, so they remain subject to Popperian testing at the population level, and no genuinely new kind of error emerges. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess the strength of each move, determine who is correct on the specific issues raised, and indicate what both disputants overlook about the relationship between internal hypothesis testing and culturally mediated cognition.
