I've been thinking about scientific models that rely on assumptions everyone knows are false—like epidemiology models that treat a city as a uniformly mixed soup where every infected person is equally likely to infect anyone else, ignoring neighborhoods, workplaces, travel patterns, and venue-specific superspreader events. I came across Milton Friedman's argument that the realism of assumptions doesn't matter if predictions hold up, and then I read about a stronger variant some call the "F-twist": the idea that unrealistic assumptions are actually at their best when they are structurally impossible—when they could never be literally true—because that prevents anyone from reading the model as a direct description and forces evaluation purely on predictive output.

My study partner wrote an answer to this exam-style question, and I need an instructor's assessment. The question was:

"Consider an epidemiological model that assumes homogeneous mixing across an entire city, ignoring geographic clustering, social networks, and venue-specific transmission pathways. Despite these omissions, it predicts the infection peak within 4% of observed data. A critic argues that the model's predictive success is hollow because the mixing assumption hides the actual causal pathways—such as superspreader events in specific venues—so the model fails to genuinely explain what is happening. How should a defender of Friedman's view, specifically appealing to the F-twist, respond? And does that response fully address the critic?"

Here is my partner's answer:

"The F-twist says unrealistic assumptions are not just tolerable but actively desirable, especially when they are impossible in principle, because that proves the model isn't smuggling in hidden realism. Friedman showed that science only needs predictive accuracy, not true premises, so explanation is really just successful prediction in disguise. In this case, the homogeneous mixing assumption is exactly the kind of false-but-useful simplification the F-twist celebrates. Because the model predicts the peak within 4%, the critic is wrong to demand that it also display the real transmission pathways. The more impossible the mixing assumption is, the stronger the model becomes, since it demonstrates that the result doesn't depend on literal descriptions of social behavior. So the epidemiologist should keep the assumption and even make it more extreme if that improves the numbers, which fully answers the critic."
