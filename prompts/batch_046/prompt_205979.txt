I'm taking a course on ancient Chinese logic and we just hit the School of Names, specifically the white horse dialogue where they argue a white horse isn't a horse because horse is the shape and white is just the color added on. Me and my study parter got into it over coffee and tried running the same logic on people, but now we're stuck in something that feels like it proves sleeping people don't have rights. Here is the chain we wrote on a napkin:

(1) In the dialogue, "horse" names only the form/shape, while "white horse" names form plus color. Because the second has a property the first lacks, they are not the same thing.
(2) Apply that naming rule consistently: "rational being" names the form (the capacity for reason), while "currently reasoning being" names form plus the occurrent mental activity.
(3) So "currently reasoning being" is not "rational being" — the relation is exactly like white horse to horse.
(4) Take someone in deep dreamless sleep. They have the form, but they aren't reasoning right now.
(5) By (3), the sleeper isn't a rational being; they're a different kind of entity, maybe just a "capacity-holder-in-abeyance."
(6) Moral and legal protections apply to rational beings, not to capacity-holders-in-abeyance.
(7) So sleeping people have no rights, which is obviosly wrong.
(8) The premises all seem solid except maybe (6), but if I drop (6) I still have to explain why the naming split doesn't matter for rights, and my parter thinks I'm just saving the conclusion by fiat.

My parter says the error is treating an occurrent state like color, but I don't see why color should recieve special treatment over mental activity. Which step should I actually reject? I really want to keep the idea that names can split reality along different property-lines, and I also want sleeping people to keep their rights. What's the least painful way out, and what does giving up that step do to the rest of the framework?
