After a long dinner argument with a friend who studies modern European history, I found myself stuck on a pattern in how we discuss the perpetrators of mass violence—specifically the debate between those who, like Christopher Browning, stress situational pressures, peer dynamics, and incremental involvement, and those who, like Daniel Goldhagen, emphasize a pre-existing cultural disposition toward eliminationist antisemitism. The pattern is not the familiar situation-versus-character dispute itself, but something more granular: when historians or interlocutors examine the fact that refusal to participate was possible and rarely punished severely—Browning’s well-known observation that some members of Reserve Police Battalion 101 asked to be excused and were permitted—this fact is used asymmetrically. To defend a dispositional account, it is read as proof that those who remained made a free, character-revealing choice; to defend a situational account, the very same fact is sometimes read as proof that coercion was minimal, hence the overwhelming compliance must be explained by other situational mechanisms. The evidentiary value of low-cost refusal seems to flip depending on which explanatory conclusion is being pursued, rather than serving as a stable constraint on both.

I have been trying to determine whether this asymmetry has a specific name in the philosophy of history, historiography, or social explanation—something that captures not mere confirmation bias, but this particular structural oscillation where one contextual feature is recruited to license opposite inferences about agency and culture. It does not quite match the fundamental attribution error, which concerns observers versus actors, nor does it seem to be straightforward motivated reasoning, since the flip happens within a single conversation about the same dataset. If there is no established term for this precise pattern, I would like a provisional name and a clear delineation of its boundaries: how it differs from selectively applying explanatory frameworks, from using double standards in moral judgment, and from the more general problem of underdetermination of theory by evidence in historical explanation. I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of scholars to read; I want to know whether the carving exists, and if not, what the edges of the phenomenon should be.
