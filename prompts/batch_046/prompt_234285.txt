I'm running an upper-level seminar on explanation and reduction, and I've got two grad students who've been arguing past each other all term about Dennett's cranes versus skyhooks and whether greedy reductionism is actually a failure mode or just a pejorative for science people find deflationary. One camp — call them the Crane Builders — thinks every legitimate explanation is bottom-up build-up, that higher-level talk is just a useful summary, and that "greedy reductionism" is usually just good explanation that feels unsatisfying. The other camp — the Phenomenon Preservationists — thinks some bottom-up accounts genuinely destroy the feature they set out to explain, that cranes don't always reach high enough to reconstruct it, and that Dennett's framing misses real cases of explanatory elimination.

I want them to produce a joint adversarial collaboration statement they could both sign. Format: first a numbered section of agreed claims — not weaselly platitudes, but precise points both accept (e.g., whether multi-level explanation is coherent, whether evolutionary accounts of altruism preserve the phenomenon, whether predictive processing models can account for illusion correction without eliminating perceptual content). Then a disagreements table with columns for each camp, phrased in neutral wording so each side recognizes their own view. Then the cruxes.

The first crux should be whether there's a principled distinction between explaining-away and explaining, and if so what the marker is — maybe something about whether the lower-level account retains the causal profile of the higher-level phenomenon, or whether it renders the higher-level vocabulary systematically replaceable. The second crux should center on a concrete case both agree is relevant: the "synthetic flock" scenario, where individual agents follow only local rules of seperate alignment, cohesion, and avoidance, yet the collective produces an emergent evasion maneuver that saves the group from a predator. Both sides agree the simulation runs and that the evasion occurs. Each should record in advance whether describing the evasion entirely in terms of individual agent updates eliminates the flock-level causal contribution or just explains it via cranes, and specifically whether the flock-level description adds irreducible predictive or causal power.

There's also a third crux about explanatory debt: does accepting a bottom-up account commit one to an ontological claim that the higher-level property is merely derivative in a way that undermines its reality?

Please draft the full document they'd submit — agreed claims, disagreements table, crux section with predictions recorded for each side, and a brief note on what empirical or conceptual result would move either camp. Write it casual, like I'm emailing them the assignment late at night. Don't ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists — they can't search the library for this. And avoid specific book titles or page numbers since I'm not entirely sure and don't want them guessing. Include a typo or two if it feels natural; I usually send these without proofreading.
