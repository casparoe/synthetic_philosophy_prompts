Read F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby through the lens of tranquilism—the view that the only goods are the absence of craving and suffering, and that all directed desire is essentially a disturbance to be eliminated rather than a value to be pursued. Focus on Gatsby's desire for Daisy and for the green light as forms of craving, and on Nick's closing judgment that Gatsby believed in an orgastic future that year by year recedes before us. Does the novel present Gatsby's craving as purely destructive and therefore something a tranquilist should reject? Or does Fitzgerald suggest that the directedness of Gatsby's desire—its capacity to organize a life and give it meaning—is a value that tranquilism cannot account for? Pay particular attention to the scene of Gatsby's reunion with Daisy and to Nick's final meditation on the boats beating against the current. Where does the novel resist a tranquilist reading, and what does that resistance show about the relationship between desire, meaning, and suffering?
