For a podcast-interview scene in my novel, I need questions for an unnamed philosopher who argues that everyday conventions—tipping, queuing, and jaywalking—generate differentiated, not uniform, moral obligations for visitors. She distinguishes tipping as a quasi-contractual expectation embedded in local labor markets, queuing as a weak fairness duty arising from coordination, and jaywalking as a negative duty not to impose risk, but she rejects both the simple maxim "when in Rome, do as Romans do" and the claim that all such norms are mere etiquette. Write me eight to ten focused interview questions that press the actual architecture of her view: how she separates contractual, coordinative, and harm-based reasons; where she draws the line between convention and morality; and what she says about ignorance, absence of observers, and cross-cultural conflict. For each major claim, include a brief follow-up keyed to her likely reply that a visitor "steps into a practice that already distributes expectations." Specifically cover whether ignorance of local tipping norms excuses poor practice; whether queuing remains obligatory when one is alone at an empty bus stop; and whether jaywalking on a deserted street violates any obligation at all. End with a question asking what kind of local convention—perhaps one involving gender segregation or religious exclusion—she thinks a visitor may legitimately refuse, and what that refusal reveals about the limits of her view.
