Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the Ex-Ante Commitment Camp and the Dynamic Self-Modeling Camp on the absent-minded driver problem. The collaboration should treat the standard two-exit formulation—where an agent aims to take the second exit for a high payoff, receives a lower or null payoff at the first, and cannot distinguish the two nodes due to an absent-mindedness constraint—as common ground, without requiring citation of particular papers or authors.

The deliverable must contain the following elements in a formal, polished register.

(i) A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover: the formal decision-tree structure, including the information sets that merge the two exit nodes; what standard causal and evidential decision theories each prescribe at the planning stage versus at each individual node; the precise tension between ex ante optimality and node-by-node optimization that generates the paradox; and any shared judgments about what a satisfactory resolution must preserve—such as consistency with basic probability calculus, avoidance of Dutch-book vulnerability where applicable, and fidelity to the intuition that the agent genuinely does not observe her position.

(ii) A disagreements table or clearly demarcated section stating the exact points of divergence, with each disagreement phrased in wording that the camp it represents would accept as accurate to its own view. At minimum, include disagreements over: whether rationality should be evaluated relative to the planning stage or relative to the agent’s epistemic state at each node; whether the absent-mindedness constraint should be modeled as an exogenous environmental parameter or as an endogenous feature of the agent’s reasoning architecture; and whether the paradox is dissolved primarily by introducing commitment devices, by refining the state-space to include meta-cognitive or indexical markers, or by revising the decision rule itself.

(iii) The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one camp substantially closer to the other. Identify at least three. One crux should concern whether graded or probabilistic memory decay (rather than binary indistinguishability) eliminates the paradox or merely transforms its structure. A second should concern whether a continuous-time formulation, in which the agent updates beliefs smoothly along the route rather than at discrete nodes, removes the conflict between planning and acting. A third should concern whether an agent who can predict her own future reasoning without observing her current state can achieve ex ante optimality without any external commitment mechanism.

(iv) For each crux, specify one concrete argument, model variant, or structural analogy that both camps agree is genuinely relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts the evaluation of that case will show. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

(v) A brief joint assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—specifically, whether disagreements about “true rationality,” “genuine choice,” or “the right evaluation point” reduce to differing stipulations about the scope of decision-theoretic evaluation—together with the reasoning both sides can accept for that assessment.

Write without introductory framing or commentary outside the prompt itself. Avoid verbatim quotations from specific texts; paraphrase any referenced formulations without quotation marks. Ensure that the injected variations—graded decay, continuous-time dynamics, and endogenous reasoning architecture—are integrated naturally into the adversarial structure rather than treated as afterthoughts.
