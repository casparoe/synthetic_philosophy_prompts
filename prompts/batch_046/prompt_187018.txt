I'm defending, in a philosophy-of-logic seminar, a reconception of logical omniscience in epistemic logic. My position: omniscience should be retained not as a descriptive psychological claim but as a normative boundary condition—rational belief requires closure under logical consequence within an agent's fixed logical vocabulary ("thin" omniscience), not knowledge of all logical truths ("thick" omniscience). I argue that resource-bounded objections target only the thick version and miss the normative point. The audience is split: modal logicians working in dynamic epistemic logic who see idealization as technically necessary; epistemologists who treat idealization as a vice unless explicitly justified; and one philosopher of mind focused on bounded rationality, who will ask about cognitive implementation. They share a commitment to descriptive fidelity but accept normative modeling when its purpose is explicit. Prepare me for the Q&A: list the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each faction would phrase them; for each, state what I should concede up front, what I must hold, a concise reply (suitable for a two-minute response), and the specific weakness in my reply that I should be ready to defend rather than hide. Order them by how damaging they are. End with the objection I am hoping nobody asks—namely, that vocabulary-relativity trivializes omniscience by allowing the vocabulary to shrink ad hoc to avoid any counterexample—and sketch how I should handle it if it comes.
