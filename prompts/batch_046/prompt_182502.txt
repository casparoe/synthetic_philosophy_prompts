I am trying to determine whether there is a unified account of virtue attribution that survives the situationist challenge without collapsing back into global trait theory. My working principle is this: a character trait qualifies as a virtue for an agent just in case it is a reason-responsive disposition that (a) explains the agent's conduct across counterfactual situations falling within a single regulative domain, where the domain is individuated by its governing evaluative ideal rather than by social role or institutional context; (b) is not entirely sustained by external monitoring, coercive incentives, or expectation of praise; and (c) is endorsed by the agent upon reflection, regardless of whether its origin was habit or reasoning.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, Marcus will enter burning buildings to rescue strangers and will physically defend the weak against attackers, yet he consistently avoids every form of social or professional risk—he never contradicts a superior, never defends an unpopular opinion in public, and never initiates a difficult interpersonal confrontation. I judge that Marcus does not possess the virtue of courage; rather, he possesses physical courage, which is a genuinely different trait, because the evaluative ideal governing physical risk (preservation of welfare) differs from that governing social risk (integrity or respect for autonomy).

Second, Sarah is truthful with friends, family, and colleagues in all ordinary matters, but she systematically deceives government officials about minor bureaucratic details she regards as unjust. I judge that Sarah lacks the virtue of honesty altogether. The domain of honesty is unified by respect for the interlocutor's rational agency, and since this applies equally to officials and friends, her deception is not a localized exception but a failure of the virtue itself.

Third, James regularly stops to assist strangers in distress, but on one occasion—when he was severely late for a decisive job interview—he walked past a lost person asking for directions. I judge that James retains the virtue of compassion. The lapse is explained by an exceptional situational pressure, and the disposition remains stable across the relevant counterfactual range.

Fourth, Elena displays perfect kindness in every observed interaction, but only because she works in a heavily monitored environment where cruelty is instantly punished; in counterfactual conditions without surveillance, she acts with calculated cruelty. I judge that Elena lacks kindness; condition (b) is violated.

Fifth, consider Frank and Grace, who display identical patterns of generous behavior across diverse situations. Frank arrived at this pattern through reflective endorsement of others' welfare; Grace arrived at it through unreflective childhood conditioning she has never examined. I judge that Frank possesses the virtue of generosity while Grace does not; condition (c) requires reflective endorsement.

Sixth—and here is the tension—consider Robert, who is patient with his own children and with strangers in formal settings, yet consistently impatient and dismissive with his subordinates at work. I am inclined to say that Robert possesses patience in some domains and lacks it in others, and that this localization is acceptable rather than a failure of a unified virtue. But this seems to conflict with my verdict on Sarah: if patience can be restricted by social role or institutional context, why can honesty not be restricted in the same way?

Is there a coherent principle that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn—must I deny that courage splits into distinct virtues, deny that honesty is domain-unified, or deny that Robert's localization is acceptable? Please state the resulting principle precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its predictions for cases I have not listed, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. In particular, apply it to the following unlisted case: an agent who is courageous in both physical and social domains, but only in situations where she expects to be observed and praised for her bravery.
