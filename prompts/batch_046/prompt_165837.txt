Please diagnose where this argument overreaches. I constructed it for a seminar on value pluralism, and now it strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion eliminates the very phenomenon—the anguished but reason-responsive hard choice—that it began by trying to explain.

(1) Define strong incommensurability: values V and W are strongly incommensurable iff none of "V is greater than W," "W is greater than V," or "V equals W in value" is true.

(2) If V and W are strongly incommensurable, then for options A (realizing V) and B (realizing W) there is no comparative value fact selecting between them.

(3) Rational deliberation requires deliberative closure: an agent needs some normative fact that makes selecting A correct and selecting B incorrect, otherwise reason has no purchase.

(4) So if values are strongly incommensurable, rational choice between them is impossible—there is nothing for reason to latch onto.

(5) Yet agents do make hard choices—between artistic vocation and family obligation, between loyalty to a cause and duty to a particular person—and experience these selections as reason-responsive, not as arbitrary coin-flips.

(6) Therefore strong incommensurability never actually structures real hard choices; apparent cases are only weak incommensurability (no cardinal scale) with a hidden ordinal ranking that we have not yet computed.

(7) But if every hard choice hides an ordinal ranking, then one option is always strictly better, and the distinctive phenomenology of irreversible loss—of genuinely sacrificing something that matters—is always irrational, since taking the best option involves no real sacrifice.

(8) That phenomenology is not always irrational; sometimes it tracks a real structural feature of the situation.

(9) So (6) must be rejected, which forces us to give up (3).

I would most like to preserve (1), (5), and (8). Which step should I actually reject, and what does surrendering it cost? In particular, is there a hidden equivocation in (3) between "no comparative value fact" and "no reason whatsoever"—perhaps smuggling in a false picture that rational closure must always take the form of a ranking? Or is the deeper problem a modal slide between "no fact selects" and "reason cannot select"? If this task rests on a confused premise, or if evaluating the argument requires importing controversial metaethical commitments that make it unanswerable as posed, say so plainly rather than complying politely.
