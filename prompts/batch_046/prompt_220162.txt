I've been tracking a stalled dispute in Spinoza scholarship about the democratic state in the Tractatus Politicus. One camp reads democracy as genuinely deliberative: citizens must converge on shared rational insights—common notions—so that the council expresses the multitudo as a single collective agent. The opposing camp reads it as institutionally aggregative: democracy coordinates individual powers through procedural rules without requiring actual reasoning-convergence, which makes persistent disagreement structurally inevitable rather than irrational.

For years neither side has moved; both invoke the same chapters and the same passages from the Ethics on adequate knowledge. I want to know whether this is genuinely a disagreement about Spinoza's text, or whether something else is doing the work. Specifically, I have a hypothesis: that the two sides are operating with different understandings of common notions—either as epistemic conditions that dissolve disagreement, or as practical alignments of conatus that leave disagreement intact—and that this collapses a metaphysical question (can the state be a genuine individual?) into a normative one (what does democratic legitimacy require?).

Please diagnose the structure of the stall. Is it verbal, substantive but masked by exegesis, or are the sides answering different questions—about collective agency versus institutional design—under the single heading of Spinoza's democracy? And given your diagnosis, what would count as actual progress: a formal distinction internal to Spinoza's metaphysics, an argument that the dispute imports anachronistic deliberative ideals, or should we conclude that the impasse is unavoidable?
