Construct a decision-tree taxonomy, for use in a philosophy-of-archaeology seminar, that sorts epistemic positions on what can be inferred about collective beliefs and social institutions from material assemblages alone. The tree should start from a shared commitment—"We possess a well-documented archaeological context containing both institutional artifacts (objects whose form is governed by explicit rules, such as standardized weights, sealed administrative tablets, or stamped legal tokens) and behavioral residues (patterned accumulations generated by repeated practice without codified regulation, such as domestic pottery distributions, butchery marks, or settlement-layout drift)"—and should ask the student to answer each branch as a concrete yes/no or either/or claim about inference, not by naming a school of thought.

Build the tree from the logical space first: each internal node must be one specific diagnostic question on which the positions genuinely diverge, and the leaves must be the named or unnamed positions that result from that combination of answers. You must include the following positions somewhere in the tree: a direct mentalist/representationalist view that institutional artifacts transparently encode collective beliefs; a functional-structuralist view that permits institutional reconstruction but treats belief-attribution as unrecoverable or epiphenomenal; a semiotic-structuralist view that believes beliefs are recoverable only through decoded symbolic conventions embedded in artifacts; a skeptical underdeterminationist view that holds material culture never uniquely fixes either institution or belief; and a reflexive-critical view that all such inferences necessarily project modern analytical categories onto the past. Where the logical space produces a combination that no known position occupies, label the leaf "unnamed" and diagnose in one sentence whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

The branch questions must address, at minimum: whether institutional artifacts license belief-inference without textual corroboration; whether behavioral residues can support any belief-attribution at all; whether symbolic or iconic content in artifacts requires reconstructed shared interpretive conventions before institutional meaning can be assigned; whether the absence of expected institutional artifacts in an otherwise rich context counts as negative evidence for the non-existence of that institution; and whether distinguishing "institutional artifacts" from "behavioral residues" is itself a stable analytical move or a modern projection. Phrase every node as a question answerable without knowing which interpretation it leads to.

The instrument should contain six to eight branch nodes and no more than seven leaves. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate—if two positions converge on every diagnostic question you have included, state that convergence rather than inventing a spurious distinction. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation prompts, or requests for named scholars' works; the model answering this should be able to construct the taxonomy from conceptual reasoning alone. Keep the framing formal and suitable for a graduate-level handout.
