I am presenting the central argument of my dissertation chapter next Tuesday at an interdisciplinary workshop on authoritarian transitions and historical sociology. The audience will include structuralist historians of interwar Europe who treat leadership as an epiphenomenon of crisis conditions; biographers and intellectual historians convinced that Hitler's particular psychology and racial eschatology were causally decisive; comparative political scientists studying democratic backsliding who will ask whether the argument travels; and several graduate students familiar with macrohistorical modeling who are skeptical of single-case counterfactuals. My thesis is a qualified structuralist one: the collapse of Weimar's presidential-parliamentary architecture, combined with the economic crisis after 1929, made some form of authoritarian nationalist consolidation highly probable by 1934 regardless of who led the NSDAP. However, I argue that the specific trajectory—totalitarian rather than conservative-military, committed to continental expansion and biologistic genocide, and characterized by the rapid bureaucratic routinization of initially idiosyncratic directives—depended on Hitler's unique capacity to bridge the institutional gap between the traditional conservative elite and the radical völkisch mass movement, and on the administrative state's subsequent amplification of his personal priorities after the Enabling Act. Include the one objection—particularly from the biographers—that I am hoping nobody raises, and tell me how to defend my weakest point rather than hoping they miss it.
