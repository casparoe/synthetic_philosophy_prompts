For a graduate seminar on collective rationality (14 students, 80 minutes, no prerequisites beyond basic logic), design a live structured deliberation that makes participants experience the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation without first being told what it is. Stage the group as an advisory board for the fictional municipality of Veldt, which operates under a charter of nested competences: lower boards have binding authority over sub-premises, but the final ordinance requires synthesis by the full board. The decision at hand is whether to adopt a climate-adaptation zoning ordinance. Assign each student one of four reasoning roles—empirical assessor, normative assessor, procedural assessor, or synthesis chair—and hand out hidden aggregation-rule cards specifying premise-based majority, conclusion-based majority, or distance-minimizing resolution. Provide these rules in writing but do not name the theoretical distinctions they represent. Timeline: 10 minutes of silent individual judgment on three premises (projected climate risk exceeds a defined threshold; the burden falls disproportionately on one district; the municipality possesses legal authority to zone) and on the final ordinance itself; 20 minutes of role-restricted small-group aggregation, during which students may cite only judgments permitted by their assigned role; 10 minutes of full-board voting that produces a collective statement; and 15 minutes in which the board must issue a binding collective justification that cites specific reasons. Design the payoff structure around deliberative credibility points: each student earns points if the judgments tied to their reasoning role appear in the final collective justification, regardless of whether the ordinance is adopted, so strategic reasoning is directed at representation rather than outcome. Arrange the premise judgments and role constraints so that, under at least one common aggregation rule, the board’s majority judgments on the premises support adoption of the ordinance while its direct vote on the ordinance rejects it, and so that switching to the distance-minimizing rule eliminates the contradiction but forces the board to abandon one of its strongly held premise judgments. Include explicit procedural rules that prevent the synthesis chair from overriding other roles. In the debrief, map each stage onto the formal structure of judgment aggregation—specifically the tension between premise-based and conclusion-based procedures, and the cost of distance-minimizing resolution—without naming specific authors or requiring citation. Be explicit about what will predictably fail the first time the activity is run, such as students conflating strategic voting with logical inconsistency or the synthesis chair dominating the room despite the rules, and state how to correct each failure. Finally, state honestly which feature of the discursive dilemma—the logical impossibility of an aggregation procedure that is simultaneously collectively rational, fully responsive to every individual judgment, and neutral among voters—cannot be reproduced within the simulation and must be supplied by the facilitator as an explicit theorem after the participants have lived through the contradiction.
