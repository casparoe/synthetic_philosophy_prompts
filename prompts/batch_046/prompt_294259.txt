I am preparing the moderator’s brief for a colloquium interview with a historian of ideas—let us call her the proponent of “substrate modalism”—who defends a specific ontological reading of Fernand Braudel’s concept of the longue durée. On her view, geographical and climatic conditions are not merely background constraints upon historical events but constitute slow-causal substrates that carry modal weight: they render certain long-range trajectories counterfactually stable without thereby reducing history to environmental determinism. She insists that this preserves Braudel’s anti-determinism while giving his tripartite division of historical time—geography, social structures, events—a genuinely metaphysical rather than merely methodological footing.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, written in British English, that press this position at its weakest points rather than asking for exposition. I want at least one question that forces her to distinguish modal inertia from causal determinism, including a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that modal weight is not causal efficacy but a form of structural resilience; at least one question on the epistemic problem of identifying which geographical or climatic features possess this modal weight without falling into post-hoc narrative construction, with a follow-up anticipating her appeal to comparative longue-durée case studies; at least one question on the status of rapid, discontinuous events—revolutions, pandemics, technological ruptures—within a framework that privileges slow structures, and whether such events genuinely disrupt the substrate or merely expose its hidden plasticity, plus a follow-up; at least one question on whether individual agency and biographical contingency are reduced to epiphenomenal noise, with a follow-up addressing the probable response that agency operates only within the interstices left by substrate conditions; and a concluding question asking what kind of evidence—historical, climatological, or conceptual—could in principle count against the substrate-modalist interpretation rather than being absorbed as a further specification of the substrate’s flexibility.

The tone should be that of a philosophy professor chairing a rigorous Q&A: precise, unsparing, but not hostile. Avoid generic biographical or methodological preamble; every question should turn on the actual content of her Braudelian position.
