I'm taking a course on constitutional theory and we got into this weird thing about how some courts practice what my prof called 'counterfactual deference' — they don't just ask if a law violates rights, they ask whether striking it down would actually improve things or if the legislature would just replace it with something worse. There's this implicit rule in some constitutional review systems, at least in parts of Europe and Latin America, where a court will only invalidate a statute if they can show the legislature wouldn't pass an equally harmful substitute, and they'll uphold questionable laws if they think judicial intervention is counterproductively replaced.

What view of democratic legitimacy, or of judicial authority, or maybe of harm and causation, does that practice presuppose? Does it assume judges can read the counterfactual will of elected bodies? And is there an implicit epistomology here about predicting legislative behavior, or maybe a hidden theory of institutional competence? I want to reconstruct the implicit philosophy behind it, check whether its parts are actually consistent, and figure out what courtroom or constitutional practice would have to change if we replaced it with a straightforward rights-based view where courts just ask whether the law violates rights without caring about the legislative replacement scenario. Also, my friend thinks this is just hidden consequentialism smuggled into constitutional law — is that right, or is it doing something different?
