I'm running a philosophy workshop — about 18 sharp grad students and undergrads, one 75-minute session, no lecture upfront. The idea is evidential cooperation in large worlds: the weird pull to cooperate with people you can never causally reach, just because your choice gives you evidence about what your copies do elsewhere. I want them to feel that pull as a puzzle in their gut, not as a theory they memorize.

Design a structured game. Split them into four "worlds" of four or five people each, seated in seperate clusters so they feel like isolated units. Give each world an envelope with a hidden correlation parameter — they dont see the others' values. Each student privately picks: Cooperate (costs 2 points, but raises a global threshold) or Defect (keep the 2, but lower it). The payoff table is arranged so that if copies across worlds mostly cooperate, everyone gets 10; if not, they get 1. Here's the niche twist I want injected: after round one, reveal that two worlds were actually "epistemic twins" — identical hidden parameters — but nobody knew which pair. So some people just made choices that were literally predictive of strangers across the room. Do not explain the jargon yet; let them argue about whether that correlation matters.

Round two: same choice, but now they know twin pairs exist, just not which world is theirs. Keep the stakes small — maybe candy or just printed "status tokens" — but make the payoff scheme depend on aggregate behavior they can only infer.

Then a debrief: map the moment they hesitated onto the evidential link. Be honest about what the activity cant reproduce — the actual cosmological large-world assumption, for one, and the missing payoff feedback from real copies. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (probably people confuse this with regular altruism, or the hidden parameters feel like cheating) and how to fix it without killing the surprise.
