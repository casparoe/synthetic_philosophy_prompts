You are an expert in philosophy of science and formal epistemology, with particular depth in the metaphysics of chance, rational belief, and the structure of admissible evidence. Before you begin writing, take a few minutes to brainstorm the core distinctions between objective chance, subjective credence, evidential admissibility, and meta-level uncertainty about statistical models. Then think carefully about the specific tension described below.

Write an essay addressing the following controversy. The Principal Principle demands that a rational agent align her credence in a particular event with her belief about its objective chance, conditional on admissible evidence. Now consider a scenario—call it the Autonomy Challenge—in which an agent possesses specialized, domain-specific expertise indicating that the standard statistical model used to derive the published objective-chance value is systematically unreliable for events of exactly this kind, even though her expertise does not bear directly on whether the specific event occurs. Because her skepticism targets the chance-estimation procedure itself rather than the outcome, it is arguably inadmissible under standard formulations of the Principal Principle. Should she nonetheless defer to the published chance value? Or does rational autonomy permit—perhaps require—her to adjust her credence downward in proportion to her distrust of the model?

Weigh the main arguments on both sides with precision. On one hand, defend the view that unconditional deference remains mandatory: allowing procedural skepticism to undermine chance-deference risks collapsing the normative distinction between objective physical probabilities and subjective opinion, and it opens the door to arbitrary credence-manipulation. On the other hand, defend the view that strict deference is epistemically servile: a rational agent should integrate all relevant expertise, including justified meta-level doubts about the reliability of the framework that produced the chance assignment, rather than treating published probabilities as epistemic black boxes. Reach and justify a clear conclusion—whether that is an unqualified defense of deference, a qualified rejection, or a nuanced middle position (for instance, that deference is mandatory only when the agent lacks independent, domain-specific justification for distrusting the model). Your essay should be rigorously structured, conceptually sharp, and should not end on a non-committal note.
