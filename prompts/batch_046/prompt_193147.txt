Next month I present at a logic and foundations seminar my view that the Church-Turing thesis should be read not as an empirical claim about nature (the Physical Church-Turing Thesis) nor as a purely mathematical equivalence result, but as an epistemic closure condition on the concept of effective calculability: it asserts that any procedure we can recognize, within our present conceptual scheme, as computing a total function is already capturable by a Turing machine, so that hypercomputational scenarios—Malament-Hogarth spacetimes, analog networks with real-valued weights—challenge ontological possibility without touching epistemic recognizability. I will argue that treating the thesis as falsifiable physics is a category error and that its non-empirical status is analogous to a principle of reflective equilibrium in conceptual analysis, not to a conventionalist immunization strategy. The audience includes mathematical logicians for whom the thesis is a trivial consequence of formal-model equivalence; a philosopher of physics committed to the Physical Church-Turing Thesis; a computer scientist who treats it as an engineering tractability heuristic; and a graduate student in analog computation who suspects I am rendering a scientific claim immune to counterexample. Give me the objections in the order they will likely come, phrased as each party would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should defend without concession, the reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and—crucially—the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook, including the charge that my framing makes the thesis vacuously unfalsifiable in precisely the Popperian sense I claim to avoid.
