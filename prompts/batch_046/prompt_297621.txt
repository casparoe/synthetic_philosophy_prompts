Consider a scholar of comparative moral sociology—call her the evaluator—who has offered explicit normative judgments about societies organized around three frameworks she distinguishes: honor cultures, in which status is external, tied to reputation, and sustained by proportional retaliation; dignity cultures, in which status is internal and protected by universal institutional rules that disregard rank; and victimhood cultures, in which status derives from demonstrated harm and appeals to authoritative third parties.

Her recorded verdicts are these. First, she holds that a pure honor culture lacking safeguards for the vulnerable is morally inferior to a well-ordered dignity culture, but she denies that honor cultures are necessarily inferior, noting that codes integrating communal accountability with proportionality—including those that permit sanctuary or redress for the defeated—can preserve dignity without abandoning reputation. Second, she judges dignity cultures superior only when they do not suppress legitimate communal obligations; where institutional equality collapses into atomistic indifference, she rates them deficient. Third, she regards victimhood cultures as generally the least defensible of the three, arguing that they incentivize grievance inflation and erode the distinction between injury and offense, though she allows a transitional exception for groups emerging from sustained systemic degradation. Fourth, she endorsed a hypothetical hybrid she termed a “reputational republic,” combining dignity-based legal equality with honor-based communal accountability. Fifth, she rejected a framework that merged victimhood-based resource allocation with dignity-based procedural rights, on the grounds that the two logics corrupt each other when made simultaneously visible. Sixth, she maintained that a culture treating all three as context-dependent registers—honor among kin, dignity in commerce, victimhood restricted to transitional justice—achieves coherence but not automatic moral superiority over a pure dignity culture.

The new case is a proposed post-conflict social contract she calls the “Covenant of Silent Debt.” Under its terms, individuals accrue reputational debt through broken promises (honor logic), but such debt may be resolved only through institutional arbitration that disregards social rank (dignity logic). At the same time, members of groups recognized as having suffered historical collective harm receive permanent structural preference in arbitration outcomes (victimhood logic). Crucially, the framework prohibits public displays of grievance; victim status is not a source of social prestige but an invisible parameter embedded in institutional remedy.

Predict the evaluator’s verdict: will she judge this Covenant morally superior to a pure dignity culture, inferior, or incommensurable with it? Identify at least two competing principles—perhaps a principle of non-corruption between visible logics, a principle allowing invisible structural preference, and a principle of hybrid coherence—that are jointly consistent with her six prior verdicts. For each principle, state what it predicts about the Covenant. Then offer your best-guess prediction, with the reasoning that favors it, and identify which of her prior judgments serves as the closest analog. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—distinct in structure from the Covenant—that would distinguish the competing principles from one another.
