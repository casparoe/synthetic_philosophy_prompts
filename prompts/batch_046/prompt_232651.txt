I'm preparing my position paper for a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and after a long discussion with colleagues about how algorithmic risk-assessment tools reshape opportunity, I've tried to map out exactly where I stand on luck egalitarianism. Before I present, I want an audit of whether these commitments hang together.

Here is my profile: (1) I accept the standard luck-egalitarian distinction—inequalities arising from brute luck are unjust, while those arising from option luck are not, at least in principle. (2) I believe that what counts as option luck must be indexed to an agent's actual epistemic situation rather than to some idealized rational baseline; if someone makes a risky choice based on systematically distorted information, the resulting disadvantage is closer to brute luck. (3) I hold that natural talents and social origins are morally arbitrary and should be pooled or equalized, but I also think people can bear genuine responsibility for skills they cultivate through sustained effort, even when the underlying capacity for that effort is partly socially distributed. (4) I have become convinced that institutional predictive asymmetries—where forecasting systems systematically misrepresent risk and opportunity for certain groups—create a form of predictive injustice that collapses the brute/option boundary in practice, yet I still want to defend that boundary as normatively fundamental. (5) I think justice requires neutralizing brute-luck effects only up to the point of equal opportunity, not equal outcomes. (6) Finally, I maintain that if two agents face identical objective risks but one has been misled by institutional forecasting, we should treat the disadvantaged agent's choice as partly brute-luck-affected and therefore partly compensable, without extending that compensation all the way to full outcome-equality.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. For each real conflict, rank its severity, say exactly what has to give, and identify the cheapest revision. I particularly want to know whether (3) and (4) can coexist, or whether accepting predictive injustice undermines my willingness to hold people responsible for cultivated effort. Also tell me which single revision—giving up (2), (3), (4), or (5)—would clean up the most inconsistencies at once, and whether there is an apparent tension (for instance, between pooling talents and honoring cultivated effort) that actually dissolves on inspection. I am not asking for a lecture on luck egalitarianism in general; I want a consistency check on this specific package.
