I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of history preparing for an interdisciplinary workshop where I will defend a position I call stratified hermeneutic overdetermination. I argue that in complex events—say, the rapid collapse of a long-standing regime—causal overdetermination, in which multiple sufficient causes converge, captures only half of the explanatory problem. The other half is what I term interpretive superposition: the claim that the event’s historical significance is not merely epistemically underdetermined by the causal facts, but ontologically ambiguous in a manner structurally analogous to a Gestalt shift, so that competing master-narratives (structuralist, intentionalist, and radical-contingency accounts) do not simply select different causal subsets but invoke incommensurable ontologies of agency and institutional persistence. The audience will include analytic philosophers of history who accept standard multi-causal models but reject any ontological pluralism about events, plus two social historians who will treat this framing as philosophical mystification of ambiguities that rigorous archival and quantitative work can in principle resolve. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would actually use, and for each specify what I should concede immediately, what I must hold without retreat, a concise reply calibrated to about two minutes, and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Be sure to address the charge that my view makes archival evidence epistemically secondary, and include the single question I am hoping nobody asks—most likely the objection that interpretive superposition collapses into a form of historical relativism that renders causal and documentary inquiry irrelevant—along with a prepared answer that does not simply retreat to pure causal reductionism.
