I've been working through my position on consciousness for a graduate seminar and want an audit before I present it. My view is structured around five commitments that I think hang together, but I suspect there is hidden friction. First, I treat the standard framing of the meta-problem as correct: the puzzle is not consciousness itself but explaining why we judge there to be a hard problem, and this explanatory task is genuine rather than dismissible. Second, I endorse a phenomenal concept strategy to solve that meta-problem—phenomenal concepts present experience in a direct, non-functional mode that creates conceptual distance from physical descriptions without denying physical identity. Third, I also accept evolutionary debunking arguments about metaphysical intuitions generally, holding that our gap-intuitions are likely byproducts of social-cognitive selection pressures rather than reliable trackers of ontological structure. Fourth, I reject illusionism and grant first-person phenomenal reports default epistemic authority regarding the existence and qualitative character of experience. Fifth, I subscribe to a Russellian monist physicalism: phenomenal properties are identical to the intrinsic physical properties of matter, but this identity is not conceptually transparent and does not dissolve the explanatory gap by analysis alone.

The tension I feel is between my second and third commitments: the phenomenal concept strategy seems to presuppose that our gap-generating concepts are epistemically respectable enough to motivate the strategy, yet the debunking argument treats them as evolutionarily suspect. I also worry that my fourth commitment conflicts with the third, since default authority for first-person reports seems hard to reconcile with treating consciousness-related metaphysical judgments as debunked artifacts. At the same time, my fifth commitment looks like it should eliminate the need for the meta-problem altogether—if phenomenal and physical are identical—yet I maintain in my first commitment that there remains a real explanatory task. So I want to know: is this package consistent? Specifically, can I distinguish first-order phenomenal access, which retains authority, from second-order metaphysical judgments about the hard problem, which are debunked, thereby saving both (2) and (3)? Or does the phenomenal concept strategy itself depend on taking second-order judgments seriously in a way that debunking undermines? And if revision is unavoidable, which commitment is the least costly to weaken or drop—the debunking move, the phenomenal concept strategy, or the claim that the meta-problem is genuinely explanatory? Please order the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether it is real or merely apparent, and specify what exactly has to give and at what philosophical cost.
