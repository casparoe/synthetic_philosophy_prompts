In our philosophy programme we've been stuck for months on Margalit versus Rieff -- the duty to remember atrocity versus the ethics of forgetting for peace. Everyone agrees the events happened, everyone agrees suffering is bad, yet the Margalit camp and the Rieff camp haven't moved an inch in over a year. The Margalit people say remembering is anchored in thick relations and that to forget is to betray solidarity with victims; the Rieff people say collective remembrance becomes a political weapon that freezes societies in resentment and that forgetting is a precondition for reconciliation. My hunch is that the stall isn't about history at all but about whether moral obligations can be inherited across generations without consent -- one side treats memory as a deontic status, the other treats it as a therapeutic technology. Is that right? Or is it a verbal dispute about what remembering means (mnemonic retention versus commemorative practice), or are they answering different questions -- one about the ethics of identity, one about political psychology? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey, and an honest answer about whether anything -- a distinction, an empirical study, a change of framing -- could actualy shift it.
