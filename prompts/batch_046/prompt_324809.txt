I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or mathematical logic, and I have been working through how intuitionistic constructivists evaluate arguments that rely on non-constructive steps. From a series of examples I have catalogued—drawn from lecture notes and published discussions—one mathematician in the intuitionistic tradition has made the following four rulings.

First, they rejected a proof that applies the law of excluded middle to establish the existence of a root for an arbitrary continuous real-valued function, on the grounds that equality between real values is not algorithmically decidable and no explicit root-finding procedure is supplied. Second, they accepted a proof that uses double-negation elimination to show that every integer possesses a prime divisor, observing that equality and divisibility are decidable over the integers, so the classical step is eliminable in that discrete setting. Third, they rejected a proof that invokes the axiom of choice to select an element from each member of an infinite family of non-empty open intervals of real numbers, even when each interval is specified by explicit computable endpoints, because no uniform computable selection rule accompanies the argument. Fourth, they accepted a proof that selects the least element satisfying a decidable predicate over the integers via an explicit definable formula, without appealing to choice, stating that a direct definable witness is sufficient.

These four verdicts appear jointly consistent with more than one underlying principle. One candidate principle is domain-sensitive: non-constructive reasoning is permissible only over discrete domains where the relevant predicates are algorithmically decidable. Another is witness-sensitive: what matters is not the domain itself but whether the proof either supplies, or can be reconstructed to supply, an explicit algorithmic or formula-defined witness for the claimed existence.

Now consider a new case. A proof in an optimization setting establishes that a gradient-descent procedure applied to a computable Lipschitz function over the reals either reaches a point where the gradient norm falls below a computable threshold epsilon, or there exists a computable descent direction that strictly improves the current point. The argument employs the law of excluded middle applied to the statement “the gradient norm is below epsilon,” which is not decidable for arbitrary real inputs, but it pairs this with an explicit computable procedure for generating the descent direction in the non-termination branch and with a computable stopping criterion for the termination branch.

If this mathematician wishes to remain consistent with the four precedents above, what verdict should they reach on this optimization proof? Which of the four earlier cases is the closest analog, and why? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what further principle would they be adopting with each possible choice—one aligning with the domain-sensitive reading and one aligning with the witness-sensitive reading? Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—identifying its domain, the non-constructive step involved, and whether an explicit witness is present or absent—that would most clearly distinguish the domain-sensitive principle from the witness-sensitive principle.
