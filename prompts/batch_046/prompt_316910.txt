In protocol engineering I keep encountering a two-agent recursive decision loop that behaves like the centipede game: at each round either agent can halt the process and take a larger payoff, but if both keep passing, the final shared reward is larger. The standard analysis predicts immediate termination—each agent reasons backward from the end and stops at the first move—yet in actual implementations these loops often run for many rounds. I understand the usual bounded-rationality explanations, but I have noticed a different pattern. Consider a distributed system where Agent A can observe Agent B's previous actions perfectly, yet cannot observe B's internal reasoning state—whether B actually performed the backward-induction calculation or merely followed a heuristic. Because of this architectural opacity, A can never be fully certain that B knows that A knows that B is rational, so the chain of common knowledge breaks not because anyone is irrational, but because the design makes reasoning states structurally unobservable. I have seen this in consensus protocols and in automated negotiation frameworks. Is there an established term for this specific epistemic failure—where backward induction collapses not from limited cognition but from the structural unobservability of reasoning? How is it precisely distinguished from the standard paradox and from bounded-rationality critiques? If it lacks a distinct name, what boundary conditions would separate it from the general case?
