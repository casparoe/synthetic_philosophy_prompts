The exam question was as follows: Explain Ruth Chang's distinction between hard choices and choices made under conditions of simple indifference. Contrast her position with Isaiah Berlin's value pluralism, specifically regarding whether rational resolution is always available when fundamental values come into conflict. Then apply both frameworks to this institutional case. A city health board has a fixed budget that can fund exactly one of two programs. Program A establishes a mobile pediatric oncology unit that will provide direct treatment to approximately two hundred identifiable children over a five-year period. Program B modernizes the municipal sewer infrastructure, which epidemiological models project will prevent roughly eighteen hundred statistical deaths from waterborne illness across a fifteen-year horizon, but which will produce no identifiable beneficiaries during the initial funding window. Analyze whether this situation should be understood as a hard choice in Chang's sense, as a tragic conflict in Berlin's sense, as both, or as neither, and defend your classification with explicit reference to the conceptual machinery of each thinker.

Here is the student's answer. Chang argues that in a hard choice, the available options are not better, worse, or exactly equal to one another; rather, they stand in a relation she describes as parity, being on a par with one another without being precisely equivalent. This means that rational comparison is impossible, and any decision is ultimately a matter of arbitrary personal commitment. By contrast, Isaiah Berlin's value pluralism maintains that values are objective and plural, but he allows that rational agents can always construct a hierarchical ordering to resolve conflicts, even if the resolution involves painful trade-offs. Applying these views to the health board: because Program B saves 1,800 lives and Program A saves only 200, there is a clear quantitative metric that makes the two options fully commensurable. Consequently, this is not a hard choice for Chang—there is an unambiguous rational answer in favor of Program B. At the same time, it is a tragic conflict for Berlin, because selecting Program B forces us to abandon the identifiable children, generating an irreparable moral loss that no hierarchical ordering can erase. Therefore, Chang's framework yields a decisive rational prescription without residue, whereas Berlin's framework acknowledges that even correct choices leave behind unavoidable tragedy.
