I serve on the Conceptual Oversight Board for the Comparative Regime Dynamics dataset, an open-index project that codes institutional change across democracies and hybrid regimes for researchers and policy analysts. We are scheduled to vote next quarter on a proposed overhaul to our democratic-backsliding indicator, and I need a formal recommendation memo I can circulate to board members who hold very different priors on this issue. The proposal, drafted by a working group after reviewing our recent country assessments for Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States, would abandon our existing binary backsliding flag in favor of a two-axis taxonomy: one axis tracking formal institutional replacement—explicit legal or constitutional changes that reduce veto players or alter election rules—and a separate axis tracking what the working group terms normative hollowing, defined as the erosion of democratic conventions and horizontal-accountability practices in the absence of immediate formal revision. The motivation is that our current binary code forces reviewers to treat judicial capture in Poland and presidential-system overhaul in Turkey as conceptually identical to the norm erosion and certification disputes observed in recent U.S. politics, which several board members—including a senior colleague who argues we are drifting toward a partisan labeling instrument—claim collapses distinctions that matter for causal inference and comparative theory. On the other side, a faction led by institutional sociologists on the board insists that separating formal and informal mechanisms privileges an outdated legal-formalist model of democracy and ignores how deconsolidation actually unfolds in mature regimes where rules remain on paper but are hollowed out in practice.

The memo should recommend either adopting the two-axis framework with explicit operationalization criteria—specifying, for example, how we would score a country where courts remain structurally independent but are systematically packed with loyalists, versus one where court structures are formally abolished—or retaining the binary flag with stricter boundary conditions that explicitly exclude informal norm erosion from the backsliding category. In either case, the recommendation must include an honest treatment of the strongest objection on the losing side: if you recommend the two-axis model, you should address the risk that it fragments the concept into unmeasurable dimensions and invites motivated reasoning in coding; if you recommend retaining the binary flag, you must address the charge that this formalist boundary protects established democracies from scrutiny and renders the dataset blind to the very mechanisms—executive aggrandizement through convention-breaking rather than rule-changing—that characterize contemporary democratic erosion in the U.S. and comparable cases. The document should be written for an institutional audience that can act on it—clear bottom line, actionable voting language, and reasoning that does not depend on external citations, bibliographies, or access to literature during the meeting—while maintaining the kind of epistemic humility and steel-manning of opposing views that this board expects from its internal reports. Please treat the philosophical stakes seriously: whether backsliding is a unified phenomenon or a family of mechanisms is not merely a measurement question, but determines who gets to decide what counts as democratic failure and whether our index serves as a genuine analytical tool or becomes an extension of partisan discourse.
