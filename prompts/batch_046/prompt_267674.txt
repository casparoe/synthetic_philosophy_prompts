I host a small philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a scholar defending what she calls the managed-illusion reading of Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness. Her basic claim is that du Châtelet doesn't just think study makes you happy—she thinks rigorous study, espescially in physics and metaphysics, actually trains you to maintain specific false beliefs—like that romantic love is freely chosen, or that your personal projects have cosmic weight—without letting those beliefs collapse into cynicism.

I'd like eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond 'what did du Châtelet think about happiness?' I want at least one pressing the tension between her determinist/Newtonian physics and her practical ethics of self-cultivation, one on whether her catalog of necessary illusions is even internally consistent—since she says illusions about love are vital but also warns that studying anatomy reveals love's physical basis—and one on why she frames so much of the advice specifically for women if the argument is just about rational agents in general. For each of those three, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give: probably that determinism and moral psychology operate at different registers, that consistency misses the point because illusions are pragmatically regulated rather than believed, and that women's social confinement makes illusion-management more urgent, not different in kind. Make the follow-ups actually dig in, not just 'interesting, tell me more.'

Also include one question asking what kind of archival evidence—say, from her correspondance with Voltaire or her annotations on the Principia—would actually count against her reading, since she presents it as historically grounded. And maybe one on whether du Châtelet's own biography (her relationship with Voltaire, her death after childbirth) undermines the optimistic tone of the Discourse, or whether that's exactly the kind of sentimental misreading she warns against.
