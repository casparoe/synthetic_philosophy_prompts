I'm assembling a comparative framework for naturalistic theories of religion—evolutionary by-product accounts, costly-signaling models, and cognitive-attraction frameworks—after noticing that critics and defenders alike tend to test them against isolated case studies rather than a shared standard of adequacy. Before I commit to one, I want the full specification: write the desiderata that any theory treating religion as a natural phenomenon must meet, with the motivating case or conceptual tension behind each.

The list should cover which phenomena it must explain—such as the cross-cultural recurrence of ritualized suffering, the persistence of metaphysical commitment in secularized populations through institutional ritual rather than doctrinal assent, and the stability of certain ritual structures despite high doctrinal variability across cultures—along with which distinctions it must preserve: between religious belief and ordinary factual belief, between adaptive function and evolutionary by-product, and between collective ritual efficacy and individual cognitive comfort. It should also state the results from evolutionary biology, cognitive psychology, and anthropology that any serious account must accommodate without reducing religion to mere error or mere social glue. Please flag any pair of desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable: for instance, whether explaining religion's rapid cultural transmission can coexist with explaining its high-cost, non-instrumental practices, or whether a strictly adaptivist account can survive the prevalence of intense religious commitment among high-cognitive-capacity individuals who otherwise exhibit low credulity toward non-empirical claims. Indicate which requirements are non-negotiable for a naturalistic theory and which are preferences of particular camps—say, strict gene-culture coevolutionists versus cognitive scientists of religion.

I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or named reading guide; this should be constructible without web access. If you briefly contrast how a by-product view, a signaling model, and a cultural-epidemiological approach fare against these criteria, do so only to illustrate where each collapses a distinction the list demands, not to deliver a final scorecard.
