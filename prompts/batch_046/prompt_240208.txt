I serve as the student representative on my school's Disciplinary Appeals and Policy Board, and we must issue a written recommendation before next Wednesday's session on an unusual appeal. A junior was suspended for thirty days after a laboratory fire; he admits he left an open flame near combustible material but argues the suspension should be overturned because our code of conduct wrongly attributes causation to him. Specifically, he invokes Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism: since God alone is the true and direct cause of every event, and human actions are only occasional conditions without any necessary connection to what follows, assigning him causal responsibility for the fire is metaphysically mistaken. He asks the board not merely to reduce the penalty, but to reject the causal framework in our disciplinary language altogether. Some board members view this as a category error—disciplinary rules concern practical accountability, not metaphysical causation—while others worry that dismissing the argument outright fails to take a serious philosophical objection to institutional justice. I need a formal memo recommending whether we should uphold the suspension with the existing reasoning, uphold it with a revised non-causal justification, or overturn it in light of the occasionalist challenge, together with a clear statement of what the school should do by the end of the semester if our recommendation is adopted. The memo should treat the occasionalist denial of necessary causal connection with intellectual seriousness—explaining why it might seem to undermine attributions of responsibility—while also giving an honest and substantial account of the strongest reason for maintaining that practical institutional judgment must operate independently of metaphysical theories about divine causation.
