Write an essay on whether G. E. Moore’s proof of an external world succeeds or instead smuggles in a hidden premise about how demonstrative identification works. Frame the controversy around a specific conceptual pressure point: call it the Principle of Demonstrative Scaling, according to which local, presentational certainty about a particular (say, that one is facing a hand) automatically licenses a global ontological conclusion (that there exists an object entirely outside the mind). Weigh the two main sides. On one side, argue that Moore correctly isolates a form of non-inferential warrant—direct demonstrative entitlement—that skeptical scenarios cannot undermine without undermining themselves, and that no illicit scaling is needed because recognizing a particular just is recognizing something external. On the other side, argue that the demonstrative “here” already carries spatial and existential presuppositions, so Moore’s inference is not a bare identification but a loaded move; once we separate semantic presupposition from epistemic entitlement, the proof looks circular in a way that the familiar “Moorean shift” defense does not dissolve, particularly if we allow that a skeptic can accept the local phenomenology while denying the ontological framing. Reach and defend a precise verdict—for example, that the proof works as a targeted rebuttal to Cartesian doubt about present experience but fails as a general argument for external objects unless one independently accepts the scaling principle, which itself requires defense. Throughout the essay, state how confident you are in each major claim, and explicitly identify the single point about which you are least sure. End with exactly this line: Verdict: [your position in one concise phrase].
