Compare moral particularism and moral generalism, not through the standard debate over context-sensitive reasons, but through the problem of genuine moral learning: cases in which an agent comes to recognize a morally significant feature that she previously missed and that does not seem derivable from any principle she already held. Does particularism accommodate such learning only by abandoning principled reasoning altogether, or can it be recast as a refinement of moral perception? Can generalism explain it as the discovery of implicit principles or as theory revision, without collapsing into particularism? In answering, draw on Iris Murdoch's idea of moral attention—the claim that moral progress often involves a shift in how one sees a situation rather than in which rules one applies—and ask whether her picture is ultimately compatible with either camp or demands a third stance. Ground the comparison in a concrete scenario, such as a caregiver gradually recognizing the moral weight of a non-verbal patient's subtle discomfort, and conclude by saying which view better captures the phenomenology of that recognition and whether either can fully absorb Murdoch's insight.
