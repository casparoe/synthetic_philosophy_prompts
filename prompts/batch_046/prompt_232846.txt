I sit on the university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and we have reached an impasse over Protocol 2024-NEU-117, a three-year optogenetics grant that proposes to use roughly forty million Drosophila melanogaster in automated high-throughput assays. The investigators seek a categorical exemption from the welfare-review thresholds that apply to vertebrates, arguing that the harm to any individual fly is negligible and that the prior probability of phenomenal consciousness in a single insect is low—on the order of one in a thousand—so the aggregate expected moral cost is trivial. A minority of committee members have pressed the scale argument: with forty million subjects, even that low probability implies an expected population of tens of thousands of sentient beings, which, if taken seriously, would place the study's moral footprint in the same range as a large mammalian protocol. The researchers respond not by disputing the arithmetic, but by proposing an institutional decision rule they call the expected-sentience threshold: protections should be triggered only if the expected number of conscious subjects exceeds an administratively feasible cutoff, and they maintain that their protocol falls well below any defensible cutoff.

The committee is split between those who see the scale argument as a speculative philosophical distraction that should not obstruct valuable neuroscience, and those who worry that discounting aggregation systematically privileges small, numerous beings precisely because they are easy to ignore at scale. I need you to draft our formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the principal investigators and filed with the institutional review office. The memo must reach a clear, actionable bottom line that the researchers could actually respond to, for example by revising protocols, accepting monitoring conditions, or filing an appeal. It should present the reasoning behind the recommendation, including an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and it should treat the philosophical tension as genuinely live rather than settled by fiat. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; the memo should stand entirely on its own reasoning. You may describe arguments structurally rather than attributing them to named authors, and you should avoid inventing specific studies or data points. Write in a formal institutional voice appropriate to an ethics committee document.
