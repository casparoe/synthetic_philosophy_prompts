I've been trying to understand why supererogation and what philosophers sometimes call suberogation—acts that are morally bad yet not strictly forbidden—are not treated as perfect structural analogues. If duty sets a floor below which we must not fall, it also seems to set a ceiling above which we are not compelled to rise. Given that symmetry, shouldn't the region below the floor be as densely populated with gradations of badness as the region above the ceiling is with gradations of goodness? Instead, we seem to treat the lower region as a uniform zone of mere permissibility, while the upper region is filled with genuine moral achievement. This makes me wonder whether the asymmetry reveals something deep about moral reasons themselves: perhaps they have an intrinsic upward orientation, capable of extending past obligation but incapable of extending symmetrically past prohibition. Or is the error in my framing—does the concept of a "moral ceiling" already presuppose that obligation functions like a midpoint rather than a boundary, and if so, does that misunderstanding collapse the distinction between being required and being permitted?
