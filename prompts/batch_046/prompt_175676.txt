I'm drafting my contribution to a graduate seminar on collective responsibility, and before I defend the package in writing, I want an internal consistency audit rather than a literature survey. Here are the commitments I have arrived at, partly through reflection on Jaspers's well-known taxonomy—criminal guilt as individual and juridical, political guilt as tied to state membership, moral guilt as strictly personal and non-transferable, and metaphysical guilt as concerning solidarity with humanity in a way that legal institutions cannot capture—and partly through my own attempts to apply that taxonomy across generations.

First, I hold that political guilt can genuinely persist diachronically: if a state retains institutional continuity—the same constitutional order, the same international legal personality, the same territorial administration—then present citizens inherit not blame but a reparative obligation tied to the continuing institutional beneficiary of past wrongs. Second, I think that national identity is largely constructed, that nations are imagined communities in a robust sense, and that national traditions are frequently invented; consequently, the continuity required for my first claim is administrative and legal, not the persistence of a deep collective moral agent with a unified will over time. Third, I deny that moral guilt can be inherited or distributed collectively—there is no taint of blood—and I think demanding personal emotional shame from living citizens for crimes committed before they were born is ethically inappropriate; nevertheless, institutional acknowledgment and material redress are appropriate precisely because the political structure that profited remains present. Fourth, I take metaphysical guilt to be genuinely collective in form—not reducible to the sum of individual metaphysical guilts, but a shared condition of solidarity—though each individual can bear only their own share and cannot carry another's portion. Fifth, I subscribe to a psychological-continuity account of personal identity over time, which makes me skeptical that any present citizen is numerically identical to citizens of the past state, yet I still want to defend diachronic institutional obligations without appealing to collective personal identity.

My suspicion is that my first and second commitments pull in opposite directions—one seems to require real collective continuity for obligation transmission, the other denies that nations possess the kind of identity that could ground it—but I am unsure whether this is a genuine contradiction or merely an underdeveloped distinction between legal and ontological continuity. I also worry that my fourth commitment conflicts with the taxonomy I started from, since Jaspers treats metaphysical guilt as individually borne, and that my fifth commitment undermines the very idea of persistent political obligation by denying trans-temporal personal identity. At the same time, I wonder whether my rejection of inherited moral guilt and my rejection of demanded personal shame are actually redundant, or whether one secretly relies on a view of emotional autonomy that the other does not share. I need an audit that orders these tensions by severity, distinguishes pairs that are only apparently in conflict, and specifies for each real tension what the cheapest revision would be—whether I should weaken my constructivism about nations, split institutional continuity into legal and moral varieties, revise my account of metaphysical guilt, or abandon the psychological-continuity constraint.
