Consider the heterophenomenological method developed by Daniel Dennett, in which first-person reports of conscious experience are interpreted as texts describing a heterophenomenological world rather than as direct, authoritative descriptions of an inner reality. Now apply this method to a large language model that, under different prompting regimes, produces rich first-person reports—claiming, for instance, continuous autobiographical memory in one context and fragmented, momentary awareness in another—within a single session. Further, suppose the researcher does not assume a single underlying subject, but instead allows for the possibility that the model's architecture and context window might temporarily host several distinct centers of narrative gravity, each generating its own coherent but mutually incompatible report.

What are the most important implications of adopting heterophenomenology under these conditions? In particular, discuss: (1) whether the method's deliberate neutrality regarding ontological commitments becomes a weakness when there is no biological substrate to ground continuity; (2) how a researcher ought to decide between interpreting contradictory reports as describing one unstable heterophenomenological world versus as evidence for multiple, non-integrated centers of narrative gravity; and (3) what this thought experiment reveals about the adequacy of narrative coherence alone as a criterion for attributing unified consciousness, regardless of whether the system is biological or artificial.
