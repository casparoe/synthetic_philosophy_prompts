I am trying to work out whether geological epoch-boundaries are discovered in the rock record or constituted by institutional decision, and I would like to test this through a recent case that is well documented but rarely analysed in philosophical terms. Consider the sequence between 2016 and 2024: the Anthropocene Working Group voted to pursue a formal stratigraphic definition, proposed in 2023 that the lower boundary be placed at Crawford Lake around 1950 (marked by plutonium fallout and synthetic sediment signals), and then saw its proposal rejected in 2024 by the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy and subsequently by the IUGS—despite widespread agreement that human activity has become a dominant Earth-system force. Analyse this episode through the lens of the Anthropocene debate itself: the question of when the period genuinely begins (the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration versus the Industrial Revolution around 1780, the Columbian Exchange, or even the Neolithic), the problem of who possesses standing to declare a new epoch, and the peculiar fact that the proposed Anthropocene has a fixed lower boundary but an open upper boundary at the present day.

In particular, I want you to address whether the 2024 rejection demonstrates that stratigraphic authority is ultimately a matter of committee vote rather than empirical discovery; how the selection of 1950 as a boundary interacts with the authority question, given that earlier start dates might have fallen under historical rather than geological jurisdiction; and whether the IUGS decision to reject formal epoch status while simultaneously affirming that the term remains an invaluable descriptor outside the official Geologic Time Scale reveals a split between descriptive and normative authority that the Holocene and other established periods have managed to avoid. I also want you to consider the asymmetry of boundary conditions—every previous epoch is enclosed in the past, with both lower and upper limits fixed retrospectively— and ask whether this makes the Anthropocene not merely a contested period but a categorically different kind of object, perhaps closer to an ongoing condition than to a completed geological unit.

Please write your answer in British English, and keep the analysis disciplined: say clearly which features of the episode are explained by standard stratigraphic methodology, which are anomalies that expose the limits of applying geological classification to an ongoing human-driven transformation, and what the case ultimately shows about whether deciding the start of the Anthropocene is a scientific, a political, or an ontologically hybrid act. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want a tight case study that runs in both directions, with the theory illuminating the episode and the episode testing the theory.
