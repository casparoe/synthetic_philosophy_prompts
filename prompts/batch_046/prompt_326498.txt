I have been trying to work out an argument about George Berkeley's economic writings—especially his idea that money represents stored labor rather than having intrinsic worth in its metal—combined with his metaphysical claim that things exist only insofar as they are perceived or conceived. I think I have shown that his view makes saving hidden wealth impossible, but the conclusion is absurd, so one of my steps must be wrong.

(1) For Berkeley, nothing can exist or have any feature except as it is perceived or conceived by some mind; if no mind is aware of a coin buried in a vault, then in the relevant sense it is not fully present.
(2) Economic value is a feature or property that a coin can have or lack.
(3) So if a coin is buried and no human mind perceives or conceives it, it lacks economic value entirely.
(4) At the same time, Berkeley treats coined money as a durable store of labor: the work of mining and minting remains embodied in the object itself, available to be used later.
(5) If labor is genuinely embodied in the coin, then the coin retains its economic value even while hidden away.
(6) Therefore Berkeley's account requires that the same buried coin both has no value at all and retains its full value.
(7) So either hidden savings are impossible, or his idealism cannot be applied to economic goods.

I want to keep (1) and (4) if possible, since they seem closest to what Berkeley actually believed. Which premise should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Is the mistake an equivocation between metaphysical presence and economic function—perhaps value is notional rather than real—or does (1) need a qualification about divine perception that saves (3) from being false?
