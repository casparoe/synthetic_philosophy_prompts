I have recently begun working on governance frameworks for adaptive recommendation platforms, and the design problem has made Amartya Sen's liberal paradox personally urgent for me. The paradox shows that minimal liberal rights—specifically, each user's sovereignty over their own information environment—can conflict with Pareto efficiency when preferences are interdependent across a network. In our system, letting every user fully determine their feed parameters prevents any global ranking that improves outcomes for all users by standard engagement metrics, yet overriding individual choice violates a basic liberal constraint.

With this concrete case in mind, I want to know how John Stuart Mill—who died long before both Sen's formalization and algorithmic curation—would likely have reasoned about it. Would he treat the user's minimal right to control their feed as an inviolable domain protected by the harm principle, even when that right blocks collective improvements? Or, recognizing that one user's informational choices reshape the option sets available to others in ways analogous to Sen's interdependent-preference examples, might he have allowed that liberal sovereignty must sometimes yield to avoid systemic harm?

Please do not provide bibliographies or reading lists. I am asking for a conceptual reconstruction of Mill's probable reasoning, not for verified textual citations. Focus on how his commitments to liberty and to the prevention of harm would apply to this specific technological form of the paradox.
