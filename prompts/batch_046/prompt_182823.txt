Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for the debate over why promises bind, intended as a workshop sorting instrument for philosophy graduate students. The tree must classify positions by the answers they give to three concrete diagnostic cases, not by philosopher-names or school-labels, so that a respondent can navigate it without knowing the literature.

Use exactly three binary branching questions, in this order:
(i) If the promisee forgets the promise entirely and never expects fulfillment, does the promisor still have a reason to perform? (This separates mental-state-dependent from mental-state-independent grounds.)
(ii) If the promisee sincerely releases the promisor, can any residual obligation remain to an uninvolved third party who relied on the promise being kept? (This separates purely two-party relational views from act-centered or multi-party views.)
(iii) If the promise was made under mild social pressure without explicit coercion, does its binding force scale with the degree of autonomous authorization the promisor subjectively experienced? (This separates autonomy-threshold views from unconditional or conventionalist views.)

The tree should generate all eight logical combinations. Six must be occupied leaves; two must be left unoccupied. For each unoccupied combination, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Every occupied leaf must carry: (a) a descriptive position-name or the label 'unnamed'; (b) one sentence summarizing its answer-pattern across the three cases; (c) its most awkward commitment—specifically the counterintuitive result it must accept in exactly one of the cases. The six required occupied positions are: a conventionalist or practice-based view; an expectation or reliance-based view; an autonomy or self-legislation view; a relational two-party view; a virtue-character view; and one view that treats promises as generating transferable obligations to the promised act itself, independently of particular persons.

Present the taxonomy as nested branches with the split-condition written at each node, followed by a table of the eight combinations with the six leaves filled as above. Then add a separate section that flags any pairs of positions the tree cannot cleanly separate—views that answer all three cases identically despite differing in metaphysical grounding—and explain why the branching questions fail to distinguish them.

Do not include bibliographies, citations, author lists, reading recommendations, or references to specific texts; the instrument must be fully usable by someone without search access. The audience is philosophically literate but should be able to locate their own view using only the case-answers, without recognizing historical labels. Keep the whole instrument to a size suitable for a single handout: no more than the three branching questions, eight combinations, and the flagged inseparability note.
