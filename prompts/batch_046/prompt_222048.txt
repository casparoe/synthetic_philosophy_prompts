I'm working through the syllabus for a moral-education seminar that assigns each student a year-long study of a single exemplary life—drawn from the Plutarchan tradition of paired biographical narratives or from modern hagiographic biography—and requires reflective essays on virtue and practical judgment. The grading rubric, however, explicitly forbids appeals to the subject's particular character or to the biographer's evaluative language; every normative claim must be justified by universalizable first principles, as though the life-study were merely an illustration of propositions that could be fully grasped without narrative immersion. The institution presents this pairing as coherent: biography supplies the imaginative and motivational context for moral development, while philosophical argument alone provides genuine justification. The tension strikes me as deeper than a mere pedagogical compromise.

What implicit theory of moral learning, moral justification, and the epistemic status of exemplarity does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it strictly entail that narrative understanding is always subordinate to propositional knowledge, or can it accommodate a stronger view—one closer to the Plutarchan conception—according to which the imaginative inhabitation of a particular life is itself a distinctive mode of moral cognition rather than a dispensable pedagogical aid? Reconstruct the philosophy embedded in the design, distinguish what the practice genuinely requires from what it merely tolerates, and specify what structural changes to assessment and curriculum would be necessary if the underlying view were replaced by a theory that treated hagiographic narrative not as illustration but as an independent form of moral understanding.
