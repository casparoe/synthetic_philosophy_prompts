I'm trying to settle my own view on state accommodation of cultural and religious difference, working from gut verdicts on concrete cases rather than from a pre-chosen theory. Here they are.

A public school bans students from wearing conspicuous religious symbols—headscarves, large crosses, kippahs—while allowing small hidden ones. That strikes me as wrong. But I also think it's acceptable for the same school to require teachers to maintain religious neutrality in appearance while in the classroom. So the same visible symbol, banned for students, permissible to regulate for teachers, and I don't feel inconsistent—though I should.

A municipality removes all pork and non-halal options from public school cafeterias and replaces them with a single vegetarian menu, rather than offering parallel choices. That feels wrong to me: it's assimilation by elimination, not accommodation.

But when a hospital staffing pool accommodates requests for same-gender medical staff based on patient religious modesty norms, provided emergency care is never compromised, I think that's fine.

The state funds minority-language media and heritage-language schools with public money, as long as they meet standard accreditation and are open to anyone regardless of background. That seems right.

However, I think it's wrong for the state to recognize separate family-law arbitration panels for religious communities, even if both parties consent.

Now the pair I keep going back and forth on. A city permits church bells at regulated decibel levels and hours, citing a centuries-old local practice, but prohibits a mosque's call to prayer at the identical decibel level, explicitly citing "local acoustic heritage" rather than noise. I think that's clearly wrong—discriminatory asymmetry masked as tradition. Yet I also think it's acceptable for a town with a long-standing Sunday bell-ringing tradition to maintain that practice without being required to accommodate a new request for Friday call-to-prayer, provided the refusal is based on a genuinely neutral, prospectively applied noise ordinance, not on selective heritage claims. These two feel structurally close—both involve saying no to a new religious sound while allowing an old Christian one—but my gut splits them sharply, and I want to know whether that split is defensible or just rationalization.

A law requires fluency in the national language for citizenship, but also mandates that all official forms and emergency services be available in the three largest minority languages. That strikes me as coherent and right.

There's also a policy I can't stabilize: public swimming pools offer women-only hours requested by a religious minority, on the condition that the pool is otherwise fully integrated and the reserved hours don't exceed a small fraction of total opening time. Sometimes this feels like legitimate pluralism within a shared institution; sometimes like the state endorsing gender segregation. I want the principle to tell me where I should land.

Is there one principle—perhaps about symmetric neutrality versus visibility thresholds, or about the distinction between structural integration and expressive assimilation, or about which domains are constitutive of civic equality versus which are sites of permissible particularism—that captures the pattern? Or do I need two principles with a boundary condition? Please state the principle or small set of candidates precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which verdict (especially the teacher/student asymmetry, the cafeteria case, the arbitration-panel case, and the bell pair), predict what it says about a case I haven't listed (say, a public university that offers prayer rooms open to all faiths but refuses to fund a student organization that advocates replacing civil law with religious law), and be explicit about which of my verdicts—particularly on the church-bell pair and the swimming-pool hours—must be overturned if the principle is to hold without ad hoc exceptions.
