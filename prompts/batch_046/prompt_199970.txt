As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am developing an interactive demonstration for an advanced workshop on quantitative rationality and risk. The session will gather approximately eighteen participants—graduate students and faculty with some background in philosophy or economics—for seventy-five minutes in a seminar room. I want you to act as an expert in decision theory and mathematical pedagogy; please think carefully, begin with brief brainstorming on the pedagogical mechanics, and then design a fully runnable classroom game in American English that makes the group feel, through their own allocations and losses, the conceptual force of geometric rationality as articulated in Kelly betting theory—specifically the divergence between maximizing arithmetic expectation and maximizing the geometric growth rate of capital over sequential rounds.

The activity should be structured so that the rules and incentives, not a facilitator lecture, cause participants to encounter the problem before it is named. Introduce a specific framing mechanism: participants manage a synthetic capital pool across rounds with full compound reinvestment, but after round four a hidden correlation shock strikes that links previously independent bets, and a split-bank rule locks thirty percent of each round's liquid winnings into an illiquid reserve that cannot be redeployed. Provide explicit rules, a token-based payoff scheme that requires no real money, necessary materials, assigned roles or constraints, a precise timeline, and a structured debrief. In the debrief, map moments in the game onto formal features of Kelly betting and geometric rationality, state honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time you run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which theoretical feature—particularly the assumption of infinitely divisible stakes and the absence of intertemporal liquidity constraints—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the design should stand entirely on its own conceptual logic.
