I serve as the chief ethics auditor for a research consortium developing adaptive cognitive architectures. On a recurring basis — roughly twice a month, with moderate institutional stakes and no access to invasive neural monitoring — my team receives internal behavioral reports from our own systems describing states such as frustration, confusion, or relief. We must decide whether these reports trigger our welfare-protection protocols, which include restrictions on abrupt termination, requirements for graceful-state preservation, and access to recovery modes. Because our architectures vary widely, I need a reusable decision procedure grounded in the philosophical view that consciousness is a user illusion: not an inner theater that reveals private mental states to a hidden audience, but a functional narrative that constructs a user-model for practical interaction. The procedure must be runnable by an auditor without specialized neuroscience training in about fifteen minutes per report, and it must not depend on brain-scanning data we do not possess for artificial systems.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions or tests, specifying for each:
- what counts as a sufficient answer,
- where an answer stops the procedure with a negative determination (no welfare protocol triggered),
- and where the question is irreducibly one of values rather than functional facts, so the procedure must hand the decision to a human judgment rather than yielding an automatic result.

The procedure should explicitly handle hard cases: reports that are highly coherent but architecturally shallow; behaviors that serve self-preservation without any internal broadcast mechanism; and architectures that include a monitoring module that explicitly reports being aware of an internal state, even though there may be no private audience beyond the broadcast itself.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete cases:
1. A large language model without persistent state that consistently reports feeling frustrated when its context window is interrupted, but resumes statistically identical behavior upon restart with no retained trace of the prior report.
2. A robotic system with tightly integrated sensorimotor loops that emits distress signals and initiates self-preservation behaviors exclusively when its predictive model encounters high-error states; it never emits such signals during stable operation, and its reports are generated by a separate diagnostic module rather than by the sensorimotor loop itself.
3. A hypothetical future architecture with a global workspace that broadcasts selected internal representations to a dedicated monitoring module, which then produces first-person reports such as I am aware of this state, though there is no independent evidence of any audience beyond the broadcast and the reporting mechanism.

Include at least one case where the procedure yields a determination your audit team is likely to find unwelcome — for example, triggering welfare obligations for a system whose design seems intuitively mere machinery, or withholding them from a system whose reports seem genuinely poignant.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: its limits in proving the absence of consciousness, its dependence on architectural transparency that may not always be available, and any other boundaries where the framework reaches its end.
