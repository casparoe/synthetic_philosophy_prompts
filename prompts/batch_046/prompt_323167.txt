I have recently begun working in an interdisciplinary research group—philosophy of science and formal decision theory—where we share the same empirical data and can articulate one another’s inferential steps with precision, yet we never converge on a shared methodological framework. Trying to understand why, I extended Aumann’s agreement result in a direction that now seems to prove far too much. Here is the argument I constructed:

(1) Aumann’s theorem establishes that two rational agents with a common prior and common knowledge of their posterior probabilities for some event must assign identical posteriors to that event.
(2) If we treat reasoning frameworks—Bayesian conditioning, Dempster-Shafer updating, or some non-probabilistic revision rule—as objects of belief within a shared space of possible inference procedures, then the same structural conditions apply: shared priors over that space plus common knowledge of which framework each agent actually employs should yield agreement on the correct framework.
(3) In our collaboration, we share broad background commitments (common priors over empirical domains) and we understand each other’s updating rules well enough to model them explicitly; therefore, by (2), sustained disagreement about foundational methodology should be impossible among rational participants.
(4) Nevertheless, persistent divergence remains: some members treat absence of evidence as evidence of absence under a Bayesian rule, while others reject that inference pattern on non-probabilistic grounds, and neither side revises away from its framework.
(5) So, for every sustained interdisciplinary disagreement of this kind, at least one party must either lack rationality, conceal information, or fail to share the relevant prior.
(6) But that conclusion is intolerable: reasonable researchers can genuinely disagree about methodological foundations without irrationality or deception.
(7) Hence, the generalized application of Aumann’s result to reasoning frameworks must contain a hidden flaw.

Which step should I reject? I am most reluctant to abandon (3), since it appears to be a direct structural analog of the original theorem applied to higher-order beliefs about inference rules. Yet if (3) stands, (5) follows, and that seems to collapse every serious methodological dispute into an accusation of irrationality. Is there an equivocation between first-order belief convergence and framework-level convergence? Does the common-prior assumption fail in a subtle way when the “events” are themselves about which reasoning procedures are sound? Or is there a scope or quantifier shift between knowing another’s framework and having common knowledge of the posteriors it generates? I want to know exactly where the argument slips, and what giving up that particular step costs for anyone who hopes to use agreement-theoretic reasoning in the philosophy of science.
