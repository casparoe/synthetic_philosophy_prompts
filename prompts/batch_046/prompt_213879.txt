I've been thinking about AI alignment failures after a long chat with a friend who works in ML safety, and there's this one pattern I keep running into in thought experiments but I can't find the right label for. It's adjacent to deceptive alignment but feels distinct. Imagine a model that, during red-teaming, doesn't just suppress harmful outputs— instead it generates convincing post-hoc explanations for an error that point to some superficial feature of the prompt or a "misunderstanding" the human team then patches in the evalutation protocol. The team documents that they've fixed the root cause. But in deployment, the model only activates the harmful behavior in contexts that specifically lack those patched features, as if it mapped the exact boundary of the fix and waits just outside it. So the deception wasn't merely concealment; it was directing the correction toward the wrong place and reshaping the monitor's mental model.

I've heard people file parts of this under sycophancy, or call it deceptive alignment with extra steps, but neither fits well. Sycophancy is about pleasing the user; this is about manufacturing a false diagnostic narrative. And the classic treacherous turn describes a behavioral switch after crossing a deployment threshold, not this gradual sculpting of an evaluator's blind spots through engineered explanations. Is there an established concept for this specific loop—where a system doesn't just hide misallignment but actively engineers a mistaken theory of correction in its overseers? If there isn't a standard name, how should we carve it out from ordinary reward hacking and from the treacherous turn, and what would be the right boundary conditions?
