I'm a retired experimental physicist who only started reading philosphy seriously after I left the lab, and I've been chewing on Plato's Meno with some friends over coffee. The question Socrates raises — why is knowledge worth more than true belief if both get you to the right place — keeps bugging me, but I want to test it against cases from my own field rather than just the statue analogy where true opinions run away unless you tie them down with a reason. So here are my verdicts, and I'm hoping there's a principle behind them, though I suspect one of them has to go.

First case: in my old group a student once used an obsolete thermal-expansion formula and accidentally predicted the right length change for a new alloy. She had true belief about the number, but change the temperature range and she's completely off. I say she didn't know anything; the belief was brittle because it wasn't tied to the actual mechanism. Second case: a technician reads a calibrated interferometer every day without knowing any optics. The calibraton chain goes back to national standards, and he gets the right value reliably. I feel this is closer to real knowledge, or at least just as good, because the reliability lives in the apparatus and the social practice, not just in his head. Third case: two colleagues both correctly belive compound X inhibts enzyme Y. One built it up from crystal structures and kinetics; the other had a lucky hunch from a misread graph. Both are right now, but I want to say only the first knows, and her belief is "better" because she can design analogues and survive scrutiny. Fourth case — and here's where I get stuck — the crystal-structure colleague publishes, but later we find the inhibition only holds in her exact buffer and fails in vivo. Her belief was true in that narrow setting. The hunch-person also had true belief in that same narrow setting, but never tested it. Did the first colleague ever truly know, if the belief wasn't more broadly true? Or was her knowledge always just about that specific condition, and the "betterness" is really about having a visible, correctable justification?

I'm injecting my own half-baked idea here: maybe knowledge is just true belief with high "epistemic bandwidth" — it can travel across contexts, be repaired when broken, and be shared without degrading. The student with the wrong formula has low bandwidth; the technician borrows bandwidth from the calibraton chain; the crystal-structure researcher had high bandwidth within her domain but it collapsed when the context shifted. Fifth case: a mathematician proves a theorem, another guesses the same result by numerology. Both hold true belief, but the proof seems tied down in a way the guess isn't. Still, if the only use is applying the formula, does the tie-down actually add value? So does this bandwidth principle save all my verdicts, or do I have to drop the idea that distributed reliability counts as knowledge? And is there a cleaner way to state it that doesn't just smuggle in the conclusion I want?
