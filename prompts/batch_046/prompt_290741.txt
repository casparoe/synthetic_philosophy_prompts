After thirty years in condensed-matter physics I have recently turned to moral philosophy, and I wish to submit a one-year independent-study proposal to a philosophy-of-science workshop. My half-formed idea concerns lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism—the view that suffering bears a lexical priority over pleasure, but only once it crosses a certain intensity threshold, below which the two remain in some restricted comparative relation. I suspect the standard debate misses a deeper conceptual problem: it treats suffering and pleasure as competing magnitudes on a single scalar axis, then introduces the “threshold” as if it were a boundary condition. From a physical-sciences perspective, this looks suspiciously like trying to compare temperature and entropy by declaring that above a certain value one “dominates” the other, without first asking whether they share the dimensions required for comparison at all. I want to investigate whether the lexical-threshold framework implicitly smuggles in a unit-conversion factor that collapses qualitative dimensional differences into quantitative ones, and whether any version of negative utilitarianism can survive once valence is treated as genuinely dimensionally heterogeneous rather than merely ordinally ranked.

Please turn this into a formal research proposal. I need the following sections in this order, with approximate lengths: (1) Precise Statement of the Question (about 250 words)—frame the issue so it has a determinate answer, distinguishing the dimensional-incommensurability challenge from the familiar aggregationist objections; (2) Why Existing Answers Leave It Open (about 300 words)—explain how current defenders and critics of lexical negative utilitarianism both presuppose a shared metric of “amount” of suffering or pleasure, and why that presupposition has gone unexamined; (3) Proposed Approach and Month-by-Month Plan (about 500 words)—outline a three-phase method: first, formalizing the dimensional analogy using unit-analysis and boundary-condition logic from physics without importing technical physics content; second, applying it to canonical normative cases (distributed minor pleasures versus localized severe suffering, threshold-crossing scenarios); third, testing whether a reconstructed lexical rule can avoid the smuggled-commensurability problem and still deliver normative guidance; (4) Criteria for Success and Honest Assessment of Failure (about 250 words)—state exactly what result would vindicate or undermine the project, including the possibility that the analogy dissolves and standard debates remain untouched; and (5) Scope, Limitations, and Timeline (about 200 words)—confirm that the work is feasible for one year without original empirical research, note what it deliberately excludes (no historical survey of utilitarianism, no neurobiological account of pain), and specify the deliverable.
