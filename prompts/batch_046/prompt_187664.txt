I'm preparing materials for a graduate seminar on social ontology, and I want a diagnostic instrument—not a literature review, but a "which view do you actually hold?" taxonomy—for the debate over collective intentionality and joint action. The audience is philosophy graduate students who know the basic terrain—Searle's non-reductive we-intentions, Bratman's shared cooperative activity, Margaret Gilbert's plural subjects, and the reductionist challenge—but need to map how the logical space divides beyond the standard labels.

Please construct a decision-tree taxonomy that begins with a concrete commitment rather than a philosopher's name: start with whether you think two agents can genuinely form a joint intention when neither has direct access to the other's mental states, only to observable behavior and a shared plan. From there, build branch nodes out of questions on which the major positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that a student can answer each without already knowing which leaf it points to. The tree must include at least these separating questions: whether collective intentionality requires mutual belief about each participant's intentions or whether functional coordination plus common knowledge of the plan suffices; whether a collective intention can survive the gradual replacement of all original members, or whether it is essentially tied to the specific individuals involved; whether artificial agents—systems that coordinate with humans but lack phenomenology—can be genuine co-participants in joint action, or whether biological consciousness is necessary; and whether joint commitment creates obligations that are irreducibly collective, owed to the group as a plural subject, or whether all obligations reduce to pairwise commitments among members.

Each leaf should carry the name of a position where there is one—such as a Searle-style collective intentionality view, a Bratman-style shared cooperative activity view, a Gilbert-style plural subject theory, a reductionist eliminativist position, an institutional or functionalist view, or a phenomenological exclusivist view—or be marked "unnamed" if the combination of answers occupies logical space no prominent theory fills. For every unnamed leaf, provide one sentence diagnosing why it might be unoccupied: incoherent, practically unstable, or merely unexplored. Please flag explicitly any pair of named positions that your tree cannot cleanly separate, where two views give the same answers across all nodes but differ only in underlying metaphysical justification. Note also any branch combination that seems incoherent and explain briefly why. Build the taxonomy from the logical space first—questions and answer-combinations—then locate the actual positions within it, with roughly six branch nodes and eight to ten leaves. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; the instrument should stand on its own for students working without external resources. Keep the phrasing formal and precise, suitable for a seminar handout.
