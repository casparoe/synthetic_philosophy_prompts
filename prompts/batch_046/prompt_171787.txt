A growing number of institutional AI governance protocols—internal laboratory review boards as well as proposed regulatory certification schemes—evaluate whether a frontier model is aligned or safe to release primarily through average-case metrics: mean refusal rates across standardized harm categories, expected calibration on safety-relevant queries, aggregate scores on broad evaluation suites, and projected average welfare outcomes in simulated deployment environments. Within these frameworks, isolated worst-case adversarial failures—such as a deterministic jailbreak that reliably elicits dangerous instructions across contexts, or a single structural failure mode that amplifies risk within a specific domain—are typically treated as edge cases or tail risks to be managed through post-deployment monitoring, patching, and iterative update, rather than as conditions that should block deployment altogether. At the same time, many of the same institutions mandate extensive pre-deployment red-teaming exercises that appear designed precisely to surface and take seriously exactly such worst-case vulnerabilities before release.

What philosophical commitments about risk, evidence, and moral responsibility does this bifurcated evaluation practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the reliance on average-case benchmarking presuppose that epistemic justification for releasing a high-stakes system is essentially statistical and aggregative? Does classifying worst-case failures as remediable post hoc, rather than disqualifying, presuppose a particular form of consequentialist reasoning—one that weights expected harm reduction more heavily than the avoidance of catastrophic tail outcomes—or can it be reconciled with a deontological constraint that certain failure modes are impermissible regardless of probability? Is the practice internally consistent with the parallel institutional commitment to red-teaming, or do these two norms quietly presuppose incompatible epistemologies, one probabilistic and one focused on structural vulnerability? Finally, if the implicit view were replaced by one that treated a verified worst-case failure mode as a necessary-blocking condition for deployment—analogous to a structural engineering failure that disqualifies a bridge design regardless of average load performance—what specific changes would be required in evaluation timelines, certification criteria, and the allocation of responsibility between pre-deployment reviewers and post-deployment operators?
