I'm preparing a ninety-minute graduate seminar—about ten students, in a comparative metaphysics course that has just finished a unit on Buddhist ontology—where I want the group to feel why Nāgārjuna's tetralemma is not merely a logical puzzle but a structural trap that collapses every conventional stance on existence. Rather than lecturing on canonical texts, I want a structured deliberation with forced choices and small stakes, followed by a debrief that maps what happened onto Madhyamaka emptiness (śūnyatā). The participants should come away seeing that emptiness is not a fifth metaphysical claim but the failure of the four-cornered framework itself when applied to dependently originated phenomena—though I also need you to flag honestly which feature of the view this classroom reconstruction cannot reproduce.

Please design a runnable classroom activity with rules, roles or group structures, a timeline, and a debrief plan. The setting is a single session with ten graduate students, no technology beyond printed case cards. I want to inject a somewhat niche framing: instead of classic chariot or fire examples, the cases should involve procedural artifacts—algorithmically generated cultural objects that exist only through distributed rendering protocols, have no singular author, and persist in fragmented caches after deletion. Design five brief case cards describing such artifacts, for instance an object identical to constantly modified open-source code; one instantiated simultaneously across servers with non-identical copies; one that survives only as relational differences to other artifacts; one whose causal effects continue after all source files are deleted; and one whose properties are entirely determined by comparative user interactions. The activity should proceed in three phases. First, about twenty minutes: in pairs, students classify each artifact using exactly one of the four tetralemma positions—exists with intrinsic nature (svabhāva), absolutely does not exist, both exists and does not exist, or neither exists nor does not exist—writing a brief justification for each. Second, about twenty-five minutes: each pair receives challenge tokens representing standard Madhyamaka objections, such as being asked to identify one non-relational property if defending position one, to account for continuing causal interactions if defending position two, to resolve formal contradiction without invoking a conventional-ultimate distinction if defending position three, or to avoid quietism about why the question was asked at all if defending position four. They respond in writing, then present to the full group. Third, about fifteen minutes: the class votes on which classifications survived all challenges; predictably none will, and the facilitator should show that every defense of intrinsic existence smuggled in relational properties, every denial of existence relied on acknowledging appearances, every both collapsed into incoherence, and every neither ended in silence. The rules should be arranged so students discover this failure themselves—through the impossibility of meeting the token conditions—before the facilitator names the tetralemma.

In the debrief, map each failure onto Nāgārjuna's framework: position one denies dependent origination by positing svabhāva; position two falls into nihilism by ignoring conventional efficacy; position three violates non-contradiction; position four abandons the explanatory task that dependent origination fulfills. Clarify that emptiness is therefore not absence but the absence of inherent existence in dependently arisen things. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time this runs: students will likely invent a fifth option—conventional existence versus ultimate non-existence—without grasping that this is precisely the Madhyamaka move they must earn rather than assume, and they may feel cheated if it is revealed too quickly; also, pairs assigned the both position often produce confusing justifications that derail discussion, and dominant voices may suppress quieter pairs. Include an honest note that this activity can reproduce the logical structure of the tetralemma and the relational critique of intrinsic nature, but it cannot reproduce the soteriological or meditative dimension—the direct, non-conceptual insight (prajñā) into emptiness that Madhyamaka treats as liberative rather than merely dialectical—so the facilitator must supply that limitation explicitly and not present the exercise as a substitute for contemplative practice.
