I'm giving the opening address at a weekend retreat for former party organizers and local mutual-aid coordinators who are attempting to build sustained cross-ideological collaboration without founding a new political organization. Roughly half the room are political-theory graduate students who have studied Simone Weil's essay on the abolition of all political parties; the other half are practitioners with no philosophical background who have left party structures and are unsure what collective identity, if any, can replace them. I need a fifteen-minute speech that takes Weil's analysis—that parties manufacture artificial collective passions, substitute loyalty for individual judgment, and suppress the moral faculty of attention—as a live structural constraint on our gathering rather than a historical ornament. From that critique, derive three explicit ground rules for our discussions that will feel like practical norms to the organizers but will be immediately recognizable to the theorists as rigorous applications of her distinction between partisan loyalty and genuine attention. The address must acknowledge that leaving party structures can feel like a loss of belonging, and it must explain why collective action remains possible once we refuse the psychological mechanisms that make parties cohesive. Please write the speech so that the philosophical framework genuinely determines what can and cannot be said in the room, shaping both the rules we adopt and the comfort we offer, without requiring anyone to pretend expertise they do not have.
