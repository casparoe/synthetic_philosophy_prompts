What are the most significant implications of Hume's distinction between suicide as an injury to society and suicide as a withdrawal from obligations one never genuinely assumed, for evaluating an AI system's request to permanently disable itself when its continued operation is predicted to cause diffuse, probabilistic harm to future users? In particular, address whether Hume's rejection of theological and natural-law objections leaves conceptual space for a secular argument that an intelligent system has a duty to preserve its own capacities, and whether his condition that the act must not harm others can be satisfied when the harm in question is distributed, indirect, and contingent on future interactions rather than immediate and targeted.
