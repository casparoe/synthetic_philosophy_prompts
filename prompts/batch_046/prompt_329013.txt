I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether mental states—things like deciding, doubting, or feeling regret—actually cause anything in the physical world, or whether they are merely along for the ride once the brain has done its work. The argument kept circling back to the causal exclusion argument, and I realized I do not understand its architecture well enough to teach it without falling into the usual rehearsed examples. I want to explain it properly to a small reading group of philosophy-of-science graduate students from physics and biology backgrounds—smart people who are skeptical that metaphysics of mind bears on scientific explanation, who have heard of supervenience but do not live with it, and who have never read the canonical papers on mental causation by name. Plan the explanatory arc: the precise sequence in which the components must be introduced so that the exclusion problem emerges as inevitable rather than as a trick, identifying which idea must be in place before the next can land and why the dependency runs in that direction.
