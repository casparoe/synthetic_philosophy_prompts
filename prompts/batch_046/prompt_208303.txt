As a novelist who has spent years thinking about how characters persist or dissolve across discontinuous plot structures, I ask you to restate Zhuangzi's position on death—his view that death is not a loss or terminus but a natural transformation within an undifferentiated process, that the boundary between living and dead is as insubstantial as that between waking and dreaming, and that grief mistakes a temporary configuration for a fixed identity—in the vocabulary of narrative craft: diegetic levels, character continuity across plot discontinuities, narrative closure, reader identification, and the ontology of fictional worlds. Be faithful: treat the transformation of forms in Zhuangzi as analogous to the persistence or non-persistence of a character across revisions, sequels, or shifts in narrative frame. Then report the residue. Which claims translate cleanly onto questions of whether a character dies when their story ends, or whether an author can resurrect them without contradiction? Which resist translation—perhaps his indifference to mourning, or his denial that there is a stable self that survives or perishes—because narrative form necessarily presupposes a reader who tracks outcomes and a structure that distinguishes before from after? And is that residue a deficiency in narrative vocabulary, or does it show that part of Zhuangzi's claim was less a substantive thesis than an attempt to dissolve a distinction that storytelling must maintain?
