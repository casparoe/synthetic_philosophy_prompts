In discussions of the dollar auction and escalation of commitment, a familiar analogy compares the bidding process to a game of chicken in which both drivers burn fuel that they must pay for regardless of who swerves: each new bid drives the vehicle closer to the cliff, the loser still owes for every gallon consumed, and the standard conclusion drawn is that rational agents should swerve immediately, since any further motion is self-defeating. This framing is often used to argue that escalation is not merely unfortunate but transparently irrational, a failure of foresight that can be corrected by recognizing the spiral in advance. I would like you to audit the analogy with some care. Begin by setting out the correspondence in detail — what corresponds to the driver, the swerve, the cliff, the fuel bill, the prediction of rational exit, and the audience watching the collision — then examine where the mapping fails, especially regarding whether the sunk payments operate as an external cost shared with the opponent or as a private loss, whether the strategic situation is genuinely one-shot or embedded in a repeated interaction with reputational shadow prices, and whether the analogy presupposes a model of perfect common knowledge of rationality that the auction structure itself tends to dissolve. I want you to distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the normative conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and to say whether the judgment that escalation is a straightforward rationality failure survives once those fractures are visible, or whether the chicken-frame was doing the argumentative work all along. If a different structural comparison — perhaps one that treats the sunk bids as a commitment device rather than wasted fuel — captures the dynamics more faithfully without importing hidden normative premises, describe it.
