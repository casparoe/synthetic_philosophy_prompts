At a workshop on welfare ethics, Mira defended a lexical-threshold form of negative utilitarianism. She held that suffering and pleasure are fully commensurable on a cardinal scale below a specific moral threshold, permitting ordinary aggregative calculus, but that suffering exceeding that threshold acquires lexical priority: no quantity of pleasure, however large, can outweigh it. Jonas objected that fixing any such cutoff requires comparing suffering across the boundary, which presupposes the very commensurability the view denies above the line; without that comparison, the threshold is arbitrary, and with it, the lexical claim collapses. Mira replied that the cutoff is normative rather than empirical—functioning like a rights boundary, not a temperature gauge—so no cross-boundary cardinal comparison is needed to justify it. Jonas then introduced a boundary-case injection: imagine a policy that pushes a small group from just above to just below the threshold, but only by stripping away nearly all pleasures across the broader population, leaving everyone in a barely-sub-threshold state of minimal welfare. If pleasure and pain remain fully commensurable below the threshold, then this massive loss of pleasure must be weighed against the small group's gain, and the policy should likely be rejected on aggregate grounds. Yet Mira's view seems to treat the small group's shift as lexically decisive regardless of the aggregate sub-threshold balance, implying that near the boundary commensurability is suspended. Mira responded that Jonas misapplies the rule: lexical priority governs states in which suffering remains above the threshold, not comparisons among states that all lie below it. Once everyone is below, standard aggregation applies, so if the policy makes everyone barely sub-threshold with enormous pleasure loss, it fails on ordinary utilitarian grounds. Jonas countered that this saves consistency only by rendering the lexical feature redundant. If cross-boundary comparison is forbidden, there is no evaluative reason to push anyone across the boundary in the first place; the intervention is justified only if being below is lexically superior to being above, which requires precisely the comparison Mira forbids. Thus, either the view allows cross-boundary weighing—in which case the lexical priority is undermined—or it forbids it, in which case the threshold lacks motivational force and collapses into ordinary negative utilitarianism with an unexplained discontinuity.
