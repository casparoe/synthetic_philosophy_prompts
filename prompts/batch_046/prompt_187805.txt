I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, and I convene a monthly seminar of about ten retired scientists and engineers—physicists, a biologist, and a mathematician—for ninety minutes in a university common room. I want a runnable in-person activity, not a lecture, that makes the group feel the force of the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarian aggregation before anyone names Rawls or the concept. Because my colleagues think in terms of closed systems and state variables, frame the rules around "isolated life-systems": each participant receives an envelope identifying them as System P_i with a local welfare state of ten physical chips and a specific long-term project card that is non-tradable. Present a "global optimization" proposal in which System P_3 must surrender seven chips so that System P_7 and System P_9 gain three and five respectively, producing a net aggregate gain of one chip. The critical mechanical rule should be that any chip leaving one envelope can only enter another if it is first deposited in a shared "boundary-dissolution bowl"; without that intermediate step the chip is discarded. The group must physically vote, execute or block the transfer with real chips, and then discuss whether they consented and on what grounds. The debrief should map the physical act of surrender onto the moral claim that there is no collective vessel over separate persons, should identify the predictable failure mode—participants treating chips as fungible currency and ignoring the envelope identity unless the facilitator redirects attention to the non-transferable project card—and should state frankly which feature of real moral harm the chip mechanics cannot reproduce. Please provide the exact envelope contents, the reallocation scheme, the vote and execution protocol, debrief questions, and an honest note on the analogy's limits. No bibliographies, citations, or reading lists are needed; the idea should emerge from what happens with the chips and the bowl.
