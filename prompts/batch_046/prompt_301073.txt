Over the past few months I have found myself mediating between colleagues who operate within fundamentally incompatible moral frameworks—some committed to a deontological ethic that treats direct, unmediated informed consent as an absolute and non-delegable duty to the patient, others formed within a virtue-ethical tradition that regards blunt disclosure of grave prognosis as a failure of compassion, communal care, and practical wisdom. Rather than seeking a superficial compromise that merely splits the difference, I want to understand whether a genuinely cooperative institutional design is possible in which each side must relax a commitment it considers genuinely non-negotiable, yet both can recognize the resulting arrangement as a moral improvement without either framework being collapsed into a hybrid or subsumed by the other.

Please construct a realistic, ordinary-life thought experiment—no science-fictional technology, no omniscient arbiter, no perfect predictor, and no appeal to a hypothetical mediator who resolves all tension by fiat—that meets the following specifications. The scenario should involve a specific institutional protocol (for instance, a tiered disclosure system in a clinical or communal setting) jointly designed by a representative of each framework. For the deontological party, the protocol must require relaxing the principle that full transparency must always be delivered directly and individually to the autonomous agent; for the virtue-ethical party, it must require relaxing the prohibition against directly conveying certain categories of prognostic information that their tradition treats as spiritually or communally harmful. The cooperative output should be a concrete procedure that neither party could justify or sustain alone, and you should demonstrate precisely why each framework can endorse the result: state the specific belief or commitment each party retains, the specific principle each relaxes, the cooperative good produced, and why that good counts as a net moral gain within each framework rather than a mere pragmatic concession. Then provide an honest structural analysis: identify exactly which feature of the case does the philosophical work (for example, the asymmetry in what is traded, the presence of a structured third-party role, or the separation of information delivery from information suppression), explain what happens to the argument if that feature is removed, and articulate how a defender of moral purity—who holds that core deontic or aretaic commitments cannot be traded away without corruption—would attack the case and why that attack might or might not succeed. Keep the scenario to no more than a paragraph, suitable for discussion among graduate students in ethics, and ensure that every verdict is generated by the theories themselves rather than stipulated in advance.
