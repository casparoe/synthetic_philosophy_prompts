I'm a PhD student in philosophy of medicine, and I serve as a peer supervisor in a hospice fellowship where trainees present case narratives to a multidisciplinary review board. The board meets twice weekly, each session lasts about ninety minutes, and I have roughly twenty minutes per case to assess whether a trainee's explanatory account of a patient's decision—refusing palliative sedation, withdrawing life support, declining hospice enrollment—operates in Sartrean bad faith. Specifically, I want to operationalize Sartre's claim that bad faith is the refusal to integrate one's radical freedom with one's facticity: treating oneself or another as pure object (wholly determined by culture, biology, or institutional role) or as pure transcendence (wholly unconditioned and therefore not responsible). The deliverable should be a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with decision nodes—that a co-supervisor without philosophical training can run on a narrative transcript in real time.

The procedure must preserve the core ontological distinction between facticity (the given conditions that constrain choice) and transcendence (the freedom that exceeds every determination), but translate it into observable features of clinical narration: whether the trainee permits that the patient could have chosen otherwise under identical conditions; whether the trainee treats their own clinical recommendation or silence as mechanically compelled by hierarchy or protocol; and whether institutional or biological constraints are presented as excusing rather than conditioning choice. At each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure yields a determination of bad faith (distinguishing self-directed, other-directed, and institutional-projection variants), where it stops with a clear negative, and where it must hand off to a values judgment rather than a procedural one. Explicitly mark the judgment-call nodes—such as distinguishing legitimate structural explanation from deterministic collapse—where simplification risks distorting the theory, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire, for instance by flagging all deterministic language even when epistemic humility about prediction is warranted.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three cases. First, a trainee who explains a patient's refusal of sedation by stating that in her culture suffering is redemptive, leaving no discursive space for individual deviation from the collective norm. Second, a trainee who accounts for their own failure to discuss hospice options by appealing to the attending's authority structure, treating their own silence as compelled rather than chosen. Third, include a case where the procedure returns an answer I expect to resist: a trainee who invokes biological determinism tied to dementia-related agitation to justify overriding a patient's prior documented refusal of antipsychotics, where the checklist flags other-directed bad faith but the clinical stakes seem to justify the override. For each, show the step-by-step run and the point at which judgment takes over.

Finally, state precisely what this procedure cannot do: whether it can reliably distinguish bad faith from genuine epistemic limitation in predicting patient behavior; whether it pathologizes all reference to structural or biological constraint; and whether Sartre's concept, being essentially first-personal, can be diagnosed from a third-person narrative review without fundamental betrayal.

One further request: if this task rests on a confused premise—if, for example, Sartre's account of bad faith is irreducibly reflexive and cannot be translated into a third-person observational checklist without collapsing the very freedom it aims to protect, or if the translation necessarily turns an existential insight into a bureaucratic audit tool—please say so plainly rather than constructing a procedure that papers over the problem. I want the operationalized framework only if it is genuinely derivable from the theory; otherwise I want the objection stated clearly.
