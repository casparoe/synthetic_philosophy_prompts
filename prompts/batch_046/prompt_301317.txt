I am preparing a short essay for an advanced workshop in philosophy of mind, provisionally titled "No Paper, No Hands: Escher, Hofstadter, and the Myth of the Inviolate Representational Ground." The piece uses M.C. Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands and Douglas Hofstadter's analysis of strange loops and tangled hierarchies in Gödel, Escher, Bach as a framing device for a problem in the philosophy of mental representation. I present my working notes below rather than a polished draft, in the hope that your comments can help me see whether the central analogy holds and where it collapses.

The core argument runs as follows. Philosophers of mind frequently presuppose a structural picture analogous to Escher's image: mental representations seem arranged in a recursive hierarchy in which each interpretive level is generated by another, yet there must be an "inviolate level"—a sheet of paper, a neural substrate, a direct environmental coupling, or a primitive demonstrative reference—that is not itself part of the loop but grounds it. I contend that this is a framing artifact rather than an ontological feature. In the drawing itself, the paper is visually present and conceptually available only because it is drawn; its apparent transcendence depends on our interpretive decision to treat one region as belonging to a different logical order. By parallel reasoning, attempts to isolate a non-representational "ground floor" for mental content—whether in subpersonal computation, unmediated world-relations, or basic perceptual states—inevitably embed representational properties in that very floor. To systematize the error, I distinguish two forms of level-violation: Type-I violations treat the substrate as causally necessary but semantically inert, preserving an inviolate causal base; Type-II violations treat higher-order interpretation as entirely detached from its material conditions, preserving an inviolate semantic ceiling. Applying this taxonomy to the current dispute between radical enactivists and representationalists about basic mental content, I suggest that both camps preserve an inviolate level—respectively, the unmediated environment and the pre-interpreted neural code—thereby obscuring the genuinely tangled structure of cognitive hierarchy.

Please comment on these notes in the following sections, keeping each to roughly the length indicated. First, "Argumentative coherence" (approximately 250 words): does the analogy between Escher's drawing and representational grounding actually do work, or does it conflate visual illusion with metaphysical argument? Second, "Conceptual accuracy" (approximately 200 words): are there mischaracterizations of Hofstadter's notion of tangled hierarchy or of the enactivist/representationalist debate that undermine the essay? Third, "The taxonomy" (approximately 200 words): are the Type-I and Type-II distinctions useful, redundant, or forced? Fourth, "Conclusion and sharpening" (approximately 200 words): given that I want to avoid both a nihilistic "everything is interpretation all the way down" and a dogmatic foundationalism, what constructive position—if any—should the essay defend about where, if anywhere, explanation stops?
