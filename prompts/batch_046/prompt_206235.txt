As a novelist constructing a narrative about a coastal community whose oral tradition insists upon a forced displacement that state archives record only as voluntary resettlement, I require conceptual clarity rather than empirical verification. The tension between collective memory and the historical record is well traversed, yet I am interested specifically in the unfinished philosophical work at the intersection of epistemology, ethics, and what might be termed mnemonic sovereignty—the claim that a community possesses a non-derivable authority over its own narrative framework, even when that framework conflicts with documented fact.

Please treat this as a request for a defended agenda, not a survey or reading list. Identify the four or five most consequential open questions in this terrain—for instance: whether mnemonic sovereignty carries independent moral weight separate from epistemic accuracy; whether structured archival absence can itself constitute a harm to a community that remembers otherwise; how to distinguish legitimate mnemonic repair from mere epistemic relativism; what institutional obligations, if any, exist to accommodate collective testimony that remains unverified by standard historiography; and whether intergenerational narrative debt—the duty of descendants to sustain an unrecorded grievance—creates obligations that survive the death of direct witnesses. Rank these in order of priority for a researcher with limited resources—say, three years of solitary conceptual work—defending your ordering with explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable without access to restricted archives, and which, if settled, would most alter practice in ethics, law, or cultural policy.

Then discuss the principal trade-offs in your ranking: where tractability conflicts with importance, which questions risk being overrated because they attract disciplinary attention yet yield little downstream change, and which you would deprioritise because their resolution would leave the core conflict intact.

Next, specify what conceptual developments—not new archival discoveries—would cause you to reorder your priorities over the coming years.

Finally, offer a brief adjudication: in cases of irreconcilable conflict between a community's sustained memory and a credible historical record, which should generally give way, and under what precise conditions?

Please write in British English, and structure your response as follows:
