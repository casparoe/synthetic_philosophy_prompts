I'm trying to identify a recurring maneuver in debates over anthropic reasoning—particularly where self-sampling and self-indication assumptions are contested. A reasoner begins with one reference class of observers, say all biological humans across history, and derives a conclusion such as a doomsday-style prediction about future population size. When challenged on the arbitrariness of that class, they shift—without a principled account of why—to a narrower class or to an observer-weighting scheme that resembles self-indication, precisely in the region where the original argument collapses. Later, in a different context—addressing the problem that self-indication seems to assign overwhelming probability to enormously populated universes—the same reasoner insists on strict self-sampling to avoid that result. The sampling principle is not defended consistently across domains; rather, it is adjusted locally, like a dial turned to dissolve whichever paradox is currently at hand. The two positions are not being treated as rival theories to be adjudicated globally, but as interchangeable fragments of a single toolkit. Is there an established term for this kind of problem-driven, localized oscillation between anthropic frameworks? If not, can you name and delimit it—distinguishing it from ordinary theoretical pluralism, from ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses, and from the legitimate claim that different reference classes apply in genuinely different epistemic contexts—and specify what conditions would have to obtain for such a switch to be principled rather than strategic?
