The modernist versus perennialist debate over nationalism—whether nations are essentially modern constructions of the kind Hobsbawm linked to invented traditions and Anderson linked to imagined communities formed through print capitalism and administrative standardization, or whether they grow out of older ethnic symbols and shared myths—has persisted for decades without convergence. Modernists point to the 19th-century standardization of language, education, and citizenship; perennialists reply that collective emotional attachments and symbolic repertoires existed long before the modern state and are not merely fabricated. My working hypothesis is that the stall is mainly conceptual, not empirical: the camps use nation for different phenomena—one as an institutional-legal category tied to sovereignty, the other as a phenomenological category of felt belonging—so they cite the same medieval or early-modern cases without agreeing on what would count as evidence for continuity. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about the density of premodern collective identity, or about which cases should count as data?
