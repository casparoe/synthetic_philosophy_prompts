As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue set in a decentralized post-colonial bureaucracy, I need an adversarial-collaboration statement drafted jointly by two camps on corruption, clientelism, and gift-giving. Camp A are liberal-institutionalist universalists: corruption is the abuse of entrusted public power for private gain, and Western-style transparency and anti-bribery norms apply across cultures. Camp B are relational-contextualists: clientelism and ceremonial gift-giving often satisfy legitimate social obligations, and imposing Western anti-corruption frameworks risks epistemic violence by misreading local reciprocity. Draft the document they should have produced: first, a numbered list of shared claims—distinguishing procedural deviation from social reciprocity, acknowledging variability in bureaucratic norms, and recognizing risks in both unaccountable patronage and culturally blind regulation; second, a disagreements table stating each residual dispute in wording both sides would accept for their own view, particularly on whether a ceremonial gift creating a long-term reciprocal bond but lacking an explicit quid pro quo counts as corruption, and on whether universal norms presuppose a particular ontology of the state; third, the cruxes—such as whether institutional loyalty and social reciprocity can ever be fully separated, and whether anti-corruption metrics require a specific historical form of bureaucracy; fourth, for each crux, a concrete hypothetical case both sides agree is relevant—specifically, a regional water commissioner accepting an annual livestock tribute from an irrigation cooperative that also expects favorable allocation reviews—with each camp’s advance prediction about whether corruption is present and on what grounds. Frame the statement formally, avoid bibliographies or named theorists, and inject sufficient conceptual specificity—distinguishing exchange-cycles from bond-cycles in reciprocity—so that the answer is not a generic repetition of standard positions.
