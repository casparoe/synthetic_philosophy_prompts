I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, over the past year, has begun reading seriously in philosophy of mind and ethics. I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Coherence, Awareness, and the Scale of Moral Concern." The audience is split evenly between physicists who still work on open quantum systems and philosophers specializing in consciousness and normative theory; the physicists will spot a false analogy to decoherence immediately, and the philosophers will notice if I conflate constitutive panpsychism with panprotopsanpsychism or if I treat suffering as an observable rather than as a normative category.

I want the remarks to do real structural work with the idea that if awareness is genuinely fundamental—present even in the simplest field configurations—then suffering may not be bounded by biological complexity but by something closer to the maintenance of coherence against thermal dissipation. I am not asking you to defend this view; I am asking you to construct a speech that lives inside its tension. Specifically: the speech must open by acknowledging that my career was spent measuring noise, not meaning, and that I have no authority to pronounce on consciousness. It must then present the "decoherence-filter" framing—not as established physics, but as a speculative bridge—suggesting that ethical weight might track the capacity of a physical system to maintain integrated information against entropy, rather than tracking neural architecture alone. From there, the speech should move to the discomfort this produces: if suffering scales with coherence rather than cognition, then our moral universe expands to include configurations we currently ignore, yet physics offers no normative bridge to tell us which configurations matter. The remarks must conclude without resolving the tension—no false comfort, no call to action that pretends we know the ontology—while still honoring the occasion and leaving both halves of the room feeling that the problem was posed with intellectual honesty rather than mysticism.

Please write the speech. It should be formal but spoken, appropriate for a retired scientist speaking to peers, and it should avoid decorative philosophy: every reference to panpsychism or physical theory should shape what I am permitted to claim and what I must admit I do not know.
