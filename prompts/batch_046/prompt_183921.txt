Here's an argument my study partner constructed against Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, and I'm suspicious because the conclusion seems to swallow its own premises.

(1) On Al-Ghazālī's view, fire does not burn cotton by any intrinsic causal power; God creates the burning directly, with fire and cotton present only as concomitants.
(2) The only basis for expecting fire to burn is God's habitual course (ʿāda), which is observed regularity with no metaphysical necessity—God could suspend it at will.
(3) So occasionalism treats scientific generalizations as summaries of past divine actions rather than modal laws, carrying no predictive authority beyond psychological expectation.
(4) Al-Ghazālī nevertheless treats miracles (muʿjizāt) as breaks in this habit that authenticate prophecy; the break is epistemically significant precisely because the habit is stable.
(5) But if the habit has no modal or predictive weight, then a deviation is not a meaningful "break"—it is just another unconnected divine act, indistinguishable from noise.
(6) Therefore occasionalism either denies that miracles can be recognized (undermining prophetic epistemology) or it must grant observed regularities enough modal force to ground predictions and identify anomalies, which restores natural causation.
(7) So occasionalism is self-undermining.

Which premise or inference should I reject? I'd prefer to keep (3) and (5) intact, but I want to know what I'm paying if I drop (2), (4), or (6) instead. Please also say how confident you are in your diagnosis and identify the specific step or distinction you are least sure about.
