Write a deadpan institutional policy document — approximately 600 to 700 words, formatted as a university department’s internal “Terms of Epistemic Use and Vocabulary Agreement” — announcing that the Department of Philosophy has formally completed its transition to a fully Rortyan neopragmatist and antirepresentationalist framework. The voice should be that of an overzealous administrative compliance office: numbered subsections, bullet points, and bureaucratic euphemism, with no winking at the reader. The target is the cluster of commitments associated with Rorty’s rejection of philosophy as a “mirror of nature” — specifically the abandonment of epistemology in favor of cultural politics, the treatment of vocabularies as tools for coping rather than representations of a mind-independent reality, and the substitution of solidarity for objectivity — but exaggerate these into concrete institutional practices rather than inventing fictional philosophical doctrines.

To keep the satire conceptually precise rather than cartoonish, build the document around three invented administrative mechanisms that extend real Rortyan themes to absurd but internally consistent ends. First, establish a mandatory “Vocabulary Rotation Schedule” requiring every course syllabus to be entirely redescribed each semester in a new metaphorical register, with explicit penalties for “representationalist residue” — for example, language that implies a mind-independent fact of the matter about course content. Second, create a “Solidarity Metrics Dashboard” that evaluates classroom disagreements not by logical validity or evidential support but by whether disputants have achieved “mutual redescription” and whether their final shared vocabulary scores highly on social-cohesion indices. Third, institute an annual “Mirror of Nature Audit” in which faculty submit reports demonstrating that none of their research presupposes correspondence between language and reality, administered by an audit office that simultaneously denies that its own reports correspond to actual faculty behavior.

The humor should emerge from the unavoidable tension between the department’s official denial that there is “a way things really are” and the bureaucratic machinery’s unavoidable reliance on precisely that notion — for instance, the audit office must determine whether a professor really committed representationalism, the rotation schedule must be enforced according to real deadlines, and solidarity metrics must be compared across semesters. Include at least one paragraph addressing a classic philosophical problem — such as induction or the mind-body relation — not by proposing a solution but by declaring it administratively resolved as a “vocabulary conflict” now governed by the rotation protocol. Do not quote, cite, or ask for bibliographic reference to any specific text; the piece should work through conceptual exaggeration alone. The result should read like a document that a specialist in pragmatism recognizes as a fair, hyperbolic extension of the view’s internal logic, while a general reader can follow the bureaucratic absurdity without prior expertise.
