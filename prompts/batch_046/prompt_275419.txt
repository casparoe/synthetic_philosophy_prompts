My decision to leave a tenure-track position in philosophy and apply simultaneously to domestic public-policy institutes and international development agencies has made the question of patriotic partiality suddenly concrete: I need to know whether compatriots carry special moral claims on me, and whether those claims vary by domain. Here are my verdicts. First, fiscal allocation by government: if my state’s budget can either repair a local bridge that prevents ten deaths per year or fund an overseas vaccination campaign that prevents one hundred, I think the state should build the bridge—citizens have a prior claim on the collective resources they sustain through taxation and political membership. Second, private charity: with the same sum in my personal account, I should send it to the more effective global cause; feeling a pull toward local cultural projects strikes me as arbitrary bias I ought to overcome. Third, democratic rights: long-term residents who are not citizens should not vote in national elections, even when policies affect them profoundly—there is a distinctive political claim held only by members. Fourth, labor markets: a private firm should hire purely on merit, with no preference for citizens; nationality should be irrelevant in employment. Fifth, emergency rescue: if I can save either a compatriot or a foreigner at equal distance and risk, I feel compelled to save the compatriot, yet I cannot defend this without seeming to invoke mere tribal preference, and it sits uneasily with both my charitable and my hiring verdicts. Sixth, conscription: citizens may be compelled to serve in national defense in ways non-citizens cannot be—this asymmetry seems legitimate rather than unjust. I want a principle, or a small family of precisely stated principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Please report its verdicts on cases I have not considered—such as whether a citizen should be prioritized for a scarce organ transplant, whether a state may restrict emigration to retain skilled workers, and whether I should feel greater guilt for neglecting a local homeless person than a distant one in comparable need—and honestly identify which of my verdicts, especially the fiscal-charity split or the rescue intuition, must be abandoned to preserve coherence. I am particularly struck by what might be called the domain-fragmentation problem: partiality seems robust in fiscal and political domains but collapses in charitable and labor-market ones, and I want to know whether this fragmentation tracks a real moral distinction—perhaps between role-based and identity-based obligations—or is merely contextual rationalization. Finally, please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration—perhaps a demonstration that all associative obligations reduce without remainder to universal duties weighted by proximity, or evidence that my rescue intuition is indistinguishable from mere in-group bias—would lead you to reject the principle you propose, and where in the reasoning you think it is most vulnerable.
