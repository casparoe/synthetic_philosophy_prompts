I'm on a clinical audit team at a mid-sized mental health authority, and every quarter we get about 90 minutes to review proposed updates to our intake classification forms before the head of authority signs off. None of us are trained philosophers — we're mostly clinicians and data people — but we've been arguing about Hacking's looping effects, where putting people in a category can change them, which then changes the category itself. A friend in a philosphy of psychiatry course kept talking about "epistemic pollution" and said we're basically corrupting our own epidemiological tracking without realizing it, but couldn't tell us what to actually do.

So I want a reusable decision procedure — like a checklist or flowchart in words — that my team can run on any proposal: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer, where it stops with a clear no or yes, and where it has to hand off to a human judgment call because it's about values not facts. It should distinguish a loop that's harmful from one that's just tracking reality more precisely, and it should be honest about what it can't catch. Then apply it to three proposals: splitting "social anxiety" into performance-only versus generalized on the form; adding a new checkbox for "complex trauma presentation" that wasn't in the old version; and removing a deprecated diagnosis to fold it into a broader category, even though clinicians are complaining they'll lose specificity they actually use in treatment planning. I want one of these to come out in a way I'd probably push back on — maybe the procedure approves the removal despite clinician complaints, or blocks the trauma checkbox even though it sounds more humane — and say why the procedure still gives that answer. Also tell us what kinds of looping this whole thing is blind to, since I know it can't cover everything.
