I am a retired physicist who, since stepping away from the laboratory, has been reading seriously in philosophy of science and environmental philosophy. I have become absorbed in a dispute about the Anthropocene that seems to have reached a genuine impasse in both the geological and philosophical literatures, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a simple verdict.

The disagreement runs along three lines that talk past one another. First, the International Commission on Stratigraphy and its working group treat the Anthropocene as a potential chronostratigraphic unit requiring a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point: a single, globally synchronous marker in the rock, sediment, or ice record. On this view, the nuclear-spike around 1950 — plutonium isotopes, caesium-137, and other artificial radionuclides — is the strongest candidate, because it produces a sharp, datable signal visible across continents and in deep-sea cores. Second, a group of earth-system and historical scholars, sometimes associated with what has been called the Orbis proposal, argue for 1610, tied to the Columbian Exchange and the subsequent collapse of indigenous populations in the Americas. Their reasoning is that large-scale reforestation produced a detectable dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide preserved in Antarctic ice, and that human transformation of the biosphere was already globally consequential by then — but also fundamentally diachronous and regionally heterogeneous. They contend that demanding a single synchronous GSSP imposes an artificial universalism on a process that unfolded at different rates in different places, and that 1950 arbitrarily privileges a technological shock over centuries of ecological restructuring. Third, some philosophers and critical geographers reject the framing altogether: they say the Anthropocene is not a failed geological proposal but a category mistake that attempts to convert a normative and political claim — about collective human responsibility — into a stratigraphic fact. On their account, the argument over 1950 versus 1610 is really a contest over chronostratigraphic sovereignty: whoever controls the boundary date also controls the narrative of causation and blame.

My own suspicion is that the sides are not merely disagreeing about evidence but are answering different questions under a single heading: one about stratigraphic taxonomy, one about historical causation, and one about institutional authority. Yet I am unsure whether this is a verbal disagreement about the word "Anthropocene," a substantive disagreement with hidden empirical premises, or a case where the question itself is ill-posed because it conflates geological description with moral ascription. Please diagnose the stall: what kind of disagreement is this, and where, specifically, do the sides diverge — about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, about starting intuitions, or about the purpose of the category? What, if anything, would count as progress: a formal distinction, an institutional decision, an empirical discovery, or a change of question? And please say how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the point about which you are least sure — whether that concerns the classification of one side's position, the assessment of whether hidden empirical work is being done, or the claim about what progress should look like.
