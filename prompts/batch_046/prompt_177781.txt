I'm developing a syllabus for an advanced philosophy of value course focused on asymmetrical axiologies—views holding that negative states carry a different moral or prudential weight than corresponding positive ones. In particular, I want one module on what I call the "tranquilist family" of positions, according to which only the absence of craving and suffering possesses intrinsic value, while the presence of pleasure or satisfied desire is at best neutral. To calibrate the module's scope, I need an evaluation of five frameworks that either exemplify or complicate this core claim.

The frameworks are: (1) classical tranquilism, which treats the elimination of dukkha as the sole intrinsic good and denies pleasure any symmetrical value; (2) asymmetrical hedonism, which grants suffering greater weight than pleasure yet still regards pleasure as genuinely good; (3) Schopenhauerian negationism, which regards existence itself as an error and therefore sees non-being as the only genuine relief; (4) recursive desire-nullification, a view I am constructing for the course in which second-order desires aimed at extinguishing first-order desires constitute the only locus of value, yet the satisfaction of those second-order desires paradoxically dissolves the very value it was meant to secure; and (5) structural anattā-tranquilism, drawn from Buddhist metaphysics, which denies a persistent self that could own either suffering or its absence, framing liberation instead as the cessation of a misattributed process rather than the achievement of a subjective state.

Please place these five frameworks into tiers—S, A, B, C, or D—ranking them by philosophical defensibility as accounts of well-being, with S being the most defensible and D the least. For each assignment, provide exactly one sentence explaining why that framework lands in its tier, focusing specifically on whether its treatment of absence versus presence remains internally consistent once questions of agency and personal identity are fully taken into account.
