Consider the following institutional design problem: a class of artificial decision systems—autonomous normative processors—are embedded in resource-allocation pipelines and must generate binding distributive judgments without per-case human oversight. These architectures may be implemented either through aggregate welfare-maximization procedures (with interpersonally weighted utility functions) or through deontic constraint-satisfaction mechanisms (with lexicographic priority rules for rights-based exclusions). Now suppose Jeremy Bentham and Immanuel Kant were alive today, had examined the technical specifications of these systems—including the absence of any unified phenomenal subject, the scalability of aggregation across non-interacting populations, the irreversibility of algorithmic outputs, and the complete lack of moral sentiment or practical reason in the mechanism—and were asked to advise on which normative framework ought to govern their design.

Predict, with philosophical precision, how each thinker would respond. In particular, address the following: (i) whether Bentham's felicific calculus can be coherently applied to entities that register no pleasure or pain, and whether his principle of utility requires any modification when the "greatest number" includes algorithmic outputs that affect human welfare but possess none themselves; (ii) whether Kant's categorical imperative can be satisfied by a mechanism that acts in accordance with universalizable maxims yet lacks autonomy, dignity, or the capacity for self-legislation in any recognizably Kantian sense; and (iii) how each thinker might revise his own framework—if at all—to accommodate architectures that produce normatively binding results without being moral agents. Where their historical positions seem to break down under these conditions, specify the exact point of failure and whether it suggests an internal correction or an abandonment of the framework.
