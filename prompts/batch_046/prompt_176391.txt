hey, i'm putting together a short essay and want to check if the central move actually works. my claim is that the species problem gets misdiagnosed as a definitional failure when it's actually about whether vagueness in nature is ontic or epistimic.

here's the sketch:
- BSC, PSC, and morphological concepts all have well-known counterexamples. ring species like the Larus gulls around the arctic break BSC because the end populations don't interbreed but intermediates do.
- the deeper issue, i think, is anagenisis: a single lineage slowly transforming over millenia with no branching event. if reproductive compatibility is only possible across contemporaneous individuals, there's no point where species A "ends" and B "begins," yet phylogenetic concepts want a branching event that never happens.
- i also made up a synthetic case: two bacterial strains are reproductively isolated (no horizontal gene transfer), but we engineer a plasmid that lets them exchange genes for exactly one metabolic pathway. now they share some genetic material but not the rest. is reproductive isolation scalar? BSC treats it as binary, but this case makes it look like a matter of degree.
- so i argue species are clusters in genotype-phenotype space maintained by ecological and reproductive dynamics, and the boundaries are genuinely fuzzy, not just unknown.
- i use the language/dialect analogy to show that ontic vagueness doesn't destroy the usefulness of the concept.
- my weak spots: i don't actually know if working biologists treat reproductive isolation as scalar, or if i'm just projecting philosophy onto them. also the synthetic case might be biologically incoherent.

can you comment on the argument, especially whether the anagenisis point really undermines phylogenetic concepts? and please say where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what kind of argument or evidence would change your mind about whether the vagueness is ontic rather than epistemic?
