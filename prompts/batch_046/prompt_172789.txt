I've been retired from physics for about five years and lately I've been trying to wrap my head around philosphy of AI safety, mostly through some essays I found online and a seminar I audited last semester. Before I bring this up at a discussion group, I want to check whether my own commitments actually hang together or if I'm talking past myself.

Here is where I stand: (a) I think 'low-impact' AI can be rigorously defined without anyone writing down a full utility function—just measure physical deviation from the counterfactual trajectory where the AI was never built, tracking things like solar-system-scale energy flows or information-state changes. (b) But computing that counterfactual means modeling what humans and other systems would have done in its absence, so we need at least an implcit model of human goals and social dynamics. (c) I'm also convinced that any complete formal specifcation of human values is both impossible in practice and dangerous to embed in a machine. (d) At the same time, I believe that labeling something a 'side effect' is not a purely physical claim—it only makes sense relative to some normative baseline about which outcomes count as intended versus unintended. (e) Despite all that, I still want to defend the idea that we can build safe systems using impact measures without 'solving ethics' first.

Do these cohere? Which ones pull against each other and which only look bad? I'm especially worried that (a) and (d) clash—if side-effects need a normative frame, then my 'pure physical deviation' idea falls apart. And (b) seems to smuggle in the very value-specifcation that (c) rejects, though maybe that's only an apparent tension if the counterfactual model is predictive rather than normative. What's the least costly revision—dropping the physicalist framing, weakening (c), or giving up on (e)? Please rank the tensions by severety and tell me what I'd have to give up in each case. I don't want to recieve a lecture, just an honest audit of where my own view-set breaks.
