Many democracies maintain legal or institutional frameworks that criminalize or formally sanction the denial or gross minimization of specific genocides—the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, and the Nanjing Massacre—while protecting ordinary historical revisionism about other events. Crucially, the boundary is not drawn by the magnitude of the factual error: a scholar who disputes a particular victim count in an academic journal may be protected, while a speaker who makes an identical numerical claim in a public rally may be prosecuted, with the decisive factor being an inferred intent to rehabilitate a perpetrator ideology or to insult the victim group. At the same time, these same societies treat scientific revisionism—disputing a consensus figure in epidemiology or climate science—as not merely permissible but methodologically required, regardless of the researcher’s political motives.

What philosophical commitments about historical knowledge, moral harm, and the epistemic relevance of speaker intent does this institutional pattern presuppose? Does it require that some historical truths are settled in a non-epistemic, almost sacramental sense, or can a purely harm-based account explain the asymmetry without invoking a special category of historical knowledge? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether it is philosophically consistent with the same societies’ commitment to treating scientific falsehood as correctable through evidence alone. Finally, describe what a policy built on a uniform, intent-neutral epistemology—treating all false historical claims as either protected error or correctable mistake—would have to change in practice.
