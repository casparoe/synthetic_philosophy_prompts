I'm preparing a graduate seminar on historical moral responsibility and have become stuck on a disagreement that seems impervious to further archival research. One group of participants defends what they call the Fischer-line view: that the documentary record—the September Programme, the calculated risk of the blank check, and the deliberate escalation of localized conflict—demonstrates that Germany's political-military elite pursued hegemonic war aims with sufficient coherence to treat "Germany" as a morally culpable agent. The opposing group accepts the same primary sources but argues that attributing blame to the nation-state commits a category error; they emphasize the structural fragmentation of decision-making across military, diplomatic, and imperial bureaucracies, the deterministic pressure of alliance systems, and the temporal discontinuity between the regime that launched the war and subsequent German states, concluding that responsibility dissolves into distributed causal factors rather than aggregating into collective guilt.

What frustrates me is that neither side disputes the evidence; the disagreement persists despite shared factual premises. I suspect the impasse is not empirical but conceptual: specifically, whether "holding a nation responsible" requires diachronic agential continuity—the idea that moral responsibility survives institutional transformation only if there is a continuous intentional subject across time—or whether causal outcome-responsibility can attach to a territorial-political entity regardless of internal fragmentation and temporal succession. One side implicitly treats the state as a single agent extended through time; the other treats it as a series of causally linked but morally discrete temporal slices, for which only present or directly involved individuals can bear blame.

Given this framing, I want a diagnosis rather than a historical verdict. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about the semantics of collective agency, one about the pragmatics of post-war moral accounting—under the single heading of "blaming a war"? Do the sides differ about hidden normative premises regarding what counts as a moral reason for collective attribution, or about bedrock intuitions about whether groups can be agents at all? And crucially, given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress: a formal distinction between agent-responsibility and outcome-responsibility applied to states, an empirical study of how present institutions relate to past regimes, or is the framing itself so conceptually muddled that we should abandon national-level blame in favor of purely institutional or individual ascriptions?
