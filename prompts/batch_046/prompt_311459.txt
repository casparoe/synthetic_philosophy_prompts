A colleague who specializes in the philosophy of interpretation has recently shared with me her verdicts on five disputes about whether an author's intention fixes the meaning of a text. In the first, an author privately told acquaintances that her novel's ambiguous ending was meant to be hopeful, but she never stated this in print, in an interview, or in any recoverable document; my colleague ruled that the hopefulness is not part of the work's meaning and that the ambiguity stands. In the second, an author used a phrase in a public essay that differs from common usage but matches a technical vocabulary she consistently employed in earlier published essays; my colleague ruled that the technical, corpus-specific meaning governs. In the third, a published poem contains a line that breaks the meter established elsewhere, and a manuscript draft reveals that the line was originally different but was altered by a printer's error that the author never corrected; my colleague ruled that the published version remains authoritative, though the draft reveals an unintended mistake rather than an intentional meaning. In the fourth, a filmmaker released a theatrical version of a film and later said in an interview that it was a mistake, then released a director's cut that contradicts the theatrical version's central theme; my colleague ruled that the theatrical release remains the authoritative work and that the director's cut is a separate artistic object. In the fifth, a translator rendered an ambiguous word with two possible meanings, and the author later told the translator he had intended the less common one; my colleague ruled that, since the context supports both readings, the author's recoverable intention should break the tie.

Now consider a new case. A recently deceased author of interactive digital fiction left behind both a public-facing narrative file and a private source-code repository. In comments embedded in the source code—never intended for readers—she writes that the protagonist's final decision should be interpreted as resignation rather than liberation. The public-facing narrative is designed so that both readings are equally supported by its internal logic, and there is no external commentary, interview, or letter addressing the point. The source code was discovered posthumously and is now being studied by scholars.

Predict my colleague's verdict. Identify at least two distinct interpretive rules that are jointly consistent with her five earlier rulings, state what each predicts about this digital case, and say which rule you think best captures her implicit framework, with your reasons. Then propose one further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a recovered private journal that contradicts a public manifesto, or an algorithmically generated variant of a canonical text—that would best distinguish between the rules you have identified. I am less interested in the specific answer than in whether her framework is genuinely unified.
