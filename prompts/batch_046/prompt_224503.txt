In a recent argument about whether consciousness could be realized across a distributed substrate with no spatial boundary—qualitative properties instantiated over disconnected nodes yet experienced as a single unified phenomenal field—my interlocutor invoked the slogan that if something is clearly conceivable then it is genuinely possible. He used it to infer that boundless phenomenal binding is metaphysically possible, and therefore that physicalist theories requiring spatial contiguity for consciousness must be false. I suspect the move conceals several distinct claims. Could you disambiguate the precise principles that this slogan might stand for—distinguishing, for example, ideal versus limited conceivability, metaphysical versus nomological possibility, and the direction of entailment—and assess for each whether it is trivial, defensible, or false? Please also say which of these readings, if any, actually support his inference from the conceivability of distributed consciousness to the failure of physicalism, and where the argument is most vulnerable if none do.
