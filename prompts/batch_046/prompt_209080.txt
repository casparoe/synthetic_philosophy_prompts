I've been arguing with my freind about whether the UK Work Capability Assessment mess was just bad implementation or if it reveals something deeper about how welfare states actually work. For context: starting around 2008, the UK replaced old disability benefits with Employment and Support Allowance, and private contractors like Atos ran these "work capability assessments" to decide who was fit to work. There was tons of media talk about "scroungers" and benefit fraud, even though actual fraud rates were tiny, and by 2012-2015 you had rising sanctions, people being declared fit for work despite serious conditions, and eventually the contractor got replaced because of public outcry. Meanwhile politicians kept tightening "conditionality" — you had to prove you deserved support.

I'm trying to think about this through a lens I'm calling the "suspicion gradient" — the idea that modern workfare doesn't just draw a line between deserving and undeserving poor, but creates a sliding scale of moral doubt where claimants are constantly sorted by how much "effort proof" they have to perform. The gradient is administratively manufactured: bureaucratic rituals (forms, assessments, jobcentre appointments) don't just detect fraud, they produce categories of suspicion and make claimants visible as morally questionable subjects. So it's not that some people are genuinely undeserving and the system finds them; it's that the system generates gradations of undeservingness as a feature of governance.

Can you analyze the WCA episode through this lens? Specifically: how does the suspicion gradient explain the shift from medical evaluation to functional assessment, the role of private contractors, and the media rhetoric about fraud? Where does the theory miss things — maybe the actual experiences of claimants, or the political economy of austerity, or the fact that fraud was genuinely rare? And what does the WCA case show back to the theory — does it confirm that suspicion is manufactured, or does it show that the gradient collapses when public backlash gets loud enough? I want the mapping to be disciplined: don't force every detail into the framework, and say where the theory is just silent.
