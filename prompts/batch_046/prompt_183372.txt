I'm writing a dissertation chapter on what I call "teleological substitution" in East Asian governance—specifically, the institutional practice by which regimes such as Singapore and the PRC justify restrictions on multi-party competition, independent press, and free assembly not merely as necessary evils but as direct entailments of high performance on prosperity and order metrics. The practice is concrete: official government reports and Party education materials systematically present GDP growth, infrastructure development, low violent-crime rates, and bureaucratic efficiency as sufficient conditions for legitimate rule, explicitly framing them as reasons citizens ought to withhold demands for procedural authorization. At the same time, these regimes maintain rigorous meritocratic selection for civil servants—competitive examinations, performance audits, and promotion tracks tied to quantifiable outputs—thereby treating procedural fairness and competitive selection as indispensable for bureaucratic legitimacy. The result is an institutional architecture that denies citizens the very procedural mechanisms it demands for its own agents.

Or does it instead rely on a hybrid view—perhaps a threshold conception in which procedural rights become morally optional once welfare outputs cross a certain level, or a domain-specific theory that treats bureaucratic roles differently from sovereign roles? I am especially interested in whether the practice is internally consistent: can the same state coherently insist that meritocratic procedure is essential for selecting officials while maintaining that no analogous procedure is required for selecting the rulers those officials serve? Reconstruct the implicit theory of legitimacy, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and identify whether rival theories—say, a consent-based view modified by emergency conditions, or a pluralist view that weights outputs and procedures separately—could license the same institutional design without the same philosophical cost.
