Read Fight Club — the movie, not the book, though you can bring in the novel if it helps — through the lens of split-brain cases and what philosophers call the unity of consciousness. The twist that Tyler and the Narrator are the same person is usually explained away as dissociative identity disorder, but I want you to treat it like a philosophical experiment: in real split-brain patients, cutting the corpus callosum can produce two hemispheres with different goals, and some philosophers say that means there are literally two subjects of experience in one skull. So does the film dramatize that kind of split, or is it just one guy with amnesia and a very convincing confabulation? Think specifcally about Gazzaniga's idea of the left-hemisphere interpreter — the part of the brain that insists on stitching a coherent story even when it doesn't have the data. Is the Narrator's big realization at the end an interpreter catching up, or is it one consciousness finally seeing thier other? The movie also cheats in interesting ways: Tyler and the Narrator seem to have separate visual perspectives in some scenes, which never happens in actual split-brain cases. Where does that break the analogy, and where does it still illuminate something about unity? I don't want a bibliography or citation list — just a serious reading that takes both the film and the neuroscience/philosophy on thier own terms, and maybe says what the movie adds to the debate that a lab report can't. If you teach this in a class, point to the specific scenes you'd use.
