I've been reading philosophy of art since I retired from physics last year, and my neighbor and I got into a argument about originals versus copies. I tried to write out my reasoning and now it feels like it proves way too much, but I can't find the exit. Here's the chain. (1) The value of any artwork must supervene on its intrinsic physical properties or on relational properties we actually care about. (2) A perfect forgery shares every intrinsic physical property with the original — same pigments, same brushstroke paterns, same chemical aging, all detectable. (3) So if there's a value difference, it has to be relational: specifically, the causal link back to the artist's intention. (4) But treating that bare causal link as valuable, when it changes nothing about experience or function, is just a kind of relic-fetishism; it's like valuing a stone because it was once near a battle. (5) So originals and perfect forgeries are equal in artistic value. (6) By the same token, the value of a historical artifact — say, the actual moon flag or a handwritten letter — either supervenes on its informational content and appearance, or on its bare provenance. (7) Once we have perfect replicas, preserving the original is just hoarding dead weight. (8) Therefore museums and archives should destroy originals after digitizing or replicating them, with no real loss. (9) But that can't be right — I wouldn't want them burning the original Declaration of Independence even with a perfect scan. So where does this slip? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) because they feel like the physicalist instincts I carried over from science, but I'm open to being told I'm smuggling in an ambiguity about "value" or "supervenience." Please tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked okay, and price out what I have to give up if I drop it.
