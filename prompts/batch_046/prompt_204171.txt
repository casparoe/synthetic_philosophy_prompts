I am preparing a 90-minute advanced undergraduate seminar on collective memory and political myth for 14 students. Design a structured in-class activity—no up-front lecture—that lets participants discover for themselves the structural tension peculiar to lost-cause narratives: the simultaneous insistence that a historical defeat is permanently unredeemed (to sustain collective grievance) and already redeemed (to legitimate present authority).

Create a fictional case, the failed “Winter Compact” secession of 1891, documented through eight brief “archival fragments” printed on cards. The fragments should contain contradictory evidence—rebel leaders’ economic motives, a post-war collaboration treaty, documented civilian suffering, and an unfulfilled amnesty promise—without resolving into a single story. Divide the class into four commissions of 3–4 students, each assigned a hidden institutional mandate: state legitimacy, reconciliation with the victorious power, cultural tourism, or archival fidelity.

Rules and task: each commission has 40 minutes to design one public remembrance ritual, one monument inscription, and one curriculum paragraph. They must cite exactly four fragments and, on a shared visible board, publicly justify the exclusion of the remaining four; each exclusion requires conceding one specific historical claim that weakens their narrative. Award reputation points through peer vote for narrative coherence, not for historical completeness.

After brief presentations, reveal the mandates. The debrief (20 minutes) should map which fragments each mandate suppressed and why; demonstrate how the same defeat was narrated as irreversible tragedy by one group and redeemed foundation by another; and name the temporal asymmetry at work. Be explicit about which feature of actual lost-cause mythology—e.g., the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, or Dunkirk—this reproduces, and which feature it cannot reproduce: specifically, the multi-generational transmission of myth outside institutional design, which the facilitator must supply verbally.

Specify the minute-by-minute timeline, the card materials, the incentive structure, what will predictably go wrong the first time (students treating hidden mandates as puzzles to solve rather than commitments to defend, correctable by requiring exclusion justifications to be delivered as public speeches), and the honest limitation that the rules alone cannot generate lived intergenerational belief. Keep everything runnable with only printed cards and a board.
