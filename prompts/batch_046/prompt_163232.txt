I'm writing a feature on how scientists defend models built on false premises, and I want to pilot a workshop design for readers who teach philosophy of science. For a 90-minute session with 18 graduate students and science journalists, design a structured simulation—call it "The Credibility Market"—that makes participants feel the pull of Friedman's F-twist, the claim that wildly unrealistic assumptions can be virtues rather than vices when predictive power is the sole criterion, without anyone lecturing them about it first.

Divide participants into six teams of three. Give each team a hidden socio-ecological dataset revealed only after submissions, plus a deck of "micro-assumption cards" listing claims such as "agents have perfect foresight," "natural capital is infinitely substitutable," or "preference orderings are complete and transitive." Every card displays a realism rating, which affects nothing, and carries a hidden predictive coefficient revealed only during scoring. Teams spend a fixed budget of credibility tokens to purchase cards, combine them into a predictive model, and submit forecasts. The rules reward only out-of-sample accuracy.

Inject one contrived pairing—an "infinite elasticity of substitution" card matched with a "zero social discount rate" card—that is jointly incoherent in reality yet mathematically complementary in the hidden scoring function. This pairing will dominate the leaderboard, forcing winning teams to defend a mechanism story that they know describes no actual system. Before the reveal, each team delivers a 30-second "mechanism narrative" explaining how their model works in the world; after scoring, require them to defend whether that narrative survives the cards they actually played.

The debrief should map these moments onto Friedman's instrumental view of assumptions, onto the hidden normative discomfort that makes teams hoard credibility tokens on realistic cards despite the rules, and onto the selection of theory by predictive success alone. Note what will predictably go wrong—participants will weaken their scores out of professional pride by avoiding absurd cards—and instruct the facilitator to freeze the budget rules firmly. Finally, be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: the long-run, cross-domain iterative testing that distinguishes predictive success from explanatory depth in actual scientific practice. The facilitator must supply that distinction verbally, since a single-round game can stage the tension but cannot resolve it.
