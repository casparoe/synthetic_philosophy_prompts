Benjamin Franklin maintained a lifelong practice of tracking thirteen virtues in a pocket ledger, rotating his exclusive focus through them one per week and marking each day's failures with black dots—treating moral improvement as a quantifiable, modular project in which progress could be tallied like accounts. What philosophical commitments about virtue, self-knowledge, and moral change does this institutionalized routine presuppose? Must it assume that virtues are separable competencies that can be trained in isolation, or is it compatible with a unitary view—say, that practical wisdom governs all particular excellences and cannot be partitioned? Does the ledger method commit him to an externalist epistemology of the self, where one's moral standing is determined by observable behavioral counts rather than by introspective judgment? And is the system consistent with his reported failure ever to master any virtue completely, or does that failure expose a tension in the quantificational model? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice strictly requires it or is compatible with rival views, and indicate what in Franklin's method would have to be abandoned if the underlying philosophy were replaced by a Stoic or Aristotelian framework.
