I have been trying to work out, from my own reflections rather than from books, when a human life counts as genuinely meaningful rather than merely pleasant or busy, and whether the absurdists are right that the universe offers none. Below are my verdicts on particular lives; I want to know whether one principle captures them all.

Case A: A woman spends forty years cataloguing every bird call in a forest that will be logged the year after she dies. She knows this, yet feels her work profoundly meaningful.

Case B: A man lives solely to maximise the comfort of strangers he will never meet, leaving no record and expecting no thanks; he reports this fills his life with purpose.

Case C: Someone achieves every conventional marker of success—wealth, recognition, family—but describes an underlying sense that none of it matters, as though he were playing a role in a play nobody watches.

Case D: A monk devotes his life to prayer for the salvation of souls he is certain do not exist; he finds this deeply meaningful, though he admits the object of his prayer is illusory.

Case E: A person spends each day maintaining a complex machine whose only function is to preserve itself in perfect equilibrium. It serves no human need, will be dismantled after her death, and she is the only person who understands its workings. She says this is the most meaningful thing she could do.

Case F: A scientist discovers a fundamental law of nature, but the discovery is immediately classified and erased; she alone remembers it, and she dies without telling anyone. She considered the moment of discovery the peak meaning of her life.

My verdicts are these. A and B are meaningful despite being futile or invisible. C is not genuinely meaningful despite its success. D is meaningful even though its belief is false. E strikes me as meaningful, though I cannot explain why. F is meaningful, but less so than A, because the knowledge dies with her and touches no one.

The case I keep going back and forth on is Case G: a person lives only to extend her own lifespan as long as possible, with no other projects, and succeeds in reaching two hundred years. Is that meaningful, or is it merely prolonged existence?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about subjective fulfilment, objective worth, or some mixture—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why? Please state precisely what the resulting principle predicts for Case G, and also for a situation I have not listed: someone whose sole purpose is to replicate, every day, the exact routine of a dead stranger whose life she never knew.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write in British English.
