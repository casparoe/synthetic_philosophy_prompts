I'm organizing a reading-group session on social ontology and want to stage an adversarial collaboration between two opposing interpretations of Hegel's master-slave dialectic. The first camp—the intersubjective-recognition camp—maintains that the dialectic is driven primarily by the normative failure of the master to grant the slave equal status as a self-conscious being, and that genuine freedom requires symmetrical, face-to-face mutual acknowledgment. The second camp—the practical-transformation camp—maintains that the dialectic's central achievement is the slave's development of self-certainty through labor upon and transformation of the objective world, and that any recognition received from the master is either structurally impossible or philosophically secondary to that practical mastery.

Please draft the joint document these camps could sign after good-faith engagement. Structure it as follows. First, a numbered list of agreed claims about the dialectic's architecture—what both sides accept about risk, dependence, labor, and the transformed object—phrased in neutral terms without attributing them to particular scholars or editions. Second, a disagreements table setting out each camp's core position in wording that its own proponents would endorse. Third, identify the cruxes: in particular, whether recognition can be mediated by an institutional or bureaucratic framework—such as a legal contract, professional credential, or organizational role that confers status without direct interpersonal encounter—and still count as the recognition the dialectic demands; and whether the slave can achieve full self-certainty in the absence of any acknowledgment from another consciousness whatsoever. For each crux, describe one concrete institutional or workplace scenario both sides would accept as relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts that scenario demonstrates. Finally, provide a brief residue report: determine whether any portion of their dispute is purely verbal—hinging on different uses of recognition—or whether the disagreement carries genuine explanatory and normative weight, and note which feature of your constructed case does the philosophical work. Write the response in American English, avoid bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists, and do not quote Hegel's text verbatim; paraphrase any specific conceptual claims in your own language.
