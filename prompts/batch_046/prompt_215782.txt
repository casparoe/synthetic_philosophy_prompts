I am a retired experimental physicist who, over the past several years, has gradually shifted my attention from laboratory work to the philosophy of science—specifically to the problem of how rational agents ought to align their beliefs with physical probabilities.

I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop entitled "Credence, Chance, and Calibration," where the audience will be divided between philosophers of science and practicing physicists who have little patience for purely textual scholarship. I want the speech to take as its organizing idea the Principal Principle—the thesis that subjective credence should defer to known objective chance—but I do not want it to function as mere philosophical decoration. Rather, I want to structure the remarks around an analogy drawn from my own career: the calibration of a stochastic detection apparatus, in which prior beliefs about instrument reliability must be tuned against statistically known distributions. The central tension, which should govern what the speech can and cannot claim, is a circularity I have been discussing with a colleague in quantum foundations: if the objective chances used to calibrate the detector are themselves inferred from measurements made by that same detector, then the demand for deference to chance seems to presuppose the very credences it is meant to discipline. This observation should be woven in without relying on obscure quantum formalism or technical literature. The address should run about five minutes, maintain a formal but conversational tone suitable to a retired scientist speaking to mixed experts, and it must avoid bibliographic references, citation lists, or appeals to texts the physicists in the room are unlikely to have encountered. Please write these opening remarks so that they genuinely illuminate the difficulty of rational belief-formation in empirical practice rather than simply announcing a philosophical topic.
