I help organize an informal neighborhood repair circle where people bring broken household appliances and we try to restore them together in a shared workshop space. Recently I have been in a sustained disagreement with one of our most experienced regulars about what actually explains why we succeed. He insists that if you trace every completed repair back to its source—who learned soldering from online tutorials, who retains specific wiring diagrams, who has an intuitive feel for mechanical failure—then you have fully accounted for what happened. In his view, speaking of a "group knowledge" or a "collective repair instinct" is merely poetic language for summed individual competencies, and planning our gatherings around the idea that we possess some shared mental resource is like organizing for a ghost. I feel there is a serious flaw in this reasoning, but I cannot quite name it.

What makes me hesitate is a pattern I have observed with old tube amplifiers. There is a specific restoration procedure that requires three of us to work in complete silence, each adjusting a different internal component according to a rhythm that seems to coordinate itself without anyone directing it. If I ask any one participant afterward to describe the full sequence, none of us can reconstruct it alone; and if we replace one of us with an outside technician who has far more formal electronics training but has never worked in our circle, the rhythm collapses and the repair fails, even though the replacement understands every individual part in isolation. My friend says this is nothing more than averaged individual guesses reinforced by social habit, and that treating the rhythm as a real capability worth protecting is sentimental. I am trying to decide how to structure our next meeting—whether I should design activities that deliberately preserve this silent, distributed procedure, or whether I should accept his view that I am only nurturing a fiction and should instead focus entirely on upgrading each person's individual skills. Is he right that breaking everything down into parts kills the explanation of the whole, or am I justified in thinking that something genuine survives the breakdown?
