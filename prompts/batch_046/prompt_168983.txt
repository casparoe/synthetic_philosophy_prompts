Before committing to any framework that treats moral status as continuous rather than binary, I want the full specification of what such a theory must deliver. I am thinking specifically of a scale running from entities with rich phenomenal consciousness through entities with disputed or minimal experience—such as insects with distributed nociception but no centralized cortical integration—down to entities with functional analogues but no clear inner life, including a synthetic homeostat equipped with predictive-processing loops and reinforcement-learning-based temperature regulation that mimics aversive conditioning without any centralized representational state. The purpose is practical deliberation: the theory must guide harm-avoidance and resource allocation without requiring metaphysical certainty about consciousness.

Write the desiderata—what an adequate gradational theory of moral status must be able to do—organized by the motivating cases and considerations behind each. For every requirement, specify whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. In particular, include:

- The phenomenological gradation requirement: how the theory distinguishes degrees of suffering or well-being across organisms with different neural architectures, using the contrast between sustained mammalian distress and reflexive insect nociception as the motivating case, and whether it can avoid collapsing that distinction into mere quantitative intensity.
- The synthetic-homeostat boundary case: a principle that places the reinforcement-learning thermostat—functional aversion, no integrated information architecture—relative to a simple biological reflex organism and to an insect with distributed nociception, with the reasoning behind each placement and without anthropomorphizing the machine.
- The biological-consistency constraint: comparative neurobiological and evolutionary results the scale must accommodate—such as the absence of cortex in insects, the presence of nociceptors across diverse phyla, and the adaptive value of avoidance behavior—without reducing moral status to taxonomic rank or neural complexity alone.
- The scalar-arithmetic condition: whether the theory must supply a commensurable metric for aggregating or comparing harms across entities, or can remain ordinal, with the case behind the demand being a conflict in which one must choose between preventing severe harm to one entity and preventing mild harm to many.
- The action-guidance criterion: that the scale yield determinate recommendations in practical conflicts—e.g., pest management versus ecosystem preservation—rather than leaving every decision to case-by-case intuition.
