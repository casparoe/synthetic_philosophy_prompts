For my philosophy of mind course, we have been examining Douglas Hofstadter's discussion of collective cognition in ant colonies, including the Aunt Hillary scenario and the ant fugue analogy, as a test case for whether distributed biological systems can possess a unified mind. I am constructing an argument about how Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz might have responded to this problem had he encountered contemporary descriptions of social-insect intelligence. Leibniz distinguishes sharply between mere aggregates, which are only phenomenally unified, and true substances, which possess a single dominant entelechy or soul. Modern accounts describe ant colonies achieving integrated problem-solving—resource allocation, nest defense, and path optimization—through decentralized pheromonal feedback loops rather than through any central controller, yielding emergent dynamics that some researchers compare to distributed processing in nervous systems. Given these descriptions, would Leibniz likely have judged a mature colony to be a genuine substance with its own windowless soul, or merely an aggregate of individual ant-monads? More specifically, if he applied his criterion that true unity requires a single dominant monad perceiving the whole, would the absence of any anatomical or functional queen-brain directing the colony's cognition lead him to deny collective consciousness, even if the system's informational integration rivals that of a simple vertebrate nervous system? Please defend your interpretation by appealing to his metaphysical commitments—particularly his criteria for substancehood and the role of a dominant monad—rather than by appealing to modern intuition alone.
