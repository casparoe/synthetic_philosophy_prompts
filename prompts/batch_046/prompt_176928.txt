I am delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on moral responsibility within algorithmically distributed bureaucracies—systems in which no individual participant possesses full knowledge of how a decision is reached, yet collectively they produce severe, predictable harm. The audience is deliberately mixed: moral philosophers familiar with Hannah Arendt's study of Adolf Eichmann and her concept of the banality of evil; organizational sociologists who work on epistemic fragmentation in large institutions; and family members of people who suffered under precisely such distributed decision processes. The remarks should run about twelve minutes. They must treat Arendt's framework not as a slogan but as a live diagnostic tool: specifically, explore whether the kind of thoughtlessness Arendt identified in Eichmann can be structurally reproduced when cognitive labor is partitioned across human and automated agents, or whether distributed ignorance introduces a different moral pathology altogether. The speech should be accessible to the non-philosophers without being condescending to them, and it should avoid generic memorial language about remembrance. It should also explicitly identify and dissolve one misconception that philosophers in the room are likely to hold about bureaucratic harm—perhaps that it is always reducible either to individual vice or to systemic design flaws—while showing why that misconception specifically blocks understanding of the suffering present in the room. Write the remarks so that Arendt's analysis shapes what can and cannot be said, rather than serving as decoration.
