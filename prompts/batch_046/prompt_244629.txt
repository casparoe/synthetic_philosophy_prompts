I've been working through the implications of Bostrom's simulation trilemma for practical ethics, specifically for the extreme longtermist view that assigns overwhelming moral urgency to shaping the distant future—what some call far-future fanaticism. I find myself drawn to a position that runs contrary to much recent discussion: rather than being ethically irrelevant, the simulation argument should actually dampen longtermist urgency. The claim is not the crude version—that we are probably simulated so our actions do not matter—but a more structured one. If there is a non-trivial probability that we inhabit an ancestor simulation run by a technologically mature civilization, then our causal influence on base-reality outcomes in the distant epoch is mediated through an opaque layer of simulation architecture. The expected value of trying to steer the "real" post-human future therefore collapses relative to goods internal to the simulation, such as reducing present suffering or preserving knowledge accessible to the simulators. Conversely, if we are in base reality, the standard longtermist calculus applies, but the simulation possibility introduces an epistemic discount that should weaken our confidence in far-future projections without generating any compensatory urgency. I want a disciplined assessment of what would have to be false for this position to fail. Specifically: what role does the ancestor-simulation versus branch-simulation distinction play, and could a high proportion of branch simulations rescue longtermist urgency? How should moral weight be distributed between simulated and base-reality moral patients—does upward propagation of moral status salvage the argument? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, for instance by allowing that even nested simulations generate real obligations to distant real-world descendants? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly whether the position risks being insulated by ad hoc adjustments in a way that undermines its normative force.
