The exam question was: Some research finds that in more gender-egalitarian countries (like Scandinavian ones), career choices are actually MORE gender-segregated—women go into healthcare/education, men into STEM/engineering—while in less egalitarian countries the split is smaller. Philosophers call this the gender-equality paradox. First, explain the two main competing explanations: one that says this reveals natural/biological differences once social pressure is lifted, and one that says egalitarian societies just allow people to express gendered preferences shaped by culture when they don't have to worry about survival/economics. Then, discuss what this implies for justice: does the paradox weaken the moral case for pushing gender parity in STEM?

Here is the student answer we were looking at:

The gender-equality paradox refers to the finding that countries with stronger gender-equality policies often show larger gender gaps in career choice, especially in STEM versus care-oriented fields. One explanation is biological: once economic pressure and legal barriers are removed, innate sex differences in interests and abilities become visible. The other explanation is cultural: in wealthy egalitarian societies, people have the luxury to choose jobs that align with gendered social identities, whereas in poorer societies economic survival forces people into whatever pays best regardless of gender norms. So the paradox actually supports both views partially.

As for justice, if the differences are revealed preferences under freedom, then pushing for strict parity in STEM might be paternalistic. The philosopher Amartya Sen warned about adaptive preferences—people wanting what they're socialized to want—but in egalitarian societies those socialized wants are expressed freely, not under coercion. Therefore, justice should focus on ensuring equal opportunity and removing barriers, not on equal outcomes in every field. The biological explanation is stronger here because cultural explanations can't account for why the gap widens specifically in the most progressive countries. So while we should fight discrimination, we shouldn't treat career segregation itself as an injustice.
