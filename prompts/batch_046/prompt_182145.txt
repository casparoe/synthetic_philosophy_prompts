hey so I'm prepping a talk on Amadeus — the 84 Milos Forman one — and I keep circling this weird ethics problem. I call it posthumous narrative debt: the idea that when you dramatize dead people who cant talk back, you owe them more than just not lying. You owe them a kind of structual fairness about what kind of story there life gets folded into. In Amadeus, Mozart is this genius-idiot who dies broke and forgotten, while Salieri is the competent mediocrity who lives to tell the tale. Both are real dead guys. I want you to read the film through that lens: where does it treat Mozart's actual suffering as just color for Salieri's jealousy? Where does it let Mozart be a full person despite the myth? Where does the film resist the framing altogether? And is there a real difference between harming the dead, which sounds metaphysically dodgy, and just using them as props for a theme about genius vs mediocrity? Don't bother with citations or a reading list — I just want an honest take on whether the movie gets away with something because Mozart definately can't object. If you can point to specific scenes where the debt gets paid or ignored, great, but paraphrase anything from the movie — don't quote dialogue from memory. Also, does Amadeus actually show us something about this debt idea that a straight philosophy paper wouldn't catch?
