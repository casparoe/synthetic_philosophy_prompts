I recently lost my father after a prolonged illness, and while organizing his study I came across a well-annotated copy of A. J. Ayer’s memoir alongside marginal notes on verificationism. That discovery has made me want to understand, with real precision, whether Ayer’s reported near-death experience genuinely disturbed his earlier logical-positivist dismissal of survival, or whether it can be fully absorbed within his empiricist semantics without doxastic revision. I am preparing to moderate a colloquium interview with a scholar who defends the latter, stricter interpretation—namely, that Ayer never abandoned the view that talk of an afterlife is cognitively meaningless, and that he merely recorded anomalous first-person phenomenology without granting it evidential weight. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual mechanics of this reading rather than on biography. I want at least one question on how the verification principle applies to first-person reports of post-mortem consciousness; one on whether the red-light episode Ayer describes can be parsed as non-propositional phenomenology rather than as evidence for survival; and one on how this interpretation handles Ayer’s explicit admission that the experience left him somewhat less dogmatic about death being the absolute end. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give—particularly the deflection that describing what it is like to undergo an anomaly need not entail any ontological commitment about what survives it. Keep the discussion focused on the tension between his empiricist theory of meaning and the first-person data he himself recorded; avoid generic opening questions about his career or his relationship to the Vienna Circle.
