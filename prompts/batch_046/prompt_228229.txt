Write a dialogue between a defender of Popper's poverty of historicism and a proponent of "computational historicism"—the view that large-scale predictive modeling of historical data reveals probabilistic but genuinely lawlike structural regularities, such as institutional path dependence or demographic transition dynamics, that support counterfactual prediction across contexts. The Popperian should argue that historical trends depend on the unpredictable growth of knowledge and that statistical patterns in past data are not laws but context-bound trends that collapse when initial conditions fail to repeat. The computational historicist should reply that these models identify invariant relations independent of particular knowledge-states, and should specifically address whether dependence on knowledge-growth undermines all structural regularities or only a subset. The Popperian, in turn, should respond directly to that distinction and to the claim that probabilistic invariance escapes the poverty critique. Both interlocutors should be equally rigorous, avoid straw-manning, and clarify whether their residual disagreement concerns the definition of "law," the possibility of historical prediction, or the uniqueness of historical initial conditions. End without resolution, but with the precise crux—whether lawhood requires determinism or merely stable counterfactual support—explicitly isolated.
