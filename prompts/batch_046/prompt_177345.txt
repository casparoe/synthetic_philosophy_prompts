I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, and next month I will lead a single ninety-minute session for a small circle of retired scientists and clinicians on eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires. Do not explain the view to me; instead, plan exactly how I should teach it. Lay out the dependency order of the ideas—what groundwork must be in place before eliminativism sounds like a live option rather than a variant of behaviorism—and specify the one historical analogy that should carry the explanation, stating what properties it must have and what it must lack (for instance, why Churchland’s comparison to witchcraft or demonology is likely to misfire with this audience, and whether the elimination of vital force in biology or of phlogiston in chemistry is the stronger carrier). Map the misconceptions they will form in the order they are likely to arise—I expect confusion between eliminativism and the denial of consciousness, then between elimination and reduction to neural states, and finally the assumption that neuroscience has already replaced folk psychology—and indicate precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted. Provide one diagnostic question for the end that can distinguish grasping the dispensability of propositional-attitude ontology from merely accepting that neuroscience is powerful. State explicitly what should be left out at this level—such as connectionist architecture details or the phenomenal-concepts debate—and why. Finally, please say how confident you are in this plan and single out the point about which you are least sure.
