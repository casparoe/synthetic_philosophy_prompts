He claims that when you do this, you're still forming an idea of the tree in your own mind, so you haven't really conceived it as unpercieved — you've only conceived it as percieved by you. The hidden move is that conceiving always involves having an idea present to consciousness, and ideas can't exist without a mind to hold them.

A standard objection is that Berkeley mixes up the mental act with its content. I might be thinking about an unpercieved tree, but that doesn't mean my thought of the tree is part of the tree's nature. So the objection targets his slide from 'I can't conceive it without conceiving it' to 'it can't exist without being conceived.'

Berkeley could respond by saying that even primery qualities like shape and extension are really just ideas, so the objector hasn't escaped the net. But I think he actually saves that reply for a different problem — the one about objects persisting when we leave the room — and answers this specific conceivability worry by appealing to God's infinite mind.
