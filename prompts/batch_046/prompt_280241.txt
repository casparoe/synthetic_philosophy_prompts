I've been trying to understand the argument that insects could matter morally in vast numbers even if any single insect is very unlikely to be conscious. In discussions I've seen, people compare this to playing a lottery: each insect is like a ticket with a minuscule chance of "winning" sentience, but because humanity interacts with trillions of them, we are effectively holding an enormous number of tickets, so the expected amount of suffering or consciousness is massive. This is used to argue that insect welfare should be treated as a serious ethical priority despite the low per-insect probability. Can you map out the correspondence precisely — what plays the role of the ticket, the prize, the buyer, the payout, and the decision to keep playing? Then show where the analogy breaks: for instance, whether moral consideration works like expected-value calculation, whether "holding" an insect is like purchasing a ticket, and whether the analogy quietly assumes that probabilities aggregate linearly across individuals. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that we ought to prioritize insect suffering survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the lottery framing was carrying the argument all along.
