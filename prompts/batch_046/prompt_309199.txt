A philosopher I follow on rationalist forums has given consistent verdicts on epistemic architectures: direct perceptual beliefs need no further justification and count as justified; beliefs supported only by an endless non-repeating chain of reasons do not, since no finite mind can hold the whole chain; beliefs locked in a tight circular loop of three mutually supporting propositions are justified, provided each link carries independent plausibility; beliefs in loops that contain one unjustified but reliable "bridge" belief are not—she treats these as smuggled foundations; and beliefs formed by self-monitoring methods are justified only when the monitoring does not presuppose the belief being monitored. Now consider a new architecture: a belief sits in a four-node loop where one node is a "damping belief"—it receives full support from its neighbors but returns only a fraction, so that total circulating support converges to a stable equilibrium rather than looping endlessly or terminating in a base. The belief is true, each link is independently plausible, and no node is externally anchored. Would she judge it justified? Which precedent—the small loop, the rejected bridge-loop, or the self-monitoring case—is the closest analog? Identify the competing rules that fit her track record (for instance, a pure coherentist view, an anti-circularity view with an exception for tight loops, and an infinitist view that accepts convergence to a limit), say what each predicts here, give your best guess with reasoning, and specify the further case that would distinguish them.
