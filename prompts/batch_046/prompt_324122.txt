I'm giving a session to first-year philosophy students on supererogation — acts that are genuinely good but not actually required. I need the teaching plan, not the lecture itself: the dependancy chain of ideas (what has to be in place before the next thing lands), the one carrying example and what it must have and lack, the misconceptions they'll show up with in order and where each gets headed off, the check at the end that proves they've grasped the category rather than just feeling inspired, and what I should deliberately leave out at this level. The audience walks in thinking morality is just "don't hurt people" plus "be nice when it costs nothing." I want the carrying example to be mundane, not heroic rescue — maybe someone who spends every Saturday for years tutouring a struggling neighbor kid, giving up leisure that was genuinely theirs to keep. How do I build the arc so they don't collapse supererogation into either "hidden duty" or "just being a good person"? What misconceptions should I anticipate about moral accounting — like the idea that good deeds earn you credits? And how do I know they've actually understood the boundary, not just that they like kindness?
