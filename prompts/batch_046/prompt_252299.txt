I have recently taken over drafting long-range investment guidelines for a family foundation intended to operate across at least the next century, and since my daughter was born eighteen months ago the problem of intergenerational obligation has stopped being abstract. I am applying for a six-month independent-study fellowship and need a polished, two-page research proposal on pure time preference and the discounting of future welfare. The proposal should frame the question with precision: can pure time preference be defended as a rational structural feature of diachronic agency—analogous to personal partiality but operating across time—without committing us, in moral or institutional contexts, to discounting the welfare of future people, and does the standard separation of descriptive from normative discounting survive once we rigorously distinguish impatience from risk- or uncertainty-based adjustments?

The proposal must explain why the existing positions leave this open. The literature ranges from Ramsey’s dismissal of discounting future utility as practically irrational, through Parfit’s arguments for temporal neutrality, to the economic pragmatism of social discount-rate frameworks; yet these treatments tend either to reduce pure time preference to empirical psychology or to treat it as a straightforward moral error. Few examine whether it can function differently in first-person practical reasoning—where an agent’s continuity conditions might license preferential weighting of nearer goods—than in third-person moral evaluation of climate or institutional policy, where such weighting seems to violate intergenerational equality. Identify this gap explicitly and argue why it is not settled by the standard literature.

State the approach you would actually take, month by month, over the six-month period: which conceptual distinctions you would build (for example, between impatience, presentism about value, and metaphysical commitments to temporal parts), what argumentative tests you would apply to the diachronic-agency analogy, and why this philosophical method is preferable to an empirical survey of time-preference experiments or to a formal economic optimization model. Specify what a successful deliverable looks like: not a bibliography or literature review, which I will supply myself, but a clear argumentative framework that distinguishes permissible impatience in personal life-planning from impermissible discounting in institutional policy, together with a precise condition under which that distinction collapses. Give an honest assessment of what would count as failure—for instance, if the diachronic-agency analogy requires a metaphysics of temporal parts that turns out to be either indefensible or redundant with existing accounts of personal identity. Keep the scope realistic for part-time work across six months, and do not include citations, reading lists, or requests for source verification; the deliverable should stand on its conceptual architecture alone.
