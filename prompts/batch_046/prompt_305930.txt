Construct a thought experiment showing that self-deception is structurally possible only under what I will call intra-agent epistemic delegation: within a unitary agent, one cognitive routine is granted evidential authority over a domain while another is systematically excluded from revising beliefs in that domain, with no modular brain separation, split-brain condition, or explicit act of lying to oneself. The scenario must be realistic (a workplace or domestic setting, not a laboratory or sci-fi intervention), confined to a single paragraph, and must avoid Freudian mechanisms. Engineer the case so that an intentionalist theory of self-deception can treat it as genuine, while a deflationary theory that reduces the phenomenon to mere motivated belief-formation fails because it cannot register the authority-delegation structure. After presenting the scenario, walk through: (a) the belief, the delegated routine, and the excluded routine; (b) why the intentionalist verdict follows; (c) why the deflationary account misses the structure; (d) which feature of the case does the theoretical work; and (e) the deflationist's best reply that you have cheated by smuggling intentional architecture into the description, together with your assessment of whether removing the delegation feature collapses the case into ordinary irrationality.
