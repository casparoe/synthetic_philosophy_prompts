As a novelist, I am preoccupied with the moment when a character's performed self cracks open and something more fundamental shows through—often under alcohol, often against their will. Plato seems to have shared this fascination: in the Laws he permits, even carefully regulates, symposia not despite intoxication's dangers but apparently because of them, while elsewhere warning that wine exaggerates whatever is already present in the soul. Write an essay on the central interpretive controversy: does Plato treat controlled drunkenness primarily as a diagnostic instrument for exposing hidden character—a kind of civic stress-test that reveals which citizens possess stable virtue and which do not—or as a communal ritual whose value lies in producing shared vulnerability and binding citizens together rather than in individual revelation? Weigh both readings against Plato's broader psychology of education and his suspicion of theatrical deception, but do not end in ambiguity; defend a specific verdict about which interpretation is more defensible. In particular, consider whether the "diagnostic" reading implicitly assumes that alcohol reveals an essential self lying beneath social performance, or whether Plato instead understands intoxication as disclosing not a hidden essence but the unguarded trajectory of habituated desire—something closer to a novelist's sense that character is not concealed behind a mask but enacted differently when the script of self-presentation fails. Reach and justify your conclusion.
