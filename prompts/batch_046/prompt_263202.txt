I'm preparing materials for an advanced seminar on political philosophy, and I'm trying to map the conceptual space around a problem that sits awkwardly between standard communitarian and liberal frameworks: the normative status of inherited, non-voluntary obligations tied to cultural transmission—specifically obligations regarding language preservation, ritual participation, and intergenerational continuity—when they come into direct conflict with an individual's autonomous life plan. Rather than asking students to identify with "communitarianism" or "liberalism," I want a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts actual positions by the answers people give to concrete branching questions.

Please construct a taxonomy with four or five diagnostic branches, each phrased as a concrete case or commitment rather than a theoretical label. The first branch should ask whether being born into a practice-generating community creates any special obligation to maintain that practice in the absence of explicit consent; use a case like a fluent speaker of a minority language choosing not to teach it to her children. The second branch should split those who answer yes by whether the obligation is owed to the community as a collective entity or only derivatively to specific individuals who depend on her participation—illustrate with a seasonal communal ritual that conflicts with an individual's career. The third branch should ask whether state protection of such practices can ever include coercion aimed at preserving the practice itself, not merely preventing harm; frame this around mandatory cultural education policies. The fourth branch should ask whether partial assimilation extinguishes the obligation entirely or merely modifies its weight—use a case of someone who adopts the community's ethical norms but rejects its religious practices.

The tree should lead to named or unnamed leaves: include pure liberal voluntarism, strong communitarian collective obligation, liberal multicultural accommodation, republican civic integration, and at least one hybrid or unoccupied combination. For any combination that no major position occupies, briefly diagnose whether it seems incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Flag any positions that the tree's logic cannot cleanly separate—such as views that accept collective obligations but reject state coercion for preservation reasons—rather than forcing them into separate leaves. The deliverable should be usable by someone with no prior knowledge of the theoretical labels, and it should be written in American English.
