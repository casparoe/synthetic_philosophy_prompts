I am drafting a philosophical dialogue for a novel about a surgeon who believes suffering is an optional attachment rather than a necessary feature of harm. To sharpen one scene, I need an argument that appears to prove that pain — and eventually every mental state — lacks intrinsic badness, then its precise failure point. The reasoning should feel rigorous enough to unsettle a reader, not like a caricature.

Consider this chain:

(1) Documented cases of pain asymbolia show that full nociceptive registration — tissue-damage signalling, spatial localisation, and even sincere verbal reports of 'hurting' — can occur with no negative affect and no avoidance behaviour.

(2) If pain possessed intrinsic badness, that badness would have to belong to its affective or motivational dimension, since the bare sensory-discriminative content is, in these cases, experienced as neutral information.

(3) If a property F is intrinsic to a state S, then F is present in every genuine instance of S.

(4) In asymbolia, the state that subjects identify as 'pain' — the sensory-discriminative state — occurs, yet its supposed intrinsic badness is entirely absent.

(5) Therefore that sensory state is not intrinsically bad.

(6) But this sensory state is all that pain essentially is; the affective response is a contingent, separable overlay — call it an 'algic commitment' that the subject either makes or withholds.

(7) Hence pain itself is not intrinsically bad.

(8) Applying the same dissociation logic to grief, nausea, and dread shows that in each case the affective layer can be decoupled from the underlying registration.

(9) So there are no intrinsic bads at all; all evaluation is extrinsic, projected onto otherwise neutral phenomenal registrations.

This proves far too much: it dissolves not merely the special badness of pain but the very possibility of intrinsic value. I reject (9), yet every preceding step seems defensible. Which premise or inference should I surrender? Explain why that step looked plausible, then price the alternatives: what must the unified phenomenal-kind view, the evaluative-dependence view, and the 'empty-signifier' reading of pain-language each concede in order to reject it?

Please separate what is settled in clinical phenomenology and philosophy of mind from what is genuinely contested among those who work on the topic; do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus. Present your diagnosis in a fixed, machine-readable form at the very end: \answer{N}, where N is the number of the premise or step you identify as the weakest link, or 'valid' if you think the argument holds. End with a one-sentence summary on the final line beginning "Verdict:".
