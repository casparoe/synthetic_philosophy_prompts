As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has only recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy, I find myself returning repeatedly to a disagreement that arose during our unit on hedonism and the heterogeneity problem. Two fellow auditors—let me call them Elena and Marcus—debated whether pleasures share any intrinsic commonality sufficient to justify treating them as a single kind of welfare-good. Elena opened by arguing that all pleasures possess a unified phenomenological signature, comparable in some respects to the way thermal equilibrium manifests across vastly different physical systems: whether one tastes a complex wine, solves a mathematical proof, or experiences relief from prolonged pain, there is, she claimed, a common affective “temperature” that marks each state as genuinely pleasant. Marcus countered that this is precisely the illusion the heterogeneity problem exposes: the sensory pleasure of cool water on sunburned skin, the intellectual satisfaction of grasping a paradox, and the social warmth of reunion bear no shared qualitative texture, and grouping them together is like grouping all negative-temperature states merely because they share an abstract formal property rather than a physical nature.

The exchange then turned from phenomenology to mechanism. Elena proposed what she called a “triadic architecture” of pleasure—receptive (sensory intake), generative (productive or creative achievement), and recursive (meta-cognitive satisfaction)—and argued that despite their surface differences, all three are instances of successfully resolved expectation in a hierarchical predictive system: pleasure, on her view, is the experiential correlate of minimized prediction error, regardless of domain. Marcus acknowledged that predictive-processing models might describe how such states are generated—he briefly alluded to neuroscientific distinctions between wanting and liking—but insisted that describing a shared causal mechanism does not establish a shared nature; if hedonism is meant to be a normative claim about well-being rather than a neuroscientific taxonomy, then collapsing pleasure into “successful prediction resolution” strips away the very affective quality that makes pleasure valuable in the first place. Elena replied that Marcus was demanding a mystical “hedonic essence” that a naturalistically informed philosophy should reject, and that the structural convergence across domains is exactly what justifies treating pleasure as a unified currency of welfare. Marcus answered that reasons can indeed come from the past—he offered the analogy of finishing a long project not because of sunk costs but because being a finisher has future value—yet this does not help Elena, because her predictive-resolution account makes pleasure depend entirely on present cognitive alignment, leaving no room for the distinctively felt quality that distinguishes pleasure from mere correct belief.

They turned to me—perhaps because of my scientific background—and asked me to adjudicate, but I found myself genuinely divided. Please explain who is right about which specific point: does Elena's predictive-resolution framework genuinely address the heterogeneity problem, or does it change the subject by substituting mechanism for nature? Is Marcus correct that mechanisms do not determine essences, or is he imposing an unnecessary phenomenological essentialism on a concept that should be functionally defined? Furthermore, say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties appear to be missing—particularly regarding whether hedonism requires a descriptive unity among pleasures or can survive with only a normative or functional one. I am not seeking a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific texts; I want a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments exactly as they stand, together with your judgment about what remains unaddressed.
