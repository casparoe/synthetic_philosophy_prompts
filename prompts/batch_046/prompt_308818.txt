I've been sitting in on this ethics module about population and arguing with my flatmate about the procreation asymmetry — you know, the idea that there's a moral reason not to create someone who'd suffer, but no matching reason to create someone who'd be happy. Most people in the seminar reach for the standard person-affecting restriction, but I think that's too blunt. My own view is that the asymmetry only holds if we accept a pretty narrow, 'thin' conception of welfare: basically just experiential states like pleasure and pain. Once you let welfare get 'thick' — including things like having a coherent life narrative, deep projects, or being embedded in relationships — the asymmetry starts to dissolve, because non-existence then looks like it's depriving the potential person of those goods too. So I'm defending a version of the asymmetry that's tightly tied to experiential welfare alone.

What would count against this specific version? I want the arguments and evidence that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. For instance, is there a formal result or intuition that shows experiential welfare can't be cleanly seperate from narrative or relational goods? Would it matter if we discovered that people consistently judge missing out on a meaningful life to be as bad as suffering? And I want an honest assessment of whether my view is just being qualified into unfalsifiability — if I keep shrinking the welfare base every time someone points to a counterexample, is that a genuine defence or just moving the goalposts? Also, which of these defeaters would a committed defender of this 'thin welfare' asymmetry themselves concede are damaging, versus which ones they'd dismiss? I'd particularly like to know if there's a well-known case or result — maybe in discussions of disability or well-being — that arguably undermines the separation I'm relying on.
