I've been trying to work out what Wittgenstien actually meant about religious belief, and I think I've landed on something like this: it's not that people believe in God the same way they believe in atoms. Instead, religious belief works through these "pictures" that hold you captive and organize your whole form of life. So when someone says God exists, they aren't really making a scientific claim that could be disproven by physics; they're expressing a commitment that structures how they live, like a frame you can't step outside of to check.

From this, I think it follows that religious people shouldn't feel intellectually threatened by science, because science and religion are in different language games. And it also means that if someone loses thier faith, it shouldn't feel like "oh, the evidence turned out wrong," but more like "the picture stopped gripping me." Same with conversion — it should come from a change in life or seeing things differently, not from winning an argument.

But I want to know what would actually count against this view. Not just the usual "but people do argue about religion" — I mean the real defeaters. What kind of observations about how people actually convert or leave religion, what results from psychology or anthropology, or what internal problems with the idea of language games itself would show that religious belief IS more like a theory after all? Which ones would someone holding this view just shrug off, and which ones would force them to concede? Rank them from fatal to just awkward.

Also, has this view been qualified so much — like saying it's a language game except when it isn't — that nothing could ever count against it? And if so, is that a bad thing for this kind of view, or is it okay?
