After retiring from a thirty-year career in physical chemistry, I have recently turned to philosophy and have been trying to work out where I stand on Richard Rorty's antirepresentationalism and its compatibility with the scientific habits I developed in the laboratory. For a discussion group I am joining, I have drafted the following set of commitments and want an audit of whether they form a coherent package.

First, I hold that scientific theories are best understood as tools for prediction and practical coping rather than as representations of an intrinsic, mind-independent reality. Second, I nevertheless want to maintain that the historical replacement of phlogiston chemistry by Lavoisier's oxidation theory was not merely a vocabulary shift or social renegotiation, but constituted a genuine improvement in our cognitive grip on combustion. Third, I accept Rorty's claim that there is no algorithmic procedure for rationality and that all justification is a matter of social practice and historical contingency. Fourth, I take evolutionary debunking arguments seriously and regard them as supporting antirepresentationalism, since our cognitive faculties appear to have been shaped for reproductive fitness rather than for tracking metaphysical truths. Fifth, despite this, I believe that certain experimental outcomes—such as the invariant half-life measurements of carbon isotopes or the consistent failure of perpetual-motion designs—impose hard constraints that operate independently of our linguistic practices and genuinely rule out theoretical possibilities. Sixth, I reject the correspondence theory of truth for empirical claims, yet I wish to preserve a deflationary, minimalist account for mathematics that allows me to say prime numbers are discovered rather than invented. Seventh, I think Rorty's distinction between normal and abnormal discourse applies well to scientific revolutions, but I also hold that abnormal scientific discourse—such as the early debates over quantum interpretation—is eventually normalized through experimental closure rather than through purely rhetorical persuasion.

Please examine this package carefully. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts are only apparent and dissolve once terms are clarified, and what is the least costly single revision if consistency requires giving something up? Rank the tensions by severity, specify the cost of each potential revision, and indicate whether one change could resolve multiple conflicts. Please answer in American English.
