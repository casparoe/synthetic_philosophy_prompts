I have just come out of a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about whether any genuinely reflective agent—human or artificial—can consistently maintain a general principle of epistemic self-trust. She insisted that the Löbian obstacle, which in its standard form shows that a sufficiently strong formal system cannot prove its own soundness for arbitrary propositions without already accepting them, is merely a technical artifact of modal provability logic: weaken the conditions on the provability predicate or allow for bounded, non-omniscient self-access, she claimed, and the difficulty dissolves entirely. I think she is wrong, but I also think that both sides in the usual debate share a hidden assumption that strikes me as unrealistic—namely, that self-trust is a uniform attitude directed at an agent’s entire cognitive architecture, when in reality many agents possess modular or hierarchically structured reasoning with reflective access to some subsystems but not others. I want to turn this suspicion into a formal research proposal of about two pages, suitable for a six-month dissertation fellowship or small interdisciplinary grant, that treats this modular case as genuinely open.

The proposal should state a precise question: does the Löbian obstacle persist, mutate, or dissolve when applied to agents with partial or tiered self-transparency rather than unified self-reference, and is whatever remains genuinely logical or merely an artifact of modeling omniscient self-access? It should explain why both the “pure formal artifact” view and the standard “unavoidable for fully reflective agents” view fail to settle the modular architecture case. The method should combine conceptual analysis of agent architectures with a careful identification of which formal conditions in the obstacle are load-bearing versus which depend on assumptions of unified access—no original formal proofs are required, but the analysis must be precise enough for evaluation by logicians and philosophers of mind. The proposal should describe a useful deliverable—perhaps a taxonomy of self-model architectures paired with a map of where the obstacle applies—specify what result would count as failure, and include an honest scope assessment for a six-month timeline, noting risks that might make the project too large. The tone should be formal and polished, appropriate for a review panel that includes philosophers and computer scientists.
