Student answer:

Expressivism holds that moral sentences express non-cognitive attitudes like approval or disapproval rather than beliefs. The Frege-Geach problem, first raised in the context of conditionals, is that in a sentence like "If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong," the embedded clause "lying is wrong" does not seem to express the speaker's attitude, since the speaker might be merely supposing it. Geach argued that this shows expressivists violate the law of non-contradiction for embedded moral terms, because they cannot assign a uniform logical form to "wrong" in both asserted and hypothetical positions. Blackburn's quasi-realist reply is that moral judgments express attitudes that we "project" onto the world through our sensibilities. In embedded contexts, the attitude is not suspended but transformed into a shadow attitude directed at the hypothetical scenario; this allows the conditional to preserve modus ponens without treating the moral premise as a belief. Because these shadow attitudes retain a world-to-mind direction of fit, they are still non-cognitive. The upshot is that expressivists can accept the validity of moral reasoning, though this comes at the cost of making moral disagreement appear as a mere clash of projected preferences rather than a conflict over representational truths.
