Write the full desiderata that any adequate theory of macro-historical contingency must satisfy—specifically, one that explains how micro-level disruptions cascade into large-scale outcomes without reducing history either to deterministic chaos or to retrospective storytelling. The theory must handle the classic “for want of a nail” chain (a lost horseshoe, a lost horse, a lost battle, a lost kingdom) but also a constructed case of “silent branching” in institutional history: two bureaucratic procedures that are initially indistinguishable except for a clerk’s differently placed comma in an internal memo, which decades later produces divergent regulatory regimes that historians treat as structurally inevitable. For each requirement, state the motivating case or distinction behind it: which phenomena must be explained, which collapses must be avoided (e.g., between causal sensitivity to initial conditions and mere narrative coherence; between ontological contingency and epistemic opacity; between structural explanation and micro-event explanation), and which results from chaos theory or historiography it cannot contradict. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which belong to particular camps (determinists, counterfactual narrative theorists, probabilistic branching accounts). Then flag any pair you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly any tension between requiring empirical distinguishability from a hidden-variable deterministic account and requiring that contingency not collapse into pure randomness. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding popular theories, with brief justification. Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citation; the deliverable is the specification itself, together with the conflict map and the exclusionary criterion.
