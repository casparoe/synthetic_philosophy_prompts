NOTES — "Functionalism from the bench"

The usual story in philosophy of mind is that mental states are just functional roles, so pain or belief could be realized in neurons, silicon, whatever. Multiple realizability is the classic anti-reductionist move: if an octopus and a human can both feel pain with totally different hardware, then reducing pain to C-fibers is dead wrong.

But in biology, saying a trait is "multiply realized" usually means we haven't found the deeper mechanism yet. Camera-type eyes in squid and vertebrates look like totally different hardware running the same visual software. Then people found Pax6 and deep homology, and it turned out the "software" was shared all along at a genetic level. So the multiplicity was mostly superficial.

I'm wondering if consciousness works the same way. Maybe its not arbitrarily realizable at all — maybe there are deep organizational constraints, like specific information-integration architectures, that have to be present. If so, functionalism isn't false, but its not the open-ended "anything goes" view people defend. Its more like: function matters, but only within a narrow family of bio-analogous structures.

Does this make functionalism empirically empty? Or does it just push the reductionist problem down one level? And is it legitimate to borrow the eye-example like this, or am I wrongly treating evolutionary convergence as the same thing as philosophical realization?

Please rate whether the reasoning smells right, say if its actually interesting or just a biologist overstretching, and specifically address that last worry about mixing up convergence with realization. No need for bibliographies — just tell me where I'm likely wrong.
