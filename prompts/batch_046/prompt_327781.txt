Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed Lewisian conventionalist and a committed evolutionary-game-theoretic theorist of norms, focused narrowly on whether silent-acknowledgment practices—such as upvoting or emoji-reacting without textual reply in large-scale asynchronous digital deliberation—count as genuine conventions, can be fully explained by evolutionary stability alone, or fall into a conceptual gap between the two frameworks. Structure the deliverable in four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: state the shared commitments about Lewis's conditions for convention (mutual expectations, presence of arbitrary alternatives, common knowledge of regularities, and conditional preference for conformity), about evolutionary accounts (replication dynamics, selection pressure, equilibrium selection, and the distinction between descriptive regularities and normative force), and about the empirical features of silent acknowledgment in asynchronous platforms (scalability, absence of synchronous interaction, visibility of aggregated responses, and ambiguity of individual intent). Second, a disagreements table: for each camp, state in wording that camp would accept for its own position whether silent acknowledgment satisfies the common-knowledge condition given the lack of face-to-face interaction; whether evolutionary stability can generate normative oughtness without presupposing prior conventional agreement; and whether the persistence or collapse of these practices under interface changes is best explained by broken common knowledge or by shifted payoff structures. Third, identify at least three cruxes—specifically: (i) whether asynchronous visibility of aggregate responses can substitute for pairwise common knowledge; (ii) whether evolutionary explanations of norm maintenance are circular if they must invoke participants' beliefs about what others will do; and (iii) whether silent acknowledgment possesses genuinely arbitrary alternatives or is technologically determined. Fourth, for each crux, specify one concrete hypothetical case or platform-design change that both sides agree is diagnostically relevant—such as a sudden removal of reaction visibility, a forced migration to a platform with only binary acknowledgment, or the introduction of anonymous versus attributed reactions—and record in advance each side's predicted interpretation of the outcome. Write formally and precisely, do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and formulate every disagreement so that neither side's wording sounds like a straw man.
