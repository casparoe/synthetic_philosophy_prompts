The debate over whether epiphenomenalism is self-undermined by our own phenomenal judgments has been at an impasse for decades, and I am trying to understand why. The standard formulation of the paradox runs as follows: if phenomenal properties are causally inert, they do not cause our judgments that we are experiencing them; but if a property plays no causal or explanatory role in the formation of judgments about it, then those judgments are not genuinely about that property, or at least are not knowledge; yet we do form such judgments, and they are largely accurate. One response—the correlationist response—accepts causal closure but denies that direct efficacy is required for aboutness or reliability; it claims that phenomenal properties can be systematically correlated with neural states that do cause the judgments, and that tracking or structural correspondence is sufficient. The opposing response—the explanatory-role response—insists that correlation is not enough: for a judgment to be genuinely about phenomenal property P, P itself must figure in the correct explanation of why that judgment was formed, and since epiphenomenalism denies P any causal role, it severs the explanatory link required for aboutness. What frustrates me is that both sides accept the causal closure of the physical, both agree that phenomenal judgments are widespread and mostly correct, and both treat the paradox as decisive if unsolved—yet neither side has conceded anything in years. My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement about mental causation but a verbal or conceptual dispute about what "explanatory role" means in theories of mental representation—whether it requires token-causal efficacy or can be satisfied by type-level structural correspondence—coupled with bedrock intuitions about whether direct acquaintance is necessary for phenomenal knowledge. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical question about concept formation, or a formal ambiguity in the notion of tracking, that actually drives the disagreement? I want an honest diagnosis of the stall: is it verbal, about standards of explanation, about which cases count as data for theories of representation, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything—a formal model of tracking without causal interaction, a distinction between token-causal and type-causal roles, or a reconceptualization of phenomenal judgment—that could actually resolve it, or should we expect the impasse to persist?
