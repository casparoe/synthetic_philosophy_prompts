I am working through Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach with a study group that includes both computer scientists and philosophy students, and I have noticed a recurring pattern in how people discuss the book's central analogy between self-referential formal sentences and conscious self-awareness. The pattern unfolds like this: someone points out that in Gödel's construction, a formula can be made to encode a statement about its own provability through numerical encoding of symbols. They then suggest that this is structurally akin to a mind being aware of its own thoughts. When another person objects that the formula is merely manipulating symbols according to fixed rules and has no understanding of what it describes, the first speaker retreats to a more modest claim—that there is only a parallel in architecture, a folding-back of levels onto themselves, and that nobody is attributing literal consciousness to arithmetic. But once the objection has passed, the speaker returns to language that treats formal self-reference and mental self-awareness as if they share something deeper than architecture—for instance, suggesting that both involve escaping a fixed level, or that both are genuinely about themselves rather than merely describing themselves. I have seen this happen repeatedly in discussions of both Hofstadter's analogy and the related Lucas-Penrose argument, though in the latter case the loop usually concerns a machine being unable to recognize its own Gödel sentence.

I am trying to determine whether there is an established name for this specific argumentative move—not for the analogy itself, and not for the Lucas-Penrose argument, but for the slide between syntactic self-reference, where a system encodes statements about its own derivations, and semantic or phenomenal self-awareness, where a subject understands or experiences its own mental states, particularly when the slide is masked by retreating to structural language under pressure and then returning to stronger language afterward. It is not exactly the motte-and-bailey fallacy, because the two positions are not an indefensible extension and a defensible core; they are more like two interpretations of the same metaphor that share vocabulary. It is also not quite simple equivocation on self-reference, because the speakers often acknowledge the distinction when pressed, only to blur it again in subsequent reasoning. Is there a recognized concept in philosophy of mind or philosophy of mathematics that captures this specific failure mode? If not, could you propose a precise definition that distinguishes it from ordinary ambiguity, from the intentional fallacy, and from the category error of attributing mental properties to formal systems? I am less interested in whether Hofstadter's analogy succeeds than in whether this particular pattern of defending it has been catalogued, and where exactly its boundaries lie relative to nearby rhetorical mistakes.
