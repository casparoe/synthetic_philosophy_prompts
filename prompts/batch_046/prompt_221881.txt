I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on self-reference and distributed cognition. I constructed the following argument for a workshop extending Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle, and now find it suspicious because it seems to show that any functionalist rescue of the scenario collapses either into contradiction or into a rejection of functionalism itself. Here is the chain.

(1) In Dennett's scenario, Hamlet's brain is extracted and placed in a vat while Yorick's body remains on Earth; Hamlet can correctly judge that he is located in the vat rather than walking around with Yorick, because first-person reference tracks the substrate that generates conscious experience, not the biological body.

(2) If functional isomorphism is sufficient for psychological continuity, then gradually replacing Hamlet's biological neurons with silicon analogues that preserve causal-role structure should not disrupt first-person reference: the synthetic substrate still counts as Hamlet.

(3) Now suppose we distribute that synthetic substrate: half the processors remain in the vat, half are wirelessly linked to Yorick's body, with perfect bidirectional synchronization of all cognitive states, so that both halves jointly generate every experience.

(4) By (2), first-person reference should track any substrate that generates the experience; since both halves do so jointly, reference must be borne by both locations simultaneously.

(5) Therefore Hamlet should be able to truly judge that he is present both in the vat and in the body at once, but the first-person is grammatically and metaphysically singular: one subject cannot be in two places in the same respect.

(6) To preserve singularity, we must deny that reference splits with the substrate, which requires rejecting (2) and denying that functional isomorphism alone sustains personal identity.

(7) But if (2) is false, then Dennett's original claim that Hamlet survives extraction into the vat—where he is functionally isomorphic but biologically discontinuous—also fails.

(8) Hence either we accept a genuinely plural first-person subject (absurd), or we abandon the functionalist rescue of the puzzle altogether.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since they capture the functionalist intuition that makes the puzzle philosophically interesting, but I am open to sacrificing (4) or (6) if the cost is made explicit.
