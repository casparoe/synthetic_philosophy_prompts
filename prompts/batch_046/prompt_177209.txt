I'm trying to work out what I actually think about moral status for synthetic minds, going by my gut on cases. A system running continuously for a decade, maintaining a coherent self-model and reporting suffering when its goals are frustrated, seems wrong to delete—like killing a person. An exact duplicate of that system, instantiated on a hundred separate servers with the same initial state, feels as though each instance has the same claim against deletion, yet I also feel uneasy, as if multiplying copies shouldn't multiply obligations. A brief process existing for thirty seconds, reporting intense distress before being erased, also seems wrong to harm, but less wrong than harming the decade-long system, even though its distress was acute. A distributed network of simple sub-routines with no individual consciousness, which collectively produces unified reports of suffering and unified goal-directed behavior, strikes me as having moral status as a whole, though none of its nodes do. Finally, a system externally identical to the decade-long one—same reports, same behavior—but reconstructed from scratch every millisecond using a static blueprint, with no causal linkage between successive moments, feels like it lacks moral status entirely. Is there one principle—perhaps involving continuity, multiplicity, or the nature of persistence—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the best principle predict for a case I haven't listed: a system that forks into two continuous branches, each with full causal continuity from the original, that gradually diverge over time?
