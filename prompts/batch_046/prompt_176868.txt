I am drafting a proposal for a four-month independent study in political philosophy, and I want to examine Amartya Sen's claim that famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters through the lens of his three standard cases—the 1943 Bengal famine, the mid-19th-century Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward in China—not to rehash empirical history, but to test whether the "political crime" framing depends on an unstated philosophical premise about collective agency and epistemic accountability that Sen never makes explicit. The existing literature tends to treat his view as either an empirical entitlement-collapse thesis or a normative governance indictment; I suspect both readings overlook a deeper ambiguity about how distributed institutional failures become morally attributable. In Bengal, democratic mechanisms existed in the imperial center but not in the colony, so calling the famine a crime requires deciding whether moral responsibility traverses administrative boundaries. In Ireland, the state's ideological commitment to laissez-faire meant food was present but not accessible, raising the question whether an ideological constraint counts as intentional harm or as a structural blind spot. In the Great Leap Forward, local bureaucrats systematically misreported harvest figures to central authorities, meaning the harm arose from a distributed information failure embedded in institutional design rather than from a single policy choice. I want the proposal to ask precisely whether Sen's framework can consistently distinguish a famine caused by direct policy decision, one caused by ideological neglect, and one caused by structurally induced epistemic breakdown—without smuggling in an unstated theory of collective intentionality. If the answer is no, then collapsing these three structurally different failures into one moral category may obscure more than it reveals. The proposal should state this question with precision, explain why current philosophical and historical treatments leave it open (they defend or attack Sen's empirical claims rather than interrogating the ontology of collective harm underlying them), outline a conceptual method—analyzing the conditions under which distributed institutional actions constitute a single "crime" versus an aggregate of separate failures—specify what a successful result would look like (a taxonomy of institutional harm-types that maps onto the three cases without forcing them into one mold), and define failure (if the analysis shows that no coherent distinction can be drawn and Sen's unified category is actually the most parsimonious). It should be realistic for a single semester, must not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading list by reference, and should open with its bottom line—the precise question and the claim that existing answers leave it open—followed immediately by the reasoning, method, and scope, with no preamble and no restating of the prompt.
