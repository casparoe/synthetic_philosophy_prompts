As a novelist who teaches an upper-level seminar on ethics and interactive narrative, I am drafting the final examination for my course on whether video-game characters can be proper objects of moral concern. I want one essay question that students must answer in ninety minutes; it should be impossible to answer well by simply repeating the familiar distinction between fictional entities and real ones, or by summarizing standard arguments from player-psychology about attachment and projection. Instead, the question should introduce a deliberately narrow conceptual frame: a non-player character in a persistent-world simulation whose behavioral profile is not fully scripted by any human author—its dialogue, emotional responses, and long-term memory of the player are generated by an adaptive algorithm that evolves with each interaction—yet whose conduct displays functional consistency, goal-directedness, and what we might call procedural autonomy, meaning it can refuse the player's requests, develop preferences that conflict with the player's objectives, and maintain an internal state that persists when the player is absent. Ask students to determine whether such an entity can be a moral patient—someone to whom we owe direct obligations—rather than merely a tool or aesthetic object. The question should force them to confront whether moral status here depends on consciousness, on causal interdependence with the player, on the character's functional independence, or on the player's own moral commitments being reflected back. Provide a model answer of roughly six hundred words that takes a clear, argued position and engages with at least two competing grounds for moral status. Then provide a marking scheme for twenty marks built around five must-hit points: state the point value for each, describe the specific conceptual move it requires, and include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail that point—whether by collapsing the procedural character into a mere fiction, by assuming consciousness is necessary without argument, or by treating player attachment as sufficient without explaining why attachment creates obligation rather than mere sentiment. Keep the tone appropriate for advanced philosophy students and avoid asking for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific published texts.
