Write the full specification—the desiderata—that any adequate theory linking brain characteristics to moral status must satisfy. Organize them by the concrete case or distinction behind each one, and flag whether each is non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp.

The list should cover: first, the metric problem—absolute brain mass, total neuron count, and brain-to-body scaling—motivated by comparing whales, elephants, and humans, since each metric picks a different winner. Second, the allometric scaling worry: if brain size mainly tracks body size, then moral status shouldn't fluctuate with body plan alone. Third, synthetic or distributed minds that lack any centralized brain but replicate the relevant functional capacities; the theory should say clearly whether they are in or out. Sixth, a hypothetical I keep returning to: a being with a planet-scale brain but neural transmission slowed by extreme cold, so its cognitive tempo exactly matches a human's—if raw brain volume determined moral weight, this being should dominate our moral calculus, yet that feels wrong.

After laying out the criteria, tell me which single requirement does the most damage to a simple larger-brain-equals-greater-weight view, and flag any pair of desiderata that seem jointly unsatisfiable—especially if one demands that status be grounded in actual biological mechanisms while another demands architecture-neutrality for non-biological minds.
