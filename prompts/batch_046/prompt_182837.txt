I am drafting a historical novel in which an Andalusian physician-philosopher, studying in Córdoba during the twilight of the taifa kingdoms, comes across Avicenna’s argument concerning the suspended man, al-insan al-mu’allaq, in the psychological sections of the philosopher’s great medical-philosophical encyclopedia. The passage, as I understand it from the older Latin and Arabic traditions, asks us to imagine an adult human being suddenly brought into existence in a perfect void, suspended in air with limbs separated so that no part touches another, entirely blindfolded and deprived of every external sensation, and even cut off from the internal feelings of hunger, thirst, or bodily warmth. Despite this total sensory isolation, Avicenna maintains that the individual would still affirm with absolute certainty that he exists—though he might be unable to say whether he possesses hands, feet, or a heart. From this, the text draws the conclusion that the soul is a substance in its own right, not identical with the body and not dependent upon sensory experience for its fundamental self-awareness.

My difficulty concerns a subtle interpretive point that I wish to render with philosophical precision for my character’s internal crisis. Some contemporary commentators propose that Avicenna’s suspended man retains an implicit, non-sensory grasp of his own spatial extension—what might be described as a minimal proprioceptive frame—without which the bare affirmation of existence would collapse into incoherence. I want to understand whether Avicenna’s reasoning explicitly excludes any such tacit bodily orientation, or whether the argument’s force actually depends upon smuggling in a residual sense of embodied position that the text never acknowledges. In other words, does the flying man genuinely demonstrate the soul’s independence from corporeal conditions, or does he reveal only that self-certainty requires a thinner, pre-reflective bodily substrate than Avicenna admits? If the latter interpretation can be sustained without anachronism, my character’s growing suspicion that immateriality is a narrative convenience rather than a metaphysical discovery becomes philosophically credible rather than merely modern. Could you help me understand what the passage actually establishes about the relationship between self-certainty and bodily absence, and whether there is textual room for the interpretation I have sketched?
