I recently missed a decisive job interview because a two-minute train delay cascaded through my schedule, and the experience has left me preoccupied with how historians weigh micro-causes in macro-collapses. I want to examine the opening of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 through a conceptual distinction I am sharpening: a “trigger event” (the immediate, apparently accidental occurrence) versus a “structural resonance cavity” (a system state in which small perturbations amplify rather than dissipate). Take Günter Schabowski’s misstatement at the press conference that evening as the potential trigger, and the institutional decay, sustained protest momentum, and border-guard ambiguity of East Germany that autumn as the potential resonance cavity. Analyze the episode through this two-part lens: was the Wall’s opening that night genuinely contingent on the misstatement, or was the misstatement only effective because the cavity was already present? What does this case reveal about chaos-theoretic accounts of historical contingency—specifically, whether they require both elements to avoid collapsing into either determinism or trivial accident-mongering? Finally, identify the weakest point in the framework: does labeling every macro-event as either “missing the trigger” or “lacking resonance” risk rendering the distinction unfalsifiable, and how might one defend it against that charge without appealing to counterfactuals that are impossible to verify?
