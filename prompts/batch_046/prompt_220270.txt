A colleague in philosophy of mind summarized Bradley's argument about the unity of the proposition for a non-specialist audience, and I want to check whether the account is trustworthy before I assign it. The summary runs as follows. Bradley observed that in any judgment—say, that a stands in R to b—the terms a and b and the relation R are initially separate. To unify them into one proposition requires something that connects R to a and to b, but that connector is itself a new term, which in turn requires its own connector, generating an infinite regress. According to my colleague, Bradley's solution was not to reject relations but to embrace the hierarchy: he treated each successive connector as belonging to a higher logical type, so that the regress becomes a well-ordered ascent rather than a vicious circle, ultimately grounded in a single formal structure. From this the colleague infers that Bradley was essentially a precursor to modern typed theories of propositional structure, and that the classic problem of unity is dissolved once we represent propositions as nested ordered pairs rather than flat lists.

I have the impression that this inverts Bradley's actual metaphysics—he is usually read as denying that relations are ultimately real and as appealing to an undifferentiated Absolute—but I am not certain where the line between simplification and error falls here. Please audit the summary: which parts correctly capture the regress argument, which parts misrepresent Bradley's response and his reasons for it, and whether the inference to structuralism and ordered-pair semantics survives once those corrections are made. Also say whether framing the problem in terms of nested formal structures preserves what Bradley meant by "unity," or whether it substitutes a different question.
