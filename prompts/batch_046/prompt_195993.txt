Daniel Dennett defends moral responsibility on compatibilist grounds: reactive attitudes and accountability practices are justified by their functional role in social regulation, not by any metaphysically pure origin of action. Gregg Caruso argues that pervasive constitutive and circumstantial luck undermines just deserts, leaving only forward-looking, rehabilitative rationales for punishment. Now consider a proposed distinction between 'source-desert'—where an agent deserves punishment in virtue of being the ultimate originator of wrongdoing—and 'practice-desert'—where an agent deserves punishment only insofar as the institutional practice of holding her responsible is justified by its effects on coordination and norm-internalization. Argue either that this distinction lets Dennett preserve a genuinely retributive element without falling prey to Caruso's luck objection, or that it collapses into mere consequentialism and therefore fails to deliver the retributive intuition Dennett seeks to defend. Address specifically whether practice-desert can non-instrumentally explain why we withhold punishment from agents who lack rational capacity.
