Write a short dialogue between two equally competent philosophers, Mara and Eli. Mara defends the view that introspection is reliable about the qualitative content of mental states—what an experience is like from the inside—but systematically unreliable about the causal mechanisms that generate them. Eli argues that this separation collapses in practice: if we cannot introspectively access the origins of a mental state, then first-person reports about our own motives are vulnerable to confabulation, and we lack a principled way to distinguish genuine commitments from post-hoc rationalizations.

They should discuss a concrete case: a climate-policy advisor who sincerely reports an intense, introspectively vivid feeling of urgency about reducing carbon emissions, yet whose revealed behavior—frequent long-haul travel, opposition to local density reforms—consistently conflicts with that urgency. The advisor insists she knows her values are genuinely environmentalist and attributes the gap to external constraints she can clearly identify.

Mara should maintain that the advisor’s access to the felt urgency remains authoritative even if hidden social-status motives partly cause it, and that treating the qualitative report as suspect undermines legitimate first-person input in ethical deliberation. Eli should counter that without access to causal origins, the claim to know one’s values is empty, and that relying on such reports in policy design risks institutionalizing self-deceptive narratives. Each character should respond directly to the other’s specific argument rather than talking past it.
