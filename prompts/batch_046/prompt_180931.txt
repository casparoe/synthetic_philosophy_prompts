I have come to hold a somewhat specific view about the phrase "Western values." Rather than treating it as a geographical label or as a loose collection of liberal-democratic habits, I understand it as referring to a distinctive institutional tension: the simultaneous commitment to universalist moral norms—with roots in Christian universalism and Enlightenment rationalism—and to procedural mechanisms of self-correction, pluralism, and legal formalism—with roots in Roman jurisprudence and scientific norms. On this view, the category is coherent precisely because it names a historical convergence in which universalist claims are systematically subjected to internal critique and institutional revision; it is not a list of virtues but a description of how certain societies structured the relationship between norm and revision.

What would have to be true for this framing to be wrong? I want an honest assessment of its exposure. Which of the following would count decisively against it, and which would merely be awkward: historical evidence that Roman legal formalism and Christian universalism did not in fact converge in the relevant institutional forms; sociological evidence that societies displaying the tension are not meaningfully continuous with the societies usually named "Western"; conceptual arguments that universalism and procedural pluralism are not genuinely in tension but are conceptually independent or mutually reinforcing in ways that make the "tension" framing misleading; or the observation that the phrase is used primarily as a rhetorical weapon in geopolitical argument, with no stable descriptive content?

More specifically: if defenders of this framing concede that "Western values" is often deployed rhetorically, does that undermine the descriptive claim, or can they absorb it by distinguishing use from reference? If the historical convergence turns out to be more contingent or less unified than the framing suggests, is that fatal or only a matter of degree? And is there a risk that the position has been qualified—say, by allowing any society with some form of self-correcting universalism to count—into unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a problem for a conceptual thesis of this kind?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate which ones a committed defender of the framing would themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to those they would dismiss. I am particularly interested in whether there is a principled boundary that prevents the category from expanding to include every modern bureaucratic state, and what kind of case—historical, conceptual, or linguistic—would force that boundary to collapse.
