I'm writing up a chapter for my PhD on embodied cognition and I keep coming back to the dialogue in The Mind's I — the one where Einstein's brain is replaced page-by-page by a book listing every neural response, so an interviewer can flip through and hold a conversation. In our philosophy of mind seminar last Tuesday everyone kept reaching for the analogy that the book is basically a computer program saved to an external drive: all the instructions are intact, but they aren't being executed on the right hardware, which is why there's obviously no consciousness, just dormant data. They use this to license the conclusion that consciousness is essentially dynamic causal processing, not static representational content.

But I think the analogy might be smuggling in more than it should. Could you audit it properly? I want a correspondence table — program, CPU, execution loop, power supply, and so on — followed by the points where the mapping clearly breaks (for instance, the book is physically instantiated in paper and ink in a way a saved file isn't, and there's no feedback loop with the environment). I also want you to flag whether the source domain quietly imports a Fodor-style language-of-thought picture — discrete symbols being manipulated by syntactic rules — without actually arguing for it, and whether the conclusion that no execution means no experience actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument.

I've also been wondering whether injecting predictive-processing ideas changes the audit. If consciousness involves continuous error-minimisation across hierarchical Bayesian channels, then maybe the book fails not just because it's static but because it lacks the right kind of world-coupled dynamics. But I don't want to beg the question in favour of predictive processing, so I'd like to know whether the computer-program analogy is already implicitly assuming something like that, or whether it's genuinely neutral between representationalist and enactivist frameworks. Please answer in British English — and do tell me if I've misrepresented Hofstadter's set-up; I might have got some details wrong since I'm working from memeory of the seminar discussion.
