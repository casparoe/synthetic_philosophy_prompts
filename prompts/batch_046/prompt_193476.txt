As a doctoral candidate in comparative philosophy working at the intersection of analytic ethics and Buddhist thought, I am drafting a one-year dissertation fellowship proposal for a committee that includes both normative ethicists and scholars of religion. I want the proposal to center on a framing that standard comparative treatments tend to flatten. Most literature on Buddhist ethics treats karma either as a long-run consequentialist calculus about rebirth or as a virtue-ethical framework oriented toward mental purification. I want to pursue a more granular question rooted in Theravada Abhidhamma: actions are assessed through two registers, conventional (sammuti) and ultimate (paramattha). At the conventional level, merit (puñña) appears aggregative and interpersonally transferable—suggesting compatibility with consequentialist reasoning about collective welfare—while at the ultimate level it dissolves into discrete mental factors (cetasika) with no persisting agent to accumulate it.

The proposal should state precisely whether this two-register analysis can sustain a non-collapsing form of consequentialist evaluation within Buddhist social ethics—specifically regarding institutional obligations such as state welfare or climate adaptation—without violating the ultimate-level denial of a persisting self. It needs to explain why existing comparative positions, particularly those that assimilate karma to either rule-utilitarianism or virtue ethics, leave this specific tension unresolved. The method should be philosophical rather than empirical or textual-historical: I envision constructing a formal distinction between first-order and second-order evaluative registers using the Abhidhamma framework, then testing whether institutional obligations can be justified at the conventional register while remaining ontologically innocent at the ultimate register. The proposal must specify what a successful result would look like, what would count as failure, and give an honest assessment of risks—especially the danger that the project collapses into either pure metaphysics or standard applied ethics, or that resolving the register problem requires more textual scholarship than a one-year philosophical project allows.
