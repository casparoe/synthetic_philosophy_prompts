I'm drafting the final examination for an upper-level seminar on practical rationality and the metaphysics of action. I should like a single essay question on Buridan's ass that resists mere rehearsal of the familiar fable — an ass, equidistant between two identical bales of hay, starving for want of any reason to prefer one — and instead forces students to ask whether the paradox genuinely threatens the principle of sufficient reason when applied to action, or merely exposes an unrealistic framing assumption.

Specifically, candidates must distinguish and evaluate three responses: that a rational agent must build in a randomising mechanism to break ties; that choice without preference demands a non-rational volitional act not justified by reasons; and that perfect symmetry is never metaphysically instantiated, so the case describes an impossible world rather than a failure of rationality. Beyond mapping these, students should assess what each implies for the coherence of 'choice without preference', and defend a view on whether rational action can ever be genuinely reasonless, with the difficulty lying in evaluation rather than exposition. The examination allows sixty minutes; answers should run to about 800 words.

Please provide the essay question, a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme allocating marks out of twenty-five. The scheme should specify the conceptual moves separating an A-grade answer — which must include at least one independent critical observation about the framing assumptions — from a B-grade answer that competently summarises the three positions without assessing their costs. It should also identify the typical error of conflating Buridan's ass with the decision-theoretic problem of indifference, and note where marks are lost for failing to address whether rationality can accommodate arbitrariness.
