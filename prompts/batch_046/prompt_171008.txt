I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy of psychology with a concentration in applied existential phenomenology. For a clinical ethics review board on which I serve, I need a reusable decision procedure that translates Sartre's analysis of radical freedom and bad faith into operational criteria for evaluating practitioners' appeals to institutional constraint. The board meets monthly to review cases in which clinicians justify regrettable actions—discharging vulnerable patients, persisting with ineffective protocols, or denying requested care—by citing rules, resource limits, or policies they themselves maintain. The procedure must distinguish legitimate appeals to facticity, where objective constraints genuinely delimit transcendence, from bad-faith evasions in which freedom is disowned and responsibility is projected onto an external order.

Structure the deliverable as a sequential checklist that a non-philosopher can apply within a two-hour review slot: the questions to ask in order; what counts as sufficient evidence of facticity versus evasion at each stage; the specific conditions under which the analysis terminates with a determination of legitimate constraint; the conditions under which it terminates with a determination of bad faith; and the precise point at which it must halt and refer the case to the board's values committee because the dispute concerns normative priority rather than existential structure. Any determination of bad faith must be marked as requiring dual sign-off by both a clinician and an ethicist.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: a psychiatrist discharging a suicidal patient solely because of bed-capacity rules; a therapist continuing a manualized treatment well past the point of clinical benefit on the grounds that the protocol mandates twelve sessions; and a clinician refusing a patient's request for gender-affirming care by citing an institutional policy that they co-authored. Include at least one case in which the procedure yields a judgment the practitioner will resist, and explicitly flag the boundary conditions—such as conflicts between institutional survival and patient autonomy—where the procedure cannot settle the matter and must defer to values-based judgment. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
