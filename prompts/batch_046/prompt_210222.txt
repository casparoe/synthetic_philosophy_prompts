I'm leading a fifty-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates on the interaction between hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation, and I wish to avoid the well-worn popular examples—the sinking of the Titanic, the 2008 financial crisis—that they will have met repeatedly in introductory writing. Rather, I want to frame the session around a more specialised conception: that hindsight bias operates not merely as a retrospective distortion of subjective probabilities, but as a narrative compression mechanism which retroactively collapses branching causal structures into linear causal chains, and that the narrative fallacy is best understood as the aesthetic-cognitive preference for these compressed, deterministic-looking sequences over probabilistically messy ones. The students have completed basic philosophy of science but have no training in formal historiography, and they will arrive assuming that hindsight bias is simply "knowing the outcome makes it seem inevitable," without grasping how that inevitability is constructed narratively.

Plan the explanatory arc: which conceptual distinction must be established first—the asymmetry between predictive and retrodictive reasoning, or the ontology of branching causal histories? Which single carrying example should anchor the session—perhaps a reconstructed account of the July Crisis of 1914 with explicit counterfactual branches left visible, or a reconstructed episode of scientific discovery in which competing hypotheses coexisted simultaneously—and what must that example possess and lack? I anticipate specific misconceptions: that they will conflate hindsight bias with metaphysical determinism; that they will treat the narrative fallacy as a mere literary stylistic preference rather than a cognitive distortion; and that they will assume the two biases are independent rather than mutually reinforcing. Specify exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and neutralised. Provide one closing diagnostic question that reveals whether they have grasped the interaction between the biases rather than understanding each in isolation. Finally, state clearly what should be deliberately omitted at this level—formal Bayesian updating frameworks, detailed historiographical source criticism, or technical counterfactual logic—so that the conceptual dependency structure remains intact without overwhelming the audience.
