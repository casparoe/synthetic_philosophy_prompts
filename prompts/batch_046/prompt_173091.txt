At a philosophy of mind reading group, two members—Marcus and Elena—got into a sustained argument about whether Dennett’s multiple drafts model genuinely dissolves the Cartesian theater or merely transforms it. Marcus opened by saying the Cartesian theater depends on a localized, unitary presentation space where experience is displayed to a central observer, and that Dennett eliminates this by distributing processing across parallel, competing drafts with no canonical version and no inner audience; consciousness, on this view, is nothing more than the availability of content for verbal report and behavioral control. Elena replied that Dennett hasn’t abolished the theater but only dispersed its function: since some drafts achieve global availability for report and consolidation while others are suppressed, there must still be a selection mechanism—what she called a “functional auditorium”—that determines which draft counts as the experienced one, and this gatekeeping role is structurally identical to a theater audience even if it lacks a spatial location in the brain. Marcus answered that this mischaracterizes the dynamics, because the selection is not an observer evaluating drafts from outside but the drafts themselves competing through functional weight and coherence constraints; the “privileged” draft is simply the winner of that competition, not a performance shown to anyone. Elena then introduced a hypothetical modification: suppose drafts are not competing for global access but are first evaluated by a “narrative coherence filter” that checks each draft against the subject’s long-term self-model before allowing any report, with drafts that conflict with the self-model being silently discarded. She argued that this filter operates as a hidden Cartesian observer—without being localized, it introduces a single point of evaluation that Dennett’s distributed architecture was designed to eliminate, so the theater survives as functional architecture rather than spatial architecture. Marcus countered that even this filter does not resurrect the theater, because it can itself be implemented as parallel constraint satisfaction across the self-model and the drafts, with no single “showing” of content to a central self; the self-model is just another draft or constraint network in the same distributed system, not an audience seated apart from the stage. They turned to me to referee and I genuinely could not settle it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate Marcus’s initial dissolution claim, Elena’s functional-auditorium objection, Marcus’s competition-without-observation reply, Elena’s coherence-filter proposal, and Marcus’s parallel-constraint response—indicating for each whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or partly right but incomplete. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a principled distinction between spatial localization and functional centralization that neither has drawn clearly, and whether Elena’s coherence filter describes a genuine departure from Dennett’s model or merely makes explicit a mechanism that was already implicit in it. Do not offer a bibliography or reading list; adjudicate only the arguments as presented.
