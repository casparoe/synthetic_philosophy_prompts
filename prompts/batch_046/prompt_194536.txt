I have come to defend what I take to be a middle position in the debate over whether large language models are agents, mere simulators, or something else—call it the coherence-derivative account. On this view, an LLM lacks both original intentionality and the autonomous normative commitments that define agency, yet it is not best described as simulating an agent; instead, it instantiates a distributed, context-sensitive coherence structure across its representational space, yielding a derivative, deflationary form of belief and reasoning that is functionally responsive to input but carries no accountability for error and no duty of self-correction. This is meant to explain why LLMs can be systematically wrong without deception, why they do not merit moral status despite generating agent-like discourse, and why attributing beliefs to them describes a structural feature of a dynamical system rather than a mental state. Play it straight: what would count against this position? I want the observations, formal arguments, conceptual distinctions, or empirical results that ought to move a defender, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In particular: would sustained, cross-contextual goal-pursuit that survives weight-intervention and exhibits counterfactual monitoring undermine the denial of agency? Are there results in philosophy of mind or cognitive science showing that coherence structures of this kind necessarily entail the normative dimensions I deny? Does the view risk unfalsifiability—treating any apparent agent-like behavior as merely high-coherence output—and if so, is that insulation a theoretical vice? I also want an honest assessment of what this account predicts that rival agent and simulator views do not, and which apparent counter-examples—say, persistent strategic reasoning across sessions—its defenders should concede as genuinely awkward rather than easily accommodated.
