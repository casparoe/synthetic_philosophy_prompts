I am a PhD student in epistemology working on Fitch's paradox of knowability for my dissertation. The paradox derives, roughly, from a knowability principle—if p is true then it is possible that p is known—combined with closure conditions, yielding that if all truths are knowable then all truths are actually known, since any unknown truth would be a known unknown. The standard responses restrict the principle or deny closure over conjunction.

I defend a resolution I call reflexive ascent failure. The error in the derivation, on my view, is not in the first-order knowability principle but in an illicit move across reflexive levels: knowing p at the object level does not entail that one can reflectively endorse knowing p, because reflective endorsement involves a distinct cognitive relation—call it second-order availability—that is structurally subject to self-reference limits. Thus the step that infers "it is known that p is unknown" from "p is unknown" illegitimately applies closure properties appropriate to first-order knowledge to a meta-level that lacks them. The result is that unknown truths can remain in principle knowable at the first order while being permanently unavailable to reflective endorsement, not through contingent cognitive deficiency but through the logical architecture of self-applying epistemic relations.
