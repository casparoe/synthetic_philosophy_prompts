I'm due to present a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on early modern philosophy and economic thought. The position I defend is that Berkeley's monetary reflections in The Querist ought not to be read primarily as a contribution to economic theory—whether proto-Keynesian or otherwise—but rather as an extension of his immaterialist attack on abstract ideas. On my interpretation, Berkeley regards paper currency and credit instruments as symbolically empty in precisely the analogical sense in which he regards the notion of material substance as empty: both are signs that appear to stand for something in reality, yet neither is anchored in any clear and distinct idea in the minds of those who employ them. Consequently, the circulation of paper money fosters a kind of collective metaphysical error, parallel to the error of materialism, by encouraging users to treat a purely relational convention as if it possessed independent substantial existence. The audience will comprise specialists in Berkeley's metaphysics, a historian of eighteenth-century political economy committed to reading The Querist as an intervention in contemporary monetary debate, and a philosopher of language familiar with Berkeley's account of signification.
