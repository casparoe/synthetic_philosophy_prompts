I am convening a philosophy of time seminar for approximately eighteen undergraduates in a single seventy-five-minute session. The students have already encountered Zeno-style division arguments in an earlier meeting, yet they have tended to treat those as purely mathematical curiosities rather than as live problems about action and determinism. I want them to experience, through structured participation rather than lecture, the central tension in the supertask known as Thomson's lamp: that every individual toggle is governed by a perfectly determinate rule, yet there appears to be no coherent answer to the lamp's state at the limit time, particularly once one recognises that the rule supplies no instruction for that limit state and that an initial condition might be left indeterminate by a completed infinite preliminary sequence. Rather than presenting this as an abstract puzzle, I need a runnable in-class simulation — with explicit rules, assigned roles, physical materials, and a reputation or point-based payoff scheme that requires no actual money — in which the problem emerges from what the participants do and discover before anyone names it.

Please design the activity as follows. Divide the group into 'Switchers' and 'Auditors'. The Switchers physically enact a rapidly accelerating analogue of the toggle sequence — using a visible timer or metronome that halves the interval between actions — with cards representing 'on' and 'off' states. Before any action begins, each Auditor must record a secret, binding prediction about the state at the designated 'limit point', with points awarded for consistency with a post-hoc revealed rule but forfeited if the prediction is arbitrary. The critical injection: include a sealed envelope or hidden card, revealed only after the limit point is reached, stating that a prior cohort allegedly completed an infinite preliminary toggle sequence, leaving the present group with an initial condition that the current rules do not fix. Arrange the incentives so that Auditors realise no justification for their prediction is available, while Switchers discover that their deterministic rule gives them no command for the final state. The rules should ensure that participants experience the coordination failure and the absence of a terminal instruction themselves, before the facilitator names the paradox.

Provide a debrief that maps each phase of the simulation onto the corresponding feature of the supertask — the convergence of actions in finite time, the absence of a final toggle, the gap between step-wise determinism and limit-state determinism, and the problem of an indeterminate initial condition following an infinite past sequence. Be frank about what will predictably fail on the first run, such as participants treating the finite analogue as if it were literally infinite, or Auditors retreating to random guessing, and suggest how to correct this without breaking the experiential force. Explicitly state which element of the genuine supertask — the literal completion of infinitely many discrete actions within a bounded duration — the activity necessarily cannot reproduce, and advise how I should supply that missing element verbally without undermining the visceral effect. The design must be executable without online access; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; and avoid dependence on obscure formal results that smaller language models are unlikely to have retained. If you refer to the original formulation of the lamp scenario, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
