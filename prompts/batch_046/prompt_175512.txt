I work as a software engineer on distributed backend systems and on tooling around large language models, and I have no formal training in philosophy. I have been trying to understand Daniel Dennett’s intentional stance—the idea that attributing beliefs and desires to a system is warranted when that attribution gives us predictive and explanatory leverage that lower-level descriptions cannot match in simplicity or practical accuracy. The concept feels familiar from engineering, where we constantly invoke high-level abstractions like saying a load balancer believes a primary node is unhealthy, even though the mechanism is just health-check packets and retry loops. But I constructed an argument that seems to push Dennett’s criterion into an absurd corner, and I cannot locate the exit.

Here is the chain. (1) The intentional stance is warranted for a system S if and only if attributing beliefs and desires to S yields predictive success that a purely mechanistic or design-stance analysis cannot match in simplicity or accuracy. (2) For modern LLMs, mechanistic interpretability—tracing attention circuits, identifying polysemantic feature representations for syntax or arithmetic, and forecasting token probabilities directly from internal activations—is rapidly closing that predictive gap; in many cases we can now predict outputs more precisely from weights and state vectors than from folk-psychological descriptions like the model wants to be helpful. (3) By Dennett’s own standard, then, as these mechanistic explanations mature we ought to withdraw belief-attributions from LLMs rather than retain them. (4) Apply the same standard symmetrically to human beings: neuroscience is also advancing toward circuit-level prediction of behavior, yet we do not think that every improvement in neural forecasting should cause us to stop attributing beliefs to people. (5) So either we must be prepared to eliminate belief-talk for humans as neuroscience progresses, or we must admit that predictive superiority is not the real criterion for the intentional stance. (6) But if predictive superiority is not the criterion, Dennett’s view offers no principled reason to attribute beliefs to humans while denying them to well-predicted machines, except an arbitrary biological boundary. (7) That seems to prove too much: it collapses the intentional stance into either universal eliminativism about beliefs or an unmotivated species chauvinism.

Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep the symmetry between humans and AI in step (4), because treating the two cases differently feels like special pleading, but I also want to avoid the eliminativist conclusion in (5). If the flaw is in (1), I would like to know what Dennett actually pays to replace predictive superiority with, and whether that replacement saves the stance for AI systems or only for biological ones. If the flaw is elsewhere—perhaps an equivocation between practical predictive availability and in-principle mechanistic completeness—please name the exact slide and explain why it looked convincing at first.
