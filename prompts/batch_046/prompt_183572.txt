As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing a feature on how classical Indian logical frameworks are being used to reinterpret American cinema, and I have been discussing with colleagues in my reading group whether Harold Ramis’s 1993 film Groundhog Day can be read through Nāgārjuna’s tetralemma—the fourfold negation of affirmation, negation, both, and neither—together with the Madhyamaka doctrine of emptiness (śūnyatā). The premise is familiar: the weatherman Phil Connors is trapped in a repeating loop of February 2nd in Punxsutawney, Pennsylvania, and only breaks free after a profound moral transformation. I want a critical reading that takes both the film’s narrative mechanics and Nāgārjuna’s philosophy seriously, rather than reducing the movie to a simple allegory for Buddhist ethics.

Specifically, I am interested in applying the tetralemma to the ontological status of Phil’s self across iterations. Does the looping structure presuppose a substantial, enduring self that accumulates memories and moral improvement, corresponding to the first corner? Or does it treat each day’s Phil as a discrete, momentary entity with no real connection to the others, corresponding to the second? Could the film be interpreted as endorsing a hybrid view—that Phil is conventionally the same person and ultimately empty of inherent existence—occupying the third corner? Or does the very possibility of escaping the loop depend on rejecting all four positions altogether, as in the fourth corner? I want the analysis to examine specific narrative turning points—Phil’s hedonistic phase, his suicide attempts, and his final altruistic day—and ask whether each stage maps onto a different logical position, and whether the film’s resolution implicitly commits to a view of personal identity that Madhyamaka emptiness would reject as reification.

At the same time, I want an honest assessment of where the reading strains: for instance, whether using temporal repetition as a metaphor for dependent origination risks conflating physical determinism with metaphysical emptiness, and whether the Hollywood ending—love and moral perfection breaking the loop—smuggles back in a substantialist notion of the self that the tetralemma is designed to dissolve. I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want an argument that a well-read generalist could follow without consulting specialized texts. Most importantly, I want to know what Groundhog Day adds to the philosophical debate about identity across time that purely conceptual argument misses—perhaps something about the phenomenology of repetition, or about how narrative closure itself shapes our intuitions about selfhood—so that my feature shows where the work resists and enriches the philosophy rather than merely illustrating it.
