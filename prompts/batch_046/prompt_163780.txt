I'm planning a fifty-minute seminar for second-year philosophy students who have just finished hedonism and desire-satisfaction theories, and I want to teach them tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering has value, and that even apparently positive states such as joy, excitement, or satisfied pride are actually subtle disturbances rather than goods. Plan the explanation arc rather than giving it: first, the dependency order—why the distinction between absence of pain and absence of craving must come before anything else, and why the claim that craving is structural (persisting even when particular desires are met) must come before the evaluation of positive affect. Second, the one carrying example—design a concrete scenario (with what it must include and what it must exclude) that shows why a life with no unmet wants and no pain can still fail the tranquilist standard, and why a state of minimal mental content succeeds; specify exactly why a simpler "sad vs. happy" comparison won't carry the idea. Third, map the misconceptions in the order students will form them—collapsing the view into negative hedonism, deriving that death is best, thinking it demands catatonia or apathy, and treating it as Buddhist nirvana without metaphysics—and say at which point in the sequence each should be headed off and with what distinction. Fourth, give me the end-of-session check: one case or question that reveals whether they understand that excitement and achievement can be deficits, not just whether they feel sympathy for asceticism. Finally, state explicitly what part of the metaphysics of craving or consciousness I should set aside at this level and name as left out.
