I'm a clinical ethics consultant who chairs a hospital committee reviewing requests for aggressive life-extending interventions when a patient is unconscious and has left an ambiguous advance directive. Our working framework is the Epicurean thesis that death is not bad for the one who dies—specifically, the experience-requirement argument that harm needs a subject who exists to suffer it, coupled with the symmetry argument about pre-existence—applied not as a metaphysical curiosity but as a welfare-accounting tool. Please construct a reusable decision procedure my team can run on any proposal: ordered questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stop points where the procedure returns a definite ethical assessment, and clearly flagged judgment calls where the answer is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must distinguish "death is not experientially harmful to the patient" from "death should therefore be sought or permitted," and it must treat objections—deprivation of future goods, anticipatory anxiety before death, and the rupture of unfinished life-projects—not as errors to dismiss but as separate welfare streams to weigh. Crucially, inject this conceptual framing: treat the patient's narrative identity—the continuity of their life-story, unfinished projects, and posthumous effects they intended to produce—as a welfare stream that the Epicurean argument does not automatically dissolve, but that must be weighed separately against experiential welfare in the procedure's final balancing step. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) continuing ventilation for a patient in a permanent vegetative state who previously expressed a wish to complete a specific memoir; (2) withholding antibiotics from a conscious, terminally ill patient who reports no fear of death but whose family demands treatment on religious grounds; and (3) a case where the procedure yields an answer the committee will not like—specifically, maintaining artificial nutrition for a patient in irreversible coma whose only remaining project is religious in nature, where the family demands continuation but the procedure's welfare accounting points the other way. For each case, show the step at which the decision is reached, where the procedure hands off to values, and what the procedure explicitly cannot determine—such as whether narrative welfare ever outweighs the absence of experiential harm, or whether family grief counts as a reason to extend a life that is not bad to end. Keep the procedure usable by someone without philosophical training, and mark every point where it will misfire or require human judgment.
