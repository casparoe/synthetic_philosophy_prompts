I was discussing with a friend until quite late whether a theory can genuinely gain support from data that everyone already accepted before the theory existed, and our argument kept circling back to a pattern that feels more specific than the usual debates about prediction versus accommodation. The pattern runs like this: there are longstanding, uncontroversial measurements—archival astronomical logs, decades-old behavioral datasets, well-catalogued chemical reactions—that sit in the literature as background noise or miscellaneous facts. Then a theoretical framework arrives that does not alter those measurements, but supplies a structural taxonomy under which they cease to be noise and become precisely calibrated outputs. The community's confidence in the framework then increases, not because the measurements were doubted, but because they have been re-situated: what was previously just more data is now the exact signature the framework demands.

I have seen this happen in discussions of old physical constants being reinterpreted through new symmetry principles, and in retrospective re-analyses of pre-existing clinical records through a novel cognitive taxonomy. My friend called this simple post-hoc fitting, but I think it differs in two respects: the theory does not relax its own constraints to absorb the data, and the data are not genuinely novel predictions; rather, an old stock of evidence is revealed to have belonged to the right category all along. Is there an established name in Bayesian epistemology—perhaps adjacent to the old-evidence problem but not identical—for this kind of retrospective categorical integration? If there is none, I want a coined term with explicit boundary conditions separating it from auxiliary-hypothesis rescue, from risky prediction, and from mere semantic relabeling.
