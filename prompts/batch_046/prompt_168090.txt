I'm looking for the established term—if there is one—for a specific inductive move I keep noticing in concept-learning tasks with very few examples, particularly in puzzles in the style of Bongard problems and in some few-shot classification benchmarks.

Here is the pattern. A learner is shown a small set of positive examples that all share a relational property between parts—say, one geometric figure is contained within another. The learner initially considers several interpretations: containment, adjacency, proportional scaling, or simple co-occurrence. Then they encounter a negative example in which the same parts appear, but arranged so that the relation is inverted in a way that is physically impossible or structurally anomalous—for instance, the larger figure is drawn inside the smaller one. Rather than discarding the relational feature altogether, the learner seems to preserve it but immediately refine it by adding an asymmetry constraint: the relation holds only when the parts are ordered in one specific direction, and the anomalous inversion in the negative case is taken not as a failure of presence but as a violation of directionality. Crucially, this refinement appears to happen without testing the revised concept against any new positive instance; the directional constraint is extracted from the internal contradiction of the negative example itself.

I have seen this in human problem-solving protocols and in the error-correction traces of some small-scale concept-learning models, where the model shifts from an undirected relational feature to a directed one after a single contradictory counter-example. Is this configuration recognized in the literature on concept induction? Does it fall under structural alignment, analogical reasoning, or negative-example-driven constraint learning? Or does it represent a distinct mechanism—perhaps something like asymmetry extraction from contradictory instantiation—that lacks a standard label? I want to understand whether the residue points to a real phenomenon that the standard frameworks overlook, or whether I am mischaracterizing a well-known step in analogical mapping, and where exactly the boundary lies between the two.
