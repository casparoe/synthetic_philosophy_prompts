A popular philosophy blog summarizes what it calls Darwin's and Turing's "strange inversions of reasoning" in the following way: Darwin demonstrated that natural selection produces exquisitely adapted biological structures without any guiding intelligence, purpose, or comprehension of the design problem, which shows that complex functionality never requires an underlying mind. Turing then performed the mirror-image inversion by showing that a sufficiently programmed machine can exhibit intelligent behavior—passing the imitation game—without any inner consciousness, understanding, or mental representation, which shows that mental properties are purely behavioral and that consciousness is an unnecessary explanatory posit. The blog concludes that the two inversions together license a sweeping principle: wherever we observe competence, we ought to deny that comprehension plays any causal or explanatory role, so that intentionality, understanding, and subjective experience are always redundant hypotheses to be eliminated rather than phenomena to be explained. Something about this feels like it collapses two distinct arguments into one overreaching thesis, but I am not sure exactly where the missteps are. Is this summary fair?
