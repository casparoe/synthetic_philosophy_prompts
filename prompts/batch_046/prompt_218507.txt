I serve on my university's International Research Partnerships Ethics and Compliance Board, and I need to circulate a formal decision memo before Thursday's meeting. The urgency comes after a long, somewhat heated argument I had last night with a close friend who works in cultural anthropology; she accused our board of practicing what she called "contractual universalism," by which she meant the unexamined assumption that any professional obligation not codified in an explicit, written agreement is automatically a species of corruption. The concrete case is whether to approve, reject, or approve with conditions a proposed three-year joint grant between our School of Public Health and a well-established research institute in East Asia.

The proposal involves practices that our compliance manual flags as high-risk clientelism: substantial ceremonial gift exchanges between principal investigators whose market value far exceeds standard hospitality caps; the routing of subcontracts through intermediaries who are close relatives of senior administrators at the partner institute, presented locally as necessary "relationship maintenance" rather than procurement; and an unitemized budget line described only as "facilitation." The partner institution has submitted a formal letter arguing that these are not payments for advantage but are constitutive acts of scientific cooperation—without them, they claim, local researchers would interpret the partnership as a signal of bad faith and withdraw. The philosophical problem embedded here concerns the ontology of corruption itself: is it defined primarily by the distortion of merit-based allocation, by the violation of formal-legal equality, or by the betrayal of a role-specific fiduciary duty? And is there any non-arbitrary, culturally neutral criterion that can distinguish legitimate relational reciprocity from institutional capture, without already presupposing that public roles and private obligations must be strictly separated?

Please draft the board memo recommending a concrete course of action—approval contingent on protocol restructuring within sixty days, rejection with an explicit redesign pathway, or another clearly defined option—with an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. In particular, the memo must take seriously the argument that labeling these practices "corruption" applies a transactional, contract-based framework onto economies where gift, obligation, and professional identity are inseparable, and it must avoid the opposite error of treating local norms as automatically valid merely because they are non-Western. Please separate what is settled—such as the shared aim of preventing institutional capture—from what is genuinely contested among scholars of comparative ethics and institutional design, including whether the concept of corruption presupposes a specifically Western separation between public role and private obligation, and whether relational gift economies can ever satisfy conditions of institutional neutrality. Do not present any contested view as the consensus.

The memo should be a formal institutional document with an actionable bottom line. At the very end, state the recommendation in a fixed, machine-readable form beginning with Verdict:, followed immediately by a one-sentence summary on the final line that captures the core reasoning.
