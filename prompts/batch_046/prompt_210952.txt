I am a high school student in an advanced biology course and a small philosophy-of-science reading group. Last week, after we discussed heritability coefficients, several classmates argued that a high value—say, around 0.75 for a cognitive measure within one population—proves the trait is biologically fixed and therefore largely immune to educational or social intervention. Others replied that heritability is only a statistical partition of variance observed within a particular range of environments and therefore says nothing about what would happen if those environments changed. I want to move past this familiar stalemate.

What complicates the picture, I think, is that heritability is not an intrinsic property of a trait but a property of a trait-environment pairing. If environmental variance is artificially compressed—imagine a society where everyone receives nearly identical schooling—genetic differences will account for a larger share of the remaining phenotypic variance, yielding a high heritability coefficient that actually reflects environmental uniformity rather than genetic inevitability. At the same time, there is active gene-environment correlation: individuals with different genotypes seek out, evoke, and construct different experiences, so pathways that appear purely environmental may partly be genetically mediated selections into environments. Given these dynamics, can heritability ever legitimately be invoked to argue that equality-promoting interventions are conceptually futile? Or does the logic of heritability itself make it incapable of supporting normative conclusions about malleability?

Write an essay that weighs the competing interpretations. On one side, consider the argument that heritability reveals genuine biological constraints—perhaps through reaction norms or developmental canalization—that limit how much a trait can shift even under enriched conditions. On the other side, consider the argument that heritability is an environmentally contingent descriptor that tells us nothing about causal effects outside the observed range, and that conflating variance-partitioning with determinism commits a category error. Reach and defend a specific conclusion about whether heritability estimates can justify resistance to environmental reform, and explain precisely where you think the strongest counter-argument fails.
