I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training. In recent discussions about integrating large language models into professional workflows, I keep encountering an analogy drawn from the extended-mind literature: using an external cognitive tool—whether a notebook or an LLM—is said to function just like biological memory retrieval, which implies that cognition can literally extend beyond the skull. The comparison maps internal memory, characterized by constant availability, automatic endorsement, and direct integration into reasoning, onto my interaction with a model that I query, whose outputs I evaluate, and then weave into my work. This analogy is being used to license a stronger claim: that with sufficiently tight integration, my conscious self or my cognitive agency includes the model, with direct implications for attributing authorship and moral responsibility. Can you audit this analogy precisely? Provide the explicit mapping between source and target, identify which features of biological memory—such as the storage-versus-generation distinction, the dynamics of endorsement, and conditions of accessibility—the analogy quietly imports that LLM interaction lacks, separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the ethical conclusion from those that do not, and suggest whether a different analogy better captures the nature of human-AI cognitive coupling.
