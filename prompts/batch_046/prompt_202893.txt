At a recent seminar on distributive justice, two participants, Marcus and Elena, entered an extended disagreement about inheritance taxes, dynastic wealth, and fair equality of opportunity. Marcus argued that inheritance taxes should be calibrated to eliminate not merely the transfer of liquid capital but also intergenerational opportunity bundles: compound advantages comprising risk-absorbing family safety nets, legacy social networks, and culturally transmitted preferences that allow wealthy families to convert money into durable, non-fungible life-chance advantages across generations. Elena replied that this framework overreaches. Equality of opportunity requires neutralizing the effects of social class on life prospects, she said, but these bundles are not discrete assets subject to taxation; they are diffuse social conditions embedded in family life, and attempting to tax them away collapses the distinction between correcting structural inequality and policing legitimate family autonomy. Marcus countered that a tax limited to cash transfers is structurally empty, leaving intact precisely the channels — preferential internship pipelines, legacy admissions norms, and the psychological security to pursue high-risk, high-reward careers — that do the real distributive work, so the state ends up symbolically taxing wealth while leaving its functional power untouched. Elena answered that Marcus's position thereby demands social engineering without a principled stopping point. Once relational goods and family culture are treated as taxable externalities, the state must measure and redistribute goods that resist quantification, and the boundary between inheritance taxation and comprehensive control over parenting dissolves. Marcus responded that this boundary objection proves too much: compulsory education, child-labor prohibitions, and vaccine mandates already regulate family transmission for public purposes, so the state already draws lines inside family life; the only question is whether those lines are drawn consistently with equality of opportunity or inconsistently to protect dynastic privilege. Elena conceded that the state regulates universal floors, but insisted that Marcus's bundling framework targets specific family advantages for dismantling rather than establishing universal baselines, and that without a metric for measuring relational goods, his proposal is administratively incoherent as well as normatively overreaching.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves outlined above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then explain who is more right overall, identify what both parties are missing — particularly regarding whether intergenerational opportunity bundles can be conceptually separated from the legitimate functions of family life — and assess whether Marcus's framework can be operationalized without collapsing into the kind of state overreach Elena fears.
