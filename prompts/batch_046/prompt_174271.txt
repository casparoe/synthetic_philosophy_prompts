I am writing to ask for help structuring a lesson, not for the lesson itself. After a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, I want to explain to a small reading group of literature undergraduates—none of whom have ever tried to translate anything—the idea that translation under formal constraint is not lossy compression but a kind of reconstruction in which form and content are interdependent. My friend kept insisting that the only thing that matters is whether the English version conveys the same message as Clément Marot's poem known as Ma mignonne; I want the students to see why preserving the poem's three-syllable couplet structure and its acoustic identity is not decorative but constitutive, and specifically to understand what I have started calling the Marot inversion: the moment when a rhyme partner forces a lexical choice that reverses the emotional trajectory of a line, turning consolation into something that sounds like reproach. I need the pedagogical architecture, not the explanation. First, what is the correct order of introduction—what concept must come before another, and why? Second, what should be the single carrying example that anchors the entire session; it must actually demonstrate the Marot inversion in action, it must not be a prose paraphrase, and it must possess the formal properties that make the inversion unavoidable while lacking any feature that would let students retreat to dictionary meaning? Third, map the misconceptions I should expect—certainly that formal constraint is mere ornament, and that a translation fails if its rhyme scheme diverges from the original, but also any others—specifying exactly where in the sequence each should be preempted. Fourth, design one final check, perhaps a comparative judgment task, that would reveal whether a student grasps preservation of form rather than preservation of semantic content alone. Fifth, tell me what to leave out at this level—should I suppress Hofstadter's autobiographical frame about his wife's death, the comparison with machine translation, or the historical context of Marot's court position—and justify each omission so the concept does not collapse. Please present this as a dependency-ordered teaching plan with the misconception map embedded at the relevant stages.
