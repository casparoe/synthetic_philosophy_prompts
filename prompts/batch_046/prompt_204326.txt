I was arguing with a friend over coffe about that Judy Benjamin problem — you know, the one where the soldier gets told if she's in Red HQ then she's in the second division, plus she's definatly not in the second division, and then philosophers fight about whether her credence in being in Red HQ should stay flat or drop. I've settled on a somewhat niche take: I think she should keep it at one-third, but not because of the standard material-conditional reply or Jeffrey conditioning. Instead, I'm defending the idea that indicative conditionals in these scenarios carry a hidden presupposition about epistemic framing — the commander is partitioning the possibility space in a way tied to her communicative intent, and this creates a separate 'frame' layer that blocks direct conditioning on the conditional content. So updating requires revising not just world-beliefs but the model of how the information was packaged, which in this case leaves the first-order location beliefs untouched.

But I want an honest assessment of how exposed this actually is. What would have to be true for this framing-layer view to be wrong? Are there philosophical arguments showing that treating indicative conditionals this way just collapses into one of the standard Bayesian replies? And is there a risk I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability — like, can I always salvage it by tweaking the framing model whenever a counterexample appears?
