Design a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts philosophical positions on the commensurability of pleasure and pain, centered on lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism but extending to adjacent views. The tree should open with a concrete starting commitment—something like accepting that extreme suffering differs in kind from ordinary discomfort rather than merely in degree—and then proceed through exactly four branching nodes. Each node must be a diagnostic question about a specific case or judgment, not a theoretical label: (1) whether any quantity of pleasure can compensate a single instance of agony that crosses a putative threshold; (2) whether that threshold, if it exists, operates within one life, across lives in a population, or at the level of discrete experiential episodes; (3) whether crossing the threshold renders pain literally incommensurable with pleasure, or merely lexically prior without breaking comparability; and (4) whether a life that stays entirely below the threshold can still be net good if its pleasures massively outweigh its pains. The leaves should include smooth hedonism, continuous negative utilitarianism, strong lexical-threshold NU with incommensurability, weak per-episode threshold views, and at least one precisely characterized hybrid. For every combination of answers that has no occupant in the logical space, briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or being unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot separate—particularly if a weak threshold view collapses into continuous NU under certain framing conditions. The taxonomy should serve an upper-level philosophy seminar audience and must be usable without external references, so do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists. To prevent the tree from reproducing standard textbook divisions alone, inject one non-standard conceptual distinction—such as whether the threshold is indexed to phenomenological intensity versus to a normative status of being wronged—so that one branch separates positions that standard surveys usually conflate.
