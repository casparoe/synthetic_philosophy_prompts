As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or linguistics, I have become convinced that the programming languages and formal calculi I work with shape not merely how I express solutions but how I decompose problems—whether I instinctively model state as mutable, computation as pure transformation, or interaction as message-passing. This feels analogous to claims about natural language and cognition, yet I want to avoid the well-rehearsed introductory debates—color-term categorization, grammatical gender, or Eskimo vocabulary—that appear in every popular treatment of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. Instead, I want to treat linguistic relativity as an open agenda with genuinely unsettled conceptual nodes, and I want to apply it to an under-discussed intersection: whether formal, artificial symbol systems—programming paradigms, type theories, logical calculi—can induce the kind of habitual conceptual restructuring that strong relativity attributes to natural language, or whether their explicit, voluntarily adopted syntax keeps them cognitively isolated.

Before committing effort to empirical design or philosophical argument, I need a defended prioritization of which unresolved questions in linguistic relativity actually matter for this intersection, ordered by dependency, tractability, and practical consequence. Specifically, evaluate and rank these three lines of inquiry.

First, the boundary problem: does relativity operate mainly at perceptual categorization, or can it restructure higher-order reasoning about causality, abstraction, and modal possibility? Second, the persistence problem: if an engineer internalizes a functional-paradigm mode of thought, do those patterns decay upon returning to an imperative paradigm, or are they permanently layered—and does the answer depend on modeling cognition as a unified representational system versus a set of context-activated modules? Third, the reverse-causation problem: can explicit training in formal logic or type theory alter one’s natural-language intuitions about necessity and possibility, implying that artificial languages feed back into ordinary cognition rather than remaining domain-isolated?

For each, defend its priority relative to the others using explicit criteria: (a) how many downstream claims about formal-language cognition depend on resolving it; (b) whether it is tractable through conceptual analysis, existing behavioral data, or structured thought experiments rather than large-scale neuroscience or field linguistics I cannot access; (c) whether an answer would change practical judgments about language design, technical education, or the cognitive architecture of AI systems, versus merely satisfying curiosity. Identify at least one question or sub-debate that should be deprioritized because, even if settled, it would not alter the theoretical or practical stakes for someone reasoning about artificial symbol systems.

Finally, specify where the analogy between natural-language relativity and programming-language cognition is weakest—consider differences in learnability, voluntary adoption, explicit formal semantics, and community enforcement of syntax—and state whether that weakness undermines the entire agenda or merely constrains its scope. Deliver a defended agenda, not a survey, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
