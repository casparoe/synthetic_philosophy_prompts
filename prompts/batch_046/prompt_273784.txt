A recent popular philosophy article summarizes the reliabilist response to bootstrapping and the so-called "easy knowledge" problem as follows: "Reliabilism holds that a belief is justified if it is produced by a process that is actually reliable, regardless of whether the believer possesses independent evidence for that reliability. From this it follows that one may use one's eyes to verify that one's eyes are functioning, then deploy that verified belief to justify further perceptual judgments. Critics charge that this permits vicious epistemic circularity—what they call bootstrapping—and that it generates easy knowledge: if I know I have hands, closure principles let me derive that I am not a handless brain in a vat, thereby dissolving skepticism without ever confronting it. The standard reliabilist reply is that basic beliefs require no prior inferential justification whatsoever; since there is no prior belief being used to justify itself, there is no circularity, and the bootstrapping objection therefore misses the point entirely. Hence reliabilism can accommodate ordinary knowledge without needing to defeat skeptical hypotheses first."

This summary strikes me as suspicious in several places—particularly in how it connects the defense of basic beliefs to the specific bootstrapping scenario, in its treatment of closure and the derivation of anti-skeptical knowledge, and in whether it correctly identifies what the "easy knowledge" objection targets. Is this a fair characterization of the dialectic? Please distinguish what is broadly accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what subtly misrepresents the positions of reliabilists and their critics. In particular, address whether the reply about basic beliefs actually answers the bootstrapping charge as it is usually formulated, whether "easy knowledge" concerns closure principles or something else, and what a proponent of the objection (in the style of Vogel or Cohen) would say this summary gets wrong about the structure of the problem.
