I am drafting an essay for a jurisprudence seminar that examines a hypothetical constitutional architecture I have devised, which I call the “double-lock” amendment framework. In this model, a polity operates under a two-tier system: ordinary constitutional provisions may be amended by a legislative supermajority, but any alteration to the amendment rules themselves—what I term a meta-amendment—must be ratified by a Constitutional Continuity Council. Crucially, the Council’s composition, tenure, and authority are defined entirely by the very amendment rules that the meta-amendment seeks to change. The essay explores whether this structure generates a paradox of legal self-reference analogous to the liar paradox, but situated within the ontology of legal validity rather than pure logic. Specifically, I argue that any successful meta-amendment either presupposes the legitimacy of the Council under the old rules, thereby binding the new rules to the old framework and defeating the purpose of self-amendment, or else severs that legitimacy, producing what I call a “foundational rupture”—a discontinuity in which the legal order cannot account for its own transition without invoking an extra-legal act of constituent power.

The essay then compares this rupture to Alf Ross’s well-known paradox of self-amendment, though I treat Ross’s formulation as a special case of the broader problem rather than its resolution. I also consider whether the paradox can be dissolved by adopting a hierarchical view of legal norms in the manner of Kelsen, or whether it necessarily exposes a limit to formalist accounts of constitutional change. I do not claim that any existing jurisdiction employs this exact mechanism; rather, I use the double-lock as a thought experiment to test the coherence of self-amending authority.

I would be grateful for your comments on the coherence and originality of this argument, particularly on whether the analogy to the liar paradox holds without overstretching logical concepts into legal theory, and whether my notion of “foundational rupture” genuinely adds something to existing discussions of constituent power or merely renames a familiar problem. I am also interested in whether the essay’s structure—moving from the hypothetical mechanism to the comparison with Ross and then to the formalist challenge—feels logically ordered, or whether it would be stronger if the discussion of hierarchical norms preceded the introduction of the rupture concept. Please respond in British English, and feel free to suggest revisions to the framing or to point out any conceptual slips in the way I have described the relationship between the Council’s authority and the meta-amendment it is asked to ratify.
