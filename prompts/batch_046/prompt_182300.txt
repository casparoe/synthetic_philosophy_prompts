I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas — philosophy of tech, cognitive science, that kind of thing — and lately I've been working on a feautre about how old existentialist ideas get misused in AI ethics debates. I was talking with a friend who's TAing an intro Sartre course, and she showed me this exam answer that a student turned in. I want to check my own grading instincts before I quote anything in my article, but I'm not an expert grader.

The exam question was: "Using Sartre's concepts of radical freedom and facticity, analyze why someone who says 'the mood-trackng app classified me as anxious, so that's what I am' is in bad faith. Be specific about how an algorithmic 'look' might figure in, and about what kind of facticity is at stake."

Here's the student's answer, more or less verbatim:

"Sartre holds that human reality is radically free, not in the sense of having choices on a menu but in the ontological sense that consciousness is always already beyond its situation. Facticity is the brute given — body, past, social position — and freedom doesn't erase it but rather takes it up as material for projects. In this case, the app's diagnosis is part of the user's facticity, specifically what we might call algorithmic facticity: the data-profile that functions like a social fact.

The user is in bad faith because they treat this profile as defining their being-in-itself, as if they were a table or rock that just is what it is. They deny transcendence. What's interesting is that Sartre's concept of 'the Look' (le regard) applies here directly — the algorithm is an Other that objectifies me, turning me into an object for its consciousness, just like in Sartre's famous example of being caught peeping through a keyhole. So the algorithmic look produces the same shame and self-objectification.

There's also bad faith vs. self-deception. Sartre's bad faith is essentially his version of Freudian repression: we hide from ourselves the anxiety of freedom by pretending we're fixed things. The student here isn't lying consciously; they're using the app's label as an excuse, which Sartre would call the 'spirit of seriousness' — treating contingent values as necessary truths. Finally, since the app's classification is part of our historical facticity, it's something we have to accept as literally unchangeable, even if we're free to reinterpret its meaning for future projects."
