Some philosophers argue that utilitarianism fails to respect the separateness of persons. Consider a policy choice between distributing 100 units of risk equally across 1,000 people (each facing 0.1 unit) versus concentrating 90 units of risk on a single individual. A utilitarian calculates total risk and prefers the concentrated option. First, explain the separateness-of-persons objection to this reasoning. Second, assess whether the objection still holds if the 1,000 people are understood not as isolated individuals but as interdependent nodes in a shared causal network—for example, a distributed computing cluster in which each node’s failure probabilistically affects the others.

Student answer:
The separateness-of-persons objection, which Bernard Williams developed against act-utilitarianism, claims that utilitarian reasoning illegitimately pools benefits and burdens across distinct individuals, treating a group as though it were one super-person with 1,000 parts. In the risk case, the utilitarian adds the 0.1 risks across all nodes to reach 100, compares that to 90, and picks the single-node failure. Williams argues this violates the moral boundary between persons: each node has its own distinct welfare trajectory, and no amount of minor risk distributed elsewhere can justify imposing a severe risk on one. If we model the nodes as causally linked in a cluster, the objection actually strengthens, because interdependence means harming one node propagates harm to others, so the separate welfare streams are already merged. Thus, in distributed systems, utilitarian aggregation is not only permissible but more accurate, and the separateness objection collapses.
