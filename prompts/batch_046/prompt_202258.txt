I have been observing a particular protective manoeuvre in scientific and theoretical argument, and I am unsure whether philosophy of science assigns it a specific name. Take a behavioural theory that predicts individuals will prefer option A over option B whenever decision conditions are fully transparent. When empirical studies instead find a preference for B, proponents do not typically abandon the core mechanism or add an explicit auxiliary law. Rather, they argue that the conditions were not genuinely transparent—citing, for instance, hidden framing effects or subtle informational asymmetries. The critical feature is that these disqualifying factors are not measured independently; they are inferred precisely from the occurrence of the dispreferred outcome B. Consequently, the boundary conditions for the theory's application are calibrated in a circle: any situation producing B is retrospectively classified as falling outside the theory's domain. I have noticed an analogous structure in macroeconomic reasoning, where claims about market efficiency are protected by redefining efficient-market conditions so as to exclude any observed inefficiency, with the exclusion justified post hoc by the inefficiency itself. This is neither the straightforward rejection of anomalous data nor the addition of a separate, testable auxiliary hypothesis. Is there an established concept—distinct from Popper's conventionalist stratagem, from ad hoc immunisation, and from the Duhem-Quine thesis on auxiliary assumptions—that captures this circular redefinition of applicability conditions? If a precise term exists, how does the described pattern differ from it; and if not, can you propose a rigorous definition together with its boundary conditions, in particular explaining how it differs from legitimate refinement of a theory's scope and whether it undermines falsifiability in a manner distinct from those other strategies?
