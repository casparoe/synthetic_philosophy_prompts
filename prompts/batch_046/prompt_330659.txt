Design a 90-minute workshop activity for approximately 16 advanced philosophy students—graduate or upper-level undergraduate—meeting in a single room with movable seating and blank index cards. The goal is to make the group experience, before it is named, Foucault’s analysis of disciplinary power as productive rather than repressive: specifically, the examination ritual that transforms individuals into documented, comparable cases and thereby co-produces knowledge and authority. Do not tell participants in advance that the exercise concerns Foucault, discipline, or the examination; reveal the framing only during the debrief.

The activity should proceed in four timed phases. Phase one (10 minutes): silent individual writing on a concrete conceptual problem—whether objective moral obligations can survive deep cross-cultural disagreement—producing one paragraph of argument. Phase two (30 minutes): split the room into eight archivists and eight subjects. The subjects discuss the problem aloud in a circle while the archivists sit outside the circle, each observing one assigned subject. Archivists record observations on cards using a rubric of three categories that are defined only negatively and ambiguously—such as not-obvious, not-derivative, not-unstable—without any positive criteria supplied. Archivists may not ask clarifying questions; they must apply the categories and, on the back of each card, assign a hidden weight (1–3) that will determine influence in the next phase. Phase three (15 minutes): archivists collate cards and, using only the accumulated documentation, assign each subject a role for the second discussion—primary speaker, respondent, or silent observer—without explaining the algorithm or showing the cards. Subjects must accept the assignment and adapt their behavior accordingly, even though they do not know what was recorded about them. Phase four (20 minutes): the group reconvenes with subjects seated according to assigned role and discusses a related but distinct problem—whether knowledge requires a stable knowing subject—under rules that grant primary speakers the right to interrupt and silent observers the right to speak only if addressed. Phase five (20 minutes): structured debrief.

In the debrief, map each phase onto a feature of the examination: the initial writing as the production of visibility; the negative rubric as the normalization of judgment that forces archivists to invent unstated norms; the hidden weighting as the internalization of the gaze; the role assignment as the transformation of the individual into a case; and the second discussion as the co-constitution of knowledge and authority. Predict two failures that will occur on the first run: subjects will likely try to extract information about the rubric from archivists, breaking the asymmetry, and archivists will likely converge on an unspoken consensus about weights faster than expected, flattening the ambiguity. Explain how to handle each without breaking the simulation—such as instructing facilitators to deflect clarification requests by stating that the categories are self-interpreting, and requiring archivists to record weights privately before any group comparison. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the long historical sedimentation of institutional architectures, the material durability of written archives, and the macro-social distribution of biopower—and state exactly what the facilitator must supply verbally to fill those gaps. Include rules that prevent appeals to fairness or transparency, specify that no real money or grades are used but that social-discursive authority serves as the payoff, and ensure that every structural feature is arranged so that participants discover the pressure themselves rather than being told about it.
