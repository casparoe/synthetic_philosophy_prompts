I am preparing a diagnostic taxonomy for my graduate seminar on Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the problem of having one thought too many. Rather than sorting views by author or school, I want the classification built from answers to three concrete diagnostic questions that split the logical space of responses.

First, in the rescue case—where an agent saves a spouse rather than strangers—does the reason to save her depend essentially on a personal project that is not reducible to an impartial principle selecting her, or can it be fully accommodated within an impartial framework that happens to weight her more heavily?

Second, does the problem of one thought too many arise primarily because utilitarianism demands excessive calculation, because it misdescribes the phenomenology of attachment, or because it treats personal commitments as dispensable permissions rather than as independent sources of reasons?

Third, can an impartial moral theory preserve integrity by restricting its domain to public or institutional decision-making, or must impartiality be abandoned altogether in favor of a pluralist account of practical reasons?

Please construct a decision tree (or equivalent taxonomy) in which each internal node is labeled by one of these questions, and each branch corresponds to one answer option. At every leaf—whether named, such as Williams's own anti-theory stance, Railton's indirect consequentialism, Scheffler's agent-centered prerogative, or unnamed—state the position's answers to all three questions in sequence, followed by a single sentence describing its most awkward commitment, especially regarding whether a morally good agent may ever weigh her own projects more heavily than an impartial calculus permits.

In addition, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate given these three axes, explaining why they converge. Identify any logical combination of answers that remains unoccupied—whether incoherent, deeply implausible, or merely unexplored—and briefly diagnose why no position occupies it. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should be usable by someone familiar with the debate but not relying on external reference. Write in formal, polished academic prose.
