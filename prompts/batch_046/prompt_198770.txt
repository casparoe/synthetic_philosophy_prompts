hey so i'm working on this philosphy-of-letterforms thing and i keep walking myself into a wall. here's the loop:

(1) If two shapes both get called an 'a', there has to be some essential feature (or feature-set) they share that makes them 'a's and not 'b's or 'd's.

(2) But the Letter Spirit approach to recogntion says it doesn't work like that. there's no fixed template. instead there's a flexible "spirit" -- a network of relational roles like "enclosure on left with tail extending right" -- that can be filled by totally different geometries. so a double-decker 'a', a handwritten loop 'a', and a blackletter 'a' all satisfy the same spirit with almost no shared strokes.

(3) So being an 'a' isn't having property F. it's just being within some tolerance radius of the cluster-center for 'a' in style-space.

(4) Now style-space is continous. type designers interpolate between letterforms all the time through grids of intermediates, and each step looks like a plausible letter in its own right.

(5) So morph an 'a' step-by-step into a 'b', then into a 'c', and so on around the alphbet back to 'a'. at every step you're inside the cluster-center of the intermediate.

(6) By transitivity, then, the original 'a' is linked through cluster-centers all the way to 'z'. there's no principled boundary separating 'a' from anything else; the alphabet is just one continuous blob with arbitrary regional labels.

(7) But that can't be right. when i read i don't experience letters as arbitrary slices of dough. i experience them as genuinely different kinds. literacy can't just be learning regional labels.

(8) So (1) must be false? or maybe (4) is wrong because interpolation creates new letters rather than preserving identity?

i'd really like to keep (2) -- that letters have spirits not fixed shapes -- and (7), that there's something genuinely categorical about reading. if i reject (3) or (5) instead, what does that cost? where's the actual slip?
