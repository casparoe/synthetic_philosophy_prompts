As a journalist covering the philosophy behind public policy, I am examining how three different legal architectures for sex work treat not the transaction itself but the people and services that surround it—security staff, drivers, landlords, and premises managers. In the Nordic model, purchase is criminalized while sale is not, and exit services are state-funded; in the German and Dutch frameworks, the trade is legalized and subject to labor, tax, and health regulations; and in New Zealand, decriminalization removes criminal penalties for all parties and subjects associated workplace support to ordinary occupational health and safety law. What strikes me is that each regime classifies these ancillary roles differently: some are treated as presumptively exploitative intermediaries, others as licensed employees or contractors, and others as unremarkable service providers. 

What philosophical commitments about coercion, consent, structural vulnerability, and human dignity does each of these classifications presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory in each architecture: does the Nordic framework presuppose that any third-party facilitation introduces an irreducible asymmetry that vitiates consent, or does it rest on a more specific claim about economic dependency? Does legalization presuppose that contractual labor relations and state inspection can render an otherwise asymmetric exchange fully autonomous, and if so, what conception of autonomy makes that transformation possible? Does decriminalization presuppose that the primary harm lies in criminalization itself rather than in the exchange, and what view of dignity permits security and premises provision to be treated analogously to support staff in any other industry?

Please distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Are these regimes philosophically underdetermined—compatible with rival theories of autonomy or harm—or do they quietly rule out specific views? Finally, if the underlying philosophical commitment in one regime were replaced—say, substituting a contractarian theory of autonomy for a vulnerability-based one—what concrete institutional changes would necessarily follow in how that jurisdiction treats drivers, security personnel, and landlords? I am not looking for a legislative history or a bibliography, but for the precise conceptual machinery that makes each architecture coherent, or incoherent, with its own premises.
