At a workshop on moral psychology, two discussants—Mira and Leo—debated whether the situationist critique undermines virtue ethics or merely forces it to refine its ontology of character. Mira argued that recent empirical patterns support what she called “domain-indexed virtue consistency”: people do exhibit stable moral dispositions, but these are indexed to social domains rather than globally uniform. She noted that an individual might display sustained honesty in financial transactions yet show strategic deception in intimate relationships, and that this is not evidence of character absence but of compartmentalized trait structures activated by domain-specific norms. Leo responded that this move saves virtue ethics only by abandoning its core commitment to global, cross-situational dispositions; once virtues are allowed to be domain-bound, they cease to function as the integrative, life-shaping qualities that virtue ethicists traditionally describe, and instead collapse into socially constructed roles. Mira countered that Leo overstates the globalist requirement: even Aristotelian virtue involves practical wisdom that is sensitive to context, so domain-indexing is not an ad hoc rescue but a natural elaboration of context-sensitive phronesis. She added that situationist experiments typically measure micro-behaviors—such as returning a found dime—under trivial stakes, whereas virtues are meant to govern actions of genuine moral weight; when studies aggregate high-stakes, domain-relevant behaviors, cross-situational consistency is substantially stronger. Leo granted the stakes distinction but argued it renders virtue ethics unfalsifiable: any failure of consistency is dismissed as occurring below a “threshold of moral significance” that is defined post hoc to protect the theory. He further observed that if virtues only manifest in high-stakes contexts, they cannot explain the moral texture of everyday life, which is precisely the domain virtue ethics claims to illuminate. Mira then pressed a comparative point: we intuitively distinguish between a person who is consistently kind in close relationships but ruthless in commerce and a person who is erratically cruel across all settings; if there are no underlying traits, both patterns should be equally unpredictable, yet our predictive and evaluative practices treat them very differently. Leo conceded that domain-indexing captures a real pattern in folk psychology but insisted it is a narrative heuristic rather than evidence of real dispositions—people construct coherent domain-specific stories to make social interaction tractable, not because compartmentalized virtues actually exist. They turned to a bystander and asked for a verdict.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: evaluate Mira’s claim about domain-indexed consistency, Leo’s charge that this abandons global virtue, Mira’s defense via phronesis and the stakes distinction, Leo’s unfalsifiability objection, Mira’s predictive-comparison argument, and Leo’s heuristic explanation. Say who is right about each specific issue, identify what both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether domain-indexing can be tested empirically without relying on the very folk-psychological narratives Leo suspects—and state who is more right overall.
