I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. My friend insists that once you formalize learning as optimization, the four categories blur into one continuum; I think the distinctions survive, but I need to see whether they depend on philosophical vocabulary that cannot be exported.

I am fluent in distributed software architecture—specifically version-control workflows, CI/CD pipelines, sandboxed testing environments, dependency management, compiled versus interpreted execution, and open-source package inheritance—but I have no formal training in philosophy of mind. Restate Dennett’s four kinds of minds entirely in that engineering vocabulary, treating each as a system architecture rather than an analogy. For Darwinian creatures, use the language of hard-coded firmware with no runtime updates and selection occurring only at build-time across generations. For Skinnerian creatures, use reinforcement-driven parameter tuning through direct production-environment feedback loops with no internal model. For Popperian creatures, use internal sandboxed simulation environments where candidate behaviors are executed in isolation before any production deployment, allowing failure to occur without system damage. For Gregorian creatures, use systems that import external libraries—language, tools, cultural artifacts—as runtime dependencies, inheriting functionality and version histories without having compiled those capacities from direct experience.

Then give me the residue report, honestly. Which distinctions translate cleanly, which require distortion—for instance, does mapping the Popperian “inner environment” onto a software sandbox smuggle in an agentive programmer or a cost-free failure mode that real minds do not have? Which parts of Dennett’s view, particularly the claim that Gregorian minds are culturally redesignable through tool inheritance, have no counterpart in dependency management? I want you to inject one specific engineering concept into the Gregorian translation—treat cultural transmission as open-source dependency injection with version conflicts, deprecated APIs, and transitive dependency chains—and report whether that captures normative commitment and intergenerational error-correction, or whether it reveals that Gregorian learning involves something software architecture cannot express.

Finally, tell me whether the residues point to a genuine gap in Dennett’s framework—perhaps something about embodied risk and mortality that the software metaphor makes visible—or whether they only show that engineering vocabulary is the wrong instrument for describing minds. Be precise about what survives, what is distorted, and what is silent, and do not let the metaphor do philosophical work it has not earned.
