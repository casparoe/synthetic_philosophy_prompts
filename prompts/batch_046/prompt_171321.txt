Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning to philosophy, I find myself puzzled by a methodological convention that seems ubiquitous in consciousness science and clinical neurology, yet rarely defended in explicitly philosophical terms. In both laboratory and bedside settings, consciousness is operationalized almost exclusively through explicit report: a subject is shown a near-threshold stimulus and asked to indicate whether she saw it; in disorders-of-consciousness assessment, the absence of command-following or verbalization is taken as strong evidence for the absence of awareness, even when autonomic or behavioral markers suggest otherwise. What strikes me as particularly telling is the auxiliary rule—common in masked-priming and rapid-presentation protocols—that reports arriving outside a brief temporal window are discarded or downgraded, on the grounds that they represent post-hoc narrative reconstruction rather than direct readout from an inner stage. Meanwhile, the same disciplines freely acknowledge subliminal priming, blindsight, and implicit learning, in which stimuli clearly influence cognition and behavior without ever being reported.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of consciousness does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it implicitly commit to what Dennett calls a Cartesian theater—a single, unified locus where information is presented to an inner observer, with reportability serving as the direct proxy for presence on that stage? Or could a proponent of Dennett's multiple drafts model accommodate the practice by treating verbal report as merely one competing draft among many, without privileging it as the authoritative broadcast? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit metaphysics and epistemology: does the binary classification of trials into seen and unseen, together with the temporal-window exclusion rule, presuppose that there is a definite fact of the matter about whether a particular draft occupied the central stage at a specific time? And is the practice philosophically consistent with its own acknowledgment of non-reported influence—does that acknowledgment force a distinction between pre-conscious processing and conscious but unbroadcast drafts, or does it simply reveal that the practice is underdetermined?

Furthermore, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Could one retain the same experimental and clinical protocols while denying that there is any central theater—interpreting the report window, for instance, not as guarding the boundary of an inner auditorium but as filtering for coherence among distributed narratives? And if we were to replace the theatrical presupposition with a thoroughgoing multiple-drafts view, what concrete changes would be required in the scoring rules, the temporal criteria, and the very concept of a conscious trial? I am especially interested in whether the practice quietly rules out distributed, non-broadcast forms of awareness, or whether it is neutral enough to be licensed by several competing theories of mind.
