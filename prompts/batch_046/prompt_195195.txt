I am a novelist constructing a cycle of novels set in the aftermath of a failed revolution, and I repeatedly face a particular compositional decision: whether to narrate a contested scene from the viewpoint of the surviving official archives—which exist precisely because the victors preserved them—or to reconstruct the perspective of the defeated, knowing that every invented detail risks imposing my own anachronistic coherence onto what was genuinely chaotic and unrecorded. Please construct a reusable decision procedure I can apply before drafting any scene that turns on a disputed historical claim. The procedure should take the form of ordered questions, specifying what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, marking the point where it mandates strict adherence to attested documents and the point where it permits—or demands—invention because the silence of the record is itself structurally significant. It should be grounded in the philosophical question of whether history is written by the victors, carefully distinguishing the causal claim that power determines documentary survival, the epistemic claim that lost perspectives are irrecoverable, and the normative claim that narrating the defeated corrects a moral imbalance; it should flag exactly where these three claims collapse into one another in practice. Apply the procedure to three cases: a scene depicting a trial transcript preserved only in the victors' version; a scene depicting a private conversation for which no direct record exists but whose outcome is documented; and a scene depicting an act of destruction evidenced solely by the absence of what ought to remain. The procedure must include one hard case in which fidelity to it forces a narratively unsatisfying refusal of closure, and it must explicitly identify which steps are judgement calls disguised as rules. Please begin with the bottom line in the first sentence, then present the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
