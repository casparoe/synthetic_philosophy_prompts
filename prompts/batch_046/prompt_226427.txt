In contemporary AI governance and safety engineering, institutions often pair two commitments: first, that high-stakes autonomous systems must be formally auditable—their decision rules explicitly codified and mechanically verifiable against a specification; second, that human oversight remains indispensable because, citing Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem, no sufficiently powerful formal system can contain all truths about itself or fully correct its own errors, so algorithmic self-trust is impossible in principle. Curiously, the same frameworks rarely invoke Gödelian limits when accounting for human auditors’ fallibility or for oversight committees’ own failure modes. What philosophical commitments about the domain of Gödel’s results—whether they apply only to syntactic formal systems, to all rational cognition, or specifically to non-biological computation—does this asymmetrical practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of formalization, self-reference, and cognitive authority that makes the pairing seem necessary rather than contingent. Is it coherent, or does it depend on a syntax-semantics distinction, or a tacit-judgment versus explicit-rule distinction, that the practice simultaneously undermines? And if we replaced the Gödelian justification with an empirical, reliability-based argument about current AI limitations, which institutional features—such as mandatory human-in-the-loop requirements or prohibitions on fully autonomous verification—would have to be reconceived or abandoned?
