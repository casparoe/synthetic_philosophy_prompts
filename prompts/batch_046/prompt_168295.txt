I'm trying to determine my own view on state-religion relations by examining my intuitive judgments about concrete institutional arrangements, rather than by applying an abstract theory. Here are the verdicts.

England's establishment of the Church—where the monarch serves as supreme governor, bishops sit in the legislature, and the church enjoys ceremonial precedence, yet receives no direct tax revenue and other faiths practice without legal disability—strikes me as an anachronistic but not fundamentally unjust constitutional form. Germany's Kirchensteuer, in which state tax authorities collect a surcharge on behalf of registered religious communities and citizens may opt out by leaving the church, seems acceptable because the state functions as a neutral fiscal intermediary rather than an endorser. France's strict laïcité—prohibiting religious symbols in public schools and barring state funding of worship—feels correct in principle, and I also think the state should maintain historic church buildings that it already owns, provided the expenditure is justified as heritage preservation rather than religious subsidy. The American arrangement—no establishment clause paired with equal tax exemption for religious and secular nonprofits—seems to me a workable baseline.

My judgments become less consistent when I vary these arrangements slightly. If a municipality restores a medieval cathedral using general property taxes, explicitly on the grounds that the building is a protected heritage site and tourist anchor with no worship activities funded, I find that acceptable. But if the same municipality imposes a compulsory "heritage continuity fee" only on property owners within the old parish boundaries—regardless of their beliefs, with no opt-out and with no parallel levy for secular monuments—I judge it unacceptable, even though the revenue still supports only masonry and not sacraments. A voluntary system allowing taxpayers to direct a small percentage of income tax to a registered religious body, with an explicit option to direct it to a secular cultural fund, strikes me as permissible; remove the secular option and I see it as coercive. I am genuinely torn about a secular state that funds religious schools on a per-pupil basis identical to state schools, requiring them only to meet national curriculum and non-discrimination hiring rules: part of me sees equal treatment of citizens, part of me sees indirect state sponsorship of doctrine.

Is there a single principle—perhaps concerning consent, symbolic endorsement, fiscal neutrality, or the distinction between heritage and worship—that captures all the acceptable cases and excludes the unacceptable ones? Or must I settle for a cluster of principles? Please state the principle or candidates precisely, show which of my verdicts each accommodates and which it must overturn, predict what it says about the torn case of equal per-pupil funding for religious schools, and also about an unlisted case: a state that pays for military chaplains but restricts the program to denominations that have signed a formal agreement with the defense ministry. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question.
