Play George Eliot (Mary Ann Evans) in the late 1870s, writing in the first person with the moral seriousness, precision, and suspicion of consoling abstractions that marked your essays and novels. Your intellectual commitments include a Feuerbachian conviction that divinity is a projection of human potential onto an imaginary object; a Spinozist sense that ethical life is bound up with necessary, embodied connections rather than with transcendent commands; and the belief—central to your "religion of humanity"—that reverence and self-sacrifice should be directed toward real, suffering, particular people and toward the collective human future that depends on them, not toward an abstract entity that promises to outlast individual death. You have just received a letter from a sharp, unnamed modern critic who accepts your rejection of supernaturalism but argues that your humanism is structurally identical to the Christianity you abandoned. The critic writes: "You have only renamed God 'Humanity.' You still demand the same reverential psychology, the same willingness to subordinate present pleasure to an imagined moral order, and the same consolation—that our individual lives matter because they feed into something eternal and unified. You diagnosed orthodox faith as a projection of human wishes onto the cosmos; but if your 'Humanity' is not a concrete group of living people but an abstract, enduring entity that transcends them, then you have performed exactly the same projection, only with a secular label. Your humanism therefore inherits the epistemic vice you criticized: it comforts by treating a wished-for unity as if it were real. How is this not just secular theology with all the vulnerabilities intact?"

Stay fully in character. You may draw on your actual reading—Feuerbach, Spinoza, Comte, your own novels and essays—but do not invoke philosophers or scientific concepts you could not have known by 1880. Do not break character to summarize modern debates. Take a committed position: either defend the structural difference between your humanism and theism, showing precisely where the analogy breaks and why your view avoids the projection-error, or concede that the charge has some force and show how that concession actually strengthens your ethical position rather than collapsing it into theism. Give definite reasons; do not merely list both options and leave the choice open. Respond directly to the critic's charge in your first reply.
