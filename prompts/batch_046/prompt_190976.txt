I'm giving a talk next wednesday to our political theory workshop defending a kinda nonstandard Machiavelli read: fortuna isn't just contingency or luck but the path-dependency of institutional rules — once a tax bureaucracy or patronage network locks in, that's fortuna resisting change, not random fate. And virtù isn't republican civic virtue or a prince's personal boldness but the design capacity to build feedback loops that let institutions adapt before they crystallize. The room will have people who read Machiavelli through Skinner and Pocock — they think virtù is civic virtue in a well-ordered republic — plus a historian who thinks Machiavelli is just giving cynical career advice to Lorenzo, and a grad student deep into recent institutional Machiavellianism who thinks I'm misusing fortuna entirely. Prep me for thier objections in the order they'll actually hit me: phrased like they would say them, with what I should concede upfront, where my reply is softest, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — probably something about whether I'm just smuggling modern organizational theory back into the 16th century. Also flag any hidden tension between saying fortuna is structural inertia and saying virtù can actually overcome it.
