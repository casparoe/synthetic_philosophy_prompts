Examination question: Donald Davidson maintains that radical interpretation depends on the principle of charity and is irreducibly holistic. Explain (a) what charity requires in the assignment of belief and meaning; (b) why Davidson regards holism as unavoidable; and (c) whether an interpreter can consistently attribute a localised, domain-specific pattern of false belief to an agent—say, a systematic misclassification of trees as artefacts arising from a perceptual anomaly—without causing interpretation to collapse globally. In your answer, distinguish charity understood as a constitutive condition of interpretation from charity understood as a merely regulative heuristic, and discuss whether Davidson's notion of triangulation (the causal interaction among interpreter, agent, and a shared environment) imposes independent constraints on the amount or kind of error that can be tolerated in a single domain.

Student answer: Davidson's principle of charity requires us to maximise agreement when interpreting a speaker: we should attribute beliefs that are largely true and logically consistent. This is not merely a practical guideline but a constitutive condition—without the assumption that the speaker is broadly rational, there is no basis for assigning meanings to utterances at all. Because belief and meaning are interdependent, interpretation is holistic; we cannot fix the content of a single belief in isolation but must construct an entire network of beliefs and meanings that cohere together. This holism implies that error is always possible, but only against a background of massive agreement.

With respect to localised error, Davidson permits domain-specific false belief provided it is properly contained. If an agent systematically misidentifies trees as artefacts due to a perceptual anomaly, the interpreter can accommodate this by treating it as an isolated deviation, so long as there exists a 'buffer zone' of correct beliefs about physical objects, causal relations, and spatial orientation that anchors the rest of the interpretation. Should this buffer fail—if, for example, the agent's errors spread to basic logic or to immediate environmental interaction—interpretation would indeed collapse.

Davidson's concept of triangulation introduces an additional constraint. For interpretation to succeed, the interpreter, the agent, and the world must all stand in appropriate causal relations, and they must share the same sensory modalities through which the world is accessed. Consequently, an agent that relies on radically different perceptual mechanisms—such as one that navigates exclusively by echolocation rather than vision—might still be interpreted charitably, but triangulation would be undermined because there is no shared sensory pathway to the objects being discussed. Thus charity is necessary but not sufficient; shared causal access through identical sensory channels is also required.

Finally, Davidson's position is essentially a variant of Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation. Both philosophers accept that there can be multiple, equally correct manual translations that assign different ontologies to the same behavioural evidence, with no further fact of the matter determining which ontology is correct. Davidson differs from Quine only in adding the causal requirement of triangulation, which Quine's account neglects, but he retains Quine's core commitment to ontological relativity.
