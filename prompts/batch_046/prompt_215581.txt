I am not an academic, but after years of volunteering as an editor for a small community archive newsletter, I have become convinced that every account of our town’s 1970s factory closures carries the fingerprints of whoever survived to tell it. Each month I must choose which first-person submissions to publish and how to frame them—whether to present them as standalone testimony, as one voice in a contested record, or as evidence of a particular economic cause. Readers write in either accusing me of printing propaganda or of suppressing the real story, and I have no formal training in history or philosophy to fall back on. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist of tests I can apply to a single submission in about fifteen minutes—that translates whatever philosophy and historical theory actually say about power, perspective, and the possibility of a neutral record into steps I can follow without jargon. The procedure should handle three specific kinds of submissions I actually receive: an account from a union organizer who led strikes; an account from the daughter of a plant manager who emphasizes fiscal inevitability; and an account from someone who was a child of a laid-off worker and remembers the closures mainly through family silence and rumor. For each, show me the worked application, including one case where the procedure tells me to frame or present the account in a way I will probably dislike. The checklist must distinguish when relabeling or contextualizing an account—calling it “one perspective among others,” substituting “the closure” for “the betrayal,” or pairing it with a contradictory source—actually changes the claim being made versus when it is a safe editorial move. It must also flag the precise points where judgment cannot be eliminated and I must decide myself, and it must state honestly what the procedure cannot do—for example, whether it can ever justify excluding an account entirely, or whether it can establish a truly victor-independent baseline for comparison. Keep the language usable by a non-specialist, fix the stakes and time constraints of a monthly publication cycle with a three-person review board that has final sign-off, and do not ask me to consult texts I do not have.
