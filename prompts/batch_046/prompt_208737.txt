I am trying to articulate my interpretation of Dewey's experimentalism regarding democracy and inquiry, and I want a consistency check before I proceed. My commitments are approximately these:

(1) Democracy is best conceived as a mode of social inquiry—a cooperative, institutional method for resolving shared problems—rather than as a mechanism for aggregating fixed, pre-political preferences.

(2) Experimentalism entails that all social institutions, democratic ones included, are working hypotheses subject to revision in light of their consequences; no political arrangement is permanently fixed.

(3) There is no independent, transcendent standard of value outside the process of cooperative inquiry; ends and goods emerge from and are continuously revised within that process.

(4) Despite (2), certain procedural conditions—protections for minority dissent, open communication, and inclusive participation—are non-negotiable prerequisites for inquiry itself and therefore stand outside democratic revision.

(5) The goal of democratic inquiry is intelligent problem-resolution, not consensus for its own sake; persistent, well-structured disagreement can indicate a healthy rather than a failed inquiry.

Which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? I am especially concerned about whether (2) undermines (4), and whether (3) is compatible with (5)'s appeal to "intelligent resolution" as a normative standard. Please rank the tensions by severity, identify what each tension forces me to surrender, and indicate whether there is a principled distinction that lets me keep both (2) and (4) without ad hoc exemption. Answer in American English.
