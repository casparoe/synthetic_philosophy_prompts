I am presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on political economy and food security. My thesis is that the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–1961 should be analyzed neither as a natural disaster driven by weather failure nor as a straightforward political crime of intentional genocide, but through Amartya Sen’s entitlement-collapse framework—specifically, that the rapid collectivization and communal dining-hall system destroyed household-level exchange entitlements in a manner structurally analogous to the market failures Sen identifies in the 1943 Bengal famine, even though the Chinese case involved direct state procurement rather than private speculation. The audience will include development economists familiar with Sen’s work, economic historians of China who emphasize either drought data or policy-error explanations, and a political philosopher who maintains that famines are always deliberate state crimes. They care about causal identification, about whether moral categories map cleanly onto economic mechanisms, and about avoiding anachronistic moral framing.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually receive, in roughly the order they will arise. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—likely whether applying entitlement analysis to the Great Leap Forward obscures culpability in the way that similar analyses of the Irish famine have been criticized for obscuring colonial policy—and provide a concise reply for it.
