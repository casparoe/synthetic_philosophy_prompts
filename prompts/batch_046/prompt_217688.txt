I'm trying to pin down a pattern I keep seeing in intutionistic and constructivist writing, but I don't really know the field's vocabulary so bear with me. There's this thing where a classic result that usually needs law of excluded middle gets 'rescued,' but the rescue isn't in the proof — it's hidden in the defintions. Like, everyone still talks about 'the intermediate value theorem,' but in constructive versions the function has to be locally nonzero or apartness-preserving, and 'hits a value' means comes within epsilon. The proof then goes through fine, but it feels like the hard logical work was smuggled into the premises. I've noticed the same with choice — people keep the name 'choice principle' but quietly restrict it to countable families with explicit selectors — and with compactness, where the space has to be totally bounded with an explicit modulus.

Is there an established concept for this specific move? Not just weakening the conclusion, but embedding the missing non-constructive strength into the vocabulary so the original theorem can stay intact? I know about double-negation translation and about Brouwerian counterexamples, but those feel different: one translates proofs, the other shows collapse. This is more like definitional drift, where the terms themselves absorb the shock. Does it have a name in the constructivist litterature, and if so how should I distinguish it from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses or from just changing the subject?
