I've been thinking on my own about whether major historical turning points are truly accidental or whether they were bound to happen eventually, and I've noticed that my intuitions shift from case to case in a way I can't quite explain. I'd like to see whether there is a consistent principle behind them, or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are my judgments. If the Library of Alexandria had survived intact through the medieval period, I think modern physics might have arrived roughly a century earlier, but its fundamental character—mathematical, experimental, mechanistic—would have remained the same. So the broad trajectory feels inevitable, even if the calendar shifts.

By contrast, if the Black Death had never reached Europe, I believe the feudal social structure could have persisted for several more centuries, and the particular form of capitalism that emerged in Western Europe might never have developed. That feels like a genuine branch point, not merely a delay.

Then there is a counterfactual I invented: suppose in 1946 an engineer in Prague independently develops the transistor just months before Bell Labs does, but the Soviet authorities suppress the patent and restrict its use to military applications. I still think widespread consumer computing arrives by around 1980, only with different corporate leaders and a different geographic center. The technology itself seems inevitable; who profits from it does not.

With political events, however, I feel differently. If Napoleon had prevailed at Waterloo and maintained the Continental System for another full decade, I think Britain's global maritime dominance might have been permanently weakened rather than merely postponed.

Yet I am less certain about democratic institutions. If the American colonies had lost the Revolutionary War, I still suspect that some form of democratic republic eventually emerges in North America by about 1900, though I am far less confident in that prediction than I am about the transistor.

Taken together, my verdicts seem to be that technological development is usually inevitable, economic systems are sometimes structurally determined and sometimes contingent on biological or demographic accidents, and immediate political outcomes depend on specific battles in ways that long-run institutional forms do not. Is there a single principle—perhaps about the number of independent pathways that can produce an outcome, or about whether a system contains internal pressures that eventually force a resolution—that can capture all of these distinctions? Or must I abandon consistency and admit that I am weighing different factors in each case? Please state any principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it explains and which it must overturn, and apply it to one further case: what should we expect if the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 had been delayed by a decade because of a different Soviet leader?
