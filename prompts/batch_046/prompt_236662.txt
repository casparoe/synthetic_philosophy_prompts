I have been trying to determine whether shared nationality generates special moral obligations by examining my intuitions across a range of cases, and I am unsure whether they cohere. Below are my verdicts.

A government facing a domestic natural disaster is permitted, and perhaps required, to allocate emergency resources to its own citizens first, even when foreign victims face greater need and the marginal aid to foreigners would save more lives, so long as the domestic crisis is genuinely severe.

An individual donor is not blameworthy for directing charitable contributions to local causes that are less cost-effective than foreign alternatives; she acts admirably if she gives abroad, but she does not wrong anyone by preferring the domestic charity.

A state acts wrongly if it withholds surplus life-saving medicines—medicines it will not use itself—from foreigners facing an active epidemic, solely to maintain a strategic reserve beyond any plausible domestic need.

Citizens have a stronger obligation to participate in democratic institutions that govern them directly than to contribute to global governance mechanisms, even when the latter affect larger populations.

In a direct, face-to-face rescue scenario—pulling one person from immediate danger when only one can be saved—a rescuer should not prioritize her compatriot over an equally endangered foreigner; shared nationality should not serve as a tie-breaker.

Parents are clearly permitted, and perhaps required, to prioritize their own children's welfare over that of foreign children, yet I do not believe citizens may analogously prioritize compatriots over foreigners in direct interpersonal contexts.

A private employer may treat shared nationality as a permissible tie-breaker between genuinely equivalent candidates, but it acts wrongly if it rejects a clearly superior foreign applicant to hire a domestic one.

These judgments seem to me partly consistent, yet they contain a visible tension: if nationality grounds associative duties in democratic participation and state-level disaster relief, why does it fail to justify tie-breaking in direct rescue or preferential hiring? Conversely, if nationality carries no weight in direct rescue, what distinguishes the state-level cases?

Is there a unified principle—perhaps drawing on institutional co-authorship, the negative-positive duty distinction, or the scope of collective responsibility—that captures the majority of these verdicts? If the principle requires abandoning one, which should go? And what does the best account predict for a case I have not evaluated: a wealthy individual who emigrates permanently, retains her original citizenship, uses public services exclusively in her new country of residence, and must decide whether her tax obligations run primarily to the state she left or to the state she now inhabits?
