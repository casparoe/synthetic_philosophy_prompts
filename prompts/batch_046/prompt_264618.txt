As a novelist whose fiction turns on the moral psychology of characters in societies under strain, I teach an occasional graduate seminar on the emotional architecture of ethical life. For the final examination, I want one essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard taxonomy—guilt as agent-centered failure, shame as audience-centered exposure, internal sanction versus social spectacle. Students must take a definite, reasoned position on two interlocked problems: which emotion is the more morally productive, and whether a culture can deliberately engineer a shift from one dominant moral affect to the other without destroying the very conditions that make that affect ethically authoritative.

The question should compel students to argue that guilt’s value depends on a form of emotional autonomy that social redesign would corrupt, while recognizing that shame’s social utility may reduce morality to visibility and performance.

Please provide:

1. The exam question, framed for a three-hour final, specifying an approximate word count and instructing students explicitly that they must defend one position rather than survey both.

2. A model answer of roughly 800 words that takes a committed stance—specifically, that guilt is the more ethically serious emotion, but that a culture cannot fully choose guilt over shame without undermining the authenticity conditions that make guilt morally binding. The answer should argue rather than list; it must address at least one serious counterexample, such as the claim that shame is more effective at maintaining cohesion in large, anonymous societies.

3. A marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer. The scheme should name the specific conceptual moves required for top marks: the distinction between self-evaluation and social exposure; the argument linking emotional authenticity to moral autonomy; the observation that shame operates through visibility while guilt operates through internalized normative structure; and the explicit defense of why the chosen position survives its strongest objection. Define a B answer as one that correctly describes both emotions and compares them competently, but refuses to commit or fails to engage the autonomy-objection.
