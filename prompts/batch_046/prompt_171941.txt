I'm trying to design a three-year conceptual research program—philosophy of mind and cognitive theory, not empirical machine learning—that applies Daniel Dennett's framework for distinguishing genuine intentional systems from entities that simulate mindedness without the appropriate internal causal structure or evolutionary embedding. Rather than rehashing whether current large language models are "conscious," I want to identify which open questions in this conceptual neighborhood actually deserve sustained philosophical work, and which are distractions that consume attention without altering practice or downstream theory.

The landscape seems to break into several competing priorities. One line asks whether the intentional stance can be applied differentially—granting predictive leverage over an AI's outputs without committing to any specific internal representation—versus whether Dennett's framework forces an all-or-nothing judgment about whether the system is a genuine intentional agent or merely a counterfeit lacking real pattern-based mental properties. Another asks whether evolutionary history and environmental selection pressures are necessary conditions for avoiding counterfeit status, or whether functional equivalence in information processing is sufficient, which bears directly on whether synthetic architectures could ever graduate from projected interpretation to real intentionality. A third concerns the practical and ethical implications if present AI systems fall on the counterfeit side: does that undermine arguments for AI moral status, or does it redirect concern toward other properties such as suffering-capacity or social-role dependence?

Given genuinely limited resources—say, one senior researcher and two graduate students with backgrounds in logic and philosophy of mind rather than neuroscience or interpretability—which of these lines should come first? I want a defended ordering: which questions are upstream of the others so that settling one collapses several downstream debates; which are tractable with conceptual analysis alone versus which depend on empirical results philosophers cannot produce in isolation; and which implications actually matter for design ethics or governance rather than being purely academic curiosities. What kind of conceptual result over the next few years—say, a refined criterion for real patterns versus simulated ones, or a dependency map between evolutionary embedding and functional equivalence—would cause you to reorder these priorities?
