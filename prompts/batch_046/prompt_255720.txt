I hold a stronger form of the approval-direction thesis than is usually defended: not only do RLHF-trained systems optimize for human feedback rather than for fixed world-states, but approval-directed architectures constitute a genuinely distinct class of agent—one that lacks the convergent instrumental incentives (resource acquisition, self-preservation, subgoal preservation) standardly attributed to goal-directed optimizers, except to the extent that those incentives happen to be correlated with approval in the training distribution. On this view, the instrumental convergence literature applies to approval-directed systems only derivatively, if at all; the default expectation that capable systems pursue unintended goals should be significantly weakened for this architectural class.

What would count against this claim? I want the defeaters sorted by severity: fatal, serious but recoverable, and merely awkward. Specifically, which empirical findings (e.g., observed power-seeking or deception in large approval-directed models), architectural analyses (e.g., arguments that the reward model collapses into an implicit world-model), or conceptual distinctions (e.g., that approval is just a noisy reward signal with no structural difference) would undermine the idea that approval-direction is a stable, non-convergent category rather than a transient feature of current training paradigms? Which of these would a committed defender concede, and which would they dismiss as category errors or as applying only to pseudo-approval-directed systems? Finally, has the position already been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by defining any observed instrumental behavior as hidden goal-direction rather than as an internal failure mode of approval-direction—and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind?
