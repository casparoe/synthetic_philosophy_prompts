Compare the moral-pedagogical logic of Plutarch’s Parallel Lives with that of late antique and medieval hagiography, focusing specifically on how each tradition negotiates the risk of “moral admiration”—the tendency for readers to confuse narrative fascination with genuine ethical improvement. For Plutarch, examine whether his practice of pairing Greek and Roman figures creates a form of “biographical triangulation” that forces the reader to adjudicate virtue across cultural and personal differences, thereby preventing uncritical identification with any single life. For hagiography, examine whether its reliance on singular, often supererogatory exemplars generates a different pedagogical mechanism—what might be called “normative dissonance” between the reader’s ordinary capacities and the saint’s extraordinary actions—that can either deepen aspiration or produce alienating distance. In your comparison, address whether Plutarch’s comparative structure inherently protects against hagiography’s vulnerability to uncritical veneration, or whether hagiography’s intense singular focus produces a more durable form of ethical formation. Conclude by arguing which framework offers the more resilient model of moral learning when biography is treated not as historical record but as a technology of self-formation.
