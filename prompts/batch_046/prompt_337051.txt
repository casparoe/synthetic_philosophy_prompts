My roommate who took a comparitive ethics class summed up Confucian role ethics like this: it's the view that you don't have any moral identity outside your positions in the Five Relationships—parent, child, ruler, subject, and so on—so the self is literally constituted by those roles and there's no "inner you" to fall back on. From that he said ren is just feeling the right warmth inside your assigned box, and yi is just knowing the etiquette for your station. He also claimed the obligations are totally one-directional: children owe parents unconditional obedience, subjects owe rulers the same, and the only "check" is remonstrance, which he said is still just a loyal subject complaining politely. So he concluded the whole framework is social determinism dressed up as ethics—if your role is corrupt, you're stuck, and any talk about "relational selfhood" by modern scholars is just philosphical window dressing for hierarchy. I feel like he's smuggling in a Western individualist assumption that you need a private self to have agency, but I'm not sharp enough on the texts to say exactly where he went wrong. Which parts of his account would a careful Confucian role ethicist actually accept, where are the subtle misrepresentations—like maybe conflating role-constitution with role-determinism, or treating reciprocal duties as one-way commands—and does his conclusion that the view can't allow moral criticism of bad roles actually follow?
