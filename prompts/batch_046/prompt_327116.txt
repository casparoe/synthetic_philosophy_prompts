After a prolonged discussion with a friend about whether groups can literally act, I want a consistency check on the view I have ended up defending. The commitments are these.

First, I hold that joint intentional action requires what is usually called shared cooperative activity: each participant intends that they perform the action together, with mutual responsiveness, commitment to supporting one another, and common knowledge of these conditions among those involved.

Second, I take the we-mode of collective intentionality seriously, in Tuomela's sense: there is a genuinely collective form that is irreducible to summed individual intentions, and in it participants think and act as group members bound by a collective goal.

Third, I am a structural pluralist about plural subjects. Groups that act are ontologically real, but only as structured interrelations among individuals; they possess no mental states, phenomenal consciousness, or minds of their own, and mental predicates applied to them are derivative or metaphorical.

Fourth, I reject the claim that collective intentionality is merely a primitive psychological mode housed in individual brains, but I accept that all intentionality—including collective—must be biologically realized in individual nervous systems. There is no disembodied group mind.

Fifth, I think joint commitment, in something like Gilbert's sense, generates obligations that run directly to the group itself rather than being merely pairwise obligations distributed among members. I explain these obligations by the structural interdependence of commitments, not by treating the group as a rights-bearing person.

Sixth, I believe joint actions display membership resilience: if one participant withdraws without notice, the remaining participants can continue to perform the very same joint action—building the wall together, for instance—provided the structural interdependence among them remains intact.

My friend insisted that these pull in incompatible directions. I am unsure which tensions are real and which are only apparent. Please audit this package: identify the pairs that are genuinely incompatible, distinguish them from pairs that only seem incompatible but can be reconciled, and for each real tension specify what must be revised and at what cost. Rank the tensions by severity. Finally, tell me which single revision would resolve the most problems if I had to choose one, and be explicit if the structural pluralism is the root difficulty—even if I prefer not to abandon it. Do not treat every tension as equally grave; I need discrimination, not a blanket rejection.
