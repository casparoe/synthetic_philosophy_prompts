In defenses of Dewey’s experimentalism, democratic politics is routinely analogized to scientific inquiry: citizens are inquirers, policies are hypotheses tested by social consequences, elections function like peer review, and democracy therefore needs no fixed teleological end because, like science, it corrects itself through experience. The analogy is usually pressed to license the claim that democratic legitimacy rests on procedural experimentation rather than on shared substantive goods, yet Dewey’s own account distinguishes democracy as an institutional mechanism from democracy as a cooperative social “way of life,” and treats inquiry not as Popperian falsification but as the transformation of an indeterminate situation into a unified one through warranted assertibility rather than correspondence truth. Audit this analogy precisely: provide a correspondence table mapping what in democratic politics plays the role of the inquirer, the indeterminate situation, warranted assertibility, experimental control, and the institutional versus social-democratic dimensions; then identify the disanalogies that matter—especially whether citizens share the cooperative conditions and shared evidential standards of a laboratory, whether political problems are genuinely indeterminate in Dewey’s qualitative sense, and whether democratic outcomes are reproducible or value-neutral in the way scientific results are meant to be; state what features of scientific inquiry the analogy quietly imports that democratic politics lacks; and assess whether the conclusion—that democracy needs no fixed ends—survives once these breaks are visible, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along. Separate what is settled in Dewey scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among those who work on his political philosophy, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus view. Deliver the response as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
