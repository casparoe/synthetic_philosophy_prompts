As a philosophy professor chairing the procedural-ethics audit panel for a regional adaptive-health consortium, I need a reusable decision protocol my team can apply every quarter. We review proposed multi-phase vaccination and medical-countermeasure allocation schedules—sequential plans that revise priorities across successive epidemiological waves. Each review must be completed within forty-eight hours; the output requires co-signature by both a scientific officer and an ethics liaison; the review team has clinical and policy expertise but no formal training in decision theory. The stakes are high: an inconsistent sequence can lock the consortium into commitments that strategic vendors or political actors exploit through induced preference reversals.

I want you to turn the principle of diachronic rationality—specifically, institutional immunity to intertemporal money pumps and dynamic consistency in collective preference schedules—into an operational audit procedure. Treat the consortium not as an individual agent but as a collective decision-maker whose revealed preferences across phases can be pumped: for instance, by a vendor who induces a reversal between Phase I and Phase II priorities, then structures a contract that returns the consortium to its original allocation while extracting value in the intermediate step. You should inject the distinction between path-independent preference aggregation (synchronic consistency evaluated at each decision node) and sequential revealed-preference stability (diachronic consistency evaluated across the full path), and show precisely where they diverge in institutional practice.

Please respond in exactly four titled sections, in this order:

I. The Auditor's Constraints and Stakes (~150 words): State the role, review frequency, time limits, expertise of the signatories, and what counts as a successful audit versus a mandatory referral to the ethics-science pair.

II. The Audit Procedure (~500 words): Present the protocol as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words. Specify the questions to ask at each node, what answers are sufficient to proceed, where the procedure halts with a rejection, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue concerns values rather than rational consistency. Include explicit tests for (a) cyclical preference reversal across phases, (b) vendor- or lobbyist-induced preference shifts that create extractable value, and (c) evidential updates that rationally justify reversal versus those that do not. Flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated.

III. Worked Applications (~350 words): Run the procedure on three proposed allocation sequences: (1) shifting booster priority from immunocompromised adults to essential infrastructure workers between Phase I and Phase II based on revised transmission-rate data; (2) reversing an earlier commitment to pediatric vaccination after a manufacturer offers a discounted bulk contract contingent on adult-first allocation; (3) maintaining constant elderly-first priority despite contradictory epidemiological updates indicating negligible mortality risk. Include at least one case in which the procedure clears the sequence as diachronically consistent but yields an allocation the ethics liaison will find morally objectionable. For each case, show the reasoning at every node.

IV. Limits, Misfires, and Collapsed Distinctions (~200 words): State what the procedure cannot do—e.g., distinguish rational preference change from strategic manipulation when epidemiological evidence is ambiguous—identify likely false positives and false negatives, and note which philosophical distinctions (such as the boundary between belief updating and preference revision, or between individual and collective agency) you have deliberately collapsed to make the protocol usable within forty-eight hours.

Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote texts verbatim; paraphrase any theoretical claims in your own formulation.
