In debates over the extended mind thesis, a standard move is to compare Otto, who relies on a notebook for memory, with Inga, who relies on her brain: the notebook is said to be functionally equivalent to biological memory—accessible, automatically endorsed, and guiding action—so cognition should extend beyond the skull. Opponents counter that this is like claiming a corporation is a person because it maintains internal records and decision protocols; functional equivalence at the organizational level does not entail that the entity bearing mental states is the same, and the analogy quietly imports assumptions about ownership, embodiment, and first-person perspective that notebooks lack. Audit both analogies. First, provide a correspondence table mapping the elements of the Otto/Inga case onto the corporation/person case (e.g., notebook entries to corporate records, automatic endorsement to board ratification). Then identify the precise points of breakdown: metabolic integration, phenomenal access, normative responsibility, and the conditions for error correction. Next, evaluate whether the extended-mind conclusion survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether it depended on suppressing the bearer problem that the corporation analogy exposes. Finally, consider whether a different biological analogy—specifically the Portuguese man o' war, a colonial organism composed of interdependent zooids that cannot survive independently yet form a single functional unit—does better work for the extended-mind advocate: does it preserve functional integration without collapsing the boundary between one mind and many, or does it instead push toward an eliminativist view of the individual cognitive subject? Reach a verdict on whether the notebook analogy can be repaired, replaced, or should be abandoned.
