I sit on the intergenerational equity panel of a regional water authority. We must recommend whether to approve a fifty-year reservoir expansion that guarantees current supply but locks in irreversible ecological debt—specifically, the permanent loss of a riparian corridor—borne by generations who, owing to the non-identity problem, cannot claim comparative harm relative to a world in which the project never existed. A minority argues that adopting a 'threshold irreversibility' criterion—granting future persons moral standing only once damage crosses a point of no return—permits the project, since engineered restoration could technically reverse the corridor loss within thirty years. The majority responds that engineered restoration fails to preserve relational goods—intergenerational continuity of place—and that the non-identity objection is misapplied because the debt is structural rather than comparative.

Please draft the panel's decision memo, recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions. The memo must treat the 'threshold irreversibility' framing as the central issue, engage seriously with the strongest consideration in favour of the opposing view, propose a concrete course of action with a defined time frame, and present reasoning that researchers or affected citizens could directly respond to. Write the answer in American English.
