I have no formal training in philosophy or evolutionary biology, but I keep noticing that people pour enormous resources into displays that do not directly help them survive — extravagant homes, rare clothing, lavish gatherings — and I recently came across the handicap principle, which explains something similar in animals: a peacock's tail is a burden, and that very burden makes it an honest signal of strength, since only a genuinely fit individual could afford to carry it. The economic parallel, costly signaling, suggests that in human societies expensive displays can serve as credible proof of hidden qualities like wealth or social standing. Reading about this made me think immediately of Jay Gatsby in The Great Gatsby — his mansion on West Egg, his parties crowded with strangers who never meet him, and the well-known moment when he shows Daisy his piles of fine shirts. I would like a philosophical reading of the novel through the lens of costly signaling and the handicap principle: are Gatsby's displays meant to function as handicap signals proving he belongs in Daisy's world?

But then I start to wonder whether Fitzgerald is actually undermining the theory rather than illustrating it. Daisy is old money and barely reacts; Tom Buchanan holds the same status without any visible display at all. Does the novel suggest that in human hierarchies the cost can be borrowed or manufactured — that new money can purchase the ornaments of old status, breaking the evolutionary guarantee that only the genuinely fit pay the price? Or is there a more subtle point: that the highest-status individuals signal precisely by refusing to signal, so that Gatsby's exuberance is not merely fake but fundamentally misdirected? I want a reading that takes both the novel and the theory seriously — identifying which scenes and choices illuminate the idea, where the work resists being reduced to an illustration, and whether Fitzgerald adds something to the philosophical debate, perhaps about collective belief or imagination, that pure evolutionary or economic argument misses.
