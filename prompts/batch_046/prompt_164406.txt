There's something weird about the actual machinery of Athenian democracy if you look past the speeches. The ecclesia votes by show of hands in the open — everyone sees who raised their arm for the Mytilene massacre, then raises it again the next day to undo it because a second ship arrived. They keep funding the Sicily expedition by public vote long after it's a money pit, then execute the surviving strategoi when they come home. Meanwhile Socrates gets tried by a jury using secret pebbles in a dikasterion, convicted for corrupting youth. So what does this split practice — open collective reversability versus hidden individual condemnation — presuppose about democratic knowledge, moral responsability, and the value of transparency? Does it assume the demos has a collective mind that corrects itself in public, while individual jurors need protection from blame? Or is there a deeper view of when accountability applies? Reconstruct the implicit philosphical commitments about collective intentionality and reversability — say whether the practice actually needs one consistent theory or just tolerates contradictions — and tell me what would have to change if we replaced the open-hand model with anonynous expert panels and made all assembly votes irreversable.
