I'm taking a grad seminar on rational belief revision and my study group cooked up an extention of Kripke's dogmatism paradox that now feels like it eats its own tail. Here is the argument we built:
(1) If an agent genuinely knows p, rationality permits her to treat p as immune from first-order defeat — that's the dogmatist core.
(2) In expert domains, agents often have second-order knowledge: they know that they know p.
(3) The license in (1) applies to any proposition that is known, so it applies to the second-order claim 'I know p'.
(4) Therefore the agent may dismiss not only counterevidence to p, but also peer disagreement, bias reports, or methodological critiques that target her epistemic status — she can treat those as misleading too.
(5) But if anyone with self-awareness about expertise can do this, rational belief revision becomes impossible.
(6) Clearly revision happens — scientists update theories, clinicians change diagnoses — so (5) is false.
(7) Hence either second-order knowledge is exstant, or (1) is false.
My instinct is that the slip is between (3) and (4): you can't just apply the dogmatic shield to meta-level beliefs without paying a price. But I want to know exactly what that price is. You're an expert epistemologist — please think carefully, start with a quick brainstorm of how the original paradox is usually handled (the true-belief vs knowledge version, the need for independent reasons, etc.), and then give me a definately committed answer: which premise do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does dropping it cost? Don't just lay out options; take a stand and defend it.
