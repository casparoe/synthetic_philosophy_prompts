I am organizing a philosophy-of-mind seminar that has moved past the standard surveys of consciousness theories and is now stuck on a narrower, more technical dispute: whether dreams are genuinely experiential states that possess concurrent phenomenal character during sleep, or whether they are purely retrospective constructions assembled upon waking. We have been examining Norman Malcolm's denial that there is any experience during sleep and Daniel Dennett's narrative-construction account, yet both seem to sidestep the neurophenomenological data from lucid-dream studies and a persistent puzzle about non-lucid dreams — namely, what might be called sleep-state amnesia, in which apparently rich REM episodes are almost never recalled despite occurring, if they occur at all, within a functioning cognitive system. Before we try to choose between these two familiar positions, I want the conceptual ground cleared.

Write the full set of desiderata that any adequate theory of dream phenomenology must meet. For each requirement, state the specific case or empirical consideration that motivates it: for example, how the theory should distinguish lucid from non-lucid dreaming; how it should coordinate REM neurophysiology with the content of subsequent reports; whether it can maintain a distinction between having an experience during sleep and fabricating a narrative upon awakening; and what consistency with findings on sleep-state memory consolidation and predictive-processing models of dream generation requires. Flag which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable — such as compatibility with basic sleep neuroscience — and which are camp-specific preferences, like demanding first-person authority over dream content or denying phenomenal consciousness outside the waking state. Identify any subset of requirements that may be jointly unsatisfiable, particularly the tension between Malcolm's claim that there is no concurrent experience and a predictive-processing view that treats dream generation as involving structured, active inference during REM; explain precisely why they conflict. Keep the weight of your answer on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on scoring the contenders, though you should briefly indicate how Malcolm's view and Dennett's view stand against the list.
