Write a taxonomy—structured as a binary decision tree—for philosophical positions on Kavka's toxin puzzle. Organize the tree by logical distinctions rather than by author names or school labels.

Use these four branching questions, in this order:

1. Is rational evaluation applied to individual actions taken in isolation, or to temporally extended strategies that include both the intention-formation and its subsequent execution?
2. Can an agent rationally cultivate an intention that she already foresees she will not execute, provided that the mental state itself is the direct object of reward?
3. At the moment of execution, does rationality require honoring the prior commitment (treating deviation as a failure of consistency or akrasia), or does it permit—indeed require—re-optimization based solely on present preferences?
4. When first-order desires (to avoid illness) conflict with second-order desires (to be consistent, or to preserve future reward-earning capacity), is there an inviolate hierarchical level that determinately resolves the conflict, or is the preference structure tangled so that no level is immune to revision by another?

For the full logical space of 16 combinations, indicate which are occupied and which are empty. The tree must include at least these leaves: (a) Kavka's own two-step position; (b) the unitary-plan rejection (the deal is irrational because the execution of drinking is an inseparable part of the package); (c) a causal-decision-theoretic endorsement of forming the intention; (d) the view that failure to drink is akrasia rather than rational revision; (e) at least one unnamed combination that you diagnose as either incoherent or genuinely unexplored; and (f) a position that applies the idea of tangled hierarchies—where no preference level is inviolate—to the puzzle, yielding an agent who oscillates indefinitely between forming and revoking the intention without stable resolution.

Each leaf should carry the position's name (or 'unnamed') and exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Wherever two named positions fall on the same branch combination, flag them as inseparable by this tree and briefly explain why. Phrase every node as a concrete yes/no question that a student could answer without already knowing which philosophical label it corresponds to.

The audience is advanced undergraduate or graduate philosophy students familiar with the basic puzzle but not with its formal logical space. Present the taxonomy in prose flowchart form—path descriptions leading from root to each leaf—with roughly eight substantive leaves, collapsing redundant or empty branches only where the logical space genuinely permits it. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; do not quote any specific text verbatim.
