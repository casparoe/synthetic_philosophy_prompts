I'm writing a feature on where the philosophy of consciousness ought to direct its limited analytical energy over the next decade. Within higher-order theories—the family of views holding that a mental state becomes conscious only when represented by an appropriate second-order representation—there is a cluster of unresolved tensions. One that rarely receives its own name in standard surveys is what I will call the boundary ambiguity problem: if a higher-order monitor represents a first-order state only under a coarse functional description (say, there is a visual processing event) rather than under its specific intentional content, does that satisfy the representational ascent the theory requires, or does it leave the first-order state phenomenally dark in precisely the way the theory was designed to avoid? This is distinct from the familiar targetlessness objection, which asks whether the monitor can miss its object altogether; instead, it asks how finely grained the monitored target must be specified for the monitoring relation to do its explanatory work.

With that framing in place, I want a dependency-aware prioritization of the live issues. Among them—how to resolve boundary ambiguity, whether global workspace dynamics are redundant with or necessary for higher-order access, and what criteria should govern attributions of consciousness to minimal artificial architectures—which should be treated as upstream? Which are tractable with current conceptual tools, and which await empirical breakthroughs that philosophy alone cannot deliver? Furthermore, which practical implications—say, for legal personhood of synthetic minds or for clinical assessment of disordered consciousness—actually hinge on settling those upstream questions, versus those that would remain essentially unchanged regardless of the answer? Give me a defended agenda rather than a survey: rank where research effort should go, state the criteria you are using (downstream dependency, tractability, practical stakes, neglect), explain the trade-offs between them, and specify what finding or argument would cause you to reorder the priorities.
