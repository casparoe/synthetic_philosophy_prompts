I'm auditing a graduate seminar on metaphysics, and two students—Mara and Jonah—got into a sustained disagreement about the special composition question, specifically whether any non-arbitrary criterion can determine when parts genuinely compose a whole. Rather than rehearsing the usual examples of scattered objects or ships, they anchored their dispute in a concrete but conceptually demanding case: a "modular synthetic-biology consortium" in which independent laboratories across continents engineer separate components of a single artificial organism, share no physical premises, coordinate exclusively through an open digital protocol, and can dissolve their collaboration without damaging any of the partial biological systems they have individually constructed.

(1) Mara argued for unrestricted composition. She maintained that if each laboratory counts as a part, then all the laboratories together are parts of something—namely, the distributed research collective—just as bricks scattered after a demolition remain parts of a scattered building. She insisted that denying this requires an arbitrary boundary between "near enough" and "too dispersed," and that any attempt to draw such a line smuggles in intuitions rather than principles.

(2) Jonah replied that unrestricted composition is not a solution but a surrender: it renders "object" ontologically vacuous by licensing arbitrary fusions. He claimed the consortium case actually demonstrates why we need a restrictive principle—perhaps causal integration or functional unity—because without one we cannot distinguish the consortium from a random assortment of labs that happen to use the same protocol. He accused Mara of conflating the existence of relations with the existence of a further composite object.

(3) Mara countered that any restrictive principle Jonah offers will be either too strong—ruling out intuitively real scattered objects—or too weak—failing to exclude the consortium, since the labs do exhibit functional unity through their shared protocol. She challenged him to state a precise condition that excludes the distributed consortium yet includes a human body, without appealing to "common sense" or to biological essentialism.

(4) Jonah then proposed a condition he called "mereological essential dependence": composition occurs only when parts stand in a relation of mutual identity-dependence, such that part A cannot exist as the particular part it is without part B, and vice versa. Because the laboratories can exist and retain their identities independently, they do not compose a further object. He conceded that this might exclude some scattered objects but argued that this was a theoretical virtue rather than a cost.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. In particular, evaluate whether Jonah's proposed condition succeeds as a genuinely principled restriction, identify what both Mara and Jonah are missing—perhaps a distinction between asking for an ontological criterion versus asking for a conceptual or pragmatic one—and specify which further case would best discriminate between the rules they are implicitly running. Keep your assessment tied to the arguments exactly as made, and avoid signaling an overall winner beyond what the individual ratings imply.
