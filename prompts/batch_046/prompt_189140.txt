I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about Isaiah Berlin’s distinction between negative and positive liberty, and about whether Philip Pettit’s republican conception—liberty as non-domination—actually adds a third, irreducible category or merely collapses into one of the other two combined with democratic procedure. I maintained that the three are genuinely distinct, and that republican liberty captures something neither of them can: not the absence of obstacles, nor the presence of self-mastery, but a specific social status—being able to stand in relations of equality without the deference that arbitrary power demands, the condition of meeting another’s gaze without submission. My friend insisted this is only negative liberty with institutional window dressing.

Tomorrow evening I am giving the retirement toast for Eleanor Voss, who spent thirty-five years as a public defender in a jurisdiction with notoriously broad prosecutorial discretion and minimal judicial oversight. The audience will be split: roughly half are constitutional lawyers and political theorists who will notice every conceptual move, and roughly half are her family and former clients, for whom Berlin and Pettit are not household names. I want the philosophical framework to do real structural work in the speech—not ornament, but architecture. Specifically, the triad should determine what I can and cannot honestly say about her career. I must not praise her for “giving clients autonomy,” since that frames her work in terms of positive liberty and risks sounding patronizing; nor can I praise her for “getting government out of people’s lives,” since that frames it in negative-liberty terms and misdescribes her practice, which constantly invoked state power—just disciplined state power. Instead, I need to describe her achievement as a transformation in the status of the people she represented: from being subject to arbitrary, unaccountable discretion—unable to contest the terms of their own treatment—to being protected by rules that were visible, trackable, and open to challenge.

Please write a five-minute toast that does exactly this. It should be accessible to the non-experts without being condescending to the experts; it should make the republican framing feel lived rather than academic; and it should explicitly navigate why the other two conceptions of liberty would misrepresent what she actually did. Do not include citations, a bibliography, suggested readings, or any phrase in quotation marks drawn from a specific published text. If you reference the idea that freedom requires the absence of arbitrary power and the capacity to contest it, do so as part of the argument’s own vocabulary, not as an attributed citation.
