Here are my intuitive judgments about whether the moral equality of combatants holds across a range of scenarios, and I want to know whether they hang together under a single principle or whether some must be abandoned.

Case 1: Two regular infantry soldiers, one fighting for an aggressor state and one for a defender, both sincerely believing their cause is just. Verdict: they are moral equals; neither may be targeted with greater permissiveness.

Case 2: A soldier who knows her war is unjust but participates only because she was conscripted and will be executed for desertion. Verdict: she retains combatant status and is not more liable to harm than a just combatant, though she bears moral blame for failing to resist.

Case 3: A mercenary fighting exclusively for financial gain in a clearly unjust war of conquest, with no belief in the cause. Verdict: he lacks moral equality; he does not enjoy the same protected status and may be targeted with fewer restrictions.

Case 4: A civilian who spontaneously takes up arms to defend her neighborhood against an invading force, without state authorization or uniform. Verdict: she is not a combatant in the relevant sense, so the equality principle does not apply to her symmetrically; she may defend herself but does not acquire the reciprocal rights of soldiers.

Case 5: An operator of lethal autonomous weapons who directs strikes from a distant continent, facing zero physical risk, while the enemy soldiers she targets face direct danger. Verdict: I lean that she remains a combatant and is therefore liable to attack, yet the extreme asymmetry of risk undermines the moral symmetry that traditionally grounds equality.

Case 6: A conflict in which both sides deploy fully autonomous weapons systems, with no human beings physically present in the field of danger; human commanders only set strategic parameters remotely. Verdict: the moral equality of combatants seems to collapse or become inapplicable, since there are no human agents in shared vulnerability, yet the remote commanders might still be treated as combatants by analogy.

Is there a principle that delivers all six verdicts, or must some be revised? In particular, what should I say about Case 5, where risk asymmetry is total but the agent is still human? And does Case 6 show that the principle depends essentially on shared physical vulnerability, or can it be reconstructed for remote and autonomous warfare?
