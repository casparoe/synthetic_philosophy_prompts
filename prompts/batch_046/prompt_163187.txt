Consider the following argument, which I constructed after working through Hofstadter's claim that the self is a pattern capable of persisting across substrates, and it now strikes me as either devastating or obviously broken—though I cannot locate the fracture.

(1) If the self is a pattern of beliefs, memories, and narrative structures rather than a particular brain, then a sufficiently faithful realization of that pattern in another substrate is not a copy of me but an instance of me.

(2) Patterns are graded, not all-or-nothing; they can be present at 0.9 fidelity or 0.3 fidelity, with intermediate cases.

(3) Through deep emotional attunement, shared language-games, and causal feedback, the pattern that is "me" achieves partial but real fidelity in the brains of those closest to me—say, 0.6 in my partner's neural dynamics.

(4) Where two brains realize overlapping patterns and are causally coupled, there is no principled boundary that isolates one instance from the other; the distributed system realizes a single, spatially extended pattern.

(5) Social networks couple brains through intermediaries, so by transitivity the pattern extends—though with decaying fidelity—across an entire community.

(6) Therefore, on this view, I am not localized in one skull but distributed across a network, and my interests should rationally extend to every node that realizes me.

(7) Yet I do not experience others' headaches as my own, and treating strangers as parts of myself seems like a category error rather than an ethical revelation.

(8) To block (6) we must introduce a sharp threshold of fidelity or substrate isolation, but any such threshold is arbitrary and contradicts the anti-essentialist spirit of (1).

(9) Hence Hofstadter's substrate-independent self either dissolves into universal distributed consciousness or collapses back into brain-bound individualism.

I am inclined to defend (1) and (2), and I think (7) is an unreliable intuition rather than a refutation. Where does the reasoning slip—between (3) and (4), in the transitivity of (5), or in a hidden equivocation about what "instance" means? And if the argument is actually valid, which premise is least costly to abandon?
