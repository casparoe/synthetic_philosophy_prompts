You sit on the board of the National Memorial and Education Center, which must vote next month on a proposed permanent gallery. The curatorial team has designed an exhibition called "The Geometry of Evil" that deliberately avoids ranking historical atrocities on any single scale of severity. Instead, it maps them within a multi-dimensional moral space defined by axes such as institutional scope, intentional design, duration, logic of victim selection, and resistance to later memorialization. The board is split. One faction argues that refusing comparative architecture allows each atrocity to be treated as an incomparable moral singularity, which flattens historical learning and makes prevention impossible; the other faction contends that any dimensional comparison still imposes an obscene calculus of suffering, particularly because the choice of axes embeds controversial normative judgments—such as weighing institutional scale against the specificity of victim targeting—that privilege certain forms of moral horror over others. The chair has asked you to draft the board's formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the curators and the dissenting members. The memo should state a clear actionable recommendation, provide reasoning that both sides could actually respond to, and include an intellectually honest account of the strongest consideration favoring the position you reject. In particular, address whether a democratic culture can legitimately institutionalize a comparative moral framework for collective memory without necessarily violating the dignity of the victims it seeks to honor.
