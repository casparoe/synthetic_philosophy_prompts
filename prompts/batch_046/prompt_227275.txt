My friend directs a youth mentoring program and is designing paired portrait panels of two alumni for the program's entrance hall. The first built a major local institution through relentless drive but was notoriously cruel to collaborators and family; the second was consistently kind and collaborative, yet every major initiative he led collapsed from lack of follow-through. She wants the panels to function as moral reference points, not mere biography, and plans to edit the first life to highlight sacrifice while minimizing the damage he caused, and to reframe the second's repeated failures as "the wisdom of knowing when to let go." She argues that teenagers need clear, navigable stars, not muddy contradictions that leave them paralyzed. I feel that smoothing the edges does the students a disservice, but I cannot say exactly what is lost—whether it's the credibility of the example, the kind of moral reasoning being taught, or something about how lives actually work as models. Is she right to sanitize these lives into coherent moral archetypes?
