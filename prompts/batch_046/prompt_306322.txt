I'm taking an advanced seminar on normative ethics, and we recently spent several sessions on T.M. Scanlon's contractualism and the well-known challenge it faces regarding the aggregation of harms—sometimes called the numbers problem. Scanlon's core idea is that an action is wrong if it violates a principle that no one could reasonably reject; he insists that the strength of an individual's reason against being harmed does not behave like a quantitative weight that can be summed across persons. My instructor introduced a subtle distinction that I have not been able to resolve on my own: the difference between what she termed "pairwise justifiability" and "plural justifiability." In the pairwise case, we compare two principles by asking whether the individual who fares worse under one principle can reasonably reject it in favor of the other. In the plural case, we ask whether a group of affected individuals, all facing an identical harm, can jointly reject a principle that imposes that harm on all of them in order to prevent a comparable harm to a smaller number of others.

My question is conceptual rather than historical. Does Scanlon's framework genuinely prevent aggregation at the plural level, or does allowing a group to coordinate their rejection of a principle amount to a disguised form of adding up individual reasons? Specifically, if each member of a large group has an equally weighty, structurally identical reason not to suffer a particular loss, and they can present their grievance collectively rather than serially, is there a principled contractualist reason to treat their joint rejection as qualitatively different from utilitarian summation? I am not asking for citations, secondary sources, or a bibliography; I want to understand whether the resistance to aggregation in Scanlon's view is a feature of how reasons are structured, or merely an artifact of insisting on pairwise comparison. Please explain the conceptual mechanics as clearly as possible, especially where the boundary between legitimate collective objection and illicit aggregation might lie.
