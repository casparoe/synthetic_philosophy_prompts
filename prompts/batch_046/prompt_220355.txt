Consider the institutional divergence between European jurisdictions that prohibit commercial surrogacy and compensated egg donation—treating such contracts as void for violating human dignity or public order—and the American framework, which permits market pricing and enforceable agreements for gestational labor and gamete retrieval. What view of bodily integrity, commodification, or reproductive autonomy does each regime strictly presuppose? Can the European prohibition be sustained without a Kantian commitment to inalienable bodily capacities, or does it depend on a gendered ontology that distinguishes reproductive labor from other compensated bodily services—such as nursing, manual labor, or clinical-trial participation? Conversely, does the American contractual model require a Lockean property-in-person framework, or can it be justified on autonomy grounds alone without collapsing into pure commodification? Identify where each practice is philosophically underdetermined—where rival theories could license the same institutional rule—and whether the differential treatment of reproductive labor reveals an implicit moral commitment that conflicts with the explicit rationale each system offers.
