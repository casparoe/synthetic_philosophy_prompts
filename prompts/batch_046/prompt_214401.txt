I am trying to map out a dissertation agenda in philosophy of mind and want to avoid spending three years on a debate that turns out to be conceptually isolated. The classic dispute between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are experiences — Malcolm emphasizing that we lack reliable criteria for false dream reports, Dennett allowing that dreams occur but are often reconstructed or confabulated upon waking — seems to sit at the center of several larger issues. Rather than asking for a literature survey or reading list, I want to know which unresolved questions or applied implications genuinely depend on settling this particular disagreement, and in what order they deserve attention.

Specifically, I see three potential directions, and I am unsure which deserve priority given limited time and no laboratory resources. One concerns the methodology of consciousness science: if Malcolm's skepticism about experiential reports during sleep is correct, then studies that rely on post-awakening descriptions may be systematically unreliable, pushing the field toward purely third-person measures; if Dennett is right, such reports might remain usable but require a reconstruction-sensitive interpretive framework. A second direction involves theories of selfhood and narrative continuity: one might ask whether sleep involves an 'offline' self-model that operates without the phenomenal immediacy characteristic of waking consciousness, and whether resolving the dream debate forces a revision in our account of personal identity across sleep-wake cycles. A third direction is clinical and applied: in therapeutic contexts such as lucid-dreaming interventions, should we conceptualize treatment as altering an ongoing experiential stream, or as training a patient's capacity for accurate post-sleep narration?

Given these options — and any others you think are genuinely upstream — which should I prioritize? I am looking for a defended agenda rather than a flat list: for each line of work, indicate whether it is tractable through conceptual analysis alone, how many other questions depend on its resolution, and whether answering it would change practice in consciousness science or clinical psychology, or whether it remains an internal philosophical dispute with little downstream effect. If there is a specific conceptual distinction or hypothetical case that would best separate a Malcolm-style position from a Dennett-style one within these applied contexts — for instance, a scenario involving immediate versus delayed reporting, or partial versus full awakening — please include it. Finally, tell me which questions you would actively deprioritize because their answer would not substantially shift the landscape, and what evidence or argument over the next few years would cause you to reorder the ranking.
