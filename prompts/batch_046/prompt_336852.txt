Question: Dennett talks about sphex wasps and sphexishness, the rigid loop they do when dragging crickets into burrows. Some philosophers say this rigidity means they cant have free will since theyre just mechanical. Others think flexibility is the real test. Now imagine Sphex flexus: a wasp that can skip steps when interrupted, but only via a hardcoded backup hierarchy. Does that flexibility prove free will? And was Dennetts point about the real wasp mainly that rigidity shows lack of consciousness, or something about how we judge behavior from outside?

Answer:
Sphex wasps dig a burrow, paralyze a cricket, drag it to the burrow, check inside, then lay eggs. If you move the cricket during the check, the wasp pulls it out and checks again, looping forever. This is sphexishness, a rigid behavioral script with no flexibility. Dennett uses this to argue that wasps lack free will because theyre pure mechanisms running on instinct. If you build Sphex flexus with hardcoded backup rules so it can skip steps when interrupted, then it does have free will, because the ability to adapt your plan is exactly what separates free agents from machines. Dennetts actual point was about consciousness, not free will: he meant that rigidity proves the wasp has no inner awareness, and adding flexible backups fixes the consciousness problem too. So sphexishness is evidence against consciousness, and flexibility solves both.
