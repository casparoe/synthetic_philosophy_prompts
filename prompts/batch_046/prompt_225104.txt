I'm trying to stabilize the following package for a seminar on personal identity and Buddhist philosophy: (1) anatta should be read not as denying continuity altogether, but as denying that continuity requires either a metaphysical substratum or a narrative center; (2) personal identity is therefore a graded, diachronic pattern of causal-functional relations among the skandhas, not a substance or a story; (3) nevertheless, phenomenal ownership—the sense that experiences belong to someone—requires a minimal, non-illusory reference point that persists across that pattern; (4) I reject standard reductionism because it collapses the distinction between third-person descriptive continuity and first-person experiential binding; and (5) I take the evolutionary continuity of self-modeling in social primates to show that this reference point is biologically implemented rather than metaphysically basic. Do these hang together? In particular, does (3) smuggle back exactly the kind of self that (1) and (2) reject, and can (4) be sustained without undermining the graded-continuity view in (2)? Rank the tensions by severity, identify any only-apparent conflicts, and state the least costly single revision that would restore consistency.
