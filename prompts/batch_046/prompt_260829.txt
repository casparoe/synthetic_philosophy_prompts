Write an essay in American English on whether populism's central appeal to "the will of the people" can survive a conceptual tension that is often glossed over in public debate. Populist movements typically invoke "the people" in two incompatible registers: as an empirical demographic—roughly, the current body of citizens or the electoral majority—and as a normative-constitutive entity that possesses an intrinsic, unified moral authority independent of any aggregation of individual preferences. If the people are defined empirically, then "their will" reduces to whatever procedural mechanism produces it, and populism collapses into ordinary majoritarianism with no special claim against constitutional checks. If they are defined normatively, then determining who counts as "the people" and what they genuinely will requires a prior moral or political judgment—one that seems to presuppose exactly the kind of elite interpretation that populism rejects.

Weigh the main attempts to resolve or dissolve this tension: appeals to shared national identity, common-good theories that treat the people as constituted by shared aims, deliberative-democratic accounts that forge unity through public reason, and the view that popular sovereignty should be understood as a regulative ideal rather than an actual collective will. Consider also the objection that this tension is not fatal—that liberal constitutionalism faces its own version of the problem, or that the normative conception can be operationalized without circularity through participatory practices.

Reach and defend a clear verdict: must populism abandon one of these two conceptions of "the people" to remain coherent? Is there a defensible third position—perhaps a diachronic or institutional conception of popular sovereignty—that preserves populism's anti-elitist force without requiring a metaphysically unified will? Or does the tension ultimately show that "the will of the people" is a rhetorical fiction that cannot bear the democratic weight populists place on it? Justify your conclusion with argument, not assertion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; write as if for a reader who has no access to external sources.
