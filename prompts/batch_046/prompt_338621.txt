During a philosophy-of-mathematics colloquium, two colleagues—Elena and Sam—debated the hypothetical scenario in which Peano Arithmetic (PA) is discovered to be inconsistent. Elena opened by arguing that such a discovery would be globally catastrophic: because PA formalizes finitary reasoning, every arithmetic result ever derived within it would become formally derivable alongside its negation, and there would be no principled method to separate contaminated theorems from safe ones. Sam replied that this overstates the damage; foundation crises have historically been localized, as Russell's paradox was resolved by restricting naive comprehension without destroying ordinary mathematics, and by analogy an inconsistency in PA would likely be traced to an overly strong axiom—perhaps the full second-order induction schema—while weaker fragments, such as ACA0 (the system central to reverse mathematics), would remain intact and continue to support concrete results in number theory and analysis. Elena countered that Russell's paradox afflicted naive set theory, not the axioms already underwriting working arithmetic; if PA itself collapses, then the consistency proofs for those weaker fragments—typically established relative to PA or stronger systems—collapse with them, leaving no non-circular justification for even basic arithmetic. Sam responded that this presupposes formal derivability is the sole source of mathematical knowledge, whereas practicing mathematicians rely on intuition, computation, and inter-theoretic coherence; moreover, finitist or predicative consistency arguments can justify weak fragments independently, and a structuralist or pluralist conception of mathematics treats formal systems as replaceable frameworks rather than absolute foundations, so PA's failure would require technical repair, not epistemic ruin. Elena then pressed that invoking pluralism begs the central question: the problem is not whether we can invent replacement axioms, but whether we retain reason to trust any arithmetic result whose proof history can no longer be reliably audited. Sam concluded that auditability was never absolute—proofs are already social, fallible, and rarely traced line-by-line back to axioms—so demanding total formal security is a philosophical aspiration rather than a mathematical prerequisite.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each contribution by Elena and Sam, assess whether it is clearly mistaken, partly correct but overstated, or clearly right, and provide brief justification for each rating. Then state who is more right overall regarding the practical and epistemic consequences of an inconsistent PA, and identify what important consideration—perhaps concerning the social epistemology of proof verification, the practical separation of concrete from abstract mathematics, or the role of non-formal justification—both parties are neglecting.
