I serve on the State Curriculum Standards Board's Textbook Review Subcommittee, and we face a binding vote next month on whether to certify an unconventional tenth-grade course proposal titled U.S. History and Comparative Memory. Rather than adopting a conventional narrative textbook, the publisher has designed a polyphonic sourcing framework that requires each contested unit to present at least three competing interpretive lenses, accompanied by explicit meta-commentary on how historical narratives are constructed and authorized. For the American founding, the materials place the 1776 Declaration’s universalist language in direct dialogue with the 1619 Project’s framing of 1619 as a foundational year, but they also embed both within a comparative module on Japan-South Korea textbook disputes over colonial-era records and comfort women, asking students to analyze how democratic societies manage irreconcilable historical memory within state-approved curricula. The publisher contends this cultivates epistemic humility; several board members, led by our chair, argue that it substitutes procedural neutrality for normative commitment and risks imposing an unworkable relativism on students who still require a coherent civic story. After our March meeting, I was asked to draft the formal opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s session. Please write a memo—no more than two pages—recommending a concrete course of action with a specific time frame, whether full adoption, a conditional one-year pilot in a limited district set with evaluation criteria, or rejection with conditions for resubmission. The memo must treat the opposing view at its strongest, including the specific objection that importing East Asian memory wars into American history instruction is pedagogically inappropriate and that students cannot be expected to adjudicate competing origin myths without first possessing basic factual literacy. It should also address the underlying philosophical tension: does the state have an obligation to maintain interpretive balance across fundamentally incompatible moral framings of national origin, or does such balance itself constitute an abdication of the civic purpose of history education?
