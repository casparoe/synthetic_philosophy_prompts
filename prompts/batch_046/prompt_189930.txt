I'm working on a long-form feature about the philosophy hidden inside clinical protocols for disorders of consciousness. In neurocritical-care units, decisions about withdrawing life-sustaining measures—artificial ventilation, nutrition, hydration—typically hinge on standardized behavioral assessments that score observable actions: visual tracking, command-following, intentional yes-or-no signaling. These institutional protocols treat the presence or absence of globally reportable, behaviorally accessible responses as the decisive marker of whether a patient is conscious. They do not operationalize any distinction between phenomenal consciousness—there being something it is like to undergo an experience—and access consciousness—the global broadcasting of information for reasoning, decision, and verbal report—which philosophers and cognitive scientists have long separated. At the same time, global workspace theory characterizes conscious access as the product of widespread neural broadcasting, and it leaves conceptually open whether phenomenal experience might occur in the absence of such broadcasting, a possibility that has been discussed specifically regarding patients diagnosed with unresponsive wakefulness.

What philosophical commitments about the nature and criteria of consciousness does this clinical-institutional practice presuppose? Does it tacitly identify consciousness with globally available, reportable information, thereby collapsing phenomenal experience into access? Or can it accommodate a genuine dissociation—for instance, if neuroimaging were to detect phenomenal states in behaviorally unresponsive patients—without undermining the ethical framework that licenses withdrawal of care? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and specify precisely what would have to change in both assessment practice and end-of-life policy if the underlying view were replaced by one that treats phenomenal and access consciousness as fully separable dimensions.
