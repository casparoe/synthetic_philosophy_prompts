I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop called Translation After Meaning, attended equally by philosophers of language and by professional court interpreters. The philosophical frame is Quine's indeterminacy of translation: not only the familiar "gavagai" case, but the stronger claim that compatible with all behavioral evidence there may be no fact of the matter about which translation manual is correct. I want this idea to do structural work, not decorative work—the remarks must explain why the workshop refuses to ask "what did the speaker really mean?" and instead asks "which translation manual best serves procedural justice in this courtroom?" To carry this for both audiences, build the address around a specific hypothetical: two certified interpreters, working independently, translate testimony from a witness whose language lacks explicit tense morphology. Interpreter A renders every verb in the past tense; Interpreter B renders every verb in a timeless, aspect-neutral form. Both translations are fully behaviorally adequate—every judicial question, objection, and reply aligns with either manual—yet they generate contradictory timelines for the events in dispute. Present this not as an error to be eliminated but as Quine's point operating inside legal practice. The speech should conclude by stating precisely what the workshop promises and refuses: not the recovery of hidden speaker intentions, but a negotiated, institutionally stabilized choice between equally adequate manuals. Write a five-minute opening that lets the philosophers recognize the philosophical depth and lets the interpreters see that the framework respects rather than insults their craft.
