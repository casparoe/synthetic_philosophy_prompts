I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on intergenerational and population ethics, and I am preparing an internal consistency audit for a workshop on climate ethics and identity over time. Over the past year I have settled on the following package of commitments, but I am concerned that some pull in directions I have not fully traced, particularly because I am trying to combine standard nonidentity reasoning with a person-affecting moral framework and a threshold view of procreative ethics. Here is where I stand.

(1) I take the nonidentity problem to establish that policies altering macro-level conditions—energy infrastructure, migration flows, economic trajectories—cannot be evaluated in person-affecting terms with respect to the individuals they bring into existence, since for any such individual the relevant alternative is non-existence rather than a better life.

(2) I hold a strict person-affecting restriction on moral wrongness: an act is wrong only if it makes some identifiable individual worse off relative to a feasible alternative, and I reject impersonal or aggregative frameworks that license welfare trade-offs across different possible populations.

(3) Despite (1) and (2), I maintain that climate mitigation is morally required in part because of the welfare levels of the actual people who will inhabit the future, and I want to say that a severely warmed world is worse not merely because of summed suffering but because of what it does to the particular people who end up living then.

(4) I endorse a threshold deontological constraint on procreation: it is impermissible to deliberately bring a person into existence if their life will foreseeably fall below a minimal welfare floor, even if that life is still worth living on balance.

(5) I accept that personal identity is modally fragile with respect to large-scale policy variables—small differences in climate pathways, economic structures, or settlement patterns can shift the genetic and relational determinants of birth—so I treat the class of future people affected by climate policy as a shifting reference set rather than a fixed group of individuals.

(6) I deny that ecological naturalness has intrinsic moral value, and I am in principle open to interventionist policies—whether climate stabilization or wild-animal welfare measures—though I am skeptical of our predictive capacity over long ecological and social timescales.

My worry is that (1) together with (2) seems to drain (3) of normative force, because if identity is fragile and cross-world comparison is impossible, it is unclear how climate policy can wrong anyone specifically. At the same time, (4) appears to presuppose exactly the kind of comparison between existence and non-existence that (1) rules out, yet I want to retain both commitments. Meanwhile, (5) might dissolve an apparent tension between (2) and (3) by showing there is no stable victim, but it might also deepen the problem by rendering the reference class for moral assessment entirely contingent on policy choice. I also wonder whether (6) is genuinely independent: my skepticism about long-range ecological prediction might commit me to a status-quo bias that conflicts with the interventionist spirit implicit in (3) and (4), or it might be a separate methodological caution.

Please audit this belief set systematically. For each pair of commitments, say whether the tension is real, merely apparent, or absent, and rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. For each real tension, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency—whether weakening the nonidentity claim, reconceiving the threshold constraint, relaxing the person-affecting restriction, or revising the climate-ethics commitment—and state clearly what that revision costs in terms of my broader theoretical aims. In particular, tell me whether a single revision can resolve the cluster of problems, or whether I face independent failures requiring separate fixes. Do not offer a general lecture on population ethics; keep the analysis anchored to these six commitments and their specific interactions.
