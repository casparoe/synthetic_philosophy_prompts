I'm preparing an advanced seminar unit on the metaphysics of infinity, and I've constructed an argument that I initially intended as a reductio of strict finitism, but now I fear it proves far more than I intended—perhaps even undermining the very distinction it relies on. Here is the chain, laid out step by step.

(1) A strict finitist denies that any actually infinite collection exists; at most, there are only potential infinities—processes that can be extended without termination but never form a completed totality. (2) Hilbert's hotel demonstrates that the concept of an actually infinite collection is perfectly coherent: if every room in a countably infinite hotel is occupied, we can still accommodate a new guest by shifting each occupant to the next room, without contradiction. (3) So if we grant Hilbert's model conceptual validity, we must accept that an actually infinite set—say, the set of all natural numbers—can exist as a completed totality, not merely as an unending procedure. (4) Once one completed actual infinity is admitted, Cantor's power-set argument applies: the power set of that infinity has strictly greater cardinality, and this hierarchy can be iterated through uncountable, inaccessible, and far larger cardinalities, each itself a completed totality. (5) Therefore, allowing even the weakest form of actual infinity—countably infinite rooms—commits us not merely to ℵ₀, but to an endless tower of larger and larger completed infinities. (6) Yet the finitist's original objection was never targeted solely at ℵ₀; it was directed at the very idea of a completed infinite totality of any cardinality. (7) Hence the argument shows that accepting Hilbert's hotel forces us into a far more extravagant ontology than the finitist ever feared, making finitism unstable rather than merely false: there is no principled stopping point once the first infinite hotel is admitted.

I feel the illicit slide occurs somewhere between the coherence of the shifting procedure and the ontological commitment to the full Cantorian hierarchy, but I cannot isolate the exact step. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if I wish to preserve the intuition that potential processes are genuinely different from completed totalities—perhaps through a potentialist arithmetic that treats infinity only as a modal property of extendable structures—must I also deny that Hilbert's hotel describes anything metaphysically possible? Or can I block the hierarchy by restricting Cantor's theorem's application without abandoning the distinction between potential and actual infinity altogether? And if I do reject one of these steps, do I pay the price of making finitism compatible with modern mathematics, or do I end up with a position that is technically consistent but philosophically hollow?
