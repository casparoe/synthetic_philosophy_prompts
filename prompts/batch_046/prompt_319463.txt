Two colleagues of mine, Sofia and David, have been arguing about whether the British state ought to issue a formal apology for its role in the Bengal famine of 1943. I was present but did not take sides, and I should like an impartial adjudication. Please provide your answer in British English.

Sofia opened by saying that state apologies are conceptually incoherent unless the apologising agent is identical to the wrongdoer. The British state of 1943 was an imperial administration; the contemporary UK is a democratic polity with a different constitutional identity. For her to apologise for her great-grandfather’s theft would be absurd, and the same applies here. Moreover, she added, an apology unaccompanied by material reparation is merely a hollow performative: it expresses regret without accepting any present cost, and therefore lacks moral weight.

David replied that states possess diachronic corporate identity through continuous institutions—the Crown, the civil service, the legal system—so the apology is not vicarious but direct. He distinguished an expressive apology, which acknowledges a moral fact, from a reparative apology, which accepts legal liability. The state can do the former without committing to the latter. He also argued that refusing to apologise is itself a present moral wrong: it signals that the state does not recognise the gravity of the harm, which actively damages its relationship with the descendants of those affected.

Sofia countered that David’s corporate-identity argument proves too much. If institutional continuity makes the present state responsible enough to apologise, then consistency demands it should also be liable for compensation; yet David wants only words. She accused him of selectively applying continuity—invoking it to justify symbolic gestures while rejecting its material implications.

David answered that responsibility and liability come apart in precisely the way promises illustrate: one keeps a promise not because one is legally liable for breach, but because one made it. The state acted in 1943 and its institutional continuity binds it to acknowledge that action now, without necessarily triggering a legal duty to pay. He concluded that Sofia’s demand for perfect identity sets an impossible standard that no collective agent could ever meet, which would render all institutional apologies—whether for slavery, colonialism, or war crimes—conceptually impossible.

Neither would concede. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess Sofia’s identity and performative objections, David’s corporate-identity and separation-of-responsibility moves, and Sofia’s “proves too much” challenge. Say who is right about which specific claim, identify anything both are missing—perhaps a distinction between political and moral responsibility, or the role of audience uptake in making an apology succeed—and indicate who, if anyone, is more right overall.
