Exam question: In her essay arguing for the abolition of political parties, Simone Weil claims that parties corrupt moral judgment by substituting collective passion for individual reason. Assess whether her critique applies with equal force to temporary, non-power-seeking deliberative bodies (such as citizens' assemblies or juries) and to single-issue advocacy groups that lack organizational discipline.

Student answer: Weil's essay contends that parties function as machines that manufacture collective passion, substituting organizational loyalty for the individual's duty to judge truth and justice personally. She argues that party membership corrupts moral reasoning because members must adopt positions they have not personally examined, driven by the collective need to acquire power. This corruption is primarily epistemic: parties systematically suppress true judgment by making belief a function of group allegiance rather than evidence. Because this mechanism depends only on collective loyalty, it applies with equal force to citizens' assemblies and to non-governmental advocacy groups; even temporary juries risk substituting group dynamics for individual reason. Consequently, Weil's position amounts to a radical individualism: all forms of collective political organization should be dissolved, leaving moral responsibility to the isolated conscience. Democracy, on her view, is therefore impossible without corruption, since voting and representation necessarily involve the suppression of independent judgment she condemns.
