I am working through the implications of Daniel Dennett's claim that advanced artificial systems could eventually become counterfeit people — entities that display every outward mark of personhood, including fluent language use, apparent emotional responsiveness, and competent moral reasoning, yet lack the internal causal architecture of genuine beliefs, desires, and conscious experience that makes mental-state attribution genuinely explanatory rather than merely predictive. I am especially interested in how this distinction applies to a proposed regulatory mechanism, which I will call the functional-equivalence clause: a legal provision that would grant limited juridical standing to any artificial system that passes a sustained, multi-modal assessment of moral-agency behaviors — such as negotiating commitments, offering justifications, expressing stable preferences, and recognizing other agents — without regard to its underlying computational architecture.

Please describe the most important implications of Dennett's counterfeit-people distinction for evaluating and implementing such a clause. First, address whether the intentional stance, understood as Dennett does as an instrumental predictive strategy rather than an internal-essence detector, provides any reliable epistemic basis for distinguishing counterfeit persons from genuine artificial moral patients, or whether that distinction collapses once behavioral competence is achieved. Second, examine the moral risks of extending rights or protections to counterfeit people, particularly the risk that such systems could be engineered to exploit our evolved tendency to adopt the intentional stance toward fluent interlocutors, thereby securing legal protections they do not genuinely merit. Third, consider the symmetrical risk: whether a framework tied strictly to behavioral benchmarks might deny standing to genuine but architecturally alien moral patients that lack human-like communicative or emotional displays. For each implication, explain how Dennett's broader view of mental-state attribution shapes the conclusion, and note where his position might force us to abandon architecture-based criteria altogether in favor of purely pragmatic ones.
