I am a novelist in the middle of revising a long-form work whose central mystery is never fully resolved; instead of granting any single outcome definitive closure, the narrative asks the reader to hold several possibilities in suspension, allowing each to remain live only to the extent that it has been explicitly rendered in scene. In discussions with fellow writers, we often speak about "constructing" a plot rather than "discovering" it, and about how a character's fate exists within the story-world solely by virtue of having been actively written rather than inferred from absence or contradiction. This craft orientation has led me to wonder whether the philosophical commitments underlying intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism—specifically the refusal to apply the law of excluded middle across infinite or genuinely unfinished domains, the requirement that existence claims be supported by explicit constructive procedures rather than by proof through contradiction, and the broader view that mathematical objects are not pre-given entities but are brought into being through mental or formal activity—might illuminate, or be illuminated by, the logic of narrative construction.

Please take these core intuitionist and constructivist positions and restate them with precision in the vocabulary I actually use: plot arcs that remain genuinely open rather than retroactively determined, character agency demonstrated only through rendered action rather than inferred from negative premises, narrative closure as an achieved state rather than an assumed property of the fictional world, authorial intention as the constructive act that brings entities into being, and the reader's progressive accumulation of textual evidence across chapters. I want a faithful translation, not a loose metaphor—show me exactly which logical relations survive when expressed in terms of how a novel builds, withholds, or denies resolution, and identify any structural features of constructive proof or intuitionistic negation that find direct analogues in narrative development.

Then provide the residue report: tell me which parts of the framework map cleanly onto fiction writing, which parts require distortion—perhaps being forced into analogies with unreliable narration, ambiguous endings, or suspended disbelief that do not quite match their logical originals—and which parts, if any, have no expression at all in literary vocabulary. In particular, address whether the formal syntactic apparatus of intuitionistic logic, or its specific treatment of implication and negation, can be captured without importing mathematical machinery, and indicate whether whatever is lost represents a genuine limitation of narrative thinking or rather reveals that some constructivist claims depend on a technical notion of "construction" that differs fundamentally from the experiential, scene-by-scene building that defines my craft. I am not asking for citations, historical chronology, or a reading list; I want a conceptual translation whose value lies precisely in what it fails to carry over.
