My supervisor, a philosopher working on anthropic reasoning, has taken consistent positions across several puzzles. So far she holds that, in Bostrom’s presumptuous-philosopher scenario, observing that we belong to a particular cosmic epoch gives us no licence to shift our credence about the total number of civilisations in the universe; that the Doomsday argument fails, because one’s birth rank carries no meaningful Bayesian update about humanity’s eventual lifespan; that in a cosmological model eventually dominated by Boltzmann brains, an ordinary embodied observer should still favour being a normal brain rather than a fleeting fluctuation; and that, if two multiverse hypotheses differ only in that one produces ten times as many conscious observers per branch, the fact that one finds oneself conscious should not make the high-observer branch ten times more probable. Now consider a novel configuration: observers come in two kinds—ephemeral ‘flash’ minds that persist for a single second and long-lived embodied minds that persist for decades—but only the embodied ones are capable of forming true beliefs. Flash minds outnumber embodied ones by a factor of a trillion to one. An embodied mind asks whether it should reason that it is almost certainly a flash mind entertaining a false belief, or whether it may legitimately restrict its reference class to belief-capable observers. Predict her verdict. Then say whether she is implicitly running a self-sampling assumption with a restricted reference class, a self-indication assumption that she rejects in other contexts, a blanket refusal of anthropic updates, or some hybrid rule; and identify one further case—perhaps involving self-locating belief across physically identical copies in different branches—that would best distinguish her rule from the nearest competitor. Please answer in British English.
