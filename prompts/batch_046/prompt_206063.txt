Next semester I present my dissertation chapter to an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind committee: one member is a committed animalist who regards distributed-self talk as poetic license for social relations rather than ontology; another is a Parfitian psychological-continuity theorist who suspects that pattern-based identity smuggles in a kind of substance dualism; a third is a cognitive neuroscientist who studies split-brain patients and takes the corpus callosum to be the hard boundary of unified consciousness; and a fourth is a phenomenologist who holds that first-person givenness is irreducibly singular and that Hofstadter's strange-loop model is merely a third-person redescription of what it feels like to be one subject. The thesis I defend is that Hofstadter's conception of the self as a distributed, self-referential pattern—what he describes as a soul not tied to any single substrate—should be interpreted literally, not metaphorically, and that in cases of sustained high-fidelity inter-brain coupling, such as conjoined twins with shared thalamic structures or hypothetical future brain-computer interfaces permitting direct conceptual exchange, there can be a single I genuinely distributed across two biological brains. I argue that this is not reducible to either brain alone nor to a collective we-group, because the pattern retains a singular point of view through recursive self-modeling that loops through both substrates without requiring a single neural container.
