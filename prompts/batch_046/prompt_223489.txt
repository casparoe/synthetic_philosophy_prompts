I'm working through the deontic structure of supererogation for a chapter, and I've encountered a conceptual knot that standard introductions tend to gloss over. Most frameworks treat supererogation as a binary supplement to obligation: an act is required, permitted, or above and beyond. But this seems to suppress a structural asymmetry. If rescuing a stranger at significant personal risk is supererogatory — excellent but not obligatory — then declining to rescue them when the risk is negligible does not seem to fall cleanly into the same permissibility category; many moral intuitions place it in a distinct region of suberogation. Please explain the precise conceptual problem in maintaining a tri-valued deontic space — obligatory, permissible, supererogatory — without letting the middle category collapse into either pole, and address whether the asymmetry between heroic action and negligent omission can be accommodated within that same framework. Specifically, I want to know whether the tension dissolves if we index moral demands to agent-specific capacities rather than treating them as universal, or whether that move simply relocates the conceptual problem rather than solving it.
