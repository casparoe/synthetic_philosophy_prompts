I've been tracking a persistent stall in rationalist-adjacent metaethics about what I will call free-floating rationales: normative reasons that apply to an agent even in the absence of any reasoner—no mind, no social practice, no evolutionary history—to generate, register, or recognize them. On one side stand the grounding naturalists, who hold that any genuine reason must be anchored in an agent's actual or counterfactual desires, life-plans, or institutional commitments; on their view, to claim that "there was reason to prevent suffering before sentience existed" is either false or elliptical for "present sentient beings now have derivative reason to care about that counterfactual." On the other side stand the normative realists, who insist that reasons can sit in what I think of as a normative ledger—badness, importance, or justification-structure that does not depend on there being anyone around to read it.

What makes the deadlock strange is that both sides converge on nearly every first-order practical judgment: suffering is pro tanto bad, existential risk reduction is warranted, epistemic norms bind inquiry. The disagreement is strictly at the meta-level. The naturalists charge the realists with smuggling in spooky metaphysics that cannot do explanatory work; the realists charge the naturalists with collapsing normative force into psychological motivation and thereby forfeiting the resources to criticize agents who simply do not care. Yet neither side seems to deny the other's central examples—they only redescribe them in their own vocabulary.

My working hypothesis is that part of the dispute is verbal—hinging on whether "reason" necessarily includes an accessibility or motivational-access condition—while another part is a hidden divergence about what counts as explanatory success in metaethics. But I am unsure whether this is ultimately a substantive ontological disagreement about norms, a terminological clash between "reason" and "reason-for," or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about the causal explanation of normative behavior, the other about the justification structure of practical reasoning—under a single heading.

Can you diagnose the structure of this impasse using what each side actually says in this kind of exchange, treating both as fully competent? Is there a precise conceptual distinction—perhaps between indexical and non-indexical reasons, or between reason-as-justification and reason-as-motive—that cleanly separates the questions? Or is the disagreement bedrock, in which case what, if anything, should count as progress: a formal framing, a change of question, or an argument that no further resolution is to be expected and why?
