You serve as senior philosophical advisor to the Civic Design Review Board, an independent body that evaluates experimental democratic mechanisms before they are adopted by municipal councils. Before drafting your response, take a few minutes to brainstorm the core tension between political equality and epistemic justification, focusing specifically on institutional design rather than ideal theory—consider how a mechanism might look different if its goal were not merely to aggregate preferences but to structure collective reasoning about concrete trade-offs.

The Board is reviewing a pilot proposal called Calibrated Deliberative Weighting (CDW). Under CDW, citizens selected by sortition for local infrastructure-budget juries do not cast votes of equal weight. Instead, each juror completes a structured, domain-specific reasoning audit—measuring grasp of relevant trade-offs such as long-term maintenance costs versus immediate accessibility—without testing general knowledge, standardized literacy, or ideological commitments. The audit yields a competence coefficient that scales the juror's vote on that specific budget line, subject to a floor and ceiling that prevent extreme disparity. Proponents argue that CDW preserves democratic legitimacy through transparent random selection while reducing the risk of systematically misinformed collective choice. Opponents argue that it reintroduces a disguised epistocracy: reasoning audits may systematically disadvantage citizens whose expertise is lived rather than formally articulated, and scaling votes by demonstrated reasoning undermines the expressive value of equal citizenship.

The Board has also identified an under-discussed design risk that internal consultants call the epistemic externality problem: if citizens know that audit performance determines voting weight, they may strategically invest in performative reasoning skills rather than genuine understanding, and the volunteer pool for sortition may self-select for individuals who enjoy audit-style deliberation, altering jury demographics in subtle but politically consequential ways. This concern has not been fully resolved in the existing literature on democratic innovation.

Draft the Board's formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the municipal council. The memo should state a clear, actionable recommendation, including specific safeguards or pilot parameters if you recommend conditional approval. Provide reasoning the council could actually respond to: address whether the epistemic-externality objection can be mitigated through audit design (for example, by decoupling assessment visibility from voting weight, or by using peer-deliberation rather than individual testing), and be explicit about any feature of democratic equality that your recommended institutional form still cannot fully reproduce. Give an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side—whether that is the egalitarian objection to weighted citizenship or the epistemic-quality argument for differentiated influence—without reducing it to a strawman. Write in a polished, institutional voice, approximately 600–900 words. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the memo should stand entirely on its own reasoning. Think carefully about whether a competence coefficient can genuinely track issue-relevant understanding rather than proxying for class or educational background, and note frankly which aspect of that tracking problem your proposal accepts as unavoidable.
