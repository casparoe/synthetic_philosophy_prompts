Consider the strong literalist interpretation of the free energy principle: that variational free energy minimization is not merely a useful formalism for describing neural dynamics, but a scale-invariant law governing all self-organizing systems, such that collective institutions—central banks, legal systems, scientific communities—literally perform Bayesian inference over macro-level hidden states without any explanatory remainder requiring distinct sociological, institutional, or historical concepts. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this conclusion to be wrong? Which features of institutional behavior, if observed, would resist translation into active inference? Could the principle survive if macro-level predictions lack the precision-weighting dynamics seen in neural hierarchies, or if institutional change is driven by power conflicts that do not map onto belief updating? Distinguish evidence that would be decisive—say, a formal demonstration that collective decision-making violates the Markov blanket conditions required for FEP applicability—from evidence that would merely be awkward, such as the absence of measurable free-energy bounds in economic data. What would defenders of this scale-invariant view themselves concede as a genuine defeater rather than a domain-specific implementation detail? And is the position well insulated by the flexibility of its auxiliary assumptions—choice of generative model, precision parameters, timescales—such that no observation could genuinely expose it, and is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a liability?
