For a seminar on Spinoza's political philosophy I have been trying to articulate a coherent interpretive profile that joins his metaphysics of power to his democratic institutionalism, and I want to check whether I have stitched together genuinely compatible threads or whether I am quietly helping myself to incompatible motivations. Here is my current position.

(1) I read Spinoza's democratic theory—especially in the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus—through the framework of constituent power: democratic authority does not derive from a social contract that delegates power upward, but resides in the collective potentia multitudinis, the actual capacity of the multitude to act as one. On this reading, democratic institutions are not external constraints on popular will but its organized expression; a council or assembly is literally the body through which the multitude's power takes determinate form.

(2) At the same time, I take the exclusions in the Tractatus Politicus—women, servants, and foreigners—to be structurally necessary rather than historically contingent. Those groups are excluded not from prejudice but because they remain under the direct power of another (husband, master, foreign state) and therefore lack the metaphysical autonomy of being sui iuris that is required to contribute to the collective democratic power. Democracy, on my account, is therefore not liberal universalism but a rationalist republic of the self-governing.

(3) I also hold that Spinoza's claim that democracy is the most natural form of government applies universally: even in societies with minimal rational development, humans form associations for mutual protection, and these associations already contain the germ of democratic collective power, regardless of whether participants possess adequate ideas.

(4) Furthermore, I think democratic deliberation for Spinoza operates not through preference-aggregation but through the collective formation of common notions—shared adequate ideas by which citizens align their rational natures. This implies that rational autonomy is not a precondition for entering democratic association but an achievement within it: the multitude becomes sui iuris precisely through the process of deliberating together.

(5) Finally, I maintain that Spinoza's institutional proposals—the aristocratic council, the rotation of offices, the limits placed on popular assemblies—are fully consistent with (1), since these structures represent the multitude's own self-organization rather than an alien imposition upon it.

My concern is that (2) and (4) may pull in opposite directions: if citizenship requires pre-political metaphysical autonomy, how can autonomy also be produced by democratic deliberation? Meanwhile, (3) seems to sit uneasily with both (2) and (4), since it extends democratic association to those who lack both the prerequisite autonomy and the rational capacity for common notions. I also wonder whether the tension between (1) and (5) is only apparent—whether treating institutions as the multitude's body dissolves the worry that they constrain popular power. I would like an audit: which commitments genuinely conflict, which only look like conflicts, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each whether the problem lies in (2), (3), (4), or (5), and indicate which single revision would clean up the most inconsistencies without collapsing the overall interpretation.
