I'm trying to identify whether there is an established term for a recurring failure mode in interdisciplinary and cross-cultural discourse—one that sits near epistemic injustice but does not quite coincide with it. The pattern is this: a speaker deploys a distinction or category that is well-formed, internally coherent, and actively used within her own conceptual scheme, yet the audience lacks not merely the term but the surrounding framework that makes the distinction salient. Rather than recognizing that they are missing a piece of the speaker's taxonomy, the audience interprets the speaker as committing a category error or as being conceptually confused about distinctions they take to be settled. For example, a scholar working in a tradition that distinguishes three forms of social harm—structural exclusion, dignitary affront, and distributive deprivation—might present an argument that carefully separates these, but listeners embedded in a binary framework (say, harm versus wrong) read her as conflating descriptive and normative claims, attributing the mismatch to her individual competence rather than to an unrecognized divergence in conceptual architecture. What distinguishes this from standard hermeneutical injustice, as I understand it, is that the speaker's community does possess the relevant hermeneutical resources; they have not been globally marginalized or rendered conceptually invisible. Instead, the resources have simply not traveled, and the audience misdiagnoses the gap as speaker error. Is there a specific name for this kind of epistemic discounting—one driven not by identity-based prejudice and not by a universally absent concept, but by an unacknowledged framework divergence that gets interpreted as individual confusion? If no established term exists, how exactly should we delineate its conditions—particularly regarding whether the audience has a duty to recognize the divergence, and whether it can still count as an injustice when the speaker belongs to no structurally disadvantaged group? And how does it differ from mere misunderstanding or from what is sometimes called "conceptual incommensurability" in philosophy of science?
