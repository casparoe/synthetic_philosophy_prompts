I recently relocated from the United States to Germany for a research fellowship in applied ethics, and the divergence in everyday moral expectations has become impossible to ignore. In my American academic circles, appealing to individual conscience was usually treated as the ultimate court of appeal, whereas here colleagues often treat adherence to formal rules—regardless of immediate outcome—as itself constitutive of moral respect toward others. Rather than asking which cultural stereotype is accurate, I want a philosophical taxonomy that maps the logical space of positions on whether German-style rule-following and social order, or American-style individual conscience, represents the sounder moral foundation, with an eye toward which distinctions actually matter for evaluating cross-cultural moral disagreement.

Please construct a decision tree that begins with a descriptive branch: do you accept that there is a genuine, stable divergence in folk moral orientation between these contexts, or do you treat the contrast as an artifact of selective observation and institutional design? From there, split by whether your normative evaluation depends primarily on the social function of morality—coordination, predictability, and the maintenance of cooperative frameworks—or on the intrinsic status of the moral agent, such as autonomy, dignity, or self-legislation. Within the social-function branch, distinguish whether rules are valued instrumentally as protections for conscience, constitutively as expressions of a collective will that makes individual judgment possible, or pathologically as mechanisms that suppress moral thinking in exchange for social stability; within the agent-status branch, distinguish whether conscience is understood as a pre-social faculty that institutions must accommodate, as a socially cultivated capacity that actually requires institutional scaffolding to develop, or as an anti-authoritarian reflex whose value lies precisely in resisting formalized norms. Each leaf should carry the name of an actual position if one exists—perhaps a proceduralist Kantian, a liberal pluralist, a communitarian constitutionalist, or an unnamed hybrid—and must include exactly one sentence identifying its most awkward practical commitment. Please flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and identify at least one unoccupied combination of answers, briefly diagnosing whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should work entirely from the conceptual commitments at each node, so that someone could navigate it knowing only the concrete claims involved.
