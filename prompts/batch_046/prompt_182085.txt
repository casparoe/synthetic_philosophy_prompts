Since my recent appointment to our university's research-integrity committee, I have become preoccupied with a policy we now enforce for all human-subjects studies that rely on automated inference. Investigators must deposit a complete, executable specification of their analysis pipeline—model architecture, training protocol, decision thresholds, and exclusion criteria—before observing any data, and are thereafter contractually prohibited from altering that specification, even if preliminary outputs reveal clear misspecification or if new theoretical considerations emerge that would rationally motivate a change. The stated aim is to eliminate p-hacking and ensure reproducibility, yet the rule treats the researcher less as an adaptive epistemic agent than as a mechanism that selects a single computer program ex ante and then executes it without revision. In effect, it institutionalizes a form of program equilibrium: rationality is relocated from real-time judgment to the pre-commitment selection of an algorithm, and peer evaluation focuses on code verification rather than on the reasoning path taken in response to evidence.

What philosophical commitments about scientific rationality, agency, and evidential warrant does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—carefully distinguishing what the policy strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with—and evaluate whether that theory is internally coherent. Does it entail that a researcher who updates her model in light of unexpected data is thereby irrational or epistemically untrustworthy, and if so, can that stance be reconciled with standard Bayesian or falsificationist conceptions of scientific learning? Moreover, what would have to change in adjacent institutional norms—such as peer-review criteria, funding-agency evaluation standards, or the legal liability of principal investigators—if we abandoned this pre-commitment framework in favor of a view that treats rational inquiry as an adaptive, self-correcting process in which the agent retains authority to revise her program at any point, provided every revision is transparently documented?
