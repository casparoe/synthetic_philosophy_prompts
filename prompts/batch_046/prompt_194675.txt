I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training currently auditing an upper-level philosophy of medicine seminar, and after discussing system reliability engineering with colleagues I find myself stuck on whether health and disease are discovered in nature or projected onto biological systems by our design goals. The analogy that keeps returning is distributed computing: we often treat a redundant server cluster as healthy when its service-level objectives—latency, availability, error budgets—are met, even though individual nodes run statistically outside the hardware-class norm, because graceful degradation is engineered rather than pathological. I want an exam question that uses this engineered-system case to push students past the usual summaries of naturalism and normativism in the philosophy of health.

Please draft a 60-minute essay exam question (approximately 1,000–1,200 words, 25 marks total) suitable for an upper-level philosophy of biology or medicine course. The prompt should ask students to evaluate whether a biostatistical naturalist account, in the style of Boorse, and a normativist or hybrid account, in the style of Wakefield, can coherently classify an engineered distributed system—specifically, a redundant cluster in which individual nodes exhibit statistically abnormal failure rates but the overall architecture maintains performance within specified tolerances—as healthy, diseased, or neither. The question must be constructed so that a student who merely summarizes the two positions earns at best a passing grade; top marks should require identifying the precise crux, namely whether statistical deviation at the component level is sufficient for dysfunction when system-level functional norms are explicitly designed into the architecture, and whether invoking design intentions collapses the normativist into pure conventionalism without biological analog.

Alongside the question, provide a model answer that outlines the specific argumentative moves a strong response must make, and a marking scheme listing at least five must-hit points with marks allocated to each (summing to 25), plus a brief one-line note on how candidates typically fail each point. The scheme should explicitly flag the misunderstanding caught by treating graceful degradation as mere statistical noise that naturalism can absorb unchanged, and by treating engineering specifications as biological teleology without independent argument. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the question should be fully answerable without web access, referencing standard positions by name without depending on specific publication titles or dates. Frame the task in the weeds—do not ask students to define naturalism in the abstract, but to apply it to the engineered analogy—so that regurgitated encyclopedia entries are useless and the assessment separates students who understand the conceptual tension from those who have only memorized the labels.
