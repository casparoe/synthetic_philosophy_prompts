In Moritz Schlick’s essay on the meaning of life he contends that existence acquires significance not through transcendent purpose but through “play” — activity pursued for its own sake rather than as instrumental “work.” I have been reading this as a normative ethical thesis: Schlick is prescribing that we ought to restructure our lives around present-centered, non-instrumental engagement, since that is the only rationally defensible source of value once metaphysical purpose-claims are eliminated by verificationist critique. Yet this framing seems to generate a tension I cannot resolve. If Schlick’s verificationism renders statements about transcendent purpose cognitively meaningless, then the normative claim that we “ought” to prefer play appears to fall into the same category — it is neither empirically verifiable nor analytically true. Furthermore, Schlick elsewhere treats moral judgments as expressions of feeling or preference, which suggests he lacks the philosophical machinery to defend one form of life as objectively superior to another. So is his essay actually a disguised metaphysical prescription that violates his own anti-metaphysical commitments? Or is the tension only apparent because “play” is meant as a descriptive psychological category — a claim about what in fact produces human fulfillment — in which case the essay offers no normative guidance at all and collapses into empirical sociology? I want an answer that identifies precisely where my framing goes wrong: is Schlick making a normative claim he cannot justify, a descriptive claim that is not really about “meaning,” or something else that dissolves the contradiction? Please also say what consideration — a specific distinction he draws between types of value, a reinterpretation of verificationism’s scope, or evidence from his ethical writings — would lead you to revise your diagnosis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
