I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm trying to work out an argument for a column. The classic Ulysses contract is tieing yourself to the mast so future-you doesn't wreck the ship. In constitutional law that's precommitment — locking in rules so later majorities can't just vote them away.

But I want to push a variant I haven't seen named properly: the "reverse mast." Instead of binding future citizens from breaking a rule, you bind present citizens from quietly rewriting the reasoning behind it once they see the outcome. Imagine a constitutional clause that doesn't just entrench a policy, but entrenchs the evidential framework that justified it — so you can change course only by updating the framework explicitly, not by backdating your motives.

Can you argue that democratic societies should adopt reverse-mast provisions? Make the case that constitutional precommitment usually fails not because future generations reject old values, but because present generations rationalize away the original reasoning after the fact. Don't lean on strong determinism about human behavior, but do lean on the idea that information environments are predictable enough that institutions should be designed to resist hindsight bias. Keep it concrete and don't hand me a bibliography — I'm writing for readers who don't have library access.
