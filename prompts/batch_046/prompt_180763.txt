I recently became a parent, and the question of what present generations owe to those who will inhabit our institutions long after they have passed has shifted for me from an abstract exercise to a personally urgent one. Next week on my podcast I am interviewing a political philosopher—let us call her Dr. Elena Voss—who has developed a view she presents not as a theory of distributive justice across time but as a theory of democratic continuity. Voss rejects the standard Rawlsian savings principle, arguing that it wrongly frames climate obligations as a problem of welfare transmission to future individuals. She also maintains that Parfit-style non-identity worries dissolve once we stop treating climate harm as a personal injury to identifiable descendants. In place of both, she proposes what she calls a stewardship covenant: present decision-makers are bound not to maximize the utility of future people but to preserve what she terms institutional option-space—the structural conditions necessary for collective democratic self-authorship to persist across generations. On her account, carbon emissions are wrong not because they reduce the welfare of particular future persons who might otherwise exist, but because they contract the range of viable governance structures available to the democratic subject that succeeds us, irrespective of which individuals compose it.

I want eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking her to restate this framework and instead press it at the points where the literature on intergenerational justice and climate ethics actually applies pressure. Specifically: include at least one question about how her view avoids collapsing into a rights-based objection, given that future generations do not yet exist to hold rights and that she rejects welfare-based framing altogether—followed by a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that rights are simply the wrong currency for structural continuity. Include a question about the measurement problem: how can contraction of option-space be assessed without smuggling in a thick, contestable conception of the good life that her structural account was designed to avoid? Include a question about asymmetry—why climate mitigation falls within the covenant while other long-term risks, such as irreversible nuclear waste storage or biodiversity collapse, might fall outside it or fall differently—plus a follow-up that anticipates her saying the covenant applies to all irreversible structural locks, and asks how she distinguishes permissible from impermissible ones. Include a question addressing the adaptation reply: critics will say democratic institutions can adapt to a changed climate, preserving self-authorship under altered conditions; I want the follow-up to ask whether such adaptation counts as preserving option-space or merely substituting one form of democratic life for another that the covenant should have protected. Finally, include one question that asks what institutional or empirical evidence could count against the stewardship covenant—what it would look like for a present generation to preserve option-space yet for the succeeding democratic subject to be illegitimate or unable to self-authorize.

Please draft the questions with each likely answer in view, so that the follow-ups genuinely tighten the pressure rather than changing the subject, and keep the tone appropriate for a formal colloquium Q&A rather than a casual conversation.
