You are a philosopher of social science with expertise in causal inference, macrohistorical explanation, and the conceptual architecture of development economics. Before answering, please take a moment to brainstorm the different ways a long-running scholarly dispute can remain unresolved without either side being factually mistaken—consider verbal disagreement, normative divergence masked as empirical dispute, differences over what counts as an explanation versus an identification strategy, hidden formal assumptions about counterfactual stability, and the possibility that a single heading conceals several distinct questions.

In my interdisciplinary seminar on long-run economic development, a debate has stalled that I think exemplifies this pattern, but I cannot tell whether the participants are genuinely talking past one another or whether the framing question itself is malformed. The dispute concerns whether we can ever know, with anything approaching scientific confidence, why some nations are rich and others poor. One faction—drawing on the institutionalist tradition—argues that inclusive political and economic institutions are the fundamental cause of prosperity, and that both geography and culture operate only through these institutional channels, making them derivative rather than autonomous explanations. Another faction—drawing on geographic and ecological determinism—holds that climate, disease burden, and agricultural suitability set hard structural constraints that institutions merely adapt to, so that attributing independent causal power to institutions is a form of post-hoc rationalization that ignores the stability of the underlying geographic parameters. A third, smaller group invokes deep cultural equilibria—intergenerational time preference, trust norms, and religious inheritance—to claim that both institutions and geography are surface manifestations of slower-moving cultural configurations that neither institutional redesign nor geographic accident can easily displace.

What makes the stall genuinely puzzling is that the participants largely agree on the descriptive record: they accept the same correlation structures, the same border-discontinuity and colonial-settlement patterns used in recent empirical work, and even the same instrumental-variable strategies. They also agree that prosperity and deprivation are real, measurable, and normatively significant. Yet for nearly two years no position has shifted. Recently, one institutionalist introduced a distinction—borrowed from philosophy of science—between causal identification (isolating a mechanism within a given model) and explanatory depth (showing why that mechanism remains stable across counterfactual worlds in which background conditions change), and accused the geographers of conflating the two: the geographers, she said, identify geographic constraints but never show that institutional variation is unstable relative to them. The geographers responded that explanatory depth is merely rhetorical escalation with no operational content in macrohistory, since counterfactual worlds in which, say, the English Channel does not exist or in which the tsetse fly range shifts are too underspecified to support comparative judgments. Meanwhile, the culturalists have begun arguing that the very unit of analysis—"the nation"—presupposes a Westphalian ontology of territorially bounded sovereign entities, which makes institutional explanations circular: institutions are defined within that ontology, so explaining national prosperity by reference to them is like explaining motion by reference to velocity.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a hidden empirical disagreement—there is no disputed dataset or experiment waiting to be run—but rather a layered disagreement about what kind of answer would count as knowing "why," compounded by a normative divergence about whether explaining prosperity requires explaining its persistence across counterfactual regimes or merely its origin in actual history, and further obscured by an unexamined ontological commitment embedded in the unit of analysis. Is my diagnosis correct? More precisely: is the disagreement substantive but mislocated (for instance, about standards of causal inference and counterfactual stability rather than about causes themselves), verbal (for instance, "institution" meaning a formal rule-set to one side and an equilibrium strategy profile to another), or a case of three different questions—one about mechanisms, one about structural constraints, one about ontological units—being pursued under a single heading? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result clarifying identification conditions in macrohistorical comparison, a conceptual distinction that separates mechanism-explanation from constraint-explanation, a reframing that abandons the nation-state as the unit, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the counterfactual base for deep macrohistorical explanation is necessarily too sparse? Please support your diagnosis by engaging concretely with the three positions I have described, and indicate which feature of the case does the diagnostic work.
