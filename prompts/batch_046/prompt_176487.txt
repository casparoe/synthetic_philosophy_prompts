I am a third-year philosophy undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar titled Philosophy of Mind and Comparative Cognition. Next Tuesday I will present my term-paper thesis to the seminar and to a visiting discussant, and I need a rigorous Q&A preparation sheet for the session that follows.

My thesis is that phenomenal consciousness is not an all-or-nothing property tied to a centralized global workspace or a unified self-model, but rather a graded, spatially distributed phenomenon capable of occurring in partially autonomous neural subsystems. I apply this framework specifically to the common octopus: I argue that the arm-centered neural architecture, in which the majority of neurons reside outside the central brain and can sustain complex sensory-motor loops with minimal central oversight, supports a form of localized, fragmented phenomenal experience—what I term distributed binding without global integration. I further argue that this has direct normative consequences: moral status should be assigned along a continuum tracking the density and functional integration of distributed phenomenal fields, rather than by species-level binary classification.

The audience is precisely composed and will press me from different angles. There are twelve graduate students in philosophy of mind, two of whom have defended mammal-centric accounts of consciousness built around a unified self-model and will be skeptical of any fragmentation thesis. There is a visiting cognitive ethologist who grants sentience to invertebrates but denies that phenomenal consciousness—experience in the Nagelian sense—exists outside vertebrates. There is also a philosopher of science who specializes in the other-minds problem and holds that behavioral and neural evidence radically underdetermines attributions of consciousness to non-human animals. The seminar culture is exacting but collegial.

Please draft my prep sheet. List the objections in the approximate order they will arise, phrased in the vocabulary each objector would actually use. For each objection, specify: whether I should concede a portion and exactly which portion; the reply I should deliver, calibrated to about two minutes; and the specific weak point in that reply that I should be ready to defend rather than gloss over. Include the single objection I am most hoping no one raises, with a reply that does not evade it. Throughout the sheet, take a committed stance: do not present this as a menu of strategic options for me to select from, but recommend definite concessions, definite lines of defense, and definite judgments about where my argument is vulnerable and why it survives anyway. I want reasons for each recommendation, not a neutral survey of alternatives.
