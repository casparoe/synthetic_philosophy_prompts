I keep noticing a recurring explanatory move in work that appeals to very long-term structures—climate regimes spanning centuries, fixed geographic constraints, or demographic baselines that change only slowly. First, the historian invokes such a structure to downplay the importance of particular events or individual choices: a crisis was bound to occur eventually, so specific actors or decisions matter little. Then, when pressed to explain why the crisis broke out in one precise decade rather than fifty years earlier or later, the very same slow-moving structure is treated as an accumulating pressure that reached a critical threshold exactly then. The structure thus functions both as a passive backdrop that dissolves contingency and as an active mechanism that fixes the date. I have seen this in environmental history and in writings influenced by the distinction between slow structures and rapid events, but I do not know whether the double use is recognized as a specific pattern, a fallacy, or merely imprecise reasoning. Does it have an established name? How should one distinguish it from ordinary multi-level explanation in which background conditions and immediate causes play genuinely different roles? And if there is no specific term, what concise label with clear boundary conditions would capture the move without collapsing it into simple determinism or post hoc storytelling?
