I was talking with my friends Mira and Jonas about Augustine's absolutist take on lying — you know, the idea that its never okay even to save a life, since language is basically meant to express your mind and lying corrupts that purpose. Mira said that makes sense if the harm is mainly to the liar's soul and to social trust in words, but Jonas pointed out that Augustine still allows keeping silent or speaking ambiguously, so the line between lying and clever omission is already blurry.

Then they got into this niche concept called mental reservation, where you technically say something true but hide a mental clause so the listener gets the wrong idea. Mira said thats just lying with extra steps, since Augustine cares about intent to deceive, not just literal falsehood. Jonas disagreed: he said Augustine's framework is about wether your words match your mental state, and if there's a hidden mental clause then technically your words do match your mind, so it might slip through. Mira shot back that this is a loophole big enough to drive a truck through — you could save lives with technically-true misdirections, which undermines Augustine's whole point.

But then Jonas introduced this weird twist he called a double reservation — you construct a sentence that is literally false under charitable interpretation but true only if you apply some obscure grammatical rule that you know the listener won't use. So your mental state technically aligns with the literal meaning, but you fully intend the charitable misunderstanding. They asked me to referee and I honestly don't know whose read of Augustine is better, or wether this double-reservation gadget shows that Augustine's view collapses into incoherence or actually reveals a hidden consistency. Can you adjudicate point by point — who's right about mental reservation under Augustine's logic, is the double-reservation case a fair test, and what are both missing about why Augustine banned lying in the first place?
