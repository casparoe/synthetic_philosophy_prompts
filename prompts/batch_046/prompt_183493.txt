A colleague who writes on practical reason has offered the following verdicts on whether particular situations count as genuine moral dilemmas—cases in which an agent faces two binding, all-things-considered obligations that cannot jointly be satisfied and between which reason offers no decisive ranking.

First, she says a physician who must choose between saving a child and saving a parent, both of whom she genuinely promised to protect and neither of whom can be saved without sacrificing the other, is in a genuine moral dilemma.

Second, she denies that a corporate officer who must either betray a confidence to a colleague or withhold material information from shareholders is in a genuine dilemma; she holds that fiduciary duties to shareholders systematically outweigh interpersonal loyalties in that institutional context, so the conflict is only apparent.

Third, she accepts that a parent without resources who must either steal food for her child or allow the child to suffer severe malnutrition, with no legal or social remedy available, faces a genuine dilemma, because both the prohibition on theft and the duty to care for one's child remain fully in force with no rational ordering.

Fourth, she rejects that a driver who must swerve left to avoid one pedestrian or right to avoid another, with equivalent risk and no other distinguishing feature, is in a genuine dilemma. She classifies this as a tragic choice but insists that the obligations are not genuinely incompatible because a fair procedural resolution—such as random selection—can determine action without leaving a rational residue.

Fifth, she affirms that an agent who, through no fault of her own, is the sole person able either to honor a promise of absolute confidentiality or to prevent a grave and otherwise unavoidable harm, with both obligations intact and neither derivable from the other, is in a genuine moral dilemma.

Consider now the following case. An archivist promised a researcher that she would destroy his unpublished manuscripts if he had not published them by a fixed date, in order to protect his reputation from unfinished work. She separately promised a historian that she would preserve any documents bearing on an active scholarly controversy. The date passes; the researcher has not published, but only because the archivist herself, years earlier, negligently misfiled a document that would have enabled him to complete the work in time. She has since discovered her error. The manuscripts are now indispensable to resolving the controversy. Destroying them fulfills the first promise; preserving them fulfills the second and violates the first.

Predict whether my colleague would judge this a genuine moral dilemma. Identify at least two distinct principles that could consistently account for her five earlier verdicts, show how each principle applies to this case, and indicate which prior judgment is the closest analog. Give your best-guess prediction with the reason for preferring that principle. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, specify exactly what further case—particularly one that distinguishes dilemmas in which both obligations are structurally parallel from those in which one obligation is causally contaminated by the agent's own prior conduct—would settle the matter.
