I run a workshop on aesthetics and transaltion — about 8 grad studnets, 90 minutes, they know poetry but not much translation theory. I want them to feel the force of the idea behind Le Ton beau de Marot: that form isn't packaging, it's part of the meaning, and saving the rhyme or meter forces you to betray the literal sense in specific, painful ways. Don't lecture up front.

Design an in-class game with roles and hidden rules. Split into pairs. Give each pair the same invented 8-line poem (you make one up — ABAB rhyme, don't pull a famous classic). One partner is the Semantic Guardian, with a hidden list of meaning points they must defend: e.g., water as death, tense shift in line 5, the diminutive tenderness of the word mignonne versus little one. The other is the Formal Guardian, with a hidden constaint sheet: preserve rhyme scheme, syllable count, and prosodic shadow — the stressed syllables must match the vowel quality of the original line's stressed syllables. They have 20 minutes to co-write one stanza, but they can't show each other their sheets until minute 15. Then present.

After that, run a market round: pairs trade translations and vote on which best preserves the beautiful tone — le ton beau — without knowing which constaints each pair prioritized.

Then debrief: map exactly which negotiation moments match the Hofstadter dilemma — the literal version that kills rhyme, the rhyme version that turns mignonne into darling and loses the diminutive tenderness. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: probably the months-long iterative refinement he describes. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first run — likely people compromise too fast and ignore the hidden-sheet rule — and how to fix it.

Keep everything runnable, no bibliography, no citations. Make the rules create the problem, not the facilitator.
