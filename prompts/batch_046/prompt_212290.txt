Design a graduate-level essay examination question on Marx's theory of exploitation and alienation, intended for a 90-minute comprehensive exam in political philosophy or social theory. The question should require students to distinguish Marx's precise technical-economic concept of exploitation—specifically, the extraction of surplus value that occurs when labor-power is sold as a commodity under capitalist relations of production—from his broader phenomenological account of alienation (Entfremdung) articulated in the 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts, particularly its fourfold structure involving alienation from the product, the labor process, species-being, and fellow workers.

To prevent answers that merely reproduce a standard lecture summary, embed the following hypothetical scenario: a democratically governed worker cooperative in which every member holds an equal share of the means of production and receives the full value of the cooperative's net output, yet all daily labor is organized by an opaque predictive-scheduling algorithm. The algorithm sets tasks, paces, and evaluation metrics in real time using biometric and behavioral data, optimizing solely for throughput. Members retain formal veto power over strategic decisions, but they do not design, understand, or deliberate about the algorithmic parameters that govern their immediate working conditions.

The exam prompt should ask students to analyze whether exploitation, in Marx's strict technical sense, can occur within this cooperative despite the absence of private capital ownership and wage labor, and whether and in what specific form alienation might persist. Students should be required to address whether the mechanisms at play align more closely with the early Manuscripts framework or with the later Capital analysis—particularly the distinction between formal and real subsumption, and the transformation of labor processes under capital without a change in property titles.

Please provide:

1. The essay question itself, calibrated for 90 minutes and expecting approximately 1,000–1,200 words.
2. A model answer of roughly 600–800 words that executes the required conceptual distinctions and applies them rigorously to the scenario.
3. A marking scheme totaling 20 points, divided into at least five must-hit analytical moves. For each move, specify the point value and include a one-line description of the characteristic error or omission that should cost marks.

The deliverable should be answerable by a student with solid command of Marx's primary texts, but the framing should be original enough that it rewards conceptual precision rather than memorized summary. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requirements; the evaluation criteria should depend entirely on the clarity of the conceptual analysis and its application to the hypothetical case.
