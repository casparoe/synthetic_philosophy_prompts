Consider the problem of whether pleasure and pain are commensurable within lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism — the view that reducing suffering takes lexical priority over promoting happiness once suffering exceeds a certain intensity, but that the comparison itself may break down if the two goods share no common measure. Rank the following five accounts of the pleasure-pain relation according to two dimensions: (1) coherence with a strict lexical threshold, and (2) fidelity to ordinary moral intuition about welfare trade-offs. The accounts are: (A) cardinal intercomparability on a single utility scale; (B) pure ordinal ranking with no cross-domain comparison; (C) a dual-currency model with a floating exchange rate that collapses to lexical separation near the threshold; (D) conceptual incommensurability, where comparison is not merely hard but category-mistaken above the threshold; and (E) a tiered exchange model in which micro-level pleasures and pains are linearly tradable but macro-level suffering and joy belong to separate lexicographic orders with no exchange rate. For each account, assign a tier (S, A, B, C, D, F) on both dimensions with a one-sentence justification. Then state which single philosophical argument or empirical finding about human welfare judgments would most likely overturn your ranking, and point to the specific step in your reasoning that is weakest.
