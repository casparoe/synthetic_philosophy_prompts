I am a senior compliance auditor for a national regulatory board that conducts two-day site reviews of high-stakes predictive AI systems in hiring, medical triage, and consumer lending. The board works with a conceptual distinction between process-based supervision, which examines the decision pathway—data provenance, proxy detection for protected attributes, human-in-the-loop checkpoints, and reasoning traceability—and outcome-based supervision, which examines the decision distribution—subgroup error rates, calibration across demographics, and documented downstream harms. I need a reusable audit procedure, structured as an ordered sequence of tests with explicit stop conditions, pass/fail/uncertain ratings, and mandatory sign-off points, that my team can execute without access to proprietary model weights, without requiring companies to submit bibliographies or theoretical justifications, and within our strict two-day time constraint. The procedure must encode a non-compensability rule: a severe deficiency in one supervisory mode cannot be offset by excellence in the other, though you must specify the exact threshold at which a failure in one mode becomes disqualifying regardless of the other. At every stage, mark which checks are factual and which require an irreducible value judgment by the auditor; also state clearly what the procedure is structurally unable to determine. Apply the procedure to three scenarios: (a) a hiring algorithm that achieves perfect demographic parity across gender and ethnicity but has no documented audit of feature importance for protected-class proxies and no human review of rejection explanations; (b) a medical triage system with fully traceable reasoning chains and documented clinician sign-off at each node, but with a thirty percent higher false-negative rate for patients from rural postcodes; and (c) a lending model that maintains both robust documentation and equitable outcome metrics, yet where the designated human reviewers routinely approve outputs without consulting the reasoning logs. Include at least one case in which the procedure yields a failing or restricted verdict that the deploying organization would strongly dispute, and flag the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated. Finally, separate what is conceptually settled about the process/outcome distinction in governance and philosophy of science from what remains genuinely contested—particularly whether procedural transparency carries intrinsic normative weight or only instrumental value—without presenting either contested position as the consensus view.
