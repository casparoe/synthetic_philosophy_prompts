Two members of a study group, Jordan and Riley, got into an extended argument about Newton's bucket experiment and what it actually establishes about space. Jordan opened by saying that the concavity of the water in a rotating bucket is best explained by rotation relative to absolute space itself, not merely relative to the bucket's walls or to distant celestial bodies, since in the canonical case the bucket and water begin at rest relative to the fixed stars and then the bucket alone is turned. Riley countered with the standard relationalist reply: if the distant stars and galaxies were set rotating around a stationary bucket, the water should still climb the sides, which shows the effect tracks rotation relative to other matter rather than to an invisible container.

Jordan then pressed an asymmetry objection: if the distant matter is actually rotated around a stationary bucket, the gravitational and inertial influence of those bodies is negligible because of their enormous distance, so the relationalist cannot explain why the water would curve with comparable strength. Therefore, Jordan concluded, the relationalist explanation fails precisely where it needs to work—accounting for the magnitude of the effect without smuggling in absolute space. Riley responded not by denying the asymmetry but by proposing a modified relationalist dynamics: local inertia is coupled not uniformly to all matter but weighted by gravitational proximity and causal connectivity within the local past light cone. On this view, if the bucket were placed inside a massive, nearby rotating hollow shell—while the distant universe remained effectively uniform—the water's curvature should track the shell's rotation proportionally, without any reference to absolute space.

Jordan objected that Riley's weighting rule presupposes an absolute spatiotemporal metric—distance, causal structure, and the very notion of a past light cone—that can only be defined relative to a pre-existing spacetime manifold. Hence, Riley had not eliminated absolute space but merely relocated it into the dynamical laws. Riley conceded that inertia on this account becomes contingent on local cosmic conditions, but insisted that the metric can be defined relationally among the bodies actually present (the shell, the bucket, and the water), and that the weighting is an empirical coupling law rather than a metaphysical container. Jordan then asked whether this makes rotation meaningless in an otherwise empty universe; Riley said yes, and argued that this is a feature, since it denies the coherence of rotating in a void.
