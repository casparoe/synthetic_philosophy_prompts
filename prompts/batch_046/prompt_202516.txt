Read Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) through the lens of the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. The film stages four mutually incompatible first-person accounts of a death in the woods—delivered by the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai (via a medium), and the woodcutter—each internally coherent and each exculpating its teller, with no authoritative arbitration among them. Analyze the epistemic architecture: does each witness implicitly treat their own testimony as the rational exception to a general rule of human fallibility, thereby reproducing the preface paradox (accepting every specific claim in one's narrative while also accepting that human testimony contains error)? And does the audience face a structural analogue of the lottery paradox, rationally assigning high credence to each individual account while knowing the set cannot be jointly true? Focus particularly on the woodcutter's final version: is it a resolution that eliminates the contradiction, or does it merely relocate the paradox, since his account too is delivered by a fallible observer with his own motives for revision? Ask whether Kurosawa deploys these consistency puzzles primarily to model rational belief under evidential conflict, or whether he uses them to dramatize moral self-deception—each narrator's "rational" belief formation serving less to track truth than to preserve self-image. Where does the paradox framing illuminate a scene or a gesture, and where does the film resist being reduced to an epistemology case study—perhaps by suggesting that the contradiction is not in belief sets but in the stories people need to live with? End by saying what Rashomon adds to the philosophical debate about belief consistency that formal argument alone does not: does fiction show that the lottery and preface paradoxes are not merely puzzles about rational acceptance, but symptoms of the way self-interest structures what we take ourselves entitled to believe?
