For a graduate seminar on philosophy of history, specify the desiderata that a theory of multi-causal explanation must meet in order to handle overdetermination without collapsing into either monocausal determinism or explanatory nihilism. Your list should cover: (1) the conditions under which two or more causal narratives can each be sufficient for a historical outcome without rendering the others explanatorily redundant; (2) the distinction—if there is one—between additive overdetermination, in which independent causes are each sufficient, and structural overdetermination, in which conditions are individually necessary but only jointly sufficient, and whether both cases warrant the same explanatory treatment; (3) a method for preserving historians' practice of assigning differential causal weights—say, between long-term structural conditions and proximate triggering events—without reducing those weights to probabilistic measures; (4) consistency with counterfactual tests of causal relevance, including the specific problem that removing one cause in an overdetermined case does not change the outcome; and (5) compatibility with the empirical testability of historical claims in principle. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it, mark which are non-negotiable for any account that respects actual historiographical practice, and identify which belong to particular camps—such as determinist, counterfactualist, or narrative-integrationist—rather than being universal. If any subset of these requirements proves jointly unsatisfiable—for example, preserving non-probabilistic differential weights together with strict counterfactual dependence—state that tension explicitly, since identifying such conflicts is the central result. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing theories.
