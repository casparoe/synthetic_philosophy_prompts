I'm prepping a film-club night on The Godfather and I don't want to do the usual "is violence glorified?" take. I've been stuck on this concept I'm calling "distributed moral authorship" — the idea that in some artworks the audience isn't just watching a moral failure happen, they're structurally invited to help build the justification for it, and that invitation is part of the craft itself. The baptism montage is the obvious place: Michael is literally becoming a godfather while ordering murders, and the editing makes the ritual and the violence feel like one continuous, beautiful machine. The film never gives us a character who breaks that spell from the outside, so the viewer ends up supplying the romantic framing themselves.

So here's my ask. Read The Godfather through the philosophy of immoral art — specifically the debate about whether we can seperate the work's aesthetic value from the artist's (or the narrative's) moral failures. But I want you to take my "distributed authorship" framing seriously: is there something in the film that actually makes the viewer complicit in Michael's compartmentalization, and if so, does that complicity count against the film's artistic value, or is it exactly what makes it great? I'm trying to avoid reducing the movie to an illustration of some theory, and I also don't want the philosophy to get distorted just to fit Coppola's scenes. Does the work add something to the separation debate that pure argument misses — maybe about how aesthetic form can recruit the audience into the very moral failure it's depicting? No need for citations or a reading list, just a real critical reading that can tell me where my framing holds up and where it falls apart.
