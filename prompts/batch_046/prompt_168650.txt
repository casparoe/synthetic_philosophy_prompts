As a novelist preparing a multigenerational narrative that moves from the Little Ice Age through proto-industrialization, I have settled on five commitments and need a consistency audit before drafting. First, I take Braudel’s longue durée seriously: over centuries, geography, climate rhythms, and slow economic structures do more explanatory work than battles, treaties, or biographies, and individual lives are largely epiphenomenal froth. Second, I reject hard climate determinism as both empirically false and ethically dangerous; structures constrain without scripting outcomes. Third, I insist that a novel must grant its characters real moral agency—readers need to feel that a decision to betray or protect carries genuine weight, not that the protagonist is merely cork floating on a Malthusian current. Fourth, I think Tolstoy was right that the great-man theory of history is a narrative illusion, and that the finest historical fiction reveals individuals as nodes in webs they cannot perceive or control. Fifth, I hold that narrative coherence at the human scale requires intentional causation—readers track motives and choices, not isotherms or grain-price curves.

Do these form a stable package? Specifically, which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? I am particularly unsure whether my Braudelian structuralism can coexist with my demand for moral weight, and whether Tolstoy’s view undermines that weight or merely relocates it. Rank the tensions by severity, say for each what must be surrendered or qualified, and tell me which single adjustment cleans up the most. Give a committed answer: argue for the specific package I should actually hold, state plainly which commitment is the weakest link, and do not lay out options neutrally for me to pick from.
