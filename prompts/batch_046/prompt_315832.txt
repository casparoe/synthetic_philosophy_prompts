A colleague in our game-theory reading group summarized the strategy-stealing argument like this: "In any deterministic, perfect-information, two-player game with symmetric rules and no draws, the second player can never have a winning strategy. If they did, the first player could open with an arbitrary commitment—reserving a region of the state space—then adopt the second player's strategy, substituting a fresh arbitrary commitment whenever the strategy demands playing inside that reserved region. Because the rules are symmetric, this stolen strategy must guarantee victory, which contradicts the premise that the second player's strategy was winning. So the first player always has at least a non-losing strategy. This is why Hex is a first-player win, and it collapses for chess only because draws are permitted and for Go only because komi breaks symmetry. She added that a recent continuous variant extends this to differential games, where the opening commitment is a measurable set rather than a discrete move, and the contradiction still holds as long as the state space is convex."

I suspect she is overgeneralizing, but I am not sure which conditions are canonical and which are her own extensions. Is this a fair summary of the classic argument?
