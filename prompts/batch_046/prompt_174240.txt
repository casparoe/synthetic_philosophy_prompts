I am designing a 90-minute session for an upper-level philosophy seminar of approximately twelve advanced undergraduates or graduate students on collective choice and democratic legitimacy. I want a runnable, three-round mechanism-design game that makes the participants feel the structural inevitability of strategic manipulation—not as an abstract theorem but as a lived feature of every aggregation rule they try—before any formal result is named.

The framing should be a simulated constitutional committee selecting among four policy alternatives, A, B, C, and D, where D functions as a reversionary default activated by ties or procedural failure. Each student receives a private, sealed preference ranking drawn from a pre-constructed pool that guarantees cyclic majorities and at least one profitable misrepresentation under every non-dictatorial mechanism employed.

Round One should use a Borda count. Round Two a sequential pairwise-elimination tournament. Round Three a plurality-with-runoff rule. After each round, students submit reported rankings; the outcome is computed publicly; then, before private preferences are revealed, students are asked whether they could have improved their result by submitting a different ranking or by withholding their vote entirely. Correct identification of a profitable deviation earns one point in a running analytical tournament—not competitive grading—with the right to propose one procedural amendment for the next round.

The critical niche injection is an abstention option that is strategically non-neutral: in Round Two, construct the tie-breaking and default rules so that a student who sincerely prefers A over B can, by abstaining, cause the reversionary default D to be replaced by B—thereby making strategic exit, not merely preference falsification, rational for that player. The students should discover this possibility through the payoff structure and the revealed outcomes, not through prior instruction.

After Round Three, run a structured debrief that maps each round onto the formal conditions of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result: universal domain (the constructed preference pool), non-dictatorship (no student controlled all outcomes), surjectivity (all four alternatives were reachable in at least one round), and strategy-proofness (the manipulation and strategic abstention they just performed). Ask the group which condition they would relax—accepting a dictator, restricting admissible preferences, rendering some outcomes unreachable, or tolerating universal manipulability—and require them to articulate the normative or democratic cost of that relaxation.

Please provide: minute-by-minute timing; exact materials (preference cards, score sheets, visible scoreboard, rule cards for each round); the precise computation rules for each mechanism including how abstention is counted and how the reversionary default is triggered; the point-allocation scheme; and the debrief questions in order.

Also include an honest assessment of what this activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the formal proof of inevitability across the infinite space of possible mechanisms rather than the inductive demonstration across three—and state exactly how you will supply that missing piece verbally without turning the session into a lecture. Finally, predict the predictable failures in a first run—likely confusion over whether abstention counts as a preference, premature comparison of private cards, or students treating points as competitive rather than diagnostic—and specify how you will handle each.

Write this as a formal, polished request suitable for a professor preparing course materials. Do not include bibliographic references, citation requests, or reading lists.
