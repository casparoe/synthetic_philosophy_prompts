In evolutionary game theory and the study of cooperation, a common analogy frames sustained interaction in the iterated prisoner's dilemma as maintaining a joint bank account: each cooperative move is a deposit, defection is a withdrawal, the shadow of the future sets the interest rate that keeps the account open, and the one-shot game is like a single cash transaction with no history—making defection rational and explaining why cooperation collapses in anonymous settings but thrives in tight-knit groups. Audit this banking analogy in detail: map each financial element onto the game-theoretic structure (deposits, withdrawals, interest, account closure, collateral, bankruptcy, liquidity), identify precisely where the mapping fractures—for instance, whether cooperation can be overdrawn without immediate penalty, whether the shadow of the future behaves like compound interest or like a hard liquidity constraint, and whether the frame silently imports normative assumptions about property rights or reciprocity that evolutionary dynamics do not contain. Then determine whether the analogy's intended conclusion—that anonymity alone drives defection and that repeated interaction is sufficient for stable cooperation—survives once these disanalogies are explicit, particularly with respect to error, noise, and the distinction between direct reciprocity (tit-for-tat) and indirect reciprocity (reputation networks). Give a committed answer: does this analogy genuinely illuminate the conditions under which cooperation evolves, or does it systematically mislead us about them? And if it misleads, what analogical frame—mechanical, ecological, or computational—would do the work more honestly?
