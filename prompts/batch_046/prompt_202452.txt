I have been trying to map a recurring move I see in debates about distributed systems, group agency, and how parts make up ordinary objects, and I am unsure whether it has an established name or belongs to a recognized family of problems. Consider two judgments that often come from the same observers. First, a committee of specialists spread across several countries, coordinating only through intermittent digital messages, produces a unified policy recommendation; people immediately ask whether there is genuinely "one decision-maker" or merely separate minds, and they treat spatial separation and the absence of a shared neural substrate as serious obstacles to counting the group as a single agent. Second, those same observers consider a human body whose cells have been entirely replaced over time, or a mechanical watch whose gears have been disassembled for repair and scattered across different rooms, and they do not demand any extra "binding substance" beyond functional organization, causal continuity, or historical trajectory; they simply accept that these are single objects made of parts. The pattern I notice is that the demand for an additional compositional "glue"—something over and above functional or causal relations—seems to arise selectively: it tightens when the parts are spatially dispersed, can in principle act independently, or belong to different kinds of thing, and it relaxes for continuous biological bodies or familiar artifacts. I am not asking for a general theory of composition. Rather, I want to know whether philosophers have identified this specific asymmetry in compositional standards, how it differs from ordinary arguments about whether scattered parts form a whole, and whether it is best understood as a principled boundary condition, a contextual shift in ontology, or an unrecognized bias in reasoning about collective agency. If there is no precise term, what would be the sharpest way to delimit the phenomenon—for instance, does it apply equally to corporate entities, to robot swarms, and to divided-consciousness cases, or is its scope narrower?
