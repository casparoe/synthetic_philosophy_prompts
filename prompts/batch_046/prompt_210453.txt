I have been trying to systematize my reading of Carnap's philosophy of science and its implications for political planning, and I want a consistency check before I treat the package as settled. Here are the commitments I have adopted:

(1) Carnap's principle of linguistic tolerance should extend beyond logic to institutional design: competing planning frameworks—say, centralized versus decentralized resource allocation—are not metaphysically true or false, but should be evaluated instrumentally according to their predictive and organizational efficacy within a chosen linguistic framework.

(2) Carnap's emotivist analysis of value statements entails that normative planning ends—distributive equality, growth targets, risk thresholds—are expressions of attitude rather than factual claims, and therefore are not candidates for scientific verification.

(3) From (2) I derive that scientific expertise in planning is strictly confined to means-ends rationality, while the selection of ends must be delegated to democratic deliberation, precisely because no expert can claim cognitive authority over values.

(4) At the same time, I take Carnap's unity-of-science program seriously: rational planning requires a single, logically unified conceptual framework to prevent contradictory policies arising from incompatible ontological or methodological commitments.

(5) I apply the internal/external distinction to planning institutions: disputes over budget allocation or technical standards within an adopted framework are internal questions resolvable by empirical methods, whereas the decision to adopt one planning framework over another is an external, pragmatic choice.

My question is whether these five positions form a coherent whole. Specifically: does (1)'s tolerance for plural frameworks undermine (4)'s demand for conceptual unity? Does (3)'s delegation of ends to democratic deliberation cohere with (2)'s emotivism, or does it illegitimately treat aggregated preferences as if they carried normative weight beyond mere expression? And is there a hidden tension between (5)'s classification of framework-choice as external and (4)'s insistence that planning must operate within one unified framework—since if framework-choice is genuinely pragmatic, why must the resulting system be logically unified rather than a hybrid or layered structure?

Please audit this set. Rank the tensions by severity, distinguish commitments that are only apparently in conflict from those that genuinely pull against each other, and specify the least costly revision and what it costs. Do not treat all five as equally problematic; I want to know where the actual fault lines are.
