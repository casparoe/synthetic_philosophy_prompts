I am currently researching a long-form piece on the origins of social norms, and in conversation with a colleague I was offered a synthesis that strikes me as either insightful or reductive—possibly both. The colleague summarised David Lewis's well-known account of convention roughly as follows: conventions are best understood as evolutionary stable strategies that emerge from repeated coordination games, requiring neither shared intentionality nor any normative commitment from participants. On this view, driving on the left is functionally indistinguishable from a biological adaptation—an arbitrary equilibrium that persists because deviation is punished by failure to coordinate, and Lewis's reference to common knowledge is merely a formal way of describing mutual expectation within a population. Consequently, the colleague argued, Lewis's framework dissolves any genuinely normative dimension: there is no independent 'ought' in convention, only the functional stability of convergent behaviour.

The summary feels plausible, yet I worry it conflates Lewis's rational-choice framework with evolutionary dynamics in a way that obscures something essential. Would you examine whether this characterisation is fair? I am particularly keen to understand where treating conventions as evolutionary equilibria is a legitimate extension of Lewis's ideas, and where it misrepresents his insistence on rational choice, the arbitrariness of alternatives, and the specific logical structure of common knowledge that sustains coordination. Furthermore, does the conclusion that Lewis eliminates normative force actually follow from his premises, or does his analysis leave conceptual space for a normative aspect that evolutionary game theory alone cannot capture?
