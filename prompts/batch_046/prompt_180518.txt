I'm noticing a pattern I don't have a word for, and it's driving me nuts. When historians write about figures from, say, the 1700s or 1800s, they routinely slam them for holding beliefs we'd now reject — hierarchical racial theories, sexist ideas about education, that sort of thing. That's presentism, I get it. But there's a weird asymetric twist. If the same kind of figure held a view that happens to match modern progressive norms — like an early abolitionist argument or a defense of women's literacy that was radical then — historians praise them for "transcending their era" or "being ahead of the curve." So modern values work as a stick for bad deviations and as a medal for good ones. Yet they almost never do the reverse: they don't praise someone for holding a view that was genuinely progressive in its own context but looks wrong today, and they rarely excuse bad views with "well, that was the standard then." It's like presentism only runs one way, always measuring the past against now and only celebrating convergence.

I've seen this in biographies of Enlightenment scientists and in medical ethics textbooks. Does this specific one-directional pattern have an established name? How is it different from ordinary presentism, Whig history, or plain anachronism? If there's no term, can you coin one, define it precisely, and lay out the boundary cases — for example, does it still apply if the "modern" values in question are conservative rather than progressive? I don't just want a bare label; I want the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
