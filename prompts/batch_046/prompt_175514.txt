I am drafting a seminar paper on the ontology of biological taxa, and I find myself repeatedly drawn to an analogy that several philosophers of biology employ: that a species is like a single organism — an integrated, bounded individual composed of parts (populations, organisms, gene pools) that exist and function for the good of the whole rather than for themselves alone. The analogy is often mobilised to defend the view that species are real, spatiotemporally continuous individuals in nature, not merely convenient classifications imposed by taxonomists. Yet I worry that the comparison quietly smuggles in features — clear boundaries, internal coordination, a unified reproductive cycle, and a kind of functional teleology — that may not survive contact with biological reality.

To test this, I would like you to audit the analogy with some precision. First, provide a correspondence table: what in the species plays the role of cells, tissues, an immune system, reproduction, senescence, and death? Then show where the mapping breaks, paying particular attention to two phenomena that seem especially disruptive: the ring-species pattern exemplified by Ensatina salamanders encircling California's Central Valley, where adjacent populations interbreed but terminal forms are reproductively isolated; and the extensive horizontal gene transfer observed in many prokaryotic lineages, which makes a 'species genome' behave more like a shared, fluid pool than like an inherited body plan. Do these cases reveal disanalogies that matter specifically for the ontological conclusion — that is, do they undermine the boundedness and integration required for genuine individuality? Or are they merely superficial exceptions? Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that species are real individuals survives once the analogy is stripped of its imported teleology and boundary conditions, or whether the analogy was doing all the philosophical work from the start. Please answer in British English, and do not provide a bibliography or list of further readings.
