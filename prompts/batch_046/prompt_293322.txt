I'm currently in an advanced seminar that pairs Abhidharma metaphysics with analytic philosophy of personal identity, and I'm struggling with what seems to be a tension at the heart of anattā. Our readings emphasize that the self is not a substance underlying the five skandhas, but only a conventional designation for a causally continuous stream of momentary dharmas—including consciousness, which itself lacks an owner. Meanwhile, in my metaphysics course we've discussed psychological-continuity and narrative-identity theories as attempts to specify non-substantialist conditions for personal persistence.

My confusion arises from a thought experiment I've been calling the split-stream case: imagine that, through some extreme intervention, a single causal stream of mental and physical events bifurcates into two streams that are qualitatively identical but causally isolated thereafter, with no shared substrate. In the analytic literature this is analogous to the fission problem, and it forces continuity theories to specify which branch counts as the original person—typically by appealing to a non-branching relation or a narrative thread. But if anattā is correct, there is no original person and no substrate to divide; there are only successive moments. So it seems the split-stream problem should dissolve entirely for the Buddhist, because there is no one whose division needs explaining—only a causal chain that now happens to fork. Yet Buddhist ethics still needs to assign karmic consequences to one branch or the other, or perhaps to both, which suggests there must be some implicit diachronic principle—something like a causal-track condition—that determines which moments belong to a single karmic trajectory.

This leads me to my question: doesn't the need for such a principle show that anattā hasn't actually eliminated the personal-identity problem, but has only relocated it? That is, hasn't Buddhism replaced the substantial self with a hidden, non-reificatory binding relation that does exactly the same work as the psychological-continuity theorist's memory-links or the narrative theorist's story-thread—namely, providing the conditions under which successive skandhas constitute a single moral agent? And if so, isn't the Buddhist position not a rejection of the analytic debate, but rather a specific variant of it—perhaps closest to a causal-continuity version of psychological continuity—disguised by the language of conventional designation? I want to know whether this reading is defensible or whether I'm illegitimately importing analytic assumptions about identity conditions into a framework that was never meant to answer them.
