I'm preparing a single seminar session for philosophy undergraduates who understand consequentialist aggregation but have never considered invertebrate ethics beyond casual intuition. My aim is to teach them the scale argument regarding insect sentience—not as an empirical claim that insects are conscious, but as a structural problem in moral mathematics: if sentience is probabilistic and continuous rather than binary, then even a minute per-individual expectation of suffering, multiplied across populations running into the quintillions, may swamp the welfare budgets we assign to vertebrates. Before they can see this as a problem rather than a curiosity, they must first distinguish epistemic uncertainty (how sure we are) from ontological gradation (whether consciousness comes in degrees). Plan the session's dependency structure: what idea must be in place before the aggregation question even makes sense, and in what order should the pieces be introduced? Identify the one carrying example—something concrete but not mammal-dependent—that makes the numerical scale visceral without triggering empathic projection. Then map the misconceptions they will raise in predictable sequence: likely beginning with treating low probability as license for zero weighting, then shifting to treating the problem as mere scope neglect in reverse, and finally appealing to evolutionary simplicity to deny gradation altogether. For each, specify the point in the arc where it should be intercepted and absorbed rather than deferred. Include a check for understanding that shows they have grasped the structural tension between binary and continuous moral patienthood, not merely become disturbed by insect death. State explicitly what should be omitted—whether technical neuroanatomy, specific formal decision-theoretic models, or citation-heavy empirical literature—so the session remains conceptual rather than scientific.
