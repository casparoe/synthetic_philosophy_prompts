Since serving as an alternate juror in a capital sentencing proceeding earlier this year, I have been preoccupied with a tension the trial left unresolved: how retributive desert, general deterrence, and a quantified risk of executing an innocent person interact when they point in different directions. For a seminar I am leading on institutional moral reasoning, I need a precisely engineered thought experiment—set in a realistic context such as a state clemency board or a prosecutor’s post-conviction review unit, and confined to one paragraph—that makes these three considerations diverge rather than converge. Specifically, construct a case in which a genuinely optimal institutional rule (one that maximizes long-run deterrence across a population of capital proceedings) mandates denying clemency in this particular instance, while an act-utilitarian assessment of the same case recommends granting it; at the same time, a retributivist analysis should reach the same verdict as the rule—denial—but on grounds that do not depend on future consequences. The divergence must depend on an explicit, quantified epistemic risk of wrongful execution—for example, a documented false-positive rate in a forensic comparison technique central to the conviction—and that risk must function as an active structural element in each theory’s reasoning, not as decorative background. After presenting the scenario, show the step-by-step reasoning for all three positions, identify the exact feature that causes the rule-based deterrence calculus to peel away from the case-specific utilitarian calculus, and state precisely what happens to that divergence if the epistemic risk is removed or reduced to zero. Conclude by explaining how a defender of the theory that fares worst—without appealing to science-fictional predictors, demons, or exotic physics—would attack the fairness of the case. The verdicts should fall out of applying the theories, not from being stipulated.
