I have been working through an argument that generalizes Popper’s demarcation criterion into a universal norm for rational belief, and it seems to collapse every domain into irrationality. Here are the steps.

(1) A belief is rationally maintained only if its holder can specify in advance an observable state of affairs that, should it obtain, would make continuing to hold the belief intellectually dishonest—call this the vulnerability condition.
(2) Mathematical truths, ethical principles, and metaphysical commitments are not vulnerable in this way: no observation could show that two plus two is not four, or that cruelty is not wrong, because these claims do not make empirical predictions.
(3) Therefore, by (1) and (2), mathematics, ethics, and metaphysics fail the vulnerability condition and are not rationally maintained beliefs but closer to conventional stipulations or articles of faith.
(4) Now take a scientific theory T. By the Duhem-Quine thesis, T never by itself entails any unique observational consequence; it does so only together with a network of auxiliary hypotheses A.
(5) So if an observation conflicts with T conjoined with A, the conflict can always be resolved by revising some element of A rather than T.
(6) Consequently, for any scientific theory T there is no single observable state of affairs that necessarily forces abandonment of T specifically; one can always invoke a conventionalist stratagem to preserve T.
(7) Hence scientific theories also fail the vulnerability condition in (1).
(8) The upshot is that falsificationism, if applied consistently as a norm of rationality rather than merely as a demarcation tool, renders every domain of belief—science, mathematics, ethics, metaphysics—equally irrational.

I want to resist (8) without surrendering (1) entirely, and I especially want to protect the intuition that science is genuinely more vulnerable than mathematics. Where does the argument slip? Is the move from (5) to (6) an illicit modal slide—from “it is logically possible to save T” to “there is no observation that rationally compels giving up T”? Or does the deeper problem lie in (1) itself, in treating vulnerability as a necessary condition for all rational belief rather than only for empirical ones? If I reject (1), what do I owe: must I accept that rational belief comes in domain-specific varieties with incommensurable norms, or can I salvage a unified account?

Please diagnose the faulty step, or identify the least costly premise to surrender, and explain why that step looked plausible. Then price the main alternatives: rejecting the modal slide in (6) by appealing to normative prohibitions on ad hoc adjustments; restricting (1) to empirical domains; or allowing that science survives only because we conventionally forbid conventionalist stratagems. Also say whether saving science in any of these ways forces me to treat mathematics and ethics as second-class citizens, or whether there is a principled distinction that lets me keep (3) at arm’s length without embracing full-blown epistemic relativism. I am not asking for citations or a reading list—just the logical anatomy and the costs.
