I'm working through a problem in the philosophy of social science with a study group, and we've constructed an argument about Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis that seems to undermine not just Weber but any attempt to explain economic outcomes by reference to culture. I want to know exactly where the chain breaks, because each link looks hard to deny.

Here is the argument. (1) Any legitimate causal claim that C caused E requires that C be specifiable in a state conceptually independent of E; otherwise the claim collapses into a tautology. (2) Cultural values—normative commitments like the Calvinist ethic of worldly asceticism—have no physical or behavioral manifestation except through the practices, texts, and economic choices they shape. (3) Therefore, to identify the presence and intensity of the Protestant ethic in seventeenth-century Geneva or New England, one must examine sermons, account books, investment patterns, and labor discipline—the very behaviors that constitute the rise of rational capitalism. (4) So in Weber's thesis, the cause (the religious value-system) and the effect (systematic capital accumulation and rational bookkeeping) are co-constituted rather than conceptually independent. (5) If they are co-constituted, then asserting that the ethic caused capitalism is no more informative than saying that capitalist behavior was capitalist behavior. (6) Weber himself insists that he is offering a genuine causal explanation, not a tautology or a post-hoc narrative, and he distinguishes the religious origin of the spirit of capitalism from its later secular, iron-cage persistence. (7) Therefore, either Weber's specific claim is false, or the general requirement in (1) must be rejected for cultural causes. (8) But if we reject (1) for culture, we allow explanations where cause and effect are inseparable, which seems to license circular reasoning in sociology generally—explaining industrialization by industrial values that are only visible in industrial practice. (9) By generalization, then, culture can never be an independent cause of economic change; it can at most be a redescription.

This feels like it proves far too much. I am inclined to keep Weber's core insight—that shared normative frameworks can direct economic action in ways material conditions alone do not determine—and I also want to preserve the possibility of cultural explanation in other domains, such as legal or artistic history. So I do not want to solve the problem by simply abandoning cultural causation altogether. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there an equivocation between epistemic access (how we come to know about values) and ontological independence (whether values can exist and operate separately from behavior)? Or is the real move to distinguish between the content of a value and its causal mechanism, allowing the former to be identified through texts and the latter to operate through psychological mediation? Please diagnose the argument precisely—name the flawed step, explain why it seemed plausible, and lay out what each of the standard positions (a strict materialist reduction, a Weberian interpretive sociology, and a practice-theoretic view that treats values and behaviors as mutually constitutive) has to pay to reject it. Write your answer in American English.
