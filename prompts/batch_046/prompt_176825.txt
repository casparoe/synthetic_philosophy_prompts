You are a senior examiner in political philosophy with deep familiarity with democratic theory, the ontology of collective agency, and the boundary problem of democratic membership. Before you begin drafting, please take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual cruxes that distinguish superficial familiarity with the standard "all affected interests" and "all subject to coercion" frameworks from a genuine philosophical grasp of why the boundary problem resists easy resolution—especially when institutional design introduces hybrid criteria that appear principled but may only relocate the difficulty.

I am writing the final examination for my upper-level seminar, "Democracy and Its Boundaries," and I want one essay question that candidates can complete in 75 minutes (target length: approximately 800–900 words). The question must be unanswerable by mere summary of familiar positions; it should force students to engage with the conceptual architecture of democratic membership itself. To that end, please embed the following concrete but philosophically constructed scenario: a transboundary water-basin authority holds binding regulatory power—over allocation, pollution limits, and resettlement—across three sovereign states. Its proposed charter introduces what it calls the "structural vulnerability criterion": voting rights are granted not to all residents, not to all citizens of the affected states, and not to every party causally impacted by the authority's decisions, but specifically to those individuals whose basic capabilities are rendered permanently vulnerable by the authority's binding choices, regardless of nationality or direct causal chain. The exam question should ask students to do three things: first, reconstruct the implicit theory of democratic membership—what view of the demos, of representation, and of the point of participation—this criterion presupposes; second, compare it rigorously with at least two established boundary principles (for instance, the coercion-based account and the all-affected-interests account), identifying precisely where it diverges and whether the divergence is principled or arbitrary; third, evaluate whether the criterion genuinely solves the boundary problem or merely relocates it into a different normative register, particularly with respect to future generations, non-citizen residents, and entities affected only indirectly.

Please provide: (1) the exam question, precisely worded so that it cannot be answered by recitation; (2) a model answer of roughly 800–900 words that makes explicit the philosophical moves a top answer must contain, including an analysis of why structural vulnerability might seem more inclusive than citizenship-based rules yet less expansive than pure affected-interests accounts, and whether it smuggles in a hidden commitment about democratic legitimacy; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 marks structured around five must-hit analytical moves, with marks assigned to each, plus a brief one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong on each point. The difficulty should come from identifying the crux—whether hybrid criteria solve or displace the boundary problem—not from obscure scholarship. Do not include any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the question must be fully answerable through conceptual reasoning alone.
