I'm running a grad seminar on rational choice and we got stuck in this back-and-forth between two students, Elena and Marcus, about the centipede game and backward induction. Elena started with the classic line: if both players are rational and this is common knowledge, then at the final node player B takes, so at node B-1 player A takes, and so on all the way back to the first node where A takes immediately. Marcus didn't deny the logic, but he said it relies on a hidden assumption that rationality is "epistemically transparent" — that is, each player can see not just that the other acted rationally, but that they reasoned via backward induction specifically. He pointed out that if player A takes at round 1, player B (if they ever got there, which they wouldn't) couldn't distinguish between "A did backward induction" and "A just has a high discount rate" or "A is risk-averse about the later rounds." So the common-knowledge chain is actually broken from the start, because rationality doesn't uniquely identify the reasoning path.

Elena shot back that in the formal model we stipulate the payoff structure and rationality axioms, so transparency is built in by definition — it's not an empirical claim. Then Marcus introduced this weird variant they were playing with, where the payoff increments are continuous rather than discrete, so there's no technically "last round" to anchor the induction. He argued that in this continuous version backward induction has no base case, which shows the discrete version's base case was doing all the work, and that work depends on an arbitrary cutoff. Elena said that's not a problem for the discrete game, just a differnt game, and accused Marcus of changing the subject.

Then things got messier. Marcus said even in the discrete game, the induction requires not just common knowledge of rationality but common knowledge of "dynamic consistency" — that players don't change their minds about future choices. Elena agreed that's required, but said it's part of rationality. Marcus then asked: if dynamic consistency is part of rationality, and rationality is common knowledge, then shouldn't players be able to predict each other's deviations? And if they can predict deviations, doesn't that change the payoff structure? Elena said no, predicting a deviation just means you take earlier, which is the induction result. I wasn't sure who was winning. Can you adjudicate this? Specifically: is Marcus right that epistemic opacitiy about reasoning paths undermines the common-knowledge premise in the discrete game? Is Elena right that Marcus's continuous variant is just a different problem? And what's the deal with the dynamic consistency point — is Marcus identifying a real tension, or is he conflating prediction of behavior with the reasoning that produces it? I don't need citations or a reading list, just a point-by-point verdict on where each of them stands and what they're both glossing over.
