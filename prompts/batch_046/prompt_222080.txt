I've been arguing with my roomate about whether law is basically just power wearing a mask, and I tryed to formalize Pascal's point about justice and force to show him. Here's the chain I built for our study group, but it proves way too much and now I'm stuck.

(1) Pascal's basic idea is that justice without force is powerless, and since we can't make what's genuinely just strong, we end up making what's strong just. So in any actual society, the content of justice is fixed by whatever the enforcement apparatus actually enforces. (2) Now imagine a country that runs housing disputes through a dual-court lottery: Court Red always rules for the tenant, Court Blue always rules for the landlord, and which court you get is literally a coin flip at filing. Both courts have identical state backing, and every ruling is enforced with the same police power. (3) By (1), then, both "the tenant must stay" and "the tenant must leave" are equally just rules in this society, since both are enforced with equal strength. (4) But justice can't be genuinely contradictory: it can't be just that Maria owes rent and also just that Maria doesn't owe rent in the exact same case. (5) So either (1) is false and justice isn't just enforced power, or we have to bite the bullet and say justice in this society is genuinely contradictory. (6) The bullet-biting option seems absurd — if justice is contradictory, it's not a guide to action at all — so (1) must go. (7) But if (1) goes, then Pascal's whole explanation of why law feels arbitrary but still binds us collapses, because there's suddenly no reason we couldn't have made what's genuinely just strong after all.

I feel like there's a hidden slide between (3) and (4), maybe about whether justice applies to particular cases or to the system as a whole, but I can't pin it down. Which premise should I actually reject? I'd really like to keep (1) and (4) if I can, since the arbitrariness of law feels real to me but contradiction still feels impossible. What's the cheapest fix, and what does giving up (1) or (4) actually cost?
