I run a 90-minute seminar in philosophy of history with 12 upper-level undergraduates. I want them to feel—through their own actions, before I name the problem—that the standard tripartite division of “Middle Ages,” “Renaissance,” and “Scientific Revolution” is a historiographical construction rather than a natural taxonomy, and specifically that it forces continuous intellectual practices into discrete, teleologically ordered bins. Design a runnable in-class game or structured role-play with clear rules, assigned roles, and non-monetary stakes. Divide the group into three “heritage societies” (Medievalist, Renaissance Humanist, Early Modern Scientist), each given a brief charter defining its core practices and temporal boundaries. Provide a shared pool of 15 brief artifact descriptions spanning roughly 1150–1650, deliberately hybrid so that each genuinely fits multiple charters—for instance, a 13th-century geometric mechanics treatise composed in a university setting, a 15th-century court-sponsored quantitative alchemical laboratory that employs recovered classical texts, and a 17th-century university logic curriculum that integrates experimental instruments. Over 30 minutes the societies must claim artifacts, defend them against challenges from rival societies, and vote on contested cases; design the payoff structure so that accumulating artifacts grants prestige points, but ambiguity forces them to invent ad hoc sub-categories or stretch their charters. After the game, include a 20-minute debrief that maps specific moments of dispute onto the historiographical issue: which features of the triad the activity reproduces (teleological sequencing, suppression of Islamic and Byzantine transmission lines, the equation of “science” with mathematization), and which feature it necessarily cannot reproduce—the actual 19th-century German institutional and nationalist context in which the schema was forged, which you must supply as facilitator. Be explicit about what will predictably fail the first time it is run—e.g., one society dominates through rhetorical force, students treat publication dates as decisive despite hybrid content, or the group resolves tension by inventing a “transitional” period—and tell me how to handle each. Specify materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the roles of participants. The participants should leave having experienced the strain of forcing continuous practices into discrete regimes, not having heard a lecture on periodization. Finally, please say how confident you are in the claims you make about the activity’s design and identify the specific point about which you are least sure—particularly whether the role-play genuinely produces the intended phenomenological shift or merely trains students to perform a generic skepticism about historical categories.
