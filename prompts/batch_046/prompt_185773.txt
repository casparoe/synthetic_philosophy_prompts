I am drafting a scene in a novel about a constitutional lawyer who must defend a small commune’s authority over burial rites against a central ministry’s standardization mandate. To write this with conviction, I need to understand subsidiarity not as bureaucratic jargon but as a structural principle that carries normative weight. I will lead a ninety-minute seminar next Tuesday for six fellow writers—none with legal training—who need enough conceptual purchase to recognize violations of the principle in both fiction and in reports on Germany, Switzerland, and the EU. Do not explain subsidiarity to me; design the pedagogical arc. What idea must be established first—before the principle can appear as anything more than a preference for local color—so that the dependency structure is intact? Select one carrying example, concrete and slightly unconventional, that can bear both the allocative dimension (who holds the authority) and the procedural dimension (what burden of justification falls on the center); specify what properties this case must possess and which common analogies—education funding, bicycle helmets, environmental regulation—it must avoid, and why. Map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form: the identification of subsidiarity with mere decentralization or federalism per se; the assumption that it is inherently democratic rather than hierarchical; and the belief that it applies only to nation-states rather than to layered authority such as the EU’s competence structure. For each misconception, identify the precise beat in the sequence where it should be anticipated and neutralized. Construct a single closing exercise—something they can perform in writing or discussion—that distinguishes genuine comprehension from mere adoption of vocabulary. Finally, declare explicitly what you will leave out of this session—perhaps the specific articles of the Swiss Federal Constitution, the jurisprudence of the European Court of Justice, or the historical genealogy of the term in Catholic social teaching—so that the session remains conceptually rigorous without becoming a citation-heavy law seminar. Present the plan as a narrative outline: every stage depends on the previous, and no example should appear that does not earn its place.
