I am drafting a literary novel in which two estranged philosophers—Dr. Elara Voss, who defends what she terms predicative inertia, and Dr. Silas Kern, who advocates projective elasticity—reconcile after decades of dispute by co-authoring an adversarial-collaboration document discovered in the narrative’s third act. The document must read as genuinely joint, not as a masked victory for either camp, and must possess the formal weight of a philosophy-of-science publication while remaining intelligible as the hidden architecture of a fictional reunion.

Write that document. Structure it as follows.

First, present a numbered section of agreed claims—concerning Goodman’s original formulation of the grue paradox, the logical symmetry between green and grue prior to any pragmatic filter, the inadequacy of purely syntactic criteria to distinguish projectible predicates, and the failure of simple conventionalism to dissolve the problem—stated with enough precision that neither Voss nor Kern could reject the wording.

Second, provide a disagreements table with two columns, one for Predicative Inertia and one for Projective Elasticity. For each row, articulate the precise point of divergence in language each philosopher would endorse for her or his own position. Include at least: whether entrenchment functions as a normative constraint on rational induction or merely records a contingent historical pattern; whether the asymmetry between green and grue reflects a metaphysically real feature of our inferential ecology or only an epistemically convenient artifact; and whether a predicate’s projectibility can shift across generations without undermining the rationality of the inferences that depend on it.

Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would genuinely move at least one party. I take there to be three: whether an independent ecology of inference selects predicates through processes analogous to natural selection; whether a synthetic predicate, deliberately introduced in a controlled observational protocol and never previously instantiated, could acquire full projectibility within a single human generation; and whether accepting predicative inertia dissolves the problem of induction or merely relocates it to a deeper level of explanation.

For each crux, specify: a concrete case or hypothetical study that both parties would agree bears directly upon it (you may invent the scenario, but it must be one both would accept as relevant); what Voss would predict about the outcome; what Kern would predict; and whether both agree in advance that the result—whatever it proves to be—would actually alter at least one of their positions rather than being absorbed into their existing frameworks.

Fourth, conclude with an assessment that separates genuine disagreement from verbal confusion. Specifically, determine whether the dispute over the naturalness of projectible predicates collapses into a disagreement about vocabulary rather than substance, and identify—if present—any portion of the debate that is merely about words.

The tone should be rigorous, neutral, and suitable for a signed statement, yet retain the narrative gravity appropriate to a document two fictional characters could endorse without betrayal of their intellectual characters. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or appeals to texts requiring verification. You may invent the hypothetical studies mentioned above, but do not invent specific publication titles, dates, or verbatim quotations attributed to real philosophers. Where you refer to Goodman’s original formulation, describe it in your own words without quotation marks.
