I work in software engineering without formal philosophy training, but I have been trying to understand why the debate over Daniel Dennett's metaphors of cranes and skyhooks, together with his notion of greedy reductionism, remains stalled in the philosophy of mind literature. Crane advocates hold that consciousness and other complex mental properties are built entirely through bottom-up evolutionary and neural mechanisms—cranes—without invoking mysterious top-down forces or skyhooks. Greedy reductionists in this camp treat phenomenal consciousness as analogous to a software interface illusion: a convenient representation that dissolves once the underlying modular architecture is fully mapped, much as a high-level abstraction hides its compiled implementation. Critics respond that this approach commits greedy reductionism by explaining away the very phenomenon that demands explanation—specifically the subjective, first-person character of experience. They maintain that no bottom-up assembly, however intricate, generates the quality of being conscious, which therefore requires an irreducible, top-down explanatory principle that cannot be decomposed into smaller functional parts.

What strikes me is that both camps accept the same neuroscientific and evolutionary data, agree that brains are physically assembled from simpler components, and yet have not converged in decades. My working hypothesis, informed by system design, is that the disagreement may not be empirical but rather concerns what an explanation owes to the phenomenon it explains—an explanatory debt. The crane camp seems to treat a complete functional architecture as fully discharging explanatory obligations, leaving no remainder; the opposing camp treats the preservation of phenomenal appearance as a non-negotiable constraint that any adequate account must satisfy rather than replace. If this is correct, the sides may be answering different questions—one about ontological construction, another about phenomenological fidelity—under the single heading of explaining consciousness.

Can you diagnose whether this is a verbal disagreement about the term explanation, a substantive disagreement about which observations count as relevant data, or a conflict rooted in bedrock intuitions about irreducibility that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden conceptual distinction—perhaps parallel to the difference between compile-time architecture and runtime behavior in software—that could clarify whether greedy reductionism genuinely removes consciousness or merely relocates it? And given your diagnosis, what kind of progress should we expect: a new distinction, a formal result, a shift in the framing question, or is the impasse permanent because the underlying commitments are genuinely incompatible?
