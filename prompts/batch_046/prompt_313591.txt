Write a 90-minute, 25-mark final-exam essay question for an upper-level metaphysics and philosophy-of-action course on causation by omission. Center the question on a constructed scenario called the Silent-Veto Protocol: five regional boards share emergency-disbursement authority, and each can block funding only by failing to submit a counter-signature within 48 hours, yet no board carries an explicit duty to sign. When drought aid is blocked, analysts disagree whether there was one collective omission, five separate omissions, or no omission at all, since every board was technically free to remain silent. Ask students to evaluate whether omission-causation applies in this case and to defend a specific account of how omissions are individuated in distributed, protocol-governed systems. Provide a model answer in outline form (about 600–800 words) that makes three specific moves: distinguishing omission-as-absence-of-action from omission-as-absence-of-expected-result; addressing whether collective omissions require collective agents or can be distributed across an institutional protocol; and applying a contrastive analysis of causation to determine whether “the boards failed to sign” is the cause of the harm. Include a marking scheme out of 25 that rewards conceptual precision over conclusion, explicitly awarding marks for each of the three moves and for clear handling of the individuation problem, while penalizing two predictable errors: treating omission causation as merely a subspecies of negative causation without engaging the distributed-agency problem, and inferring that because no individual board was obligated to sign, no omission occurred. Do not require bibliographies, citations, or recall of specialized literature; the difficulty should come from reasoning through the constructed case rather than from summarizing standard textbook positions.
