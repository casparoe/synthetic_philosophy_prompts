As a journalist covering science and philosophy for a general audience, I sit on the advisory ethics panel of the North Pacific Coastal Restoration Authority. The Authority must vote next month on whether to approve Phase II of the Tidal Fragmentation Pilot — a proposal not to develop land, but to deliberately divide a 4,000-hectare continuous intertidal zone into eight isolated marsh islands using engineered berms, with the explicit stated aim of reducing aggregate wild-animal suffering. The ecological model submitted predicts that fragmentation will suppress local predator populations, lower parasitic transmission rates, and reduce the reproductive output of high-suffering r-selected species, yielding a net welfare gain despite elevated boundary mortality. Opponents on the panel contend that cross-species suffering aggregation is methodologically incoherent, that welfare calculus cannot override the direct conservation duty owed to intact ecosystems, and that authorizing habitat destruction for an indirect suffering-reduction purpose treats the marsh as a factory rather than as an object of preservation in its own right. Draft the formal decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It must recommend one concrete course — full approval with a binding five-year monitoring and reversal mandate, conditional approval restricted to two pilot islands, or outright rejection — with explicit reasoning the researchers and ecologists could respond to. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest argument for the position it rejects: namely, that even if the suffering calculus is correct, the Authority lacks the normative standing to destroy an intact ecosystem on the basis of an idealized welfare aggregation that no actual stakeholder has endorsed. Keep the document actionable, specify any timeline or conditions attached to the recommendation, and write it as a board-adoptable opinion rather than personal counsel.
