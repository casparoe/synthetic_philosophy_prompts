I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been working through George Berkeley’s claim that to exist is to be perceived, and especially the “master argument” that if I try to conceive of something—say, a tree—existing with absolutely no mind present to perceive it, the very act of conceiving it already places it before my own mind. I keep testing this privately and failing to escape it.

Write a Shakespearean sonnet (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, roughly iambic pentameter) in the voice of someone attempting exactly that escape. In the octave, the speaker should try to build a scene that is genuinely mind-independent: no observers, no sounds heard, no colors seen, only raw being outside all perception. At the volta, line 9, the argument should turn on itself—the speaker notices that holding this scene in thought is already perceiving it. The sestet should not resolve the tension with a sermon or a shrug; it should leave the paradox intact, perhaps by admitting that the attempt to banish the observer keeps finding the observer waiting inside the attempt itself. Choose rhyme words so that the shift from “outside” language to “inside” language is audible—pair terms of absence with terms of presence in the rhyme positions—so that the form enacts the collapse.

Please deliver:
- The Sonnet (14 lines)
- Commentary on the choices made (approximately 150 words): explain how the volta, the rhyme scheme, and any specific imagery carry out the structure of the master argument, and note where you had to adjust or distort Berkeley’s point to make the poem work as poetry rather than summary.
