I am an undergraduate presenting in my philosophy of biology seminar, and next week I defend the view that the species problem should be dissolved through a context-sensitive process realism rather than resolved by selecting a single concept. Specifically, I argue that species are real but ontologically heterogeneous—some functioning as evolutionary lineages, others as reproductive communities, others as ecological niches—without any shared essence or universal membership condition, and I propose that they are best modeled on analogues to dissipative structures: temporary, far-from-equilibrium stabilizations of evolutionary and genetic flow that maintain functional boundaries only so long as selection pressure and gene exchange continue to sustain them, so that extinction is not the disappearance of a substance but the collapse of a stabilized process. The audience is a philosophy of biology reading group consisting of: (a) a taxonomist committed to the Phylogenetic Species Concept who regards pluralism as scientific laxity and demands monophyletic rigor; (b) a metaphysician who holds that biological realism requires either essential properties or at least unified natural-kind structures; (c) an ecology graduate student who applies the Biological Species Concept operationally in field identification and considers philosophical debates about ontological heterogeneity irrelevant to practical taxonomy; and (d) a philosophy-of-science postdoc sympathetic to pluralism but worried that my process-analogy collapses into conventionalism or anti-realism about extinction. Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come: phrase each as that particular interlocutor would phrase it, indicate for each whether I should concede a point up front, what I must hold, where my reply is weakest, and include the objection I most hope nobody raises—namely, whether treating species as dissipative structures implies that they cannot genuinely go extinct, since by definition such structures persist only while energy and matter flow through them, which seems to erase the normative and causal weight we ordinarily assign to conservation biology's concern with extinction events.
