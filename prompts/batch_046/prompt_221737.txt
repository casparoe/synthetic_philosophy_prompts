The nonidentity debate—between person-affecting and impersonal accounts of our obligations to future generations—has been at an impasse since Parfit's original formulation. Both sides grant that we can wrong someone only by making them worse off relative to some baseline, yet they diverge radically on climate and reproductive ethics without ever resolving whether the disagreement is metaphysical (about cross-world identity and the conditions for comparative harm), normative (about whether existence can be a harm at all), or conceptual (about whether wronging requires a victim who could have existed otherwise). I want a diagnosis, not a survey: is this a substantive dispute masked as a formal one about modal comparison, a verbal confusion over the term harm, or a bedrock clash of intuitions about the value of being? And if the stalemate is structural, could a distinction between comparative and non-comparative wronging, or a formal constraint on counterfactual baselines, actually break it?
