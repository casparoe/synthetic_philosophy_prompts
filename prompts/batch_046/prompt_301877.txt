After thirty years in molecular biology, I have recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy, and I am drafting a take-home examination for an upper-level metaphysics course on personal identity. I want a single essay prompt that treats Buddhist anattā as a live conceptual challenge rather than as a historical footnote, and that cannot be answered by reciting Hume’s bundle theory or summarizing Parfit’s reductionism.

Please construct the following. First, the exam question itself: it should ask students to reconstruct how an Abhidharmic defender of anattā can maintain rigorous karmic accountability across lives despite analyzing consciousness into momentary dharmas with no enduring substrate. The question should then require students to evaluate whether that reconstructed position retains a distinctively Buddhist commitment—perhaps a particular account of causal dependence among impermanent mental events—or whether it collapses into a standard Western psychological-continuity view.

To prevent a purely textual or exegetical answer, embed this hypothetical scenario: a neuro-regenerative protocol gradually replaces every biological substrate of a patient’s brain over ten years, preserving only functional patterns and causal connections among mental states. Ask students to apply both the Abhidharmic framework of momentary mental factors and a Western reductionist framework to this case, and to argue whether the Buddhist view has resources to distinguish genuine personal continuation from mere pattern-inheritance that reductionism lacks.

Second, provide a model answer of roughly 800 to 1,000 words that demonstrates the specific conceptual moves a top response must make: correct reconstruction of momentariness, explicit handling of the karmic-continuity problem without positing a self, rigorous application of the neural-replacement scenario, and a clear distinction between Buddhist causal continuity and Western psychological continuity.

Third, provide a marking scheme appropriate for a 48-hour take-home worth 40 percent of the grade, with an expected response of about 1,500 words. The scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the required moves—such as addressing impermanence at every level, avoiding the conflation of anattā with physical impermanence, and not applying Locke’s memory criterion uncritically—and should list specific errors that should reduce marks, such as ignoring karmic accountability, treating the Buddhist view as simple nihilism about the self, or failing to engage the hypothetical scenario.

The prompt should be formal, written in American English, appropriate for graduate students, and should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. If specific doctrinal claims are needed, paraphrase them without quotation marks rather than relying on verbatim texts. The difficulty should come from the conceptual structure and the injected scenario, not from obscure scholarship or figures that a well-prepared student would not recognize.
