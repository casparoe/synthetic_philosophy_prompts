I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in moral philosophy, but I have been discussing demandingness objections to consequentialism with a colleague in applied ethics. She has expressed clear verdicts on several cases, and I should like to know how she would apply them to a situation closer to my own work.

Her verdicts so far are as follows. First, she holds that a person with a comfortable income who does not donate at least ten per cent of disposable earnings to well-evidenced global-health charities is failing in a serious moral obligation, not merely being less than virtuous. Second, she thinks that saving a drowning child by wading in and ruining expensive clothing is clearly required, and that refusing would be gravely wrong. Third, she judges that donating one kidney to save the life of a healthy stranger is not morally required; it is admirable and heroic, but beyond the call of duty. Fourth, she believes that abandoning one's career, family obligations, and personal projects entirely in order to work full-time for the highest-impact charitable cause is not required, however praiseworthy it might be. Fifth, she considers that purchasing an expensive non-essential luxury item rather than donating the equivalent sum to famine relief is wrong, though not as severely wrong as refusing a direct rescue.

These verdicts seem jointly consistent with more than one underlying principle. One possibility is a form of threshold consequentialism, according to which one is required to prevent serious harm whenever the personal cost is not substantial, where substantial means risking life, health, or the fundamental structure of one's life projects. Another possibility is an agent-relative moderate view, on which obligations are strongest in direct, immediate interactions and for modest, routine contributions, but one retains legitimate permission to prioritise family, career, and personal commitments over large, diffuse benefits to distant strangers, even when the cost is temporary.

Now consider a new case. I maintain an open-source security library on a voluntary, unpaid basis. I have discovered a vulnerability that, if left unpatched, will likely be exploited to cause widespread data breaches affecting thousands of users. By spending one weekend—approximately sixteen hours—writing, testing, and releasing a patch, I could prevent this harm. Doing so, however, requires cancelling a long-planned family commitment and working through the weekend without compensation. I have no contractual duty to maintain the library, the beneficiaries are anonymous strangers, and the harm is statistical rather than personal.

Please predict my colleague's verdict on whether I am morally required to spend the weekend patching the vulnerability. Identify which of the two rules above—threshold consequentialism or the agent-relative moderate view—better explains her track record, point to the closest analog among her earlier cases, and state honestly whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. If they do underdetermine it, please specify what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, suggest one further case that would best distinguish the two rules.

Please provide your answer in British English, using formal but accessible language suitable for a reader without specialist philosophical training.
