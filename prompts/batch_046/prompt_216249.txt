i've been maping out this argument about higher-order defeat and now it feels like it eats itself. here's the chain:

(1) if i have first-order reasons for P but also higher-order evidence that the reasoning producing those reasons is unreliable—say i know i'm tired, or that i'm prone to confirmation bias on this exact topic—then rationality demands i lower my confidence in P.

(2) but for literally any reasoning i do, i can always construct a plausible bias story: affect heuristic, in-group bias, motivated reasoning, whatever. i can never conclusively rule out that some hidden distortion is at work.

(3) so i always have availble higher-order reasons to distrust my first-order reasoning.

(4) therefore i should never hold any reasoned belief with high confidence; at best i'm always in a state of suspended or severely weakened judgment.

(5) but (4) itself is the product of reasoning, so by the same token i shouldn't be highly confident in (4).

(6) the only way out is to deny (1): say that knowing your own reasoning might be biased never actually undermines your first-order belief.

(7) but then epistemic akrasia becomes perfectly rational—i can knowingly cling to beliefs i think are formed by compromised reasoning, as long as i don't let that knowledge touch the belief itself.

where does this slip? it feels like there's an equivocation between 'i have available a bias story' and 'i have actual higher-order evidence of unreliability,' but i'm not sure that's enough to stop the regress. if i reject (2) instead, am i just being naive about my own cognition? and if i bite the bullet on (6), does that mean all self-knowledge about bias is epistemically inert? i want to keep (1) because it seems right that learning i'm reasoning poorly should matter, but i also can't live with (4). which step is the weak link, and what's the cost of cutting it?
