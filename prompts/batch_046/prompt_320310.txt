I'm working through Kant's political philosophy for a graduate seminar, and I've constructed an argument about his conditions for perpetual peace that now strikes me as suspiciously strong—so strong that it seems to make perpetual peace unattainable within the very framework Kant lays out, without the world state he famously rejects. I want to diagnose where the reasoning slips, and in particular whether I can preserve the structural analogy I draw between individual property rights and state sovereignty, or whether saving that analogy forces me into an unwelcome commitment about the federation's authority.

Here is the chain as I have it. (1) In the Doctrine of Right, Kant distinguishes provisional right, which holds in the state of nature, from peremptory right, which requires a civil authority possessing coercive power. (2) He applies this distinction to property: ownership of land is only provisional until entry into a civil condition renders it peremptory. (3) By parity of reasoning, territorial sovereignty is a collective property right over territory, and therefore should also be merely provisional outside a global civil authority capable of enforcing it. (4) Kant's proposed federation of free states is explicitly not such a global authority; it is a voluntary league that retains the sovereignty of each member and lacks coercive power over them. (5) Therefore, within Kant's federation, every state's territorial claim remains provisional rather than peremptory. (6) But a right that is only provisional cannot ground binding obligations to refrain from war, since provisional rights are precisely those subject to unilateral revision until a civil condition secures them. (7) Hence the federation cannot guarantee perpetual peace; member states retain a form of sovereignty they may revise by force. (8) To render territorial sovereignty peremptory and thereby secure peace, states must enter a global civil condition—that is, a world state. (9) Yet Kant rejects the world state as either a soulless despotism or an impracticable universal monarchy. (10) So Kant's system either fails to achieve perpetual peace or requires the very institutional form he prohibits.

I feel the error is somewhere between (3) and (6), perhaps an equivocation over what "provisional" means when applied to collective entities rather than individual proprietors, or perhaps a hidden modal slide from "not fully secured" to "not binding at all." If I have to surrender one premise, I would prefer to keep (3) and the analogy between individual and collective rights, but I want to understand exactly what that costs me: does it commit me to treating the federation as coercive after all, or to denying that sovereignty functions as a Kantian right? Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what do the standard interpretive options—those that read the federation as genuinely non-coercive versus those that read it as a proto-civil condition—each have to pay to accommodate that rejection?
