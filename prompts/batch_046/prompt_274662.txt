I'm a high school junior who has been asked to deliver the closing address at my school's annual "Global Citizenship and Local Roots" symposium. The room will be split: philosophy teachers and debate-team students on one side, parents, local officials, and classmates who take charity-begins-at-home as self-evident on the other. I want to explore whether compatriots have special moral claims over strangers abroad, but not through the usual slogans about national loyalty. Instead, I want to work with an idea I am calling distributed partiality—the claim that special obligations to compatriots arise not from shared identity but from the structural fact that local institutions make our actions causally efficacious in ways that cannot be replicated at a distance, generating duties that are genuinely non-transferable and therefore incommensurable with cosmopolitan obligations. This framework should do real structural work in the speech: it must prevent me from resolving my personal dilemma—whether to spend next summer in local community service or in an international relief program—by weighing both options on a single scale of good done. Rather, the speech should show that recognizing this partiality introduces an irreducible moral remainder: whichever path I choose, the value of the other is not merely overridden but genuinely sacrificed, and no formula can cancel that loss. Please write a six-minute speech that makes this accessible to an audience with no philosophy background without reducing it to a cliché about balancing priorities, and that lands with the teachers as precise conceptual reasoning rather than decoration.
