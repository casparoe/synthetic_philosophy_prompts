Below are notes for a short essay. The standard discussion of the alignment tax treats it as a static capability penalty: safety work slows development, leaving aligned actors vulnerable to less cautious competitors. I want to argue that this framing misses the structural dynamics. If we model AI development as a frequency-dependent evolutionary game rather than a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, the tax becomes endogenous. Safety investments by aligned actors do not merely reduce their own payoffs; they reshape the selective environment. In high-elasticity regimes—where small capability gaps determine strategic dominance—the withdrawal of aligned actors increases the relative payoff to defection, accelerating selection for unaligned systems. In low-elasticity regimes—regulated monopolies, high capital barriers, or cooperative equilibria—the same investments can stabilize the environment by reducing the marginal return to escalation. So the alignment tax is not a uniform cost but a function of the competitive dynamics' elasticity. The essay would conclude that alignment strategies should be assessed not by direct capability loss, but by their effect on evolutionary stability. I'd welcome comments specifically on: (1) whether this evolutionary framing genuinely differs from standard repeated-game or market-competition analyses; (2) whether the elasticity distinction is coherent for AI development; and (3) whether I am illegitimately conflating market selection with evolutionary selection.
