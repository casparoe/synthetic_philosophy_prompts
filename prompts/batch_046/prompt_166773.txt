Hey, I'm not a philosopher but I read some metaphysics for fun and I've noticed a move people make that I don't have a word for. Here's the pattern: someone claims a thing has a hidden ability or tendency — like a metal that 'really' reacts violently with water, or a person who's 'deep down' really generous, or even an AI model that 'fundamentally' has a reasoning bias. Then you point out that in every real situation that ability never shows up, because there's always some feature of the thing itself that blocks it — the metal has a built-in oxide layer, the person's brain has a built-in anxiety circuit, the AI has a built-in safety filter. The defenders say 'see, the blocking feature proves the underlying tendency is there, it just needs the right conditions,' but those conditions never actually occured in the thing's normal life. So the 'hidden' property is only visible in a hypothetical stripped-down version that doesn't exist.

I've seen this with chemicals, with personality theories, and lately with debates about AI. Is there an established concept for this — where a disposition is defended by pointing to an intrinsic blocker that is always present? I think it might be near those ideas about finks or masks I ran into, but those seem to be about external interruptions, not about a blocker that's part of the thing's own design. If it is near them, how exactly does it differ? And if there's no name for the intrinsic-blocker version, can you name and define it, with boundary cases that separate it from ordinary hidden dispositions or from just being wrong about the property?

Please seperate what's pretty settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. (Also sorry if I misused any terms — I'm learning as I go.)
