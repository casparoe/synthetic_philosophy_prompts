I am writing from an upper-level philosophy of decision theory course. Our seminar has been treating ambiguity aversion not as a settled bias but as a live dispute about the structure of rational belief, and I have been asked to construct the document that an adversarial collaboration between the two leading camps should have produced.

Please draft the joint statement that a rationalist ambiguity-averse decision theorist—one who holds that preferring a bet on a known-probability urn to an otherwise identical bet on an urn with unknown color proportions is a defensible response to epistemic structure, not a failure of consistency—and a strict subjective Bayesian—one who holds that all rational uncertainty can be captured by a single subjective probability distribution and that ambiguity-averse patterns violate structural consistency conditions—could both sign after sustained good-faith negotiation.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims about the formal structure of the standard two-urn choice situation (what outcomes are available, what information is absent, and what the relevant consistency conditions structurally require), about the empirical regularity that many subjects systematically prefer known-risk bets, and about what each camp takes to stand in need of explanation. Where there is a purely verbal disagreement—for example, over whether “uncertainty” denotes first-order ignorance about states or second-order ignorance about one’s own probabilities—identify it precisely and say which part of the dispute it explains away.

2. The exact formulations of the residual substantive disagreements, each phrased in wording that the side it represents would accept for its own view. In particular, state clearly whether the disagreement is about the correct representation of belief, about the normative status of preferences over acts, or about both.

3. The cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one party. I take two likely cruxes to be (a) whether ambiguity can be rationally represented without collapsing into a unique prior, and (b) whether observed ambiguity aversion survives in a compound-ambiguity setting—specifically, a sequential variant in which the decision-maker faces nested ambiguity about both the urn’s color distribution and the correlation structure of successive draws, with an available option to pay a fixed cost for partial resolution of one layer but not the other.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or structural argument that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side’s explicit prediction or interpretation of what that case shows. If either side predicts that the compound-ambiguity setting eliminates or transforms the preference pattern, record that prediction in the wording that side accepts.

Format the deliverable as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Where consistency conditions are relevant, describe them structurally rather than relying solely on eponymous labels. The goal is a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with every remaining disagreement rendered exact rather than rhetorical.
