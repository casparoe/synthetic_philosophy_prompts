I am writing a paper for an advanced course on eighteenth-century moral philosophy, and my instructor circulated a synthesis essay that attempts to read Adam Smith’s account of sympathy and the impartial spectator through contemporary social neuroscience. The essay summarizes Smith like this: sympathy is essentially mirror-neuron resonance, in which one literally feels another’s affect in one’s own body, and moral judgment is simply the alignment of these resonant states; the impartial spectator is then reconstructed as an internalized predictive-coding algorithm that minimizes error against prevailing community norms, so that being moral just means calibrating one’s emotional predictions to match the group. From this, the author concludes that Smith’s framework is fundamentally conservative, because the spectator is only the internalized average of one’s society, leaving no cross-cultural standard for criticizing practices such as slavery or gender hierarchy except by substituting one local calibration for another, and that Smith’s occasional appeals to universal propriety are merely rhetorical. The summary also claims that Smith explicitly denies any role for reason in moral judgment, reducing his system entirely to a sentiment-based predictive-processing model.

The framing strikes me as clever, but I recall that Smith usually distinguishes sympathy from literal emotional contagion by emphasizing imaginative projection into another’s situation, and that the impartial spectator is meant to be well-informed and disinterested rather than merely statistically average. Before I adopt this interpretation, I want to know where the synthesis is faithful and where it slips. Please audit the summary point by point: identify which claims are accurate to Smith, which are defensible modern reconstructions that nonetheless distort his view, which are conflations or overstatements, and whether the conservative conclusion and the denial of reason actually follow from anything correct in the summary. I am particularly uncertain about whether sympathy for Smith requires shared feeling or can operate through imaginative understanding alone, and whether the spectator functions as an idealized counterfactual observer rather than a social norm-encoder.
