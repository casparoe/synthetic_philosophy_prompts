As a retired biologist now working through philosophy systematically, I am studying Nietzsche’s Genealogy of Morals, particularly the first essay’s discussion of the priestly inversion of values. In the passage I have in mind, Nietzsche portrays the priestly caste as physically powerless yet spiritually inventive: barred from direct action, they declare their enemies—the strong, the noble—to be evil, and themselves—the weak, the suffering—to be good. He insists this is not mere denial but a genuinely creative act that establishes an independent moral framework with its own internal coherence.

What I cannot resolve is the precise relation he asserts between the psychological origin of this valuation and its normative standing. In evolutionary biology we distinguish sharply between a trait’s phylogenetic origin—say, a spiteful strategy that evolves because it harms a competitor—and its subsequent functional role in an organism’s ecology. Reading Nietzsche through that distinction, I wonder whether he treats priestly morality as analogous to such a spiteful origin: ressentiment explains why the system arose, yet once established it operates as an autonomous normative order whose authority should be assessed independently of its genesis. Or does he argue instead that the origin in impotent hatred permanently vitiates the system, so that to understand its genesis is simultaneously to expose its lack of legitimate authority?
