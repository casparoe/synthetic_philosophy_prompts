I am preparing an opening piece for a workshop on supertasks and the metaphysics of infinite divisibility, and I would like a poem that treats the classic lamp paradox—in which a lamp is switched on and off at intervals that halve repeatedly, compressing an infinite sequence into two minutes—not as a logical curiosity but as a crisis in the concept of change reaching a terminus. Please write a Shakespearean sonnet, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, in which the rhyme pairs in the quatrains enact the toggling: each rhymed pair should juxtapose an image of illumination with one of darkness (for example, filament and shadow, heat and cool, presence and absence), so that the rhyme scheme itself becomes the switch. The octave should build the intuitive mechanics of the supertask, with each quatrain representing a finer division of the interval; the volta at line nine should occur at the precise moment when the expectation of a "final" toggle collapses, since an infinite series of discrete changes has no last member to determine the state at the limit. In the sestet, develop the idea that the lamp's condition at the two-minute mark is not merely unknown but genuinely underdetermined—the causal history contains perfect symmetry between on and off, with no asymmetry to break it—and that this is an ontological feature of the limit, not an epistemic gap. The closing couplet should present both states simultaneously, without resolving them. Keep the imagery concrete (the filament, the switch, the compressed time) and let the metaphysics remain implicit in the structure rather than stated as doctrine. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of time recognizes the logical architecture, yet it should stand as verse rather than illustration.
