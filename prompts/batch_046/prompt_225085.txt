I serve on the Joint Committee on Historical Reasoning in Security Policy, an institutional review body that must approve, reject, or conditionally clear strategic white papers before they reach decision-makers. We are reviewing a draft memo titled "Analogical Warnings in Maritime Containment Strategy," which recommends accelerating multilateral naval coordination rather than bilateral concession in a current territorial dispute, grounding this recommendation in three historical analogies: the Munich Agreement of 1938 (invoked to show that accommodating revisionist demands invites further escalation), the Vietnam conflict (invoked to warn that incremental escalation produces commitment traps with no clear exit), and the Thucydides trap (invoked to suggest that structural power transitions between a ruling and a rising state tend toward conflict regardless of intentions). A dissenting appendix by our internal philosophy-of-science advisor argues that all three analogies commit what she calls "structural isomorphism errors": Munich fails because 1938 involved non-nuclear, territorially contiguous actors without institutionalized crisis-communication channels, conditions absent in the present nuclear-armed, institutionally embedded dispute; Vietnam fails because the escalation dynamics depended on a specific domestic political consensus mechanism that does not map onto the current alliance architecture; and the Thucydides trap fails because it treats state-level power transitions as deterministic mechanisms rather than as probabilistic tendencies mediated by regime type, economic interdependence, and alliance commitments. The advisor proposes that we should approve analogies only if they satisfy a three-part test: (1) causal pathway preservation—the mechanism producing the historical outcome is demonstrably present in the target case; (2) institutional response homology—the decision-makers being advised face incentive structures comparable to those in the historical case; and (3) counterfactual robustness—the analogy remains informative under plausible variations in background conditions. The memo's authors reply that this standard is impossibly high, that analogies in strategic policy are necessarily heuristic rather than predictive, and that without them decision-makers lack any cognitive framework for unprecedented situations.

Write the Committee's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—recommending whether the strategic memo may be circulated in its current form. State a clear recommendation with actionable conditions if applicable; address the philosophical question of whether analogical reasoning in strategic policy can be legitimate despite structural dissimilarities; treat the strongest consideration from the opposing side honestly (either the authors' defense of heuristic value or the advisor's demand for isomorphism); and specify exactly which elements of the draft must be revised, retained, or qualified. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to scholars; reason solely from the distinctions and criteria set out above.
