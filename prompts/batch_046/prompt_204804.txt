Construct a formal branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with diagnostic internal nodes and named or unnamed leaves—of ethical positions on dramatizing real, deceased persons in historical fiction and biopics, intended for use in an applied-ethics or film-philosophy seminar. Each internal node should pose a concrete, answerable question about our obligations to the dead (for example: whether posthumous psychological harm is metaphysically possible; whether legal heirs can inherit a veto over representation; whether artistic value ever outweighs fidelity to documented inner life; whether access conditions on private archives generate moral constraints beyond property law). Each leaf should carry a position label or “unnamed,” a one-sentence statement of its core commitment, and one sentence identifying its most awkward implication. The tree must include, among other leaves, strict archival fidelity, artistic-license-with-public-disclosure, posthumous-dignity rights, consequentialist artistic-value maximization, and the null view that the dead hold no moral standing. You must also locate within the tree the “archival covenant” stance—defined here as the claim that anyone granted access to private papers, sealed correspondence, or family archives for dramatization purposes enters an implicit moral agreement not to fabricate or substantially distort the subject’s documented inner life, irrespective of legal ownership or artistic gain. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a catalog of named theorists, so that a respondent can trace their own commitments without prior knowledge of philosophical labels. Note any combination of answers that yields an empty or incoherent position, and briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from logical contradiction, practical unattractiveness, or mere historical neglect. Where the tree cannot separate two positions, flag them explicitly. Use concrete cases to ground the branches—such as inventing an undocumented private romantic dialogue, altering a verified cause of death for narrative effect, or depicting psychiatric details drawn from sealed records released under an access agreement—but do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure named theories that a well-read generalist could not be expected to recall from training. Keep the framing formal and polished, with each branch labeled by the precise claim that divides it from its siblings.
