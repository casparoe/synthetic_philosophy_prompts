In the manuscript for my forthcoming novel, *The Unquiet Seminar*, a disillusioned lecturer administers a final examination to graduate students in existential theology, and I wish the scene to possess intellectual authenticity rather than generic classroom dialogue. To that end, please compose an original, rigorous exam question—complete with a model answer of roughly seven hundred words and a detailed marking scheme—centered on Kierkegaard’s concept of the teleological suspension of the ethical as it unfolds through the figure of Abraham. The prompt should be answerable only through conceptual reconstruction rather than summary: ask the student to adjudicate the coherence of a hypothetical figure I have devised for the narrative, the “silent architect,” an agent who, like Abraham, suspends universal ethical obligations for the sake of a singular, incommunicable telos, yet who receives no explicit divine command and can offer no justification that the ethical sphere could recognize. Specifically, the question should require students to determine whether this figure represents a defensible secularization of Abraham’s religiousness or instead collapses into aesthetic caprice or madness.

The model answer must make five distinct moves: first, a precise reconstruction of the aesthetic, ethical, and religious spheres and an explanation of why only the religious sphere permits a genuine suspension rather than a mere moral conflict; second, an analysis of Abraham’s silence and incommunicability as structurally necessary rather than psychologically contingent; third, an evaluation of whether a non-theistic singular telos can preserve the asymmetry between the individual and the universal without reducing the suspension to subjective preference; fourth, a critical engagement with the objection that, absent God, the suspension becomes a dangerous romanticization of violence; and fifth, a reasoned conclusion on whether the silent architect succeeds. The marking scheme should allocate twenty-five marks total, with five marks for each move, and include for each a one-line note on the characteristic failure—such as conflating suspension with ethical dilemma, treating Abraham’s silence as emotional trauma, or ignoring the incommensurability between spheres. Design the examination for an upper-level graduate seminar, allow ninety minutes, and explicitly forbid bibliographic references or citations, since the students in the scene sit in a closed examination without access to external texts. Please avoid verbatim quotations from Kierkegaard’s writings; paraphrase his concepts in your own formulation.
