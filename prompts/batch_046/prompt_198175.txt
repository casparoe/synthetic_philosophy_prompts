I am preparing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on the ontology of affect, and I have noticed that my students keep reaching for the same analogy: emotions are like perceptual states—fear is to danger as seeing-red is to a red surface, grief is to loss as vision is to absence, anger is to offense as touch is to resistance. The analogy is not decorative; it is meant to carry the full weight of an anti-cognitivist conclusion, namely that emotions are not judgments or propositional attitudes but non-conceptual registrations of value-laden properties in the environment, registered with the same immediacy and correction-resistance as sensory qualities. To test whether it actually bears that weight, I want a precise audit. Please lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the sensory modality, the distal stimulus, the representational content, the possibility of illusion or misfire, the mechanism of correction, and the boundary between transparency and opacity—then trace where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in three pressure points: emotions directed at objects that do not exist and therefore lack any corresponding distal property; emotions such as indignation at an institutional injustice, which seem to require prior conceptual framing before the affect can even arise; and the asymmetry between correcting a perceptual error (one looks again) and correcting an emotional error (one must reinterpret the situation or revise one's values, not merely "feel again"). In my own teaching I have been distinguishing between "affective transparency," where an emotion presents itself as immediately given, and "affective opacity," where the subject must discover or interpret what she feels; I want you to say whether this distinction maps onto anything in the perceptual case or whether it reveals a structural disanalogy. Finally, tell me whether the anti-cognitivist conclusion—that emotions are perceptions rather than judgments—survives once these breakdowns are on the table, or whether the analogy was smuggling it in all along, and if the analogy collapses, suggest a better comparison—perhaps to proprioception, or to some other model—that preserves the phenomenology of immediacy without importing the representational machinery of perception.
