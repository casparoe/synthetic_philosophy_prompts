Please rank the following five institutional designs—each intended to solve an intertemporal collective-action problem by combining Ulysses-contract logic with constitutional precommitment—from the most philosophically defensible to the least defensible as mechanisms that genuinely bind future agents without collapsing into mere paternalism.

A. A constitutional cooling-period rule that delays any tax-policy change for twenty years, with an emergency override available only through unanimous consent across three successive legislative assemblies.

B. A delegated-binding arrangement in which citizens permanently assign future welfare-state preferences to an algorithmic advisory body; its recommendations become law unless overturned by a legislative supermajority within seventy-two hours.

C. A demographic-autorevision sunset clause requiring all social programs to expire after fifteen years and be renewed only by the same cohort of citizens—tracked longitudinally—who originally authorized them.

D. A meta-entrenchment provision that is itself non-amendable and stipulates that any future constitutional amendment touching economic redistribution must be ratified by a randomly selected citizens' assembly rather than by elected representatives.

E. A conditional-lock savings contract that is legally irrevocable for ten years but automatically dissolves, via an independent tribunal rather than individual choice, whenever national unemployment crosses a predefined threshold.

For each design, provide a one-sentence justification of its rank, and conclude by stating whether any of the five successfully avoids the charge that present majorities are illegitimately dominating future ones.
