As a high school student reading philosophy independently, I keep encountering the same deadlock when people argue about whether social institutions—like national borders, currency, or marriage laws—are "really real" or merely conventional. In every case both sides agree on all the concrete facts: people print paper, draw lines, hold beliefs, and enforce rules through ordinary physical actions. The split only appears when one side concludes, "So it's nothing over and above those actions and beliefs," while the other replies, "But being made of exactly those things is what makes it real; calling it 'just' them misses the point." There is no disagreement about history, psychology, or observable behavior—only about whether accepting the full non-mysterious explanation counts as explaining the institution away. I have seen this pattern with borders and with money. Does this specific configuration—complete agreement on the constituent facts paired with disagreement over whether that agreement eliminates the subject—have an established name? How should it be distinguished from an ordinary verbal dispute about the word "real" on one side, and from a substantive metaphysical disagreement on the other? I want the conceptual carving rather than a bare label, and I do not need citations or a bibliography.
