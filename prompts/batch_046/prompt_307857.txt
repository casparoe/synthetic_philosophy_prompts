I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or history. I want to run a 90-minute workshop for about 15 technically-minded colleagues on pseudohistory and demarcation—specifically using Atlantis narratives, ancient-astronaut claims, and Fomenko’s New Chronology as reference points—so that the group feels firsthand why there is no clean algorithm separating legitimate historical revision from pseudohistory. Please design a runnable in-class activity or game for this setting.

The core idea participants should encounter through action, not lecture, is that formal demarcation criteria (falsifiability, predictive power, peer review, source transparency) can be satisfied in form by pseudohistorical arguments, and that applying them mechanically with a checklist produces false positives and false negatives.

Structure the activity as follows:
- Divide the 15 participants into three groups of five. Give each group one chronology to defend or reject using only a shared “Demarcation Protocol” checklist you provide: (1) a mainstream textbook account of a specific dynasty; (2) Fomenko’s statistical-duplication thesis about compressed dynastic timelines; and (3) a synthetic “compressed-linguistic-drift” chronology I have constructed for this exercise, which claims that phonetic divergence models allow collapsing two centuries into one. The synthetic case should be new and not a real published theory, so the exercise tests the checklist rather than prior belief.
- Rules and incentives: Groups have 25 minutes to apply the checklist and prepare a 3-minute arbitration brief. They may not invoke authority, popularity, or “common sense”; they must cite only checklist items. A rotating “review board” of three participants (one from each group) then hears the briefs and votes to accept or reject each chronology using only the checklist scores. The board receives no external feedback.
- Timeline: 10 minutes for setup and checklist review; 25 minutes for group work; 15 minutes for arbitration briefs and board voting; 30 minutes for debrief; 10 minutes buffer.
- Debrief: Map moments in the activity onto parts of the demarcation problem—e.g., the moment a group realizes Fomenko’s argument uses real statistical methods, the moment the synthetic case scores well on predictive-power items despite being fabricated, and the board’s inevitable split vote. Note what will predictably go wrong the first time (engineers will over-trust the checklist’s formal completeness and treat demarcation as a classification problem rather than a contextual judgment). Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the social, ideological, and motivational dynamics that cause people to adopt pseudohistorical beliefs despite failing institutional checks.
- Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources.
- Respond in no more than 400 words. Lead with the bottom line in one sentence stating the core mechanism (e.g., “forced arbitration using an inadequate formal checklist”). Then present rules/timeline, debrief with failure predictions, and the honest limitation, in that order.
