You have been invited to deliver the opening address at the annual convening of the Meridian Allocation Trust, a philanthropic body that simultaneously funds direct basic-income pilots in low-income neighborhoods and a long-term, high-cost institutional commitment—specifically, a thirty-year community trauma-repair initiative that absorbs nearly half the Trust's annual disbursement. The audience is deliberately mixed: about half are researchers in welfare economics and applied ethics who are comfortable arguing that resources should flow to wherever the expected social return is maximized, even if that means extreme concentration in a single program; the other half are local organizers and grant administrators who regard any defense of 'utility monsters'—whether hypothetical creatures or institutional analogues—as a moral alibi for abandoning dispersed, immediate aid. Your task is to write a fifteen-minute speech that uses a modified, structural version of the utility-monster problem to do real philosophical work. Rather than the classic case of a creature that derives extraordinary welfare from each unit of resources, construct and defend what you call the 'reverse utility monster': a scenario in which one recipient's need is so deep and structurally embedded—requiring sustained, exclusive commitment over decades—that distributing the same pool broadly produces near-zero durable welfare for anyone, including the dispersed recipients, because the resources fall below the threshold needed to achieve meaningful change in any individual case. The speech should not resolve whether the Trust ought to favor concentration or dispersion; instead, it should demonstrate to both factions that their standard objections miss the structural feature that makes the dual mandate philosophically coherent rather than hypocritical. Write for delivery without notes: avoid technical terms such as 'marginal utility' or 'expected value,' but do not dilute the conceptual point. The address must engage philosophers in the room without patronizing the organizers, and vice versa, and it should close by framing the symposium's theme—'Concentration versus Dispersion'—as a question about structural thresholds rather than about compassion versus calculation.
