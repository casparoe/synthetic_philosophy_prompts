I've been thinking about David Lewis's old essay on penal substitution and I keep comeing back to these cases. First, a devoted innocent friend volunteers to take prison for a justly convicted offender — I think that's noble but it doesn't actually satisfy justice, teh offender still owes the debt. Second, Christian believers accept that Jesus bears punishment for sinners — that feels okay, not like a miscarriage. Third, a rich guy pays a poor guy's fine to avoid jail — that feels corrupt, like buying justice. Fourth, a parent offers to do community service for their kid's vandalism — sweet, but I don't think it wipes teh slate. Fifth, a country legalizes paid substitute punishment and people start renting out bodies for prison terms — that seems monstrous, almost like slavery.

The hard part is that case one and case two look structurally identical: innocent person takes guilty person's punishment. Yet I want to say one's wrong and one's okay. Is there one principle that saves both verdicts without being totally ad hoc? Maybe something about institutional authorization, or about whether teh substitute is also teh moral representative? I also want to know about a case I haven't listed: what if teh offender and volunteer are identical twins with shared memories and neither knows which one actually did it? Would that change anything?

If no single principle works, which verdict should I drop? And please say what your best principle predicts for teh twin case.
