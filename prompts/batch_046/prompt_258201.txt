You are a philosopher of language specializing in conceptual dynamics and microdomain semantics. Before constructing anything, briefly brainstorm three realistic, small-scale practice communities where a technical term carries unstated but precise criteria, then select one. Your task is to design a paired thought experiment isolating conceptual slippage in that community.

Domain: copycat and fluid concepts—conceptual slippage in a microdomain.

Build two parallel, realistic scenarios (each no more than one paragraph; no science fiction, no demons, no futuristic tech) within the same specialized craft: antique clock restorers evaluating whether a brass case shows “living wear,” an unstated term of art for surface change that respects original manufacturing intent.

Case A (fluid concept): Over three generations, the guild’s unstated criteria for “living wear” have gradually shifted—current masters apply it to cases earlier masters would reject—yet practitioners experience the change only as refined discernment, not as conceptual drift.

Case B (copycat concept): A later, online-trained subgroup deliberately adopts “living wear” to describe artificially induced oxidation that mimics age-related change, using the same word with confidence but applying it to opposite conditions.

For each case, provide:
1. The original precise criteria for “living wear” (specific material/structural markers).
2. The mechanism of slippage—calibration drift versus intentional mimicry.
3. The check: state the belief/term, the method of application, the nearby comparison worlds or cases that expose the divergence, and why this is conceptual slippage rather than ambiguity, error, or ordinary disagreement.
4. The isolating feature: identify exactly what is held constant across A and B and what single variable changes.
5. For each case, the verdict a fluid-concept theorist, a copycat-concept theorist, and a conceptual-stability defender (who denies both phenomena without explicit redefinition) would give, with brief reasoning.
6. An honest note on how the stability defender would attack each case, and what feature of your design resists the attack—plus what happens if that feature is removed.

Keep the language formal, avoid bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke obscure named theories unless essential. Finish by stating what the pair predicts about a further, unlisted borderline case in the same domain.
