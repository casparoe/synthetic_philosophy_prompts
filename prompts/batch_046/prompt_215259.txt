I'm a philosophy PhD student working on value theory and I've gotten myself twisted up in an argument about tranquilism that feels like it proves way too much. Here is the chain I'm stuck on:

(1) Tranquilism holds that only the occurrent absense of craving and suffering has intrinsic value; pleasure, love, or achievement carry exactly none.

(2) Any conscious being who is awake necessarily maintains occurrent motivational states—breathing, equilibrium, the persistence of awareness—even when those states are fully satisfied and cause no discomfort.

(3) So every moment of waking life contains at least trace negative value, while deep sleep or certain meditative states contain pure absense and thus maximal value.

(4) Non-existence is just an eternal, risk-free version of that pure absense, with no chance of waking into craving.

(5) Therefore replacing any being's future with non-existence maximizes value over time and eliminates all risk of suffering.

(6) By the same token, creating any new conscious being introduces inevitable negative-value moments, with no compensating positive value since tranquilism denies that pleasure matters.

(7) So we ought to peacefully end all current conscious life and prevent any future births.

(8) Obviously that's monstrous, but I can't find the exit.

I'd really like to hang onto (1) if I can—it's the core of the view I'm defending—but (2) and (4) feel like they're sneaking something in. Where exactly does this slip? Which premise do I reject, and what does giving it up cost? If you think the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is least painful to drop.
