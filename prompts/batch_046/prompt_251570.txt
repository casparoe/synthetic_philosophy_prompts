I am preparing a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion, and we are working through the closing propositions of Wittgenstein’s Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus—particularly the sequence that moves from the claim that the sense of the world must lie outside it, through the remark that God does not reveal himself within the world, to the concluding observation that what is mystical does not consist in how the world is but in the fact that it is, and that this shows itself rather than being said. I want my students to understand not merely what Wittgenstein denies—that religious or ethical statements are not factual propositions about empirical states of affairs—but what, if anything, he allows religious belief positively to be. The conceptual difficulty I keep encountering is this: if religious belief is entirely removed from the domain of what can be said, if it concerns only what can be shown, then in what sense can it remain a belief rather than collapse into a silent posture, a grammatical confusion, or a merely subjective preference?

I am especially interested in whether there is a coherent middle path that avoids both standard non-cognitivism—which often treats religious language as merely expressive—and a hidden mystical metaphysics that Wittgenstein seems explicitly to reject. On this middle path, religious belief might involve genuine doxastic commitment—actually holding certain things about the structure of reality to be the case—without being empirically or logically assertible, and its rational assessment might depend not on evidential support but on the coherence and depth of the form of life it expresses. Yet I am unsure how to read the Tractatus passage in a way that grants religious commitment this kind of structural importance without violating the transcendental framing that places all value outside the world. Could you help me understand the conceptual architecture that would allow a reader who accepts the limits of language to still make sense of religious belief as something more than a grammatical mistake? I am particularly keen to anticipate the misconceptions my students are likely to form—perhaps that Wittgenstein is simply dismissing religion as nonsense, or conversely that he is secretly endorsing an ineffable metaphysical reality—so that I can address them directly in our discussion. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of interpretations; rather, I want a careful analysis of how saying and showing interact in this context, and whether religious belief can be understood as a rational, non-propositional framework rather than either a false theory or a mere emotional attitude.
