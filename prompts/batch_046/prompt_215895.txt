I am a high school senior presenting my independent-study thesis on tranquilism—the claim that only the absence of craving and of suffering possesses non-instrumental value, while every other apparent good, including virtue, knowledge, creative achievement, and intimate relationships, is valuable solely as an instrument for reducing craving—to my school’s interdisciplinary humanities faculty review panel next Thursday. The panel consists of Dr. Elena Vasquez, my ethics teacher and a committed Aristotelian who holds that eudaimonia requires the actualization of rational capacities in meaningful activity and that a life of mere tranquility without such activity is closer to dormancy than to human flourishing; Mr. James Okonkwo, my world-religions teacher, who is deeply familiar with Buddhist accounts of tanha and dukkha and with Stoic practices of apatheia, and who is sympathetic to the idea that craving is the root of suffering but worries that tranquilism collapses into a quietist nihilism that undermines the moral urgency of compassion; and Dr. Sarah Lin, my psychology teacher, who works within the positive-psychology tradition and accepts that the reduction of suffering is important but insists that flourishing requires the presence of positive capacities—autonomy, mastery, and relatedness—rather than the mere absence of negative states.

My thesis introduces a distinction I have not found in standard treatments: I separate first-order craving, which is desire directed at particular objects or outcomes, from meta-craving, which is the second-order desire to preserve, intensify, or justify one’s own first-order desiring structure. I argue that conventional ascetic practices typically suppress first-order craving while leaving meta-craving intact—hence the well-documented phenomenon of spiritual pride—so that genuine tranquility requires the suppression of meta-craving as well, and that once both are absent there is literally no remaining intrinsic reason to pursue any further state or activity. The panel has read my fifteen-page paper, which develops the argument from first principles using structured thought experiments about an agent who gradually eliminates all cravings and then examines whether any non-instrumental reason to act survives, without relying on citations to contemporary analytic literature on well-being.

Please prepare me for the objections these three will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they are likely to be and phrased in the vocabulary each panelist would use—Aristotelian activity-language for Dr. Vasquez, Buddhist/Stoic conceptual vocabulary for Mr. Okonkwo, and flourishing-capacity language for Dr. Lin. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold firmly, and where my reply is weakest so that I can defend that point rather than hope they miss it. Be sure to include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks—the one that targets my distinction between first-order and meta-craving by asking whether suppressing meta-craving is itself just another first-order craving for a particular mental state—and provide the concise two-minute reply I must deliver if it comes. End with a line beginning Verdict: that identifies the single most dangerous objection and the one reply that absolutely has to land for the thesis to survive.
