I am working on a novel whose protagonist is a curator at a small maritime museum that has just discovered its founding endowment derived from slave-trading profits, and in the public forums she attends—city council hearings, academic panels on institutional ethics, and tense family dinners across generations—I keep noticing the same rhetorical oscillation. When an interlocutor argues that the present-day community bears a kind of enduring moral stain for that origin, critics immediately object that guilt is not heritable and that the actual perpetrators are dead. At that point the speaker usually retreats to a narrower register: not personal guilt, but a collective, institutional responsibility to repair, commemorate, and restructure. Once the objection is neutralized, however, the speaker resumes talking as though the stronger, stain-like guilt has been vindicated—citing the very duty of repair as proof that the city remains “guilty” in the deeper, metaphysical sense. The two registers—one an inherited moral atmosphere, the other an administrative duty—are treated as interchangeable once the pressure has passed, yet they are never explicitly reconciled, and I have seen the same pattern in debates about reparations, in post-colonial museum ethics, and in arguments within families that benefited from expropriation.

This is not quite the familiar motte-and-bailey structure, because the stronger claim is not an indefensible extension of the weaker one; they are genuinely different ontologies of blame, each with its own defenders and critics. Nor is it mere equivocation, since the speaker is not simply sliding between meanings of a word but actively using the acceptance of the institutional claim to license the metaphysical claim in subsequent sentences.
