There's this unit they run in a lot of UK secondary schools — a 'moral biography' programme where students pick one historical figure, read a condensed life, and then write an essay matching three of the figure's decisions to virtues like courage or temperance. What's odd is that the teaching guides always tell staff to skip any episode where the person acted out of self-interest or contradicted themselves; if you're doing a Plutarch-style parallel comparison, you only keep the bits that line up neatly. The marking rubric even rewards finding 'consistent moral character' across the whole life.

What philosophical commitments does that practice presuppose about learning from lives? Does it need teh idea that biography teaches virtue by presenting an integrated self, not just a string of acts? Is it assuming a kind of hagiographic filter — that moral education requires suppressing contradiction? Or could the same classroom exercise work if you believed people are basically inconsistent and we learn mainly from seeing them fail and recover? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually needs it or is just comfortably compatible with several views, and tell me what the unit would have to change if we replaced the underlying view with one where moral learning comes chiefly from mess and repair.
