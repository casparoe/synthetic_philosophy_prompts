I’m a journalist who covers neuroscience, religion, and identity for a magazine that runs long profiles. Lately we’ve hit a cluster of stories where a subject comes back from coma, deep brain stim, or a radical religious conversion and seems like a different person—new values, missing memories, but same body. My editor wants me to decide in about 20 minutes per feature whether we treat them as the same person for bylines, photo captions, and corrections. I was talking with a friend who studies buddhism and she threw anatta at me: no fixed self, just the five agregates—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness—held together by convention. I want to turn that into an actual editorial procedure my copy desk can run, not just philosophy talk.

So please build me a reusable checklist—call it an aggregate-continuity audit—that I can apply without being a monk. It needs to ask questions in order about each agregate: is there physical continuity? Are feelings and perceptions recognizably linked? Do mental formations (values, intentions, habits) show a bridge or a break? Where consciousness reports a seamless stream versus a rupture? For each step, tell me what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure stops with "same person—update the profile," where it stops with "different person—new byline," and where it must hand the decision to my editor because it’s a values judgment, not a fact. Be honest about where it misfires: partial memory but total value shift, same brain but claimed "rebirth," etc. I need it practical—no citations, no reading lists, I’m on a train with spotty wifi and my editor has to sign off.

Then run it on three cases. First, a coma patient who wakes after ten years with no autobiographical memory but recognizes her sister and feels "like a new person." Second, a writer who after conversion disavows every old essay and asks us to retract her prior byline. Third, I want one where the procedure says "same person" but the answer feels wrong to me—maybe the agregates mostly line up except for one massive narrative break that makes calling her the same person seem like a betrayal. Finally, tell me exactly what this audit can’t do—like resolving legal identity or spiritual status—and flag the cases where an editor just has to decide. I misspelled agregate twice in my notes already, so I’m clearly not the expert here.
