I've been working through an argument about imprecise probabilities that worries me because its conclusion seems to dissolve the framework entirely. Here are the steps:

(1) If available evidence underdetermines a unique precise probability, rational agents ought to hold imprecise credences—sets of probabilities rather than single values.

(2) Given imprecise credences, the expected utility of any act in a decision problem becomes an interval rather than a point.

(3) A rational agent should select an act only if it interval-dominates every rival—that is, only if its worst-case expectation exceeds every alternative's best-case expectation.

(4) In realistic medical, moral, and financial choices, these utility intervals overlap, so interval dominance almost never isolates a unique act.

(5) Hence genuinely imprecise credences make rational choice impossible; the agent is permanently paralysed.

(6) But rational agents clearly do choose.

(7) Therefore premise (3) is false, and we must supplement imprecision with a tie-breaking rule that picks one precise point inside each interval.

(8) Once such a rule operates, the agent behaves identically to one who held precise credences equal to those selected points.

(9) So imprecise probabilities are either paralysing or representationally redundant, and should be abandoned for precise probabilities with explicit ambiguity aversion.

I want to keep (1) and (6) if I can, and I have a suspicion that (8) commits something like the representationalist's warning that behavioural equivalence does not entail structural redundancy—though I am not sure that is the right diagnosis. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
