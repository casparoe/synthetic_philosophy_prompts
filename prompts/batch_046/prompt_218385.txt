I've spent the last several months developing a variable-font system in which letterforms are not defined by fixed outline coordinates but by continuous deformation fields governed by writing-pressure dynamics and stroke-sequence topology. Working through edge cases—severely distorted but legible handwritten glyphs, hybrid forms that share geometric properties with two different letters, and instances where weight and slant vary radically—has left me dissatisfied with both the feature-invariant and the mental-prototype accounts of letter recognition. Please argue that what makes every instance an 'a' is not an intrinsic geometric core shared across tokens, nor statistical resemblance to a canonical example, but rather the preservation of a specific relational structure: the letter's capacity to be transformed continuously into any other valid instance within its class without crossing a recognizability boundary that would cause it to be parsed as a different letter or as non-letter noise. Your argument should explain why this relational or topological conception handles hybrid and distorted cases more naturally than intrinsic-feature or prototype theories, and it should draw out the implication for the concept of style—namely, that style is not a decorative overlay applied to an essential form but rather the particular trajectory through deformation space that a given instance traces. I am not asking for a software specification or for references to particular papers; I want the conceptual reasoning developed clearly, with attention to what counts as a non-negotiable constraint on any adequate theory of letterform identity and what counts as a negotiable preference of particular design traditions.
