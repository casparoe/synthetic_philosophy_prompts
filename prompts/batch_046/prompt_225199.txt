In a philosophy-of-mind working group, a participant repeatedly invokes Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment to block questions about whether bodily sensation is necessary for self-awareness, offering the one-liner "the self is present without the body" as if it ends the discussion. I want to take this to the group as a disambiguation exercise. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing — for instance, (i) that there exists at least one actual or possible mode of self-awareness that occurs without concurrent sensory input; (ii) that the self is a bare particular whose identity conditions are wholly non-corporeal; (iii) that personal identity over time is metaphysically independent of bodily continuity; and (iv) any further radical or deflationary readings you can distinguish. For each, say whether it is trivial, defensible within Avicenna’s own framework, false on conceptual or empirical grounds, or unsupported by the structure of the flying-man argument itself. Then identify which of these readings, if any, actually undermine the claim that self-awareness is constituted by sensorimotor engagement rather than merely compatible with its absence — that is, which readings do the argumentative work the slogan was being made to do in our session, and which are red herrings. Finally, distinguish the interpretations that treat the flying man as establishing a metaphysical possibility about disembodied souls from those that treat it as a phenomenological point about the immediacy of first-person access, and flag any reading that requires attributing to Avicenna a specific doctrine about the "thinness" or non-relationality of the self that the argument does not clearly license.
