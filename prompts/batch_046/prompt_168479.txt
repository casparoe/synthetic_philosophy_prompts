I am a novelist writing counterfactual historical fiction, and under my publisher's contract I must justify every deviation from documented fact through a historical integrity review that I conduct myself, evaluating three to five proposed alterations per chapter with no research staff and usually within half an hour, after which my editor signs off; the stakes are contractual breach and the erosion of reader trust, yet I possess no formal training in philosophy of science. I want you to turn Karl Popper's theory of verisimilitude—specifically the idea that a false account can lie closer to the truth than another false account by retaining more true consequences and fewer false ones—into a practical, reusable decision procedure I can apply repeatedly, while being honest about the well-known logical obstacle that formal comparison of false theories for truth-content tends to collapse once contradictory entailments enter the frame, so that the procedure must rely on an operational proxy rather than on pure logical measurement. The deliverable should be a step-by-step protocol expressed entirely in continuous prose: first, identify the documented causal and explanatory structures that the proposed alteration touches; second, classify whether the deviation introduces direct contradiction, indirect divergence, or structural replacement of a mechanism; third, judge whether the fictional scenario retains enough of the original explanatory architecture to account for the true historical outcomes that remain unchanged elsewhere in the narrative; fourth, apply a threshold permitting the alteration only when retained explanatory coherence clearly outweighs introduced contradiction, with explicit flags marking which steps are mechanical checks and which remain unavoidable judgment calls—particularly the weighing of narrative necessity against explanatory fidelity. The procedure must specify where it stops with an unambiguous refusal, such as when an alteration eliminates a necessary cause without substituting an equivalent mechanism, and where it must defer to a personal value choice rather than to fact, such as when character psychology conflicts with plot demands. Please apply this protocol to three proposed alterations in my current manuscript: changing the documented storm pattern that enabled a particular naval engagement in 1588, inventing a precision measuring instrument that was never actually constructed but rests squarely on scientific principles available at the time, and altering a diplomat's documented refusal to sign an accord so that he signs, which serves my dramatic structure but conflicts with his established psychological profile. I expect the second case to fail the procedure in a way I will find unwelcome, and I want you to show that the third case lands in a zone the procedure cannot settle because it involves a value judgment about consistency of character versus thematic necessity. Finally, state clearly what this procedure is unable to do—whether it can assess aesthetic merit, predict audience response, or resolve conflicts between historical fidelity and emotional resonance—and present the entire response in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written as a single formal letter.
