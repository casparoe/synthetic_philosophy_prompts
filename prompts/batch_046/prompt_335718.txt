As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at the closing dinner of my company's annual retreat on algorithmic design and user autonomy. The audience is divided evenly between machine-learning engineers who build recommendation systems and family members of staff with no technical background. I want a fifteen-minute address that uses Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor — specifically the claim that people will willingly surrender freedom in exchange for comfort, security, and relief from the burden of choice — as the actual structural backbone of the speech, not as decorative literary reference. Please organize the talk around the three temptations traditionally associated with the Inquisitor's argument — miracle, mystery, and authority — but translate each into a concrete design tension: predictive personalization that removes the need for discovery, opaque ranking logic that spares users from understanding, and default architectures that make opting out more difficult than opting in. Because this framing should do real work, the remarks must avoid both romanticizing total user autonomy and celebrating frictionless engagement as pure benevolence. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to secondary scholarship; the language should land for both engineers and non-technical guests without assuming anyone has read the source novel. Keep the tone formal, precise, and accessible.
