Rather than assessing it as an empirical prediction, I want to treat it as a secularized providential framework: not a forecast about the future, but a normative lens that governs which events we treat as historically significant.

The draft essay I am preparing argues that modern institutions of memory—museum curation, textbook committees, and commemorative planning—do not simply document progress but actively construct it. By systematically preserving artifacts of successful reform movements while allowing records of failed strikes, reversed legal gains, and suppressed dissent to decay or remain uncatalogued, these practices create an epistemic environment in which counter-evidence to the “arc” thesis is literally missing. I compare this institutional filter to the providential historiography of early modern chroniclers, who read political outcomes as signs of divine intention, except that the modern version substitutes an implicit teleology of justice for explicit theological commitment. My central worry is whether I am conflating an epistemic selection effect—we do not see failures because our archives hide them—with an ontological claim that history genuinely tends toward justice. I also fear the comparison to providence may be strained, since contemporary curators rarely hold explicit theological beliefs, and I am unsure whether pointing to missing documents is enough to undermine a metaphysical conviction.
