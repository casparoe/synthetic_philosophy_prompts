At a meeting of a clinical-trial design committee, two members—Alex and Blake—disagreed about whether the committee should always collect an optional biomarker screening dataset, available at no direct cost, before finalizing the patient-allocation protocol. Their exchange proceeded as follows.

(1) Alex argued that what decision theorists call Good's result applies: if information can be acquired without cost and beliefs are updated by Bayes' rule while preferences remain stable, then observing it can never reduce expected utility. Therefore, categorically refusing the screening is irrational.

(2) Blake replied that the result presupposes preference stability, but in this case learning the biomarker profile might shift the committee's values—say, from pure efficacy to equity concerns—so the utility function ex post is not the one that governed the ex ante calculation.

(3) Alex conceded that values might shift, but insisted that any anticipated shift should be incorporated into the current evaluation: if you foresee that knowing X will diminish your concern for Y, that anticipation belongs in your present utility calculus, and Good's reasoning still holds from that unified standpoint.

(4) Blake then objected that the screening is not genuinely costless: it delays patient enrollment, and the welfare loss from delay must be priced into the outcome. Once that cost is included, the information is no longer free in the sense required by the theorem, rendering the result inapplicable rather than violated.

(5) Alex responded that pricing delay into outcomes is precisely what rational evaluation demands. The theorem does not require that information have zero price in every dimension; it requires that the choice to observe be available without sacrificing other options. Here, the committee can still observe and then decide; the fact that observation carries a priced-in downside means only that the net expected gain might be negative, not that refusing observation is justified by principle.

(6) Blake's final move was structural: the committee is not a unitary agent with a single prior but a collective with heterogeneous beliefs and weights. Compelling all members to observe the same biomarker data could polarize beliefs—causing some members to update strongly in opposite directions—thereby reducing the group's ex ante expected welfare even if each individual, taken in isolation, would prefer to see the data. Applying Good's result to the group therefore commits a category mistake.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly correct with brief justification for each rating. Then state who is more right overall and identify what important consideration both sides have overlooked. Please answer in American English, and end with a single line in exactly this form: Verdict: [a concise summary of your overall assessment].
