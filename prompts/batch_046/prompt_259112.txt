In a seminar on moral partiality, a speaker dismissed the suggestion that parents should weigh funding their child’s education against donating the same sum to global health initiatives by invoking the phrase one thought too many, in the manner Bernard Williams made familiar. I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature survey; I want you to treat that phrase as a slogan that can conceal several distinct philosophical claims. Lay out, as a menu, the precise theses it could be expressing—such as a psychological claim about calculative reasoning corrupting intimate motivation, a normative claim about ground projects supplying exclusionary reasons that block comparative weighing altogether, a structural claim about the incommensurability of personal integrity with impartial maximization, and any other defensible reconstruction you can formulate. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and indicate which ones actually support dismissing the charitable comparison rather than merely describing emotional resistance. Then take a committed position: choose the reconstruction you think is strongest, apply it directly to a hard case in which a physician must choose between saving her own child and saving five strangers with marginally better survival odds, and argue whether her refusal to compute that comparison is genuinely required by integrity or only psychologically understandable. Do not stay neutral among the readings—defend your choice with reasons.
