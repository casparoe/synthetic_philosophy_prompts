I was arguing with a friend late last night—she is in my political theory seminar—about whether conscription in permanently neutral or non-aligned states is genuinely a democratic institution or rather a sophisticated mechanism for normalizing state intrusion into private life. We kept circling back to the same concrete institutional arrangements: Switzerland’s constitutional militia principle, which prohibits a standing army and obliges male citizens to serve in reserve units that train alongside their civilian professions, with service rifles traditionally kept in private residences and ammunition regulations handled separately; Finland’s universal male conscription, which uses psychological and cognitive assessments not merely to filter recruits but to assign draftees to different service tracks—ranging from roughly six months to nearly a year—sorting them into leadership, specialized, or basic roles regardless of personal preference; and Sweden’s recent return to selective conscription, open to all genders, where candidates are screened for physical fitness and psychological suitability, but also explicitly evaluated on motivation, with only those who score well across these dimensions being called up. My friend insisted that these practices presuppose nothing deeper than a fair distribution of collective risk in societies that reject permanent militarization. I found that difficult to square with the granular classification of citizens and the material presence of state-issued weapons in domestic space.

So I want to press the philosophical question directly: what cluster of commitments about citizenship, autonomy, and political obligation do these specific design choices actually presuppose? Must one accept a civic-republican view that treats sovereignty as shared and therefore demands continuous, embodied readiness from every citizen—rather than a liberal-contractual view that treats military service as an exceptional, explicitly consented burden? Does the Finnish and Swedish practice of sorting draftees by aptitude and motivation presuppose that the state possesses legitimate authority to read, rank, and direct citizens’ inner dispositions for collective ends, or can that be explained away as mere administrative efficiency compatible with liberal neutrality? And does the Swiss integration of reserve duty with civilian professional life, combined with home storage of equipment and periodic refresher obligations embedded in ordinary calendars, quietly presuppose that the citizen is never fully a private individual but always a potential instrument of collective defense—thereby ruling out strong versions of liberal individualism that deny pre-political duties of readiness? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined: could the same institutional architecture be justified on purely consequentialist or egalitarian grounds without any deep theory of citizenship?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory—or theories—of the person-state relation that make these arrangements hang together. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and tell me whether a genuinely liberal-individualist framework, which rejects the idea that citizens have inherent obligations of martial readiness, could accommodate universal or selective conscription, aptitude-based sorting, and the domestic storage of service weapons without contradiction. If not, specify which institutional features would have to change—would selection have to become a random lottery, would home storage have to be eliminated, would service have to be restricted to explicit volunteers—should the underlying philosophical commitment be replaced. I am not asking for a bibliography, a legal history, or a survey of Nordic defense policy; I want the conceptual anatomy of the practice itself.
