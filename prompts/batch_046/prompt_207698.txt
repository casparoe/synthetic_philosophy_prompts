Question: Daniel Dennett argues that real patterns are those regularities that support both predictive success and data compression, and he distinguishes the physical, design, and intentional stances we may adopt toward a system. First, explain why predictive success alone is insufficient for a pattern to count as real in Dennett’s sense, specifying what additional feature—related to projectibility or counterfactual stability—is required. Second, distinguish the intentional stance from the design stance, not merely in terms of system complexity but in terms of the kind of coherence each stance tracks: functional decomposition versus rational coherence among attributed states. Third, a critic proposes that beliefs and desires, on Dennett’s account, are ontologically analogous to a center of gravity—both are abstract patterns lacking discrete physical locations, yet both are causally inert fictions useful only for calculation. Evaluate this analogy, paying particular attention to whether intentional-stance patterns possess cross-stance counterfactual robustness or whether they dissolve into lower-level descriptions once internal architecture is examined.

Student Answer: Dennett holds that real patterns are regularities allowing accurate prediction together with efficient data compression; when behavior can be encoded more concisely by appealing to a higher-level regularity than by tracking physical microstates, that regularity is ontologically genuine. This applies across the physical stance (particle dynamics), the design stance (functional parts and purposes), and the intentional stance (beliefs and desires).

The intentional stance differs from the design stance in its explanatory logic rather than merely in target complexity. The design stance decomposes a system into components serving a function—gears, algorithms, or evolved organs. The intentional stance, by contrast, attributes propositional attitudes and evaluates behavior for rational coherence; it asks what an agent ought to do given its beliefs and goals. Crucially, the intentional stance presupposes that the system’s architecture has been shaped by engineering or evolution to approximate rational information processing, so it cannot be adopted independently of prior design-stance analysis.

The center-of-gravity analogy is illuminating. Like a center of gravity, a belief lacks discrete physical parts and specific neural location, yet Dennett treats both as real patterns rather than fictions. The analogy is often misused, however, to claim that beliefs are causally inert devices. In fact, intentional patterns support rich counterfactuals—if the agent had desired X rather than Y, it would have acted differently—giving them cross-stance robustness comparable to physical patterns. Nevertheless, because they lack discrete physical instantiation, they dissolve into design-stance descriptions whenever we examine internal architecture closely; their predictive utility is therefore confined to the macroscopic level and does not survive a shift to lower stances. Thus they are real but ontologically dependent abstractions, possessing no causal powers beyond those already present in the underlying dynamics.
