I'm enrolled in an upper-level philosophy seminar—my school permits cross-registration for advanced coursework—where we've spent several weeks on classical hedonism and its modern variants. During a recent session on the heterogeneity problem, my instructor raised the familiar challenge that pleasures seem radically diverse: the satisfaction of solving a proof, the taste of something sweet, and the relief of pain cessation share little that is introspectively obvious. This led to an extended debate among my classmates about whether the disagreement between quantitative hedonists and defenders of qualitative distinctions (in the vein of Mill's well-known argument) has genuinely stalled, or whether the literature has been recycling the same moves for decades without noticing a deeper fracture. Some peers contend that the dispute is merely verbal—different philosophers using "pleasure" to cover intentional objects, phenomenal tones, and functional rewards—while others insist there is a substantive metaphysical question about whether affective states possess a unified phenomenal core or only a family-resemblance structure anchored by neural reward-circuit activation.

My own working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily about introspective data, but about incompatible explanatory standards: one side demands that a unified account identify a single determinable quality common to all pleasant experiences, while the other accepts cross-modal hedonic comparison only as a calibrated behavioral or neurological similarity. I am trying to determine whether this is actually a hidden disagreement about what counts as an adequate explanation of subjective value, or whether the sides are answering different questions—one about phenomenology, one about cognition—under the single heading of "what pleasures have in common." Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement using the actual positions in play, rather than surveying every historical view? And if the stalemate is substantive, what specific conceptual distinction—perhaps involving phenomenal calibration, intentional mode, or the separation of affective valence from sensory content—might actually move the debate forward, or should we conclude that the question requires reformulation rather than resolution?
