I am a doctoral researcher in philosophy of medicine, and I have been invited to lead a fifty-minute tutorial for final-year medical students on conceptual vagueness in clinical classification, taking the sorites paradox as the central framework. I do not want a direct explanation of the paradox; instead, I need a pedagogical plan. Please specify the dependency order: which conceptual component must be secured before the paradox can be introduced so that it appears as a genuine conceptual tension rather than a linguistic trick. For the carrying example, I wish to avoid the standard cases of baldness or heaps of sand; I propose using the gradual attenuation of integrated neural responsiveness in the determination of brain death. Assess whether this clinical case carries the necessary properties—continuous gradation, an apparently sharp legal and medical boundary, and intuitive resistance to arbitrary cut-off points—or whether it requires adjustment (for instance, by introducing a specific diagnostic scale or by pairing it with a parallel non-medical analogue). Then map the misconceptions these students are likely to form: I anticipate they will treat vagueness as an epistemic limitation of our imaging technology rather than a feature of the category itself, and possibly confuse the sorites with statistical uncertainty about prognosis. Indicate precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and neutralised. Provide one closing diagnostic question that would reveal whether they understand the problem as conceptual rather than merely practical. Finally, state explicitly which formal logical responses—such as supervaluationism or fuzzy degree theory—should be acknowledged but withheld at this level, with a brief justification for each omission. Please present your answer in British English.
