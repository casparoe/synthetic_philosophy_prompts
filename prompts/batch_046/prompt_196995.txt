I have been trying to work out whether reasons can exist without anyone actually reasoning them — what I have heard called free-floating rationales. Below are my verdicts on several concrete cases. I want to know whether there is one principle that delivers all of them, what it predicts for cases I have not listed, and, if the verdicts conflict, which one I should abandon.

Case 1: A bridge collapses because its main cable was severely corroded. The corrosion genuinely explains the collapse, and it seems correct to say there is a reason the bridge fell. Yet no one was reasoning about corrosion at the time; the explanation is purely structural. My verdict: this is a free-floating rationale.

Case 2: Seven is prime because it has no divisors other than one and itself. The because here points to something real, but no mind is performing the explanation. My verdict: this is also free-floating.

Case 3: I ought to return a borrowed book because I promised to return it. Here the explanation seems inseparable from me as an agent who made the commitment and can be held accountable. My verdict: this is not free-floating; the reason requires a reasoner.

Case 4: A particular painting is beautiful because of the precise balance among its colors. The balance is a physical fact about pigment, but beautiful seems to require a viewer who experiences it. My verdict: I am inclined to say this is not free-floating — the rationale depends on a perceiver.

Case 5: During an automated market event, trading algorithms executed sell orders in a cascade that no human monitored in real time. The rules had been designed by engineers, but the immediate explanatory chain involved no live human reasoning. The crash is explained by those algorithmic rules. My verdict: this is free-floating, even though humans wrote the rules originally.

The tension: Case 3 and Case 5 both involve human design in the background — I authored my promise, and engineers authored the algorithms. Yet I judge Case 3 agent-bound and Case 5 free-floating. Is there a principled distinction?

Two further cases for your principle to address: first, the elliptical orbit of a planet, explained by the inverse-square law of gravitation; second, the claim that causing unnecessary suffering is wrong, considered as a moral fact.

Please provide the principle (or a small set of competing principles) that best captures my verdicts, stating precisely which clause applies to each case. If one principle saves Cases 1, 2, and 5 but forces me to overturn either Case 3 or Case 4, say so explicitly and defend the choice. Also predict, with reasoning, whether planetary orbits and moral wrongness come out as free-floating on your account.
