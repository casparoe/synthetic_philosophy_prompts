I am a novelist constructing a narrative set in a moral and cosmological world sharply discontinuous with our own, and in researching the historical substrate I have come to hold a strong presentist thesis: that historians cannot in principle escape the normative and conceptual standards of their own epoch. The failure is not limited to explicit moral judgment; it operates at a deeper structural level—what counts as causation, as an agent, as a system, even as an event worthy of narration, is indexed to present-day ontologies that remain invisible precisely because they are the conditions of thought. Any attempt to recover past actors' reasons on their own terms therefore reproduces those present conditions in the very form of the explanation, and the historian's aspiration to bracket her commitments is itself a symptom of them. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want the arguments, historiographical practices, formal properties of historical explanation, and empirical findings—perhaps regarding cross-cultural conceptual stability or the interpretive autonomy of archival traces—that would genuinely defeat it. Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed defender of this thesis concede as genuinely counting against it, and which would they dismiss as presupposing the very neutrality they deny? Finally, assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent counterexample can be redescribed as another hidden layer of presentist framing—and whether that insulation is a strength or a weakness for a claim about the conditions of historical knowledge.
