Construct a diagnostic decision tree that classifies implicit philosophical positions on first-person self-location in brain-transplant and duplication scenarios, using Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle (the case in which a brain is copied and the original reports being in a different location) as the anchoring framework but extending it with three constructed diagnostic variants designed to force distinctions that standard presentations leave ambiguous: (i) a staggered-backup case in which two cortical copies are activated with a thirty-second delay and the original reports conflicting first-person memories of that interval; (ii) a distributed-body case in which one brain copy controls two robot bodies simultaneously with merged sensory streams and reports being both bodies at once; and (iii) a gradual-substrate case in which neurons are replaced one by one over years but the patient reports intermittent blank spots in autobiographical memory rather than continuous presence.

The tree should have exactly seven decision nodes and eight terminal leaves. Each node must pose a single concrete yes/no or either/or question about what happens in one of the cases above, phrased so that a respondent can answer without knowing any philosophical labels such as psychological continuity, animalism, or narrative identity. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of named theories: start with a root commitment about whether first-person reference tracks the physical brain, the informational pattern, the causal-functional role, or no determinate fact; then branch through answers to the three constructed variants and one standard teletransportation case.

The eight leaves must include, at minimum: (a) a physical-brain-continuity view that permits duplication ambiguity; (b) a psychological-continuity view with a no-branching clause; (c) an embodied/enactive view tying self-location to singular bodily control; (d) a narrative-identity view allowing fragmentation across copies; and (e) a deflationary no-fact-of-the-matter view. The remaining three leaves should occupy the logical spaces that are genuinely available given the branching structure. For every combination of answers that does not map onto one of these eight leaves, include a brief parenthetical diagnosis at the corresponding empty branch: state whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or being unexplored, and identify which two positions the tree fails to separate if any.

The intended audience is advanced undergraduates in philosophy of mind. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for named theories. Do not ask respondents to identify themselves with labels. The deliverable should present the tree in indented branching notation followed by a scoring key that describes each leaf in one sentence stating what the holder of that path believes about where I am in the staggered-backup case. If any pair of well-known positions remains inseparable by this tree, flag them explicitly and explain which diagnostic case would need to be added to split them.
