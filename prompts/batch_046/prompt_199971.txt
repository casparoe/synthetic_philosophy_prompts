I run a philosophy grad workshop, about 13 people, one 80-min session. I want a runnable activity that gets them to actually feel the tension in asking whether destroying habitat lowers wild animal suffering—not just talk about it in the abstract.

Split them into three roles with printed cards: Land Trust (protect the marsh intact), Welfare Accountants (get a hidden scoring sheet that ranks options by "net experienced welfare per hectare per year" using a metric they have to defend on the fly—includes reproductive subsidy and density-dependent mortality), and Stakeholders (local people with non-instrumental attachments to the landscape). One observer takes notes but doesn't vote.

The case: a 300-acre beaver marsh with extremely high vole and frog reproduction, but also massive predation and starvation—basically a replacement-heavy suffering factory. Give them three options: do nothing, drain half the marsh to collapse the breeding habitat, or introduce predator control that doesn't destroy the habitat but lowers predation. They must reach unanimous agreement in 25 minutes or the funding defaults to do-nothing. Only the Accountants see the actual numbers; everyone else just knows there's "a lot of life" there.

After the vote, reveal the hidden ledgers: draining eliminates about 90% of the vole/frog populations and therefore thier suffering; predator control keeps reproduction high but lowers experienced pain; do nothing keeps the cycle running. Then map the arguments onto the real conceptual debate—counterfactual comparison (are we saving lives or preventing lives?), the epistemic gap between quantitative welfare scores and ecological values, and the fact that real ecosystems don't have boards deciding for them.

The debrief should cover: the moment the Accountants tried to use figures everyone else ignored (thats the communication failure in actual debates), the Stakeholders saying "you're treating life like a spreadsheet" (the moral status objection), and an honest note that the game can't reproduce the actual scale of suffering over evolutionary time or the problem of small populations—so I have to supply that part verbally.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong: the Welfare group will dominate because they have concrete numbers, and the others will feel bulldozed. Fix: give Stakeholders and Land Trust one veto card each that forces a 5-minute pause. Make it paper-only, with a printed map of the marsh and role cards—no web access needed. And they shouldn't hear the phrase "wild animal suffering" untill the debrief, if then. There's probably a typo or two in these instructions—leave them in.
