hey — i'm drafting a novel about memory and inheritence, and i built this argument that feels like it proves too much but i can't find the hole. (1) If we're going to identify something as belonging to a kind, there has to be some accessable difference-maker that distinguishes members from non-members; otherwise it's just arbitrary labeling. (2) Now for any object seperated from its earlier stage by enough time or entropy, there's always some information that's not merely hidden but structurally lost. (3) So across those gaps there's no fact of the matter about whether the later thing is still the same kind. (4) Therefore long-range kind-continuity — people, institutions, even my protagonist's family line — is never literally true, just a useful fiction. (5) But then she can't literally be bound by her grandmother's promises, and inheriting debts is ontologically groundless. (6) I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than drop them. Where does the chain break, and what does it cost?
