I am drafting a short essay for a philosophy of social science course about whether culture can genuinely function as a causal factor in historical explanation, using Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis as the central case. The standard philosophical objection is that values are normative and therefore belong to the wrong logical category to cause material outcomes: they prescribe rather than produce. Weber's familiar reply is that religious values enter the causal chain not as physical forces but through human motivation and institutional selection. Yet this reply strikes me as ambiguous between two registers that are rarely separated with precision. In the motivational register, believers internalize inner-worldly ascetic norms and consequently work harder, save more, and reinvest profits. In what I want to call the structural register, the shared ethical framework operates as a causal scaffold: it stabilizes expectations about contract reliability and creditworthiness among merchants who may not personally share the theology, lowering transaction costs and making rational capitalist bookkeeping sustainable at scale. Here culture is not pushing individuals like an efficient psychological cause but holding open a social space within which certain economic practices become stable. I want to argue that Weber's defenders usually collapse these two registers, and that only the structural register genuinely answers the category-mistake objection—though at the cost of weakening Weber's specific claim about Calvinism's irreplaceable contribution, since other shared normative frameworks might scaffold capitalist rationality equally well. The essay therefore concludes that culture can indeed be a cause, but only when causation is understood as scaffold-maintenance rather than efficient motivation.

Please comment on these notes. Specifically, I want your assessment of whether the motivational and structural distinction is philosophically sound, whether the notion of a causal scaffold genuinely illuminates Weber's argument or merely rebrands standard institutional explanations, and whether my conclusion—that saving culture-as-cause undermines Weber's uniqueness claim—actually follows from the premises.
