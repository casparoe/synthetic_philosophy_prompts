I'm a novelist drafting a philosophical novel about counterfactual lives, and I want a consistency check on my working commitments about verisimilitude before I write the central chapters. Here is where I stand: (1) I take verisimilitude to be a form of "counterfactual resilience" — a fictional scenario holds up not because it copies actual events, but because it remains coherent and compelling across unstated variations in background conditions. (2) Even so, I think achieving this resilience in practice requires granular tracking of real-world psychological and social dynamics; pure internal coherence will not do the work on its own. (3) I also believe there is a distinct "emotional verisimilitude" gained precisely by violating those dynamics for symbolic or mythic effect, and that this is not a failure of realism but a separate artistic achievement. (4) I hold that when readers call something "unrealistic," they are almost always reporting a breach of genre convention or learned expectation, not identifying an actual impossibility. (5) Finally, I think an author bears an obligation to avoid "epistemic distortion" — systematically misrepresenting how a particular social world operates — even when such distortion serves artistic ends.

Please audit these five commitments for joint consistency: which pairs pull against each other in a genuine and non-obvious way, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and for each real tension what the least costly revision would be and what it costs. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. In doing so, separate what is settled in the philosophy of fiction and narrative theory from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretation — including my own framing of counterfactual resilience — as if it were the consensus view.
