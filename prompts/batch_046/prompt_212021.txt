As a novelist constructing a fictional metropolis that behaves like an organism—exhibiting economic cycles, collective moods, and institutional intentions that no individual inhabitant possesses—I have become entangled in an argument that seems to dissolve emergence entirely. The reasoning runs as follows: (1) Every macro-property of such a city supervenes on the micro-properties and relations of its citizens and buildings; arranged identically, the city could not differ. (2) If B determines A in every possible configuration, then A is reducible to B without ontological remainder. (3) Therefore the city's apparent emergent properties reduce entirely to individual psychology and architecture. (4) But if reduction is complete, emergence is either redundant—merely naming the parts—or false, claiming a surplus that does not exist. (5) Scientists nonetheless treat emergence as substantive and non-redundant, not merely heuristic. (6) So either supervenience fails across science, or emergence is an illusion sustained by ignorance. I wish to preserve both the reality of emergence in scientific explanation and the metaphysical coherence of collective urban properties, yet the chain appears to force a choice. Which premise or step should be surrendered, what does rejecting it cost for scientific practice and for the ontology of collective entities, and is there a stable intermediate position that a novelist might consistently inhabit?
