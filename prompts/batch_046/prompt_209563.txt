I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and ethics, and I find myself preoccupied by a conceptual puzzle that seems to straddle both fields. In my reading I have encountered the so-called wrong-kind-reasons problem—typically discussed in contexts such as love, moral admiration, or aesthetic appreciation—where certain considerations genuinely support an attitude only if they belong to the appropriate category, while other considerations, though they might causally produce or sustain that attitude, are defective or corrupting precisely because they are of the wrong kind.

Please explain this conceptual issue in detail, but extend it into somewhat less familiar territory. Specifically, I want you to examine whether an analogous distinction arises for the collective acceptance of scientific theories within research institutions. In particular, address the tension between what we might call constitutive epistemic reasons—those that bear directly on explanatory power, predictive accuracy, or evidential coherence—and surrogate institutional reasons, such as grant continuity, laboratory prestige, disciplinary boundary maintenance, or career incentives, which may sustain commitment to a theoretical framework without engaging its cognitive merits. I am not asking for a bibliography or for references to specific scholars; I want a purely conceptual analysis of whether these institutional pressures function like wrong-kind reasons applied to collective doxastic attitudes, or whether the norms governing group belief-formation are sufficiently different that the analogy collapses.

Please organize your response into the following sections, with approximate lengths as indicated:

1. The canonical ethical framing (about 150 words): Clarify the distinction between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons using familiar examples from love or moral admiration, and explain why wrong-kind considerations are thought to be conceptually defective rather than merely non-ideal or practically inconvenient.

2. The analogical translation to scientific practice (about 200 words): Develop the idea that research programs can be sustained by surrogate institutional reasons. Explain whether these operate analogously to wrong-kind reasons for individual belief, or whether collective theory-choice is governed by pragmatic or sociological norms that dissolve the analogy altogether.

3. Stability of the distinction (about 150 words): Evaluate whether the boundary between constitutive and surrogate reasons can be maintained without collapsing into either a purely causal account of belief-formation or a purely normative account that ignores institutional reality. In particular, discuss whether robust instrumental success—such as technological application or predictive reliability—counts as a right-kind reason for theoretical commitment, or whether it risks becoming a sophisticated wrong-kind reason when it substitutes for direct evidential engagement.

Please write in American English, maintain a formal and polished philosophical tone, and avoid providing citations, reading lists, or attributions to particular texts. I am interested in the conceptual architecture itself.
