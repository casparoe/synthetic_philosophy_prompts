I'm on the board of trustees for our city's Museum of Urban History and we have to vote by next Friday on whether to greenlight a $400k permanent gallery called "Hinge Points." The curators want visitors to use touchscreens to tweak specific local decisions—like the 1973 riverfront zoning vote or the 1991 school-board redistricting—and watch simulated counterfactual timelines unfold for the next fifty years. They're explicit that the whole thing runs on the hinge-of-history hypothesis: that history isn't smooth but has discrete "hinge events" where path dependency locks in, and that spotting these hinges teaches citizens where agency actually mattered. To flag the hinges they use a three-filter method—irreversibility, cascade potential, and something they call "non-substitutability," meaning no other nearby event could have played the same branching role. They claim this framing is now standard in public-history pedagogy, though I'm pretty sure that's overstated.

Half the board is excited; half thinks it's philosophically sloppy. The critics say the exhibit treats deep structural forces—racist housing policy, capital flight, federal funding rules—as background noise while spotlighting individual votes, and that the non-substitutability filter is basically circular reasoning dressed up as methodology. There's also the worry that if we present these simulations as authoritative, we're baking in one contested philosophy of history without labeling it. I need a memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, plus a concrete timeline and steps. It should take the curators' framework seriously rather than dismissing it, but also treat the structural-erasure objection as the strongest counter. One small typo is fine if it sounds like a real rushed draft.

Please separate what's settled about historical contingency and path dependency from what's genuinely contested among philosophers of history, and don't present the hinge framework as consensus—flag where you're leaning on a disputed reading. Don't ask for reading lists or citations; I just need reasoning I can defend in the room.
