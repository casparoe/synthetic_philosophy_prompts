Please adopt the role of a senior philosophy of science examiner specializing in evolutionary biology and the philosophy of psychology. Before drafting anything, take a moment to brainstorm the core epistemic tension: what separates a predictive evolutionary psychological hypothesis from a post-hoc accommodationist narrative when both invoke selection pressures in the Pleistocene?

I am a retired field ecologist who spent thirty years studying phenotypic plasticity and canalization in wild populations, and I have recently begun auditing upper-level philosophy courses. I am helping design the final examination for a philosophy of biology seminar. I need one essay question that a well-prepared student could answer in about ninety minutes without consulting external sources or offering bibliographic citations.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard spandrels-style critique of evolutionary psychology. Instead, embed the following specific case study and conceptual framework. Present students with this research program: evolutionary psychologists propose that human preference for facial symmetry is an adaptation for assessing mate quality, citing cross-cultural consistency, rapid neural processing, and correlations between asymmetry and parasitic load. Critics counter that symmetry also tracks early nutritional stress and immune competence during development, so the preference might emerge from domain-general statistical learning calibrated by local ecological conditions, with no dedicated psychological module required. Critics further invoke phenotypic buffering—developmental canalization that suppresses genetic and environmental variance—arguing that because development buffers trait expression, the observable distribution of symmetry preference in modern populations is an unreliable guide to the selection pressures that may or may not have shaped it.

Ask students to evaluate whether this program escapes the charge of just-so storytelling. Require them to address four specific issues:

(i) The distinction between an accommodationist narrative and a predictive adaptationist model—what concrete predictions must the latter generate that the former avoids?
(ii) The necessity of comparative phylogenetic evidence and the implications of its absence for inferences about Pleistocene selection.
(iii) Whether invoking an environment of evolutionary adaptedness avoids circularity when that environment is reconstructed solely from the behavior being explained.
(iv) How phenotypic buffering complicates the inference from current trait distribution to past selection pressure, and whether this undermines the adaptationist claim or merely raises the evidential bar.

Write the question in formal examination language. Then provide a model answer of approximately seven hundred words. Follow this with a marking scheme organized around five must-hit points, allocating marks to each for a total of twenty, and include a one-line note for each point describing the characteristic mistake candidates make. The answer should depend on conceptual reasoning rather than on memorized names, specific publication dates, or direct quotations. Ensure accuracy with respect to evolutionary biology and philosophy of science, but paraphrase any references without using quotation marks.
