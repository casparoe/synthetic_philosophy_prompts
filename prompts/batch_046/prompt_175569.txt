I just finished a long argument with a friend about quantum immortality and quantum suicide, and I want to audit the central analogy he kept pressing. He said the idea is essentially Schrödinger's cat scaled up to a conscious observer: just as the cat is dead and alive inside the box until an external measurement opens it, a person running repeated quantum suicide experiments is dead in some branches and alive in others, and since consciousness can only be present in the branches where survival occurs, subjective experience necessarily tracks only the living ones. From this he concluded that under the Many-Worlds Interpretation an observer has probability-one subjective survival, and that the objection "you die in almost all branches" is confused because there is no observer in those branches to register anything.

I want a precise correspondence audit. Lay out the mapping: what plays the role of the box, the poison mechanism, the cat, the external observer who opens the box, and the branching worlds. Then identify exactly where the analogy breaks—particularly regarding decoherence and whether it eliminates the need for an "opening," the status of the Born rule in weighting branches rather than counting them equally, and whether consciousness can coherently be treated as an observable that collapses or selects branches rather than being distributed across them. Does the analogy quietly import a single-world intuition about one definite outcome that Many-Worlds rejects? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion—that subjective experience must attach only to survival branches—survive, or was the cat picture carrying the entire argument? If there is a better analogy for what an observer's situation actually is across decoherent branches, say what it would be.
