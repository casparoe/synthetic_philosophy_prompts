I retired some years ago from a long career in evolutionary biology and have since begun sitting in on philosophy of mind seminars at a local university. In a recent session on cognitive architectures, a speaker presented what he called Daniel Dennett’s four-tier taxonomy—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—and I reconstructed the account from my notes. Before I present it to my philosophy reading group, I want an accuracy audit, because several points struck me as either biologically naive or philosophically overreaching.

As I understand the summary, Darwinian creatures possess behavioral repertoires fixed entirely by natural selection and do not learn. Skinnerian creatures add individual learning through operant conditioning, yet they do so only by inheriting predisposed behavioral templates that are refined through repetition, never constructing genuine internal representations of rewards or environments. Popperian creatures transcend this by generating explicit internal models of the world and running exhaustive mental simulations of every available action path, thereby selecting optimal behaviors with near-complete foresight and effectively eliminating behavioral risk. Gregorian creatures are then characterized as essentially language-equipped Popperians: they verbalize their internal simulations to share them with others, but all cognitive labor remains strictly brain-bound, with culture, tools, and external artifacts playing no distinctive architectural role. The speaker concluded that, on this framework, only Gregorian beings possess genuine consciousness or moral standing, while Darwinian, Skinnerian, and Popperian organisms are merely sophisticated biological automata devoid of subjective experience.

My own suspicion is that this summary conflates genetic inheritance with individual learning in the Skinnerian case, overstates mental simulation into something omniscient in the Popperian case, and reduces Gregorian cognition to “language plus internal modeling” while ignoring external scaffolding. I am also wondering whether evolutionary niche construction—where organisms actively modify selective environments—would collapse the boundary between Skinnerian conditioning and Gregorian culture, or whether Dennett’s taxonomy can absorb such feedback intact. Could you assess the summary part by part, indicating what is accurate, what is a subtle misrepresentation or overstatement, and whether the speaker’s conclusion about consciousness and moral status actually follows? Please answer in American English, and address each tier separately so that I can revise my notes with precision.
