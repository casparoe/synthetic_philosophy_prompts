I work in a long-term rehabilitation unit that assesses patients after severe neurological trauma or prolonged dissociative episodes, and my colleagues and I have been arguing about whether our current checklists for identity continuity are too Cartesian—treating the self as a hidden substance we either find or lose. I want to operationalize the philosophical view that treats the self not as an inner essence but as a center of narrative gravity: an abstract point around which memories, intentions, bodily actions, and social attributions organize, exerting a kind of pull that keeps experiences orbiting together rather than flying apart. To make this usable, I have been experimenting with three constructed metrics—narrative mass (the density and emotional weight of experiences that orbit the center), narrative velocity (the rate at which new events are incorporated into the ongoing story), and narrative escape velocity (the threshold at which a cluster of experiences gains enough internal coherence to break away and form a competing gravitational well)—but I need a rigorous, reusable decision procedure my team can actually run in a forty-five-minute evaluation session, with clear stopping points, explicit value-judgment flags, and honest admission of where coherence measurement fails. The procedure should ask questions in sequence, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, indicate where it hands off to human judgment rather than delivering a verdict, and apply itself to three cases: a patient with complete retrograde amnesia who retains fluent first-person speech but no autobiographical orbit; a patient presenting alternating first-person accounts with discrete temporal tracks; and a confabulatory patient whose autobiography is richly coherent, emotionally integrated, and entirely false, where the procedure will likely declare a unified center intact despite the fiction. Please present the result as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a detailed letter, and include a brief statement of what the procedure cannot do—particularly its inability to distinguish true narrative coherence from manufactured coherence, and its silence on whether plural gravitational centers should be treated as pathology or variation.
