I am drafting a scene for a philosophical novel in which my protagonist, Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of time who has spent years in quiet archives rather than conference halls, must defend her thesis at a formidable seminar. She argues that Aristotle’s classic sea-battle paradox should be reconstructed not through the familiar denial of bivalence—what every undergraduate now recites—but through what she calls “deliberative causal asymmetry”: the claim that future-contingent propositions lack determinate truth-values not because the future is ontologically open, but because present agents occupy a structurally privileged node in the causal graph, so that the verification conditions for future-tensed claims are themselves responsive to present intervention. Her audience is calibrated to wound her precisely: two eternalists who regard presentism as a category error; a modal logician who will demand a formalization she refuses to provide; a phenomenologist drawn to Husserl’s living present who suspects she is smuggling metaphysics into epistemology; and a decision theorist who will ask whether her framework avoids Dutch-book vulnerability without invoking subjective probability. I need you to prepare her Q&A defense sheet exactly as she would review it the night before, in a study filled with unsorted papers: the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased as that specific interlocutor would phrase it; her two-minute reply to each; the precise point in each reply where she is weakest; what she should concede up front without surrendering her core thesis; and, finally, the one objection she is praying nobody raises—the one that exposes the tension between her causal-graph vocabulary and her refusal to quantify over future states. Write the prep in her voice, formal and unhurried, as if she were speaking to herself about strangers who know her argument better than she wishes they did.
