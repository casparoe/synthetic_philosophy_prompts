In metaphysics of science, the long-running dispute over whether biological taxa—especially species—are natural kinds has reached what looks like a stable stalemate. Essentialists appeal to shared genetic mechanisms or reproductive isolation; homeostatic property cluster theorists point to stable causal mechanisms that maintain phenotypic similarity; and nominalists treat species as convenient partitions of continuous evolutionary change. What makes the impasse frustrating is that recent work in phylogenetic systematics—particularly the move toward rank-free classification and the recognition that horizontal gene transfer blurs lineage boundaries in prokaryotes—does not seem to adjudicate between these views. Everyone updates their examples, but no one updates their position. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about biology at all, but about what a taxonomy is supposed to do: mirror evolutionary history, support inductive predictions, or serve practical intervention. Is that right? Diagnose the disagreement using what the sides actually say: is it verbal, about hidden normative standards for scientific classification, about which empirical cases count as data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding reality that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there any conceptual distinction, formal result, or empirical finding that could actually move it, or should we conclude that "natural kind" is being used to answer different questions—one metaphysical, one methodological—under a single heading?
