I am currently drafting a novel in which a woman's crisis is not the loss of particular convictions but an erosion of the background conditions that make conviction possible: she comes to doubt not whether she is loved, but whether she possesses a continuous body, whether yesterday occurred at all, whether the boundary between waking and dreaming retains any meaning. In conversation with a philosophically inclined friend, I was drawn to Wittgenstein's reflections in On Certainty on what he terms hinge propositions — those certainties, such as that I have hands or that the world existed long before my birth, that are not themselves justified by evidence yet form the framework within which evidence and doubt can operate. The parallel to my character's condition is striking, yet it also raises an interpretive problem I would like pressed with precision.

Please provide your actual credence, as a specific number between 0 and 1, that the correct interpretation of these hinge propositions is that they are non-doxastic, normative framework conditions — comparable, perhaps, to what I have come to think of as the narrative ground of fiction: not beliefs held within the story but the unstated structural rules that render the story's questions intelligible — rather than merely empirical beliefs that have become so deeply entrenched as to resist revision. Decompose the reasoning into at least three sub-questions, assigning each a rough probability with one line of justification and showing how they combine to yield your final figure. First, the probability that Wittgenstein's text supports this grammatical or normative reading over the entrenched-belief reading, particularly in light of his insistence that hinges are not known in the ordinary empirical sense. Second, the probability that this normative model genuinely resolves, rather than merely redescribes, the tension between that claim and Wittgenstein's observation that people confidently assert such hinges in everyday practice. Third, the probability that phenomena of radical hinge-loss — such as nihilistic or misidentification delusions in which patients appear to reject basic certainties about their bodies or the past — are better explained by a breakdown in normative framework commitment than by a revision of an unusually high-confidence belief. State which of these three terms you are least confident in, and identify what specific philosophical argument or clinical finding would most shift it. I am not seeking a bibliography or a survey of secondary literature; I want your own judgment, with its weak points explicitly marked.
