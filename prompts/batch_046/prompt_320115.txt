I am presenting my paper at an interdisciplinary seminar on rationality and self-monitoring. My thesis is that standard treatments of epistemic akrasia—where an agent believes p but also believes that her belief that p is irrational—treat higher-order evidence as automatically authoritative over first-order judgment. I defend a counter-model: when higher-order evidence that one is biased or impaired is itself generated by the very cognitive subsystem whose reliability is being assessed, the agent should discount that higher-order signal and maintain the first-order belief. The resulting state looks structurally identical to akrasia from an external viewpoint, but I argue it is rationally required rather than defective; I call the triggering condition a reflexive bias loop. The audience will be ten philosophers of mind and epistemology, mostly trained in formal and Bayesian epistemology; two defend the view that higher-order and first-order evidence must be rationally compartmentalized; one is a cognitive scientist skeptical of philosophical distinctions not operationalized in calibration studies; and the chair is an expert on self-deception who consistently redirects discussion toward practical rationality and action-guidance. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup actually uses—defeat, calibration, reflection principles, evidential symmetry, practical reason—with what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks: the one that connects my reflexive-bias-loop condition to a familiar skeptical scenario about global self-correction failure and asks whether I have accidentally licensed universal skepticism about any agent’s ability to revise her own beliefs. For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for a twenty-minute Q&A window, and flag explicitly where the reply relies on a distinction that sounds ad hoc or that concedes too much ground to the compartmentalization view. I supply this sketch of my argument so the objections can target it: I begin with cases where a subject knows her visual system is currently unreliable due to fatigue, yet she also knows that the fatigue-monitoring mechanism draws on the same depleted attentional resources, so treating the fatigue-report as decisive defeat would be circular. I then generalize this circularity to any case where the higher-order tracker and the first-order process share an underlying resource or algorithmic substrate.
