I'm preparing a feature on why physics still lacks a settled explanation for the direction of time, and I want to avoid recycling the usual pop-science narratives. Before I evaluate the competing accounts, I need a rigorous conceptual audit. Write the desiderata: specify exactly what an adequate theory of the thermodynamic arrow must deliver — the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the results with which it must remain consistent. Include, with the reasoning behind each: the explanation of the low-entropy initial boundary condition without circularity; the reconciliation of time-reversible microdynamics with macroscopic irreversibility; the treatment of open subsystems that maintain local entropic gradients despite being embedded in larger environments; the dissolution or explanation of Boltzmann-brain scenarios; the preservation of causal asymmetry without presupposing it; and — this is the point I want pressed — the capacity to account for arrow-like behaviour in cosmological regions separated by event horizons, where no single observer can access a global macrostate description, without smuggling in a privileged cosmic foliation or an indexically loaded notion of now. Flag any pair of desiderata you believe are jointly unsatisfiable, particularly where global statistical explanations conflict with local, observer-relative state counting. Then, briefly, assess how the Past-Hypothesis statistical-mechanics programme, causal-interventionist accounts, and decoherence-based quantum approaches fare against this list. Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria themselves — which are negotiable, which are non-negotiable, and which does the most work in excluding candidates — rather than on the scoring.
