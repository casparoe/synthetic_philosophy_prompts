I am writing for an advanced high-school ethics course, and I have been trying to determine whether there is a coherent principle behind my intuitions about when promises genuinely bind us. Rather than asking for a survey of philosophers, I want to test whether my concrete judgments can be captured by a single account. Here are six scenarios and my verdicts on them.

First, if I promise my younger sister that I will take her to the natural history museum on Saturday and then skip the outing to socialize with friends instead, I have clearly wronged her. The promise binds me, and she is entitled to resent my failure.

Second, during a board-game night in which everyone understands that nothing is literally at stake, I say, “I promise I will trade you my ore for your wheat next turn,” but then I change my strategy and refuse. Even though I uttered the word “promise,” I do not believe I violated any genuine moral obligation.

Third, I sign a formal contract guaranteeing delivery of a specialized component by March, but an unforeseeable geopolitical crisis makes production literally impossible. I think I am released from the duty to deliver, though I may still owe compensation; the impossibility dissolves the binding force.

Fourth, at age sixteen I make a solemn religious vow to devote my life to poverty relief, but by age twenty-five my deepest values have genuinely transformed. I do not believe my past self can still bind my present self; the vow no longer obligates me, though I may feel sincere regret.

Fifth, standing at the grave of my late mentor, I promise aloud that I will complete and publish the manuscript we began together. She cannot hear me, benefit, or hold me accountable. Nevertheless, I feel genuinely bound by that commitment.

Sixth, during an intensely vivid lucid dream, I encounter a figure indistinguishable from my mentor and make the exact same promise with identical emotional gravity. When I wake, the feeling lingers, yet I do not think the dream-commitment binds me in the slightest.

These judgments feel right to me, but they are hard to square. Cases one, three, four, and five suggest that feasibility, the promisee’s responsiveness, and the persistence of my values all matter in different ways, yet case five and case six share the feature of addressing someone who cannot actually receive the benefit, and they still feel radically different. Meanwhile, case two shows that explicit utterance is insufficient, but I am unsure exactly what distinguishes it from case one.

Is there a single principle—perhaps about autonomous self-legislation, about the creation of legitimate expectations, about participation in a social practice of trust, or about directed moral address—that delivers all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn in order to preserve theoretical simplicity, and why? Please state the principle precisely, indicate what it predicts for a case I have not listed—such as a promise made to a highly realistic humanoid robot that I know lacks consciousness, or a promise encoded in an automatically executing smart contract that disregards my later consent—and be explicit about which of my six verdicts your account cannot accommodate.
