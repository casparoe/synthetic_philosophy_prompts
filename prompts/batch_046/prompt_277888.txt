In an aesthetics seminar last week, a classmate dismissed concerns about continuing to study and exhibit a filmmaker's oeuvre after serious new biographical revelations by saying, "You have to separate the art from the artist." The phrase seemed to carry the argument, but I cannot tell exactly what thesis was being asserted. Could you lay out the distinct, precise claims that slogan could be expressing—treating it as a family of theses rather than a single doctrine? I want a menu that includes at least: (1) the claim that a work's aesthetic or artistic properties are causally independent of its creator's psychology or conduct; (2) the claim that moral defects in the maker never entail, justify, or cause corresponding aesthetic defects in the work; (3) the claim that knowledge of the artist's biography should never rationally affect a viewer's evaluation of the work's merit; (4) the claim that artistic value and moral value belong to non-interacting domains with no legitimate cross-domain influence; and (5) any further readings that belong in the taxonomy, such as distinctions between intrinsic properties of the work, the total experience of appreciating it in context, and the social practice of exhibition or canonization.

For each reading, say whether it amounts to a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible philosophical position, or something that is actually false. Then tell me which of these readings genuinely supports the conclusion that we ought to keep teaching, screening, or exhibiting the work despite the revelations about the creator, and which readings—if consistently held—would actually allow that knowledge of the artist's conduct can diminish our aesthetic response, our judgment of artistic merit, or our reasons for public promotion of the work. In particular, is there a non-trivial version of the slogan that distinguishes the work's internal properties from the broader situation of its appreciation, and that therefore permits moral knowledge to affect the latter without violating the separation? Finally, identify the reading most people invoke when they use the phrase to shut down a moral objection in institutional settings like a syllabus or museum board decision, and say whether that reading survives scrutiny.
