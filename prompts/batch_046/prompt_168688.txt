I was chatting with a grad student after seminar yesterday — they were pretty fired up about that scale argument with insects. You know the one: if there's even a 0.01% chance each ant or beetle is sentient, then with the trillions out there you've basically got statistical proof that enormous numbers are suffering. I feel like there's a category error here, treating a metaphysical prior like a lottery with enough tickets to guarantee a win, but I'm struggling to pin it down precisely. It also seems like people who reject insect sentience appeal to phenomenal binding — the idea that consciousness needs some integrated neural architechture that arthropods just don't have — but then they turn around and use the scale argument to say 'even if it's unlikely, the numbers are too big to ignore.' So are they using the argument consistently? Or does applying the scale argument already presuppose that sentience is the kind of thing that can be distributed probabilistically across individuals, which is exactly what the binding objection denies?

I'm prepping a unit on nonhuman ethics for next term and I want to make sure I'm not smuggling in contradictions when I present both sides. Could you untangle whether the scale argument actually proves widespread insect consciousness, or whether it's only about moral risk, and if the binding objection undermines one but not the other?
