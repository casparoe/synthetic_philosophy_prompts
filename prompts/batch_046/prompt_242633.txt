I'm drafting an argument for my philosophy class on just war theory, and I've reached a conclusion that seems absurd, so I want to check where the reasoning breaks. Here are my premises, numbered:

(1) The moral equality of combatants claims that soldiers on opposing sides have identical moral status regarding killing and being killed, regardless of whether their war is just.

(2) If that equality is genuine, then the permission to kill an enemy soldier cannot rest on the justice of one's own cause; it must rest on a feature both sides share.

(3) The shared feature usually cited is direct lethal threat: combatants are liable because they pose an immediate danger, while non-combatants do not.

(4) However, direct lethal threat is not the same as wearing a uniform. A remote drone pilot directs lethal force, while an unarmed military chaplain does not.

(5) So, if we apply the equality thesis consistently, liability must track this functional role—posing a direct lethal threat—rather than formal military status. Call this the functional-combatancy criterion.

(6) By that criterion, a civilian weapons engineer who maintains attack systems is as liable as an infantry soldier, and a uniformed medic is as immune as a civilian bystander.

(7) But this dissolves the combatant/non-combatant distinction that just war theory treats as foundational, and it allows the intentional targeting of civilians while protecting soldiers—exactly the inversion that the principle of discrimination was meant to prevent.

(8) Therefore, accepting the moral equality of combatants consistently forces us to abandon either the equality thesis itself or the discrimination principle.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to keep premise (1) and premise (3), since they seem central to just war theory as I understand it, but I want to know what I am paying if I do. Which premise should I reject, why did it seem plausible, and what would the standard positions—say, the traditionalist view that ties liability to just cause, and the revisionist view that ties it to individual responsibility—each have to say about the functional-combatancy criterion? Also, if the argument is actually valid and the real issue is that one of my premises is false, which one is least costly to give up?
