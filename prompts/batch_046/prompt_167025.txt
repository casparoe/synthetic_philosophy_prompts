In my graduate seminar on collective ethics, we have spent the past several months debating whether individual actions—specifically, personal carbon emissions, the purchase and consumption of meat, and casting a vote in large-scale elections—can be morally wrong or required even when they make no perceptible difference to outcomes. Every participant accepts the empirical premises: atmospheric dynamics are not sensitive to a single person’s footprint; industrial animal agriculture operates through inventory buffers and fixed production schedules that decouple individual purchases from marginal supply; and in elections with millions of voters, the probability of any one ballot being decisive is negligible. Nevertheless, one faction maintains that these actions remain subject to genuine moral evaluation because they express complicity in harmful collective patterns or violate duties of integrity, while the opposing faction insists that without causal efficacy—without some non-zero expectation of altering the outcome—no moral reason can apply to the isolated agent. Despite repeated discussions, neither side has shifted, and our arguments now feel circular.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not about causation in any straightforward empirical sense, but about a concept I am tentatively calling normative dilution: the question of whether a moral reason can retain its full justificatory force once its causal efficacy has been distributed across so many agents that no individual contribution is distinguishable. The interventionists appear to treat reasons as partly relational or expressive—grounded in one’s position within a collective practice—while the non-interventionists treat them as necessarily tied to expected outcome differences, so that once efficacy falls below a threshold, the reason dissolves entirely. I am unsure, however, whether this diagnosis identifies a substantive disagreement about the nature of moral justification, a verbal dispute about what counts as a reason, a divergence in background intuitions about individual responsibility in massive systems, or a case of the two sides answering different questions—one about what it is rational to do, the other about what it is right to be. Could you diagnose the actual structure of our impasse, drawing on what each position actually commits itself to? And given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction, a formal model of collective agency, or a reframing of the problem—perhaps by separating causal contribution from participatory role—that could genuinely advance the discussion, or should we conclude that the disagreement is irreducible?
