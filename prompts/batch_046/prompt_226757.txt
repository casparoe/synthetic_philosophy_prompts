Been arguing with a friend about when a legend actually becomes history, using Troy, Arthur, and the Exodus as my test cases. I built an argument that seems to collapse everything into legend or proves historians are just being arbitrary, and I can't find the exit.

(1) A narrative counts as history rather than legend only if there's independent material or textual corroboration of at least one specific claim inside it, outside the narrative tradition itself.

(2) The Trojan War story mentions Hissarlik, and Hittite texts reference Wilusa and Ahhiyawa, so there's independent corroboration; Troy crosses into history.

(3) Arthurian stories mention Cadbury and some Welsh anals, but nothing from the 6th century mentions Arthur specifically; so Arthur stays legend.

(4) The Exodus mentions Pi-Ramesses and Pithom, and Egyptian inscriptions mention Israel, but there's no 13th-century archaeological trace of a mass departure or the plagues; so by (1), Exodus is legend.

(5) Apply (1) consistently to the Babylonian exile: Babylon is real, and there are cuneiform deportation lists, but there's no direct archaeological trace of the specific Judean families listed in Chronicles. So (1) should make the exile legend too.

(6) But nobody treats the Babylonian exile as legend; it's standard history.

(7) So either (1) proves too much, or historians apply it selectively without saying so.

(8) The fix might be that (1) should require corroboration of the central causal claim, not just peripheral places. For Exodus, the central claim is the departure and conquest, wich lacks corroboration; for the exile, it's deportation, wich the lists corroborate.

(9) But then for Arthur, the central claim is a war-leader uniting Britons, and there's peripheral evidence of warfare in the right period but not of the leader; so he's still legend.

(10) The problem is that (8) saves the distinction only by letting the narrative itself define what's "central," wich feels circular.

I keep feeling the slip is between (4) and (5) — treating missing archaeological traces of mass movement as missing corroboration for the core event — but I can't name it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I keep (1) intact, do I have to accept that the exile is also legend? Or is there a hidden equivocation in how I'm using "independent corroboration" across these three?
