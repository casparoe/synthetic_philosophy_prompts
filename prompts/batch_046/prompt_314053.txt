You're a philosophy educator who specializes in experiential learning — think like a workshop designer, not a lecturer. Before you sketch anything, do a quick brainstorm: what usually goes wrong when people teach Nietzsche's essay on history's uses and disadvantages for life through straight lecture alone?

Now design a 90-minute classroom activty for 12 upper-level philosophy students in a seminar. No lecture up front. The core idea is that history serves life in three modes — monumental, antiquarian, critical — but each turns toxic if it dominates. I want particpants to feel the pull and the poison before anyone names the categories.

Inject this niche framing: "mnemonic debt" — the hidden cost each mode pays to the others.

Set up a structured role-play called the Kleinburg Triage. Kleinburg is fictional — a small town whose archives burned, leaving only 30% of records intact. Split the class into three hidden-role groups of four: Monumentalists, Antiquarians, Critical Historians. Don't reveal these map onto Nietzsche's modes until the debrif.

Give them three concrete artifacts and fake chip budgets (no real money):
1. A heroic founding statue plus its myth (monumental resource).
2. A complete but dull census and weather log, 1900-1950 (antiquarian resource — completeness is the goal).
3. A hidden doc cache showing the founder collaborated in a local purge (critical resource).

Rules and payoffs arranged so the problem hits them through play:
- Monumentalists earn 5 chips per token on the hero narrative; they lose 3 if critical docs get full funding.
- Antiquarians earn 2 chips per saved item, but if total saved items exceed 5, every Antiquarian takes a 4-chip "memory burden" penalty.
- Critical Historians earn 6 chips if the collaboration is fully exposed, but take a 5-chip "narrative collapse" penalty if the hero narrative is completely erased — they need a target.

They have 25 minutes to collectively sign one "Official Town Memory" document. If they fail to agree, everyone loses 3 chips.

Predictably, round one will suppress critical docs, over-fund the statue, and paralyze Antiquarians with completeness anxiety. Tell me exactly how you'll handle that — maybe appoint a silent observer to note who hasn't spoken, then force a second round.

In round two, add the mnemonic-debt twist: no group can spend chips on its own artifact unless another group spends at least one chip on a different artifact. This makes them feel interdependence — Critical needs Monumentalists to preserve the hero; Monumentalists need Antiquarians to legitimize preservation.

Afterward, run a 25-minute debrif. Map chip moves and negotiation moments onto the three modes: monumental history becoming life-denying by suppressing truth; antiquarian turning into hoarding paralysis; critical risking destruction of the very past it needs. Be frank about the feature this game can't reproduce: here critical history looks mainly like destructive exposure, but Nietzsche also saw it as liberating present life by breaking with the past. You'll need to supply that verbally.

Include: the setting (seminar, 12 students, 90 min), what they should come away seeing (that dominance of any mode is toxic and the modes are secretly interdependent), a honest note on what will go wrong the first run, and confirmation that the rules — not your explanation — must make them feel the force.
