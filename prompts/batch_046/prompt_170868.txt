I am preparing notes for an advanced seminar on the ontology of space, and I would like a poem that treats Newton's bucket argument with enough technical precision that a student of the substantivalism–relationism debate could recognize the moves being made, without the poem ever becoming a mere list of names or citations. Please write a sestina using the standard six-line stanza form and the rotating end-word scheme; the six end-words must be exactly "bucket," "stars," "surface," "empty," "spin," and "relation," and they should appear in that order in the first stanza. The speaker should adopt the voice of a relationist who accepts Newton's observational premise—the concave parabolic surface of the water in a rotating vessel, the twisted hanging rope—yet denies that this demonstrates absolute rotational motion with respect to an underlying spatial substance. Instead, the poem should embed Mach's principle into its own formal logic: each recurrence of "spin" ought to be coupled with a different proposed reference frame, moving from the local walls of the bucket, to the water's own surface, to distant stellar masses, and finally to their absence in an "empty" cosmos, so that the mechanical rotation of your end-words mirrors the relationalist's perpetual need to relocate the basis of motion.

Keep the philosophical reasoning accurate rather than ornamental. The envoi must bring "bucket" and "relation" together in its closing lines, either showing how the concavity is fully accounted for by cosmic relations or deliberately leaving the explanation suspended, and the poem as a whole should avoid asking for bibliographies, named editions, or scholarly references that a reader without library access could not verify. Let the imagery—parabolic curvature, fixed celestial bodies, the void of an unpopulated universe—carry the argument, and let the sestina's compulsive returns suggest both the elegance and the potential exhaustion of trying to ground every local motion in something beyond the local.
