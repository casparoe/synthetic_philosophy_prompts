I'm reporting a feature for a general science-and-ideas publication on why the paradox of phenomenal judgment remains unresolved in philosophy of mind, and I want the article to include an interactive decision tree that lets readers locate their own view—not by asking which philosopher they admire, but by working through concrete commitments.

Please construct a decision tree that begins from two shared premises: that phenomenal experience genuinely exists, and that the physical world is causally closed (no non-physical causes of physical events). From that starting point, design successive branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete yes/no or either/or question that a reader can answer without knowing the philosophical labels in advance. The branches should cover: (a) whether phenomenal judgments are physically caused through the same mechanisms as other beliefs; (b) whether the causal exclusion argument applies to mental-to-mental relations or only to physical-to-physical ones; (c) whether phenomenal concepts carry a special epistemic status that allows reference without the usual causal linkage; and (d) whether introspective reports are better understood as predictive inferences about an internal model rather than direct registrations of experience.

The leaves should be named positions—specifically, hard epiphenomenalism, reductive physicalism, interactionist dualism, the phenomenal-concept strategy, and a predictive self-model view of introspection—but build the logical space first (the combinations of answers), then map the positions onto it. For any combination of answers that does not correspond to a known view, briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or simply being unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, and explain what shared commitment collapses the distinction.

The audience is educated but non-specialist; avoid bibliographies, citation requests, and references to obscure sub-literatures. The goal is a sorting instrument that reveals what someone actually believes about causal closure, mental causation, and the nature of introspection.
