As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy with any seriousness, I have become preoccupied with Nietzsche's distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical uses of history—not in politics or culture generally, but in the internal historiography of the natural sciences. Modern science seems to demand all three at once: monumental exemplars to justify funding and education, antiquarian preservation of obsolete instruments and data formats, and critical dismantling of Whiggish progress narratives—yet these demands appear to pull scientific institutions in incompatible directions.

If one were to treat this tension as a live research agenda in philosophy of science rather than a cultural observation, which open conceptual questions deserve priority, and in what order? I ask you to defend a ranking among the following three lines of inquiry: first, whether the monumental mode can be reconceived for an era of distributed, automated, and largely anonymous scientific production without collapsing into mere institutional marketing; second, whether antiquarian preservation of scientific artifacts and superseded theoretical frameworks serves any genuine epistemic function for practicing scientists, or remains purely curatorial; and third, whether a rigorously critical history of science undermines the normative authority that scientific communities require to maintain methodological coherence.

Please order them by which is most upstream—resolving it should most alter how we ought to pursue the others—while weighing tractability for a philosopher without specialized archival access, and specify what institutional or empirical development over the coming decade would cause you to revise that ordering. I am not seeking a survey of Nietzsche scholarship, but a defended agenda for where conceptual effort should actually go.
