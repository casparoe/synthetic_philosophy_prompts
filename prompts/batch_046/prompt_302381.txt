I have been turning over the classic "utility monster" objection in my own mind—the scenario in which one being derives so much pleasure from a given resource that it outweighs the suffering of many others—and I want to see whether it actually damages your view. Here is my opening challenge: instead of a single monstrous individual, imagine a "distributed monster" made up of ten million ordinary people, each of whom gains only a modest but genuine increment of pleasure from the same pool of resources. Taken together, their collective gain outweighs the severe deprivation of one other person, yet no single beneficiary is grotesquely favored. If you say we should still allocate the resources to the group, your defense seems to rest on a principle of aggregation that you have not independently justified—one that treats the sum as morally decisive regardless of concentration. But if you reject this distributed case while accepting the single-monster case, you appear to be relying on an intuition about fairness or individual dignity that your theory explicitly rejects. How do you distinguish the two cases without smuggling in a non-utilitarian commitment?
