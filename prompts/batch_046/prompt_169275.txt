I am planning a single ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group of educated non-specialists—lawyers, scientists, and writers—on Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals." The goal is not to present cultural relativism in general, but to teach one precise conceptual move: Montaigne describes the Tupinambá practice of eating prisoners with clinical detachment, then asks why Europeans who dismember bodies with red-hot pincers on the rack should call that practice barbarous. The pedagogical challenge is to show participants that this is not an endorsement of cannibalism, nor a claim that all cultures are incommensurable, but a rhetorical mirror held up to European cruelty. Plan the teaching sequence: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why (whether the framing of Montaigne's own encounter with the cannibals in Rouen should come before or after the torture comparison, at what exact point the distinction between descriptive and normative relativism must be secured, and why the clinical description of the ritual must precede the reversal), the single concrete textual comparison that should carry the session and what properties it needs (it must include both the cannibal ritual and the European practice, and it must lack any explicit moral verdict from Montaigne), the misconceptions the group will form (that he endorses cannibalism, that he denies all moral judgment is possible, that he offers an accurate ethnographic report rather than a mediated philosophical fiction), the point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off, one final diagnostic question that reveals whether they grasp the mirror-structure rather than merely repeating that "Montaigne was tolerant," and what should be explicitly refused at this level—such as the precise ethnographic sources behind his account, the relation to Pyrrhonian scepticism, or the debates about whether he invented details—together with the reason for leaving each out. Please provide your answer in British English.
