I'm grading responses for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar on sentience and moral status, and I'd like a careful assessment of one student's answer to our midterm question. The prompt we used asked students to apply a specific distinction we developed in lecture: graded phenomenal consciousness—where experience ranges from minimal affective valence up through self-modeling—versus what we called integrative binding capacity, the degree to which an organism's internal states are unified into a single phenomenal field rather than processed in parallel without subjective integration. The exam question presented a thermostat regulating temperature via feedback loops and an insect exhibiting nociceptive withdrawal, then asked students to use that distinction to explain why one might grant both minimal phenomenal consciousness yet assign them radically different moral status, and finally to evaluate whether the distinction merely collapses into a restatement of cognitive complexity.

Here is the student's answer. They write that both devices possess minimal phenomenal consciousness because both maintain states that are "about" external conditions—temperature for the thermostat, tissue damage for the insect—and both display goal-directed behavior. They then argue that the moral divergence comes from integrative binding: the insect binds nociceptive signals with other sensory modalities into a unified experiential field, so its suffering is experienced as a coherent whole, whereas the thermostat processes thermal data across parallel, non-interacting channels with no phenomenal unity. From this they conclude that the thermostat merits no moral status whatsoever, while the insect earns a weak, derivative status precisely because its discomfort is integrated. Addressing the collapse objection, they claim the distinction survives because binding capacity concerns phenomenal unity specifically, not raw information-processing power; thus a hypothetical system with enormous parallel computing but zero integrative binding could be cognitively complex yet entirely lacking moral standing.
