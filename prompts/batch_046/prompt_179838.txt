I'm a high school student trying to work through some ideas about rational choice, and I'm puzzled by restaurant tipping in large urban settings where diners often pay cash, rarely return to the same place, and have no ongoing relationship with the staff. In those situations, the customer receives the full meal and service before leaving any gratuity; once the food is consumed, there is no legal obligation to pay extra, no risk of being denied future service by that particular server, and little chance of reputational harm in an anonymous crowd. From a strictly forward-looking standpoint, leaving money appears irrational—much like Derek Parfit's hitchhiker, who is dropped off and then asked to pay a driver who can no longer help or harm him. Yet the social norm remains strong and is enforced by shame and exclusion. What conception of practical rationality does this institution presuppose? Must it assume that agents can bind themselves to act against their immediate interest—what Parfit discusses as rational irrationality—or can it be sustained by indirect reputation, emotional identification, or social signaling that keeps payment instrumentally rational even in anonymous settings? Reconstruct the implicit theory of agency and commitment embedded in the tipping norm, distinguishing carefully what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it well. Then explain what would have to change in restaurant compensation or social enforcement if we concluded that honoring an unenforceable commitment is never rational once all future benefits are removed.
