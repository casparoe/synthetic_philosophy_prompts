Two colleagues, Jordan and Taylor, fell into a debate about the absent-minded driver problem during a seminar break. Jordan sketched the classic structure: a driver must reach home by taking the second highway exit; the first exit strands him in a remote area with no return route, the second gets him home, and missing both also leaves him lost. The driver suffers a specific, stable memory failure: once he has passed the first exit, he cannot distinguish being at the first exit from being at the second, so he must apply an identical decision rule at both nodes. By backward induction, he should continue straight through—since at the second node continuing produces the worst outcome—yet because his information sets are collapsed, he applies that same rule at the first node, where continuing is actually correct. The result is a paradoxical ex ante plan in which he drives past both exits and ends up stranded, despite knowing in advance that he wanted to exit eventually. Jordan argued that this reveals a tension not between preferences but between planning rationality and act rationality: the driver should pre-commit to an unconditional exit strategy and accept the risk of misfiring at the first exit, because that is the only plan that respects his ex ante aim of eventually reaching home.

Taylor responded in four moves.

(1) Taylor said Jordan was importing a false analogy to temporal preference reversal. In hyperbolic discounting, the agent's preferences actually shift over time; here they are constant—the driver always prefers home to being stranded. The difficulty is purely informational, not motivational, so framing the nodes as "different temporal selves playing a strategic game" is a category mistake.

(2) Jordan replied that informational fragmentation is structurally identical to strategic interaction across nodes. Even with stable preferences, the epistemic position at exit one differs from that at exit two, and each position must anticipate the behavior of the other. The ex ante self wants the ex post self at exit two to take the exit, but the ex post self at exit one, unable to rule out being at exit two, calculates that taking risks a misfire. This interdependence generates the backward-induction failure regardless of preference stability.

(3) Taylor then introduced a variant: imagine a silent passenger who observes the exits perfectly but is contractually forbidden to speak. The driver knows she is present and knows she knows his condition. Jordan had suggested that such an observer could split the driver's information set, letting him condition his action on her involuntary reactions—tensing at exit one, relaxing at exit two. Taylor argued that this does not dissolve the paradox but embeds it in a higher-order inference loop: the passenger, anticipating observation, may suppress her reactions, and the driver, anticipating suppression, must interpret her stillness. Because he still lacks direct access to location, he now faces recursive belief hierarchies about common knowledge and silence rather than a simple memory failure. Thus the variant transforms the problem from one about imperfect recall into one about multi-agent belief reasoning.

(4) Jordan countered that the passenger is an ornamental complication. Even without her, the core issue is that standard decision theory requires actions to be executable at every information set, and imperfect recall collapses sets in a way that makes optimal ex ante plans inexecutable ex post. The proper response is not to invoke common knowledge but to revise decision theory for imperfect recall: we need a principled distinction between plans (ex ante commitments) and acts (ex post choices), evaluating rationality primarily at the planning stage and allowing binding mechanisms—such as an autopilot programmed to exit at the second signpost. Taylor's insistence on evaluating rationality solely within collapsed information sets, Jordan concluded, abandons the concept of planning altogether.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each of Taylor's four responses, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say whether Jordan is correct that the silent-observer variant is merely ornamental, or whether Taylor is correct that it shifts the problem's philosophical category. Finally, identify what both parties are missing—perhaps regarding how an artificial agent with episodic-state representations but no explicit counter might face an analogous structural problem, or regarding whether a genuinely distinct resolution is available—and state what your adjudication implies for an unconsidered case: a driver who writes himself a detailed note before departure explaining exactly which exit to take, but knows that once past the first exit he will be unable to understand his own handwriting.
