At our philosophy study group last week, two regular members—Jordan and Riley—got stuck in a disagreement about whether tranquilism, the view that only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering are intrinsic goods, can account for the value people place in long, deliberately unfinished projects.

Jordan opened by applying the view directly: if someone is working on a decades-long project and feels no present suffering from its incompletion, then either continuing it serves only to prevent future suffering (making it instrumentally rational) or it expresses a hidden craving—an attachment to a future self-image or outcome. Jordan distinguished “instrumental persistence,” where one continues to avoid the suffering of regret or abandonment, from “pure persistence,” where one continues despite having no emotional investment at all. Jordan claimed a true tranquilist should be genuinely indifferent between finishing and stopping, provided neither path introduces craving or suffering, and that any appearance of commitment beyond this is just attachment misldescribed.

Riley replied that Jordan was missing a specific state Riley called “teleological poise”—a stable, non-urgent orientation toward a distant good that actively structures choice and attention without causing present suffering and without being reducible to mere absence of disturbance. Riley offered the case of a mathematician who has spent thirty years on a conjecture she suspects she will never solve: she is not suffering from the lack of proof, nor is she obsessively craving it, yet her life is genuinely shaped by the orientation. Riley argued that if tranquilism is correct, this orientation must be either a disguised form of craving or an instrumental mistake, which seems to misdescribe the phenomenology. Jordan countered that Riley was simply relabeling a mild, chronic attachment—that “teleological poise” is either dormant craving (kept in check by partial satisfaction) or, if it truly has no affective weight, merely a behavioral habit with no intrinsic value. Riley then charged that Jordan’s account makes the concept of craving unfalsifiable: any non-absent orientation gets reclassified as hidden attachment, so tranquilism protects itself by definition rather than argument.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn’t.
