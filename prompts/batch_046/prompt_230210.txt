I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I need one essay question with a model answer and a marking scheme. The extended mind thesis has recently become personally urgent rather than abstract: over the past year I have become the primary caregiver for a parent in the early stages of Alzheimer’s disease, and I have observed them construct a distributed mnemonic system—smartphone reminders, spatially arranged household objects, and family members who serve as real-time retrieval proxies—that operates not as mere external storage but as an integrated loop in practical reasoning and decision-making. I want the exam to use this clinical context to prevent students from answering by summarizing Clark and Chalmers’s canonical parity argument.

Please design a 90-minute essay question expecting approximately 900 to 1,100 words. The question should ask students to evaluate whether the extended mind thesis applies with equal force to these neurodegenerative prosthetic systems as it does to standard cases like Otto’s notebook. Specifically, students should be required to identify a structural asymmetry between clinical cognitive scaffolding and ordinary cognitive offloading—such as the fact that clinical prostheses involve a dependency relation that may undermine an implicit autonomy condition in parity reasoning, or that the absence of phenomenological transparency in family-mediated retrieval may matter for constitution rather than mere causal coupling—without allowing them to retreat into a generic rehearsal of the coupling-constitution fallacy. To keep the question from being answered by regurgitation, inject one niche conceptual distinction into the prompt itself: ask students to distinguish between functional parity, which compares informational inputs and outputs across a boundary, and integrative parity, which demands reciprocal dynamical coupling with cross-boundary error-correction and adaptive restructuring. The question should be answerable without access to obscure literature or citation, yet impossible to answer well without recognizing why the clinical case either exposes a hidden normative premise in the thesis or reveals that standard parity arguments conflate tool-use with cognitive incorporation.

Then provide a model answer of roughly 600 words demonstrating the specific moves an excellent response must make, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit points—for instance: correct application of the parity principle to the clinical mnemonic system; precise identification of the autonomy or dependency asymmetry; evaluation of whether integrative parity can rescue the thesis in this context; engagement with one serious objection specific to neurodegenerative scaffolding; and a precisely argued conclusion about whether the clinical case strengthens or weakens extended cognition. For each point, specify the marks, name the specific move required for an A-level response versus a B-level response, and include a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation prompts, or requests for verbatim quotations.
