I have been working through Hegel's account of war in the Philosophy of Right, particularly his image of conflict as a breeze that prevents the stagnant waters of civil society from putrefying in private interest. This sounds to me like a functional, almost medical claim: war restores the state's ethical health as exercise restores a body. From this it seems that Hegel must regard prolonged peace not merely as risky but as an active moral failure—an allowing of corruption—and ought therefore to endorse some constitutional mechanism for periodic, limited warfare, comparable to mandatory public-health inspections. Yet commentators treat his position as far more restrained, even reluctant. Is my hygienic reading mistaken because I have conflated war's ethical moment with an instrumental prescription, or is there a deeper reason this therapeutic logic does not generate an institutional duty to fight? And if the logic is indeed confined, does that not collapse his distinction between wars of sovereign preservation and wars of conquest, since both could be justified as restorative? Please correct the misunderstanding in my framing, state the precise principle that limits legitimate war for Hegel, and reply in one paragraph not exceeding 150 words.
