After a long argument last night with a friend about whether an 'a' remains an 'a' because of some hidden geometric invariant or because it simply occupies a particular relational niche within a given alphabet's style-space, I have to deliver the opening address at the Museum of Typographic Form's new exhibition on "The Alphabet's Invisible Architecture." The room will be split between graphic designers who think in font files and philosophers of art and cognition who think in categories, and neither group will forgive me for talking down to the other. Write a fifteen-minute formal speech—polished, not academic—using the Letter Spirit problem (what allows every 'a', from blackletter to neon tube, to be recognized as the same character despite sharing no literal pixels) as its structural backbone. The speech should quietly reject both Platonic essentialism and a pure Wittgensteinian family-resemblance account, and instead argue that letter identity is constituted by role-based coherence within a design system: a double-decker 'a' and a single-storey 'a' are the same letter not because they share a feature, but because each plays the equivalent functional role relative to its neighboring letters in that particular style-space. It needs to convince the designers that their craft is constrained by something real, and convince the philosophers that misrecognition is possible in principled directions rather than being mere convention, without ever citing specific scholars by name, asking for a bibliography, or letting the argument collapse into "it's just social agreement." The tone should acknowledge, without being defensive, that this sounds like an absurdly narrow thing to build a speech around—until you realize that every act of reading depends on it.
