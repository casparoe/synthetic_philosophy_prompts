As a high school student trying to think systematically about political obligation, I have adopted what I call the domestic-shield reciprocity thesis: universal conscription is morally permissible only when paired with strict armed neutrality and an explicit constitutional bar on expeditionary deployment, on the grounds that the state may compel citizens to risk lethal harm in defense of the territory only if it simultaneously guarantees that the prepared force will never be used outside that territory. Applying this to the militia model in Switzerland, the recently reactivated selective conscription in Sweden, and Finland's broader universal service system, I think Finland's participation in multinational exercises and alliance structures weakens its moral claim relative to Switzerland's stricter neutrality, while Sweden occupies an ambiguous middle ground. What would have to be true for this thesis to be wrong? I want the decisive defeaters separated from the merely awkward ones: which empirical findings about actual deployment practices in these three countries would undermine the moral argument rather than just complicate it; which conceptual distinctions—say, between collective defense obligations and offensive expedition—would break the reciprocity claim; and whether the view can be saved by redefining "expeditionary" in ways that make it unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that insulation is itself a defect. I also want an honest assessment of what defenders of Finnish or Swedish conscription would themselves concede as counting against them, versus what they would dismiss by appealing to security alliances, and whether there is any result in the philosophy of obligation or just war theory that the thesis implicitly relies on and that might collapse under scrutiny. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
