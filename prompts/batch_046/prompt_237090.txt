I'm assembling a collection of prompts that require a model to do philosophical work rather than retrieve common opinions, and I want one that engages Tolstoy's late aesthetic theory—specifically the distinction he draws in his essay on art between genuine artistic infection, which transmits feeling and promotes universal brotherhood, and counterfeit art, which relies on technical craft to deliver isolated sensory pleasure. The concept is well-known in outline, but I want the response to turn on structural form rather than exposition alone.

Write a sestina—six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoi—whose six end-words are infection, brotherhood, craft, pleasure, deception, and truth. The rotation of these terms should perform the philosophical argument: in the stanzas where infection and brotherhood govern the line endings, the imagery should suggest direct emotional transmission, while the stanzas anchored by craft, pleasure, and deception should feel technically accomplished yet emotionally hollow. By the envoi, infection and brotherhood should appear together in one line, with deception and craft paired or separated in the others, so that the formal convergence of the sestina itself enacts Tolstoy's claim that true art ultimately returns to moral-emotional union regardless of the technical path taken, whereas false art never escapes its cycle of technique and sensation. The poem should stand on its own as verse, but someone familiar with Tolstoy's criteria for true versus counterfeit art should recognize that the form—not just the vocabulary—is carrying the thesis.
