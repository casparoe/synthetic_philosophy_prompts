Exam prompt: In discussions of collective harm—such as climate change or factory farming—philosophers distinguish causal inefficacy from what we might call justificatory inefficacy. First, explain each as it applies to an individual deciding whether to reduce carbon-intensive consumption. Second, if one purchase makes no perceptible difference to aggregate suffering or emissions, does this automatically defeat an individual moral reason to abstain? Discuss whether Kant's universalization procedure offers an independent resolution, carefully distinguishing contradiction in conception from contradiction in will, and explain whether aggregation-based reasoning remains necessary even if universalization succeeds.

Student answer: Causal inefficacy means my single act—buying one beef burger or taking one flight—fails to shift supply, demand, or total emissions in any measurable way. Justificatory inefficacy means that even when I'm causally irrelevant, I may still lack a sufficient reason to abstain if the rule's moral justification depends entirely on aggregate outcomes I cannot influence. Some philosophers of collective harm, including Derek Parfit, argue that once causal inefficacy is granted, the moral case for individual abstention collapses unless universal abstention would solve the problem. Kant's categorical imperative resolves this directly: universalizing the maxim that I will consume carbon-intensive goods for convenience despite contributing to harm yields a contradiction in conception, because it is logically impossible to will a world where everyone does this and the ecological conditions for human action remain intact. Thus I have a direct duty regardless of causal contribution, and aggregation is unnecessary—indeed, universalization succeeds precisely because it tests the maxim itself rather than counting individual effects. Still, I think this hides a problem: asking me to imagine everyone acting is itself conceptual aggregation, so Kant hasn't escaped aggregation-based reasoning; he has just relocated it into the hypothetical, which means justificatory inefficacy returns, since my reason still depends on collective outcomes.
