I'd like a philosophical reading of Shakespeare's Julius Caesar through the problem of populism and the coherence of the will of the people. Most treatments treat the Roman crowd in Act III as a simple study in mob irrationality: Brutus offers reason, Antony offers emotion, and the crowd flips. I want to push past that into a more precise conceptual tension that democratic theory often glosses over—namely, the conflict between treating popular will as a social-choice aggregation (with all the Arrow-type intransitivity and cyclical-majority problems that implies) and treating it as a substantive, unitary identity that transcends individual preference. The play seems to stage both simultaneously: the crowd's preferences flip like an intransitive cycle, yet Antony addresses them as a single wounded body with one true heart he can heal.

Please read the forum scenes, the funeral orations, and the later street violence through this dual lens. Does Julius Caesar implicitly take a side—say, by showing that any appeal to popular sovereignty must oscillate unstably between these two incompatible pictures? Or does it vindicate one? Where does the drama illuminate something about the incoherence of the will of the people that pure argument misses—perhaps in how the crowd's physical presence, its noise, and its violence make both pictures seem plausible at different moments? And where does importing this framework risk distorting the play—perhaps by treating the Roman plebs as a modern electorate, or by ignoring the specific political economy of the Republic?

This is for an essay aimed at a rationalist audience that takes both the philosophical literature on social choice and the play's historical context seriously. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the play to an illustration of Arrow's theorem, nor should it twist democratic theory to fit Shakespeare's plot. If you conclude that the play ultimately undermines populism, or that it reveals the category to be unstable in a specific way, say so clearly.

Finally, please state explicitly what would change your mind: which interpretation of a specific scene, or which argument from democratic theory, would lead you to a different verdict about whether the play supports, undermines, or merely dramatizes the tension? And flag where your reasoning is most vulnerable—whether in over-reading the crowd's psychology as a philosophical position, in anachronistically applying modern preference-aggregation formalism to Roman politics, or elsewhere.
