I sit on the technical review board of an international regulatory body that approves binding standards for autonomous logistics networks. Each quarter we evaluate proposals that reshape competitive dynamics across firms. Write a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the logic of multipolar traps and races to the bottom, that I can run in under an hour without game-theoretic computation: the ordered questions to ask of any proposal, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the stopping rules that yield approval, conditional approval, or rejection, and the explicit points where the decision becomes a normative judgment rather than a factual one. The procedure must identify whether a standard risks locking competitors into a collectively harmful equilibrium and whether it contains a credible internal coordination mechanism to escape it. Apply it to three proposals: a real-time data-sharing mandate that makes non-participation economically fatal; a uniform labor-safety floor with no enforcement mechanism; and a carbon-offset accounting rule that rewards strategic misreporting by early movers. Include the case in which the procedure demands rejection despite large promised efficiency gains, and state clearly what the procedure is powerless to resolve.
