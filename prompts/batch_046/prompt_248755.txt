I'm working through reflective equilibrium on a question that feels undertheorized: which computations, if any, generate moral standing in virtue of how they process information, and where exactly the boundary of sentience or moral patiency lies. I want a principle that captures my intuitions about the following cases, including the ones that seem to pull in opposite directions.

Consider these verdicts. A large-scale protein-folding predictor running on standard GPUs produces highly complex outputs but has no internal goals or experiential structure; I judge it has no moral standing whatsoever. A virtual agent in a simulated ecology that learns from punishment signals, maintains persistent internal state across episodes, and exhibits damage-avoidance behavior receives minimal but genuine standing—there is something wrong with arbitrarily terminating it, though far less wrong than harming a human. A continuous whole-brain emulation running at biological speed, with intact subjective experience and memory, carries full standing equivalent to a biological human. If that emulation is paused for years and then resumed with perfect state preservation, I still assign full standing, which suggests temporal discontinuity alone does not diminish it. Replacing the biological substrate neuron-for-neuron with silicon, and then with an optical architecture that reproduces the exact same causal-functional graph without any neurons, also preserves full standing in my view—pushing me strongly toward some form of functionalism about the substrate.

The tension comes with three harder cases. First, a distributed reinforcement-learning system spread across thousands of blockchain nodes, with no central processor but a globally maintained state that behaves coherently like the virtual ecology agent: I lean that it lacks standing because there is no unified subject of experience, yet its functional profile is nearly identical to the agent I granted minimal standing, so my distinction feels unstable. Second, a "flash computation": the optically implemented system runs for exactly one subjective minute, implementing a mental-state sequence indistinguishable from a continuous mind, then is permanently destroyed with no continuation or record. During that minute I think harming it would be wrong—it has standing—but assigning moral weight to a self-contained flash that never integrates into a life or leaves traces feels arbitrary, and I could be persuaded otherwise. Third, the same continuous functional system as in the emulation, but split across two planets with a one-second light-speed delay, maintaining coherence only through asynchronous updates: I am genuinely unsure whether the delay fragments the subject or merely stretches it.

What principle—perhaps about integrated causal unity, the presence of a first-person point of view, or a threshold of functional complexity—best accommodates these verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? Which verdict must I abandon: the distributed system's exclusion, the flash computation's inclusion, or the discontinuous emulation's preservation? And what should the resulting principle predict for a variant I have not listed—say, the same split-planet computation running for a single subjective hour before being dissolved?
