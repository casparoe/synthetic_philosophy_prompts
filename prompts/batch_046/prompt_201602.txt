I'm working through Plutarch's Parallel Lives for a course on ancient biography, and I keep returning to a tension that the standard account of moral exemplarity doesn't seem to cover. The usual story—whether drawn from Aristotelian imitation or from later hagiographic tradition—assumes that a biography teaches by offering a single life as a more or less coherent template: the reader absorbs the subject's virtues, recognizes the vices, and adjusts accordingly. Plutarch, however, never presents isolated lives; he pairs a Greek and a Roman figure and closes each pair with a direct comparison of their characters and fates. In the pairing of Alexander and Julius Caesar, for instance, both men display extraordinary political and military genius, both suffer from what Plutarch treats as a kind of overreaching ambition that leads to violence and premature death, and neither emerges as a clear moral superior. The comparison does not resolve the tension; it sharpens it, leaving the reader with two structurally similar yet culturally distinct figures who are equally admirable and equally flawed.

What I want is an analysis of this specific pairing through the lens of moral-learning theory and the distinction between biography and hagiography. If moral learning from lives works by providing an exemplary model, then Plutarch's comparative structure seems to defeat its own purpose: the reader is offered not one template but two imperfect ones, and the appended comparison invites judgment rather than straightforward imitation. Does the theory of exemplarity have resources to explain how a reader is supposed to learn from simultaneous, flawed exemplars? Or does the pairing expose a gap—perhaps pointing to something like comparative moral calibration, where the value lies not in copying either figure but in measuring the distance between them? I am also interested in where the theory is silent: hagiography tends to suppress a subject's failures to preserve exemplarity, yet Plutarch includes Alexander's drunken violence and Caesar's monarchical pretensions prominently. Does this mean Plutarch is not practicing hagiography at all, or does it mean that hagiography can accommodate negative exemplarity in a way the standard theory hasn't recognized? And finally, what does this episode show about the theory itself—is the comparative biography an anomaly that undermines the claim that lives teach morality, or does it reveal that the theory was always implicitly about pairs and contrasts rather than solitary heroes?
