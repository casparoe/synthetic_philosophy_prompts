I have spent the past year trying to understand a puzzle about consciousness that I first encountered in popular writing about visual illusions, and I am stuck on a disagreement that seems to go nowhere. The case is the color-phi phenomenon: if a red dot appears briefly in one position and then a green dot appears briefly nearby, observers report seeing a single dot moving between the locations and changing color in the middle of its path, even though no physical stimulus ever occupied the intermediate space or displayed an intermediate hue. The standard explanation is that the brain does not finish constructing the percept until after the second dot has arrived, which raises a strange question about the timing of experience. Some thinkers describe this as an Orwellian revision: consciousness delivers a complete narrative of motion and color change, but only after the fact, rewriting the history of what was experienced so that it seems as though the intermediate state was lived through in real time. Others describe it as Stalinesque: the brain may present something in the moment, but then it airbrushes the record, altering the memory of what was seen rather than inventing the experience itself. For years I have read both sides argue over whether consciousness is therefore always delayed and reconstructed, or whether it is genuinely present-tense but our reports are edited afterward, and neither camp ever seems to concede ground. They cite the same illusion, invoke the same neural timing data, and yet reach opposite conclusions about what it means for the present moment to be "real."

Because I have no formal training in philosophy or neuroscience, I want to ask whether I am misunderstanding the structure of the dispute. I have developed a private hypothesis that might be naive: perhaps both sides assume there must be one unified event called "the experience" that either happened veridically or was fabricated, and they miss the possibility that what registers in the moment could be genuinely fragmentary—perhaps motion is registered without a determinate color, or color is registered without a determinate path—while only the later narrative consolidation assigns both properties together. I have noticed something vaguely analogous in my own fatigue: there are moments when I am certain I saw something move across my vision, yet I cannot retrieve whether it was light or dark, as if the movement left a trace but the chromatic detail never consolidated. If that intuition points to anything real, then the debate might not be about whether the brain lies or tells the truth, but about whether consciousness can exist in an indeterminate state before it is packaged for recall.

Given all this, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque deadlock a hidden empirical disagreement about neural latency that better experiments could settle? Is it verbal, turning on different uses of words like "experience," "present," or "consciousness" that neither side defines? Are the two camps actually answering different questions—one about the causal construction of perception, the other about the epistemic authority of first-person report—while treating them as the same issue? Or is the impasse rooted in bedrock intuitions about whether the present moment must be either fully authentic or fully illusory, intuitions that neither side can argue for? And if your diagnosis is correct, what would actually count as progress: a formal distinction between sensory registration and narrative consolidation, an experimental design that isolates pre-report states, a change in the question being asked, or an argument that no resolution should be expected because the framing is ill-posed? I am not asking which side is right about color phi; I am asking what kind of disagreement this is, and whether my suspicion about fragmentary registration is a useful way to reframe it or just a confusion born of ignorance.
