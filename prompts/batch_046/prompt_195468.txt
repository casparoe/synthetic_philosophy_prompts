I was arguing with my roommate about Parfit's hitchhiker—y'know, the desert rescue where you promise to pay the driver after he saves you, and then once you're safe it's rational not to pay, except it's also rational to be the kind of person who'd pay. My roommate formalized it into a general argument and now I'm stuck. Here are the steps he jotted down:

(1) In the hitchhiker case, it's rational to form the intention now to pay later, even though after rescue there's no causal leverage left. (2) What's doing the work isn't backward causation; it's that rationality evaluates dispositions by their total payoff across cases, not single acts. (3) So rational choice is always about selecting character traits, and particular actions are rational only derivatively—as expressions of an optimally chosen trait. (4) If a disposition produces net global benefit—honesty even when nobody's watching, say—rationality requires adopting it. (5) Once you've adopted the trait, breaking it in one particular instance doesn't undo the global benefit you've already secured by being that type. (6) But if we evaluate particular acts only by local consequences, then breaking the promise after rescue is correct. (7) So to avoid contradiction, local act-evaluation must be subordinated to global trait-selection in every domain. (8) That means you should keep promises to dead people, pay debts to bankrupt firms, and so on, whenever honesty is part of your optimally-selected character. (9) Yet these acts have literally zero causal impact—no predictor, no observer, no feedback loop to anyone. (10) Therefore, rationality demands maintaining costly dispositions even in causally isolated contexts, which makes it indistinguishable from a kind of moral fanaticism. (11) So either step (2) is false, or we have to accept that rational agents are systematically locally irrational everywhere.

He says this proves causal decision theory collapses into character-utilitarianism and eats itself. I feel like there's a sneaky scope shift between (3) and (7), or maybe an equivocation on what 'rationality requires' means at different levels, but I can't name it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Does it blow up the original hitchhiker solution?
