I am attempting to articulate a recurring configuration in discussions of profound value change for which I lack a precise term. The pattern is not simply that a person’s priorities shift—for instance, a lifelong advocate for economic growth who, after witnessing large-scale ecological collapse, comes to regard GDP as morally irrelevant, or an ambitious academic who, after a sudden neurological injury, regards intellectual prestige as entirely empty—but that the shift is accompanied by a specific kind of epistemic rupture. The individual can report what they once believed and why they once believed it, yet they are unable to re-enter the evaluative framework in which those reasons had force; the prior reasoning now appears to them not as false but as conceptually confused, and they cannot reconstruct how it ever seemed sound. This differs from the familiar account of transformative experience, where the difficulty is typically prospective—one cannot anticipate what the experience will be like; here the difficulty is retrospective, involving the loss of access to one’s own prior normative architecture. It also differs from Kuhnian incommensurability, since there is no shared empirical language being replaced, and from ordinary rationalization, since the perplexity about one’s former self is genuine rather than strategic. Does this specific combination—transformative value change coupled with retrospective opacity of prior reasons—have an established concept or technical term? If so, how exactly is it distinguished from transformative experience, epistemic closure, and value incommensurability? If not, how should it be named and bounded, particularly with respect to cases where the prior framework remains partially accessible versus cases where it becomes entirely alien?
