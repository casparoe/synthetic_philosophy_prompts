As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I have been trying to understand why the literature on judgment aggregation and the so-called discursive dilemma seems to have reached an impasse. The basic formal result—that aggregating individual judgments on logically interconnected propositions can yield collective inconsistency even when every individual is consistent—is now well established, yet philosophers and social-choice theorists remain divided on its significance. One group treats the dilemma as a profound challenge to the possibility of rational collective deliberation, insisting that any aggregation rule must preserve logical consistency at the cost of other desiderata. Another group regards it as a technical curiosity, arguing that we should simply adopt premise-based or conclusion-based procedures and accept that collective rationality requires sacrificing either independence or anonymity. After years of refinement, neither side appears to have persuaded the other, and the arguments have become increasingly formal without shifting the underlying intuitions.

What I find puzzling is whether this stalemate is genuinely substantive or whether it masks a deeper disagreement about what collective belief is supposed to represent. In physics, we distinguish sharply between state descriptions that are invariant under transformation and those that depend on the choice of measurement frame; I wonder whether the aggregation debate is analogously split between those who view collective judgment as a representation of an underlying social state that must be consistent, and those who view it as an instrument for reaching actionable conclusions, where consistency is only instrumentally valuable. Is the disagreement really about the normative weight of logical coherence in democratic theory, or is it verbal—centered on ambiguous uses of terms like rationality and group belief? And if it is substantive, is there any conceptual distinction—perhaps analogous to distinguishing gauge-dependent from gauge-invariant quantities—that could dissolve the apparent conflict, or is this a case where progress requires abandoning the expectation that collective opinion must mirror individual logic?
