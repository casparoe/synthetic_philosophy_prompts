The prompt was: 'In Tahafut al-Falasifa Al-Ghazali defends occasionalism against the philosophers view of necessary causation. Explain his argument from the fire and cotton example, and discuss one objection he raises to the claim that causes operate by nature (tabi'a).'

Student answer:
'Al-Ghazali's occasionalism says God is the real cause behind everything. In the Tahafut he uses the fire and cotton example: we see fire near cotton and then it burns, but we don't actually see a causal link. So he claims the philosophers are wrong to say fire burns by its own nature, or tabi'a. Instead God creates the blackening in the cotton right when the fire is there, but not because of the fire. One objection he makes is that if burning were truly necessary by nature, then God couldn't stop it — like in the miracle where Abraham is in fire but doesn't burn. The philosophers reply that miracles are just God changing nature's habits, but Al-Ghazali rejects that because he thinks nature doesn't have habits, only God does. So for him, every instance is a fresh direct act. It's basically Hume's constant-conjunction view with God inserted as the hidden cause.'
