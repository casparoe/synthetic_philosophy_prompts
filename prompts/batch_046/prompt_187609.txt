I've been working with a formal model of indicative conditionals that treats them as operators on belief-revision paths rather than as propositions bearing static unconditional probabilities. Specifically, for an agent with prior credence function P, evaluating "if A then C" involves computing a trajectory T(A,P) that updates P by successive conditionalizations on A through intermediate hypotheses, converging to a fixed-point revision P_A*. The strength of the conditional is measured by an integral of P_k(C) along the trajectory, weighted by the rate of convergence to P_A*, rather than by P(C|A) alone. I understand that Lewis's triviality results show that if one identifies the probability of an indicative conditional with the corresponding conditional probability and treats the conditional itself as subject to the standard probability calculus, then the unconditional probabilities of conditionals collapse into dependencies solely on the probabilities of their antecedents and consequents. My concern is whether this collapse extends to my framework. Even though my conditionals are not propositions with static probabilities, each intermediate stage P_k(C|A) satisfies Adams's thesis, and my trajectory-integrated measure is built entirely from these conditional probabilities. So is there a generalized version of Lewis's argument that forces the integrated strength of "if A then C" to depend only on P(A) and P(C), rendering the revision-path structure redundant? Or does the dynamic, non-propositional nature of the model genuinely block the triviality proof, and if so, precisely which assumption—propositional status, closure under the probability axioms, or direct application of Adams's thesis to the conditional itself—fails to carry over?
