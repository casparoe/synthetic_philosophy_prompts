As a philosophy professor, I am preparing the opening remarks for a workshop co-hosted by our philosophy department and the School of Public Policy, tentatively titled Demandingness, Prerogative, and Institutional Design. The audience is deliberately split: half are moral philosophers well-versed in the demandingness objections to consequentialism—familiar with agent-centered prerogatives, cost-sensitive thresholds, and the standard literature—while the other half are policy practitioners, nonprofit administrators, and graduate students in public administration with no philosophical training. I want the remarks to run about seven to eight minutes and to do genuine conceptual work, not merely welcome the room. The structural framing problem is this: if individuals genuinely possess a prerogative not to maximize the good—if the moral demand on any single agent is capped at some cost-sensitive threshold—then the moral weight that remains unclaimed by individuals does not simply vanish. Call it residual demand. The workshop asks whether institutions can be designed to pool or absorb this residual demand without either violating individual prerogatives (by taxing agents beyond their threshold) or collapsing back into institutional consequentialism that treats every organizational resource as subject to the same maximization calculus. Please construct remarks that make this tension govern what the speaker can and cannot say. The speaker must welcome practitioners and honor benefactors without implying that private charity is sufficient, and must acknowledge the philosophers' technical distinctions without turning the occasion into a seminar. In particular, distinguish carefully between what the widespread practice of charitable giving presupposes—individual discretion within a personal threshold—and what an institution built for residual pooling would presuppose—collective obligation without direct individual cost-absorption. Introduce the vocabulary—prerogative, pooling, residual—through the institutional problem itself rather than through abstract definition. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific texts; do not quote named authors; and avoid formal notation or modal logic. The philosophical idea should shape the architecture of the speech so that both halves of the room recognize that the workshop is not about how much we should give, but about where the unclaimed moral weight goes.
