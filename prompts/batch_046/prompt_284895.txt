I'm a quality-assurance coordinator at a manufacturing firm that buys industrial adhesives and sealants in bulk, and part of my job is reviewing supplier reports that contain universal claims—statements like "every batch of this adhesive passes the freeze-thaw test" or "all components in this lot are free of the structural defect." I don't have a background in formal logic, but I recently read about a philosophical puzzle—sometimes called Hempel's raven paradox—which seems to show that observing a green apple can technically count as evidence that all ravens are black, because the apple is neither black nor a raven. That struck me as both strange and relevant: if I'm trying to check whether a universal claim about batches is true, I need to know which observations actually test it and which ones are just logically compatible but useless, and I need this in a form my inspectors can run without a philosophy degree.

Please act as an expert in philosophy of science and logical confirmation, and construct for me a reusable decision procedure—a step-by-step checklist with ordered questions and a small set of quick tests—that my team can apply whenever we receive an observational report and need to determine whether it supports, undermines, or is irrelevant to a supplier's universal claim. The procedure should distinguish direct confirming instances, direct counterinstances, and instances that are evidentially empty despite being logically consistent; it should flag where an auxiliary assumption (like "this item belongs to the class named in the claim") is doing hidden work; it should identify the points where logic runs out and a human judgment call about practical relevance is unavoidable; and it should be honest about cases where applying the procedure mechanically will produce a counterintuitive result. Before you begin drafting, please think carefully about which distinctions from confirmation theory can survive being turned into a factory-floor checklist and which ones must be dropped or marked as judgment calls.

Then run the procedure on three reports: first, an inspection finding that a green apple in the warehouse storage area shows no adhesive residue—my analog to the classic "green apple" case; second, a lab result showing that one batch of the adhesive failed the freeze-thaw test; and third, a report that a different product line from the same supplier, using an unrelated chemical formula, passed its own quality test. I want the procedure to yield one answer that my team will find wrong or uncomfortable, and I want you to state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle on its own—especially when the observation involves something outside the class defined by the claim but within the supplier's broader operation.
