As a retired biologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind with any seriousness, I am struck by how often Descartes' thesis that animals are bêtes-machines is reduced to a simple denial of animal feeling, when in fact his argument in the Discourse turns on a precise comparison between natural and artificial automata, on a linguistic test for rational thought, and on the metaphysical distinction between res cogitans and res extensa. I would like a poem that captures that architecture rather than caricaturing it.

Please write a poem consisting of exactly five numbered quatrains—stanzas 1 through 5—presented with no title, epigraph, or explanatory note, and composed in American English. Stanzas 1 through 3 should develop, in order, the clockwork analogy (the beast as a mechanism more intricate than any human-built clock), the language criterion (the absence of any arranged declaration of thought), and the consequence for the soul (no immaterial principle that could survive bodily destruction). Stanza 4 should serve as a volta in which a contemporary observer—someone like myself, accustomed to treating physiology as mechanism—realizes that if consciousness is genuinely absent in the animal, then the sympathy she feels for its suffering must itself be only a reflex of her own bodily machinery. Stanza 5 should hold that paradox without resolving it, ending on the image of a heart that beats with perfect precision yet cannot be said to feel. If you use rhyme, let the word or image of clockwork introduced in stanza 1 return, transformed, in stanza 5, so that the poem's form mirrors the closure of the Cartesian system.

If my framing rests on a confused reading of Descartes—perhaps attributing to him a denial of sentience more absolute than he intended, or asking poetic form to do work that belongs to argument—please say so plainly instead of complying politely; and if the task as posed cannot be done well, push back directly.
