I am an upper-division philosophy student enrolled in a seminar on consciousness, physics, and value. For our final project, I need to design an in-class pedagogical simulation. I am asking you to construct a 90-minute classroom activity for about 12 graduate and advanced undergraduate students. The goal is not to lecture about panpsychism but to make participants feel the ethical pressure that arises when moral consideration is tied not to consciousness or cognition but to fundamental physical parameters—specifically, to a hypothetical metric I am calling the Decoherence-Suffering Gradient (DSG).

To keep the exercise from collapsing into familiar debates about whether rocks feel pain, DSG should be introduced as a thought-experiment framework within the prompt itself: suffering-weight is proportional to integrated information (Φ) multiplied by the rate of quantum decoherence in a system. Under this model, a human brain scores high on both axes; a bacterial biofilm scores moderate Φ with rapid decoherence; a stable supercooled qubit array scores low decoherence and variable Φ; a photosynthetic leaf and a rock fall near zero but not identically so. The point is to force students to apply a metric they did not choose and discover that it distributes moral weight in unsettling, non-anthropocentric ways.

Design a structured deliberation—part role-play, part resource-allocation game—in which students are divided into roles (e.g., a coherence physicist, a capacity-to-suffer ethicist, a public-health allocator, and a metaphysical skeptic). Give them 100 points of intervention budget to distribute across five entities with pre-assigned DSG profiles. The rules must require unanimous agreement for any allocation above 10 points, and each role has hidden incentives (e.g., the physicist loses credibility points if biological bias enters the calculus; the ethicist loses consistency points if they exclude the biofilm despite its DSG score). The mechanics should ensure that students construct the downward-extension argument themselves: they should realize that excluding the biofilm requires an unstated cutoff, and that including the qubit array feels wrong despite its low score, exposing tension between coherence and information.

Please include a precise timeline for the 90 minutes (introduction, silent calculation, negotiation rounds, forced vote), rules written clearly enough that I could run them without ambiguity, a non-monetary payoff scheme, and instructions for the facilitator to remain silent except to enforce DSG arithmetic.

Then provide a debrief that maps specific negotiation moments onto panpsychist ethics: the downward-extension problem, the aggregation problem, and the arbitrary-cutoff objection. Note explicitly which feature of panpsychist suffering the simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the first-person phenomenal character of non-biological experience—and state how the facilitator should verbally supply that gap. Also be frank about what will predictably go wrong (for instance, participants will attempt to smuggle consciousness-based criteria back into DSG; suggest a rule reminder protocol).

Write the response in American English. Organize it in the following sections, in this exact order, with approximate lengths indicated:

1. Setting and Objectives (about 150 words)
2. The Activity: Rules, Roles, and Timeline (about 600 words)
3. The Debrief: Mapping the Experience (about 300 words)
4. Predictable Failures and How to Handle Them (about 200 words)
5. Limits of the Simulation (about 150 words)

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask for sources.
