I have been working through Berkeley's master argument with a study group, and we have reached an impasse that feels structurally similar to other long-running philosophical stalemates. Berkeley claims that whenever one attempts to conceive of a tree existing unperceived, one necessarily forms an idea of that tree being perceived in the very act of conception; therefore, the notion of an absolutely mind-independent object is incoherent. His critics—ranging from eighteenth-century common-sense philosophers to more recent commentators—reply that they can perfectly well entertain the proposition that this tree exists while no mind perceives it, without contradiction or any hidden perceptual relation in the content of the thought. Despite centuries of exchange, neither camp seems able to concede ground: the Berkeleians insist that critics have smuggled perception back into their conception, while the critics maintain that Berkeley has confused the act of conceiving with the content conceived.

I am trying to determine what kind of disagreement this actually is, and why it has stalled so thoroughly. Is it a hidden empirical dispute about the architecture of human cognition—specifically, whether every occurrent mental representation of an object necessarily includes a de se indexical framing? Or is it a normative disagreement about what counts as a legitimate philosophical explanation, with one side demanding that conceivability entail metaphysical possibility and the other rejecting that bridge principle? Might it be that the two sides are using "conceive" in technically different ways—one as a phenomenal, imagistic capacity and the other as a purely propositional attitude—so that the debate is largely verbal, even though both present themselves as making substantive metaphysical claims? I want a diagnosis that engages the specific moves each side actually makes, rather than a survey of interpretations, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a refined distinction between representational format and representational content, a formal model of mental representation, or a change in the framing question—could actually resolve the impasse, or whether we should expect permanent stagnation because the dispute rests on bedrock intuitions about the transparency of mind to itself.
