I am preparing for a seminar presentation defending a restricted, physically grounded version of the Lucas–Penrose argument. Rather than claiming that human cognition transcends every formal system, I argue that any physically instantiated normative reasoning architecture—including formal verification systems used in AI alignment—must contain an epistemic blind spot with respect to its own consistency conditions. The limitation is not Gödelian incompleteness applied to an abstract system, but a constraint on self-referential verification in bounded, embodied computation: a system cannot simultaneously execute its normative framework and provide a complete, consistent model of that execution without either circularity or an unmodelled substrate.

The audience is a joint philosophy-of-mind and AI-safety colloquium. Roughly half are mechanists working in predictive-processing or computational cognitive science, for whom the brain is a hierarchically organised inference engine with no special status. Several are logicians familiar with the standard refutations of Lucas—particularly the issues around self-reference, the distinction between a system knowing its Gödel sentence and merely being unable to prove it, and Benacerraf’s point that consistency is not a psychological property. There are also AI alignment researchers who are sympathetic to formal verification but deeply sceptical that Gödelian results constrain machine-learning architectures, since modern systems are not single axiomatic theories but stochastic, distributed approximators.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face, not generic anti-mechanist critiques. Order them by the combined damage they would do if I fail to answer well and the likelihood they will be raised. For each objection:

- Note whether it comes primarily from the mechanists, the logicians, or the alignment researchers.
- Give me a concise reply suitable for the Q&A setting—what to concede up front, what to hold, and the single sentence that must land.
- Identify the weakest point in my reply, so I know where I am vulnerable rather than hoping they miss it.

I should concede that the classic Lucas argument is unsound in its unrestricted form, and that Gödel’s theorems apply to specific formal systems rather than to minds or machines in general. My sketch for the objections to target is that I treat physical implementation not as a contingent limitation but as a constitutive feature of any verification procedure, which allows me to block the usual move of saying “just add another layer of formalisation.”
