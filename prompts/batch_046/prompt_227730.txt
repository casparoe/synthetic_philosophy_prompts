You are a professional philosopher and speechwriter with deep experience in ethics, aesthetics, and public communication for mixed audiences. Before drafting anything, please think carefully and begin with brief brainstorming: map the conceptual dependencies that make obligation-to-the-dead non-obvious, identify the single concrete scenario that must carry the argument, and list the predictable misconceptions you will need to defuse. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I need you to write a polished five-minute speech for me to deliver.

The occasion is the opening remarks at a public-library workshop titled “Drifting from the Record: Ethics in Historical Film.” The audience is deliberately mixed: independent filmmakers, local historians, a few philosophy graduate students, and relatives of a recently deceased community activist whose life is being dramatized in an upcoming biopic. The director has invented a private scene in which the activist—despite her lifelong public writings and speeches—secretly doubts her own cause; the scene is intended to humanize her, but her family regards it as a fabrication of her inner life.

The philosophical idea must do real structural work, not serve as decoration. I want the speech to navigate the tension between what we might call “narrative autonomy after death”—the interest a dead person retains in not having their documented character contradicted by invented interiority—and “memorial fidelity,” the living’s duty to preserve the coherence of a life story that partly belongs to the community. Use the invented doubt-scene as the carrying example. The remarks should determine exactly what can and cannot be said in defense of artistic license, addressing whether the wrong here is best understood as harm to posthumous interests, betrayal of audience trust, or corruption of collective memory. They must be rigorous enough that the historians and students will not spot fallacies, yet clear enough that family members and filmmakers without philosophical training can follow every step. Specify the order in which the pieces must appear, the point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off (for instance, that artistic license is absolute, that harm requires consciousness, or that family grief alone grants veto power over representation), and what technical distinctions—such as that between harming interests and damaging reputation—should be named but not fully unpacked at this level. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write the remarks in the first person, as if I am speaking to the room, and keep them to roughly five minutes when spoken.
