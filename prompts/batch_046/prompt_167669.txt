I'm planning a fifty-minute session for second-year philosophy of science graduate students on I. J. Good's theorem on the value of information, and I want to avoid the standard medical-testing framing that dominates textbooks. Design the explanatory arc. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual distinction—say, between evidential and causal value of information, or between prior and posterior decision rules—must be fixed before the theorem is even stated, and why reversing that order collapses the result into a tautology. Second, propose one carrying example that involves a sealed predictive record and an irreversible action; specify exactly what feature it must have (information is genuinely free but potentially self-undermining) and what it must lack (direct causal influence on the state) so that the wrong intuition is not triggered. Third, map the misconceptions in the order students will form them: I expect they will first conflate "free information" with "causally inert information," then try to apply the theorem to license evidential reasoning in Newcomb-like predictor environments; identify the precise point in the sequence where each should be blocked. Fourth, provide one diagnostic question that distinguishes students who grasp the dependency structure from those who have merely memorized the non-negativity result. Finally, state explicitly what portion of the formal proof—the expectation over posteriors—I should leave out at this level and say so, and note the one niche extension, namely the tension with causal decision theory in predictor-dependent choice environments, that I should mention only to mark it as beyond the session's scope.
