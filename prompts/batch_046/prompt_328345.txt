I'm currently enrolled in an advanced seminar in social epistemology, and during a recent tutorial session two of my fellow students became entangled in a disagreement about peer disagreement that I think raises issues somewhat beyond the standard readings we've covered. I should be clear that I am reporting this as a bystander: I have not signalled my own view to them, and I should like an impartial adjudication.

The case is this. Leo and Amara are both philosophy postgraduates with equivalent training in modal logic and philosophy of science. After a long discussion of a particular argument in the philosophy of quantum mechanics — specifically, whether an interpretation we'll call Q is more plausible than its principal rivals — Leo concludes that Q has roughly a 70 per cent probability of being correct, whilst Amara assigns it roughly 30 per cent. They agree that they are epistemic peers: they share the same evidence, have debated for several hours, and neither can identify any failure of reasoning in the other. Leo takes this as a textbook case for conciliationism. Because they are symmetrically situated, he argues, rationality requires them both to revise towards the midpoint — approximately 50 per cent — since Amara's disagreement gives Leo defeasible reason to doubt his own assessment, and vice versa.

Amara rejects conciliationism in favour of steadfastness. She argues that she retains first-order access to the internal coherence of her own reasoning chain, and that Leo's disagreement is merely one additional piece of evidence. She does not see why it should automatically outweigh the structural features of her argument that she can verify directly. She then introduces a distinction: she claims that their disagreement operates at the object-level (about interpretation Q itself) rather than at the meta-level (about the reliability of their reasoning processes). Only meta-level disagreement, she suggests, should trigger revision; treating object-level disagreement as if it were meta-level defeat is a category mistake. She also notes that they hold different methodological priors — Leo favours ontological parsimony, Amara favours explanatory completeness — and contends that these framework commitments are not the kind of thing over which peers ought to conciliate, since they are not empirical hypotheses but constitutive principles of interpretation.

Leo responds in two stages. First, he argues that Amara's appeal to first-order access is precisely what peer disagreement undermines: if she could genuinely verify her reasoning independently of any peer check, she would not find herself in a symmetric disagreement with an equally competent philosopher. Second, he suggests that if methodological priors are never subject to conciliation, then philosophy fragments into incommensurable camps with no rational pathway between them, which undermines the very possibility of philosophical progress.

Please go through their exchange move by move. For each of Leo's and Amara's central arguments, indicate who is right about what, where each argument is weakest, and whether either party is missing an important distinction — for instance, between different kinds of peer disagreement, between rational requirements and practical ones, or between suspension of judgement and revision of credence. Finally, say who is more right overall, and whether there is a position that respects both of their concerns without merely averaging their credences.
