I've been working through Derek Parfit's hitchhiker problem on my own—I'm an architect, not a philosopher, but I keep returning to the idea that rational people can bind themselves to act against their immediate interests. In the scenario, a driver in the desert will only take you if she predicts you'll pay her once you reach town, but after you're safely delivered there's no practical reason to hand over the money: the ride is finished, and she can't undo the pickup. Despite that, I feel strongly that paying is still required, not just nice. At the same time, I hold that rationality must be judged from where you actually stand—there is no "view from nowhere" that can turn a local loss into a win. I also think people care about what I might call "narrative coherence": it's rational to want your life to form a consistent story, and breaking a promise fractures that story in a real, if hard-to-measure, way. But I don't think narrative coherence can override local rationality at the exact moment of choice; it can only motivate you to build guardrails—reputation, contracts, habit—so that your local choices align with the larger arc. If those guardrails fail and you're alone with the cash, then refusing to pay is still rational, just damaging to your story. Finally, I think a culture can label certain actions "irrational" by shared convention—like promise-breaking—even when those actions are locally smart, and I want to keep that social judgment separate from the individual decision-theoretic one.
