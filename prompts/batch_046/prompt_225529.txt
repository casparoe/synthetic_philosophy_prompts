Analyze the Anglo-French Concorde program—specifically the decision in the early 1970s to continue development and enter commercial service despite the withdrawal of all American airline orders, the certainty that development costs could never be recovered, and the clear projection that operating the aircraft would generate perpetual losses—as a case study in whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. I want a disciplined mapping rather than a history lesson: identify the actual decision-makers (the British and French governments, the manufacturers, the airline customers, the treaty partners), the concrete options they faced (cancellation with diplomatic and contractual penalties versus continuation with guaranteed operating deficits), and what exactly counts as the “cost” that was sunk. In particular, distinguish—if you think the distinction holds—between direct financial outlays and what we might call credibility-sunk costs tied to diplomatic commitments, technology-spillover promises, and the signaling value of completing a joint European project.

Then apply the theory rigorously. Does standard rational-choice analysis, which treats all unrecoverable past investment as irrelevant to present choice, correctly explain why they continued? Do not merely lay out both sides; make a definite judgment, with reasons, about whether the decision to continue was irrational in the relevant sense and what that implies for the claim that honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Give me that judgment directly, and say explicitly whether the standard theory survives this case intact or needs the conceptual revision I have sketched.
