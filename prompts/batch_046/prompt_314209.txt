At a philosophy department colloquium dinner, two colleagues—Maya and Jonas—got into a sustained disagreement about whether Bostrom-style simulation reasoning should temper what they call far-future fanaticism, by which they mean the extreme prioritization of outcomes trillions of years ahead. I sat with them but stayed out of it, and I genuinely do not know who is right. Can you go through their exchange move by move, say who is right about what, and indicate whether either is missing a distinction that would change the verdict?

Here is what they said, in order.

Maya began by saying that if we grant the simulation argument any non-trivial credence, then heavy investment in the far future starts to look structurally unstable: we might be running inside an ancestor or instrumental simulation that could be truncated, rolled back, or never reach those distant states at all. Near-term suffering we prevent is experienced regardless, but the far-future goods longtermists prize depend on causal continuity that simulation architecture specifically undermines.

Jonas replied that this mistakes ontology for decision theory. Even if we are simulated, the internal future of the simulation is real to its inhabitants; the reasons to care about that internal future are unchanged. The simulation possibility does not remove the value of what happens inside it, so it cannot by itself dampen fanaticism about outcomes within the simulation.

Maya conceded the internal-value point but said Jonas was ignoring an asymmetry she labeled epistemic truncation. Near-term actions have evidentiary support because we observe their causal chains; far-future actions depend on continuity we cannot verify, and in a simulated environment that continuity is mediated by an unknown computational budget and termination conditions. This is not generic risk—like an asteroid—because it specifically erodes our basis for expecting autonomous causal reach into distant states.

Jonas answered that this just collapses simulation uncertainty into ordinary risk-weighting. We always face unknown termination conditions—stellar evolution, vacuum decay, societal collapse—and longtermists already discount for them. Maya’s truncation adds nothing philosophically distinctive; it is just another low-probability failure mode dressed up as an intuition pump for skepticism about long-range agency.

Maya pushed back: physical risks are modelable, but simulation termination is an epistemic black box. We do not know the simulator’s goals, the fidelity requirements, or whether our actions even propagate to base-reality outcomes. This specifically undermines the evidential basis for expecting our present choices to have far-reaching effects, which is exactly what far-future fanaticism requires. It is not that the future is risky; it is that our causal ownership of it becomes opaque.

Jonas said that if Maya’s standard is that causal ownership must be transparent, then no long-term planning survives—not in base reality either, since quantum noise and chaos also make distant causal chains opaque. He accused her of using simulation as a rhetorical device to smuggle in a generic anti-longtermist intuition rather than a distinct philosophical objection. He also noted that if she genuinely believes simulation truncation is likely enough to matter, she should be equally skeptical of any institutional planning beyond a few decades, which she is not—she supports climate policy on century-scale timelines.

Maya replied that climate-scale planning relies on observable physical dynamics within the system, whereas far-future fanaticism often invokes values—like digital consciousness or cosmic resource allocation—that depend on the simulation running long enough and in high enough fidelity to realize them. The truncation threat hits precisely those speculative, high-fidelity dependencies that characterize the most extreme longtermist commitments, not routine institutional planning.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate Maya’s opening, Jonas’s decision-theoretic reply, Maya’s asymmetry and truncation claim, Jonas’s risk-collapse reply, Maya’s black-box rebuttal, Jonas’s transparency-parity and consistency challenge, and Maya’s climate-scale distinction. For each, indicate whether it is clearly right, partly right but overstated, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and whether either side is missing a distinction—perhaps between ancestor and instrumental simulations, or between value internalism and evidential reach—that would change the verdict. If the framing itself rests on a confused premise—for instance, if fanaticism is being used inconsistently, or if the simulation argument cannot actually bear the weight being put on it—say so plainly rather than forcing an adjudication. I want an honest, pushback-inviting assessment: be as critical of the premises of the exchange as you are of the arguments within it.
