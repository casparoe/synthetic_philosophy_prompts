I was recently appointed to the ethics board of a small regional foundation that holds both cultural artifacts and public-health endowments, and in reviewing three allocation proposals I have found my own judgments pulling in directions that feel principled yet contradictory. In the first case we must choose between fully funding climate-controlled preservation of a unique, deteriorating collection of indigenous textile works—objects that embody living techniques that exist nowhere else and that our institution was explicitly founded to safeguard—and fully renovating our public galleries for accessibility and educational programming that serves thousands of residents each year; my verdict is that we ought to preserve the textiles even at the cost of the renovation, because allowing them to decay would be an irreversible betrayal of our specific custodial charge. In the second case we face a choice between digitizing an irreplaceable archive of oral histories from local factory workers—recordings that will vanish forever once the remaining speakers die—and funding a mental-health outreach initiative for the same community, an initiative that other agencies could replicate but that our board is uniquely positioned to launch now; here my verdict is the opposite: we should fund the outreach and allow the archive to lapse, because honoring living persons takes precedence over memorializing them, even though the loss is permanent. These two judgments seem hard to reconcile, since in both cases an irreplaceable cultural record is weighed against a broad, replaceable social benefit, yet I come down differently.

There is also a third situation, purely hypothetical but vivid to me, in which we must choose between saving one of two irreplaceable collections—say, the textiles versus the oral histories—knowing that either choice means the permanent destruction of the other; I feel that whichever we choose, we commit a genuine moral failure that leaves a residue of wrongness, not merely regret. By contrast, when the choice is between two replaceable goods—renovating the galleries versus mounting a temporary exhibition—I experience no such residue, only a straightforward calculation of benefit. Finally, I am convinced that splitting our budget to partially preserve the textiles and partially fund the outreach is worse than choosing one, because dividing resources fails to honor the integrity of either obligation and produces a kind of institutional cowardice.

I have tried to articulate a tentative principle behind these reactions—something like the idea that obligations carry different weights depending on whether they are custodial, directed at specific irreplaceable goods entrusted to us, or distributive, directed at general welfare, with the added claim that conflicts within the custodial domain are tragic and that partial fulfillment is impermissible—but I am unsure whether it can accommodate both of my opposing verdicts without becoming ad hoc. Is there a coherent, non-ad-hoc account of hard choices and incommensurable values that captures the pattern I have described, including the asymmetry between replaceable and irreplaceable conflicts, the presence of moral residue only in certain pairings, and my resistance to compromise solutions? If so, please state the principle or a small set of competing candidates precisely, indicate which clause of your account is motivated by which of my cases, explain what verdict it delivers on the further case of choosing between the textile collection and a climate-adaptation fund that protects an entire neighborhood, and be explicit about which of my judgments—particularly the tension between my first and second verdicts, or my claim that splitting is worse than choosing—it forces me to abandon. I should add that I need the answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written as one might write a thoughtful letter; I am not looking for citations or bibliographies, since I need to work through this without access to a library.
