As a doctoral student in metaphysics with a side interest in philosophy of literature, I am preparing a seminar session on persistence and personal identity, and I want to avoid the usual reductive exercises where novels merely illustrate arguments. Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray strikes me as an especially rich and under-exploited test case for endurantism versus perdurantism: Dorian himself appears to be wholly present at every moment of his life despite radical qualitative change, while the portrait accumulates alterations—aging, corruption, violence—seemingly as successive temporal parts of an object that is otherwise spatially and causally continuous with Dorian. I want a reading that takes the metaphysics seriously—invoking temporal parts, the problem of temporary intrinsics, and the distinction between stage and worm theories—while also letting the novel push back, particularly on whether the portrait can be treated as an external temporal part without violating the internalist commitments of four-dimensionalism, and on whether psychological continuity across Dorian's stages does explanatory work that pure perdurantism renders redundant.

Each point should pair a specific scene or narrative turning point—such as Dorian's initial wish in Chapter 2, Basil's murder in Chapter 20, or the final destruction of the portrait—with a precise claim from the endurantist/perdurantist debate, state where the philosophical framework illuminates the scene and where the literary detail complicates or breaks the analogy, and avoid reducing either the novel or the metaphysics to a mere illustration of the other.
