As a journalist who writes on science and ideas, I am drafting a briefing document for a speaker who must defend a precise thesis before a sceptical, interdisciplinary audience, and I need a rigorous Q&A prep sheet rather than a generic defence. The speaker will present at the joint seminar of the Institute for Historical Studies and the Centre for Technology Policy — an audience comprising contingency-minded historians of science who reject teleological explanation, behavioural economists who study cognitive bias and demand operationalisable mechanisms, and policy advisers who want actionable conclusions and fear that emphasising historical contingency will paralyse decision-making. The thesis being defended is that standard explanations of major technological and institutional transitions suffer from a compound epistemic error that the speaker terms “retrospective path-locking”: the collapse of genuinely open branching points into a single “inevitable” trajectory through the interaction of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, which suppresses what the speaker calls “counterfactual leverage” — the policy-relevant weight of near-miss alternatives that archival evidence actually supports. The speaker proposes that policy-relevant historiography should therefore adopt a “branch-weighted narrative audit” to correct this distortion.

Please prepare the speaker’s objection sheet in British English. Order the objections by how likely they are to arise in this specific room and by how damaging each would be, phrased exactly as these historians, economists, and advisers would phrase them — using their vocabulary of contingency, operationalisation, and actionable risk assessment. For each objection, provide: a concise reply suitable for a two-minute response; an honest note on where that reply is weakest or concedes too much; and a clear indication of what should be conceded up front. Identify the single reply that absolutely has to land for the thesis to survive the session, and name the one objection the speaker is hoping nobody raises. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask for them; keep the focus on the arguments, the dynamics of the room, and the strategic management of concessions.
