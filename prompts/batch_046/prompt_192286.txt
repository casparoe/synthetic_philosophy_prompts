As a retired comparative physiologist who has recently begun working through philosophy of biology in earnest, I serve on the scientific advisory board of the Meridian Institute for Synthetic Ecology. Next Thursday we must vote on whether to approve a three-year grant to Project Aquifer, which proposes to engineer a de novo osmotic-regulation chamber in a brackish-water model organism. The chamber has no evolutionary antecedent; it was designed ab initio to maintain ionic homeostasis through a mechanism not found in any extant lineage. Consequently our board is divided along precisely the line that separates etiological from causal-role accounts of biological function. Members inclined toward etiological views maintain that, absent a selection history, attributing biological function to the chamber is conceptually mistaken, and that our evaluation criteria should therefore be restricted to engineering-performance metrics rather than to functional-success terms drawn from physiology. Members inclined toward causal-role accounts hold that the chamber’s sustained contribution to organismal survival and reproductive capacity is sufficient for functional attribution regardless of origin, and that the grant should be assessed by physiological-outcome measures alone. I need a formal written opinion—no longer than two pages—to circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action: full approval, approval with specified conditions and a six-month pilot review, or rejection with a stated rationale and timeline. The reasoning must treat the strongest version of the opposing view with genuine intellectual seriousness rather than dismissing it, and it must be written so that board members without philosophical training can follow the argument, though several members are familiar with the literature. The distinction between etiological and causal-role accounts should do structural work in the recommendation—shaping what conditions, if any, are appropriate—rather than serving as decorative framing. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific authors by name; I want a document the board can act on without external lookup. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for an institutional memo.
