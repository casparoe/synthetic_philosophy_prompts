As a retired physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy, I have been trying to clarify my own intuitions about individuation and identity across physical, biological, and artificial domains. I should like to know whether they hang together under one principle, or whether I must abandon some of them. Here are my verdicts on six cases.

First, consider the familiar symmetric-universe scenario: two perfectly spherical bodies of identical radius, temperature, and composition, separated by a constant distance in an otherwise empty space. I judge that there are genuinely two objects, not one. Their spatiotemporal relations seem sufficient to distinguish them, even though they share every intrinsic property.

Second, take two electrons in the ground state of a helium atom, with opposite spins but otherwise identical quantum numbers, their wavefunctions overlapping extensively. Here I am inclined to deny that there are two separately identifiable individuals. To speak of “this electron near the nucleus” and “that one farther away” strikes me as a misleading reification; there is only a two-particle system, and any assignment of individual identity is arbitrary.

Third, imagine two human embryos developed in separate artificial wombs, with identical nuclear genomes and indistinguishable phenotypes, never interacting causally. I have no doubt that these are two distinct potential persons. Independent spatiotemporal trajectories and separate causal histories seem fully adequate to individuate them, qualitative identity notwithstanding.

Fourth, suppose two perfect digital emulations of a conscious mind run on separate, physically isolated quantum processors, with identical internal states and no communication link. I am inclined to say there are two distinct subjects of experience, each with its own first-person trajectory, rather than one subject instantiated twice.

Fifth, consider a teletransportation case: a person is scanned with molecular precision, destroyed, and reconstructed on Mars with perfect qualitative fidelity. My verdict is that the Martian replica is not the same person. Spatiotemporal continuity has been broken, and qualitative identity alone cannot preserve personal identity.

Sixth, reflect on a macroscopic crystal whose atoms are replaced one by one with identical substitutes until none of the originals remain, the lattice structure never disrupted. I think the crystal survives as the same object throughout; gradual material replacement does not destroy identity so long as structural and causal continuity are maintained.

These judgments feel coherent, yet they sit uneasily together. Cases one, three, and four allow spatiotemporal separation and independent causal history to individuate qualitatively identical entities; case five shows that continuity is necessary for diachronic identity; but case two denies that separation and causal independence always yield distinct individuals, since quantum symmetry seems to block individuation altogether. Meanwhile, my tentative principle—that an entity counts as a distinct individual just in case it possesses a unique spatiotemporal world-line that is not perfectly coincident with another’s, except where quantum statistics forbid the assignment of separate world-lines—seems to accommodate cases one, three, five, and six, but struggles with case four (do digital minds have world-lines in physical space-time?) and may be ad hoc in case two.

Could you analyse whether there is a single, more elegant principle—perhaps drawing on primitive thisness, causal-profile uniqueness, or informational boundaries—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Furthermore, please apply your proposed account to an unlisted case: two virtual instances of an identical conscious mind running within logically partitioned regions of the same quantum computer, with no physical separation and no causal interaction between the partitions. Would they be one subject or two?

In your response, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
