I serve on the board of the Meridian Cultural Foundation, which oversees a consortium of regional archives. Two years ago we committed $3.8 million to migrate our analog photographic collections to a proprietary digital preservation platform; the vendor collapsed six months ago, leaving roughly forty percent of the collection in an unstable intermediate format. Our technical director recommends abandoning the migration and adopting an open-standard framework, which would require writing off the $3.8 million and spending an additional $1.2 million. A faction of trustees argues that we have a fiduciary duty to finish what we started and salvage the investment by contracting a rescue vendor at an estimated $2.1 million. I just finished a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend who is a decision theorist: she insists that honoring the sunk expenditure is a textbook fallacy and that any recommendation must be strictly forward-looking, comparing only future costs and benefits of the two pathways. I countered that institutional commitments generate a form of diachronic integrity—abandoning a publicly announced preservation mandate mid-stream might erode donor and partner trust in ways that do not appear on a standard cost-benefit ledger, and that this is not merely emotional attachment to past expenditure but a structural feature of institutional rationality. For Thursday's board meeting I need a short, formal written opinion—no more than two pages—that recommends either (a) authorizing the rescue contract with a twelve-month completion deadline, or (b) terminating the project, writing off the loss, and redirecting funds to the open-standard migration with an eighteen-month timeline. The memo should treat the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it: if you recommend cutting losses, you must address the diachronic-integrity concern head-on; if you recommend finishing, you must explain why the forward-looking rationality objection does not defeat the recommendation. It should also specify the concrete conditions—technical, financial, or contractual—under which the board should revisit the decision.
