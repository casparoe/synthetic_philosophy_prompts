I'm trying to pin down my actual commitments about when higher-order evidence should override a first-order judgment, and when sticking to your guns despite knowing you're in a risky reference class is rational rather than epistemically akratic. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases, followed by a tentative principle I'm testing.

Case 1: A mathematician uses a non-obvious heuristic to collapse a lemma. She then learns that mathematicians relying on this heuristic in similar contexts are wrong about thirty percent of the time. She re-checks her proof, finds no visible error, but also can't fully audit every implicit step. Verdict: she should reduce confidence somewhat; the outside view is real evidence even if she can't locate the flaw.

Case 2: A calibrated forecaster assigns 70% to a geopolitical event. She then learns that her forecasting cohort systematically overestimates outcomes in this region by about ten points. However, she also knows she explicitly corrected for that bias in her reasoning, and she can point to the specific adjustments she made. Verdict: she should keep her 70%; the group-level statistic is blocked from applying to her particular reasoning trace.

Case 3: A philosopher believes a modal argument for the existence of abstract objects is sound. She then learns that philosophers with her exact training and temperament split roughly fifty-fifty on the conclusion. She still finds the premises independently compelling and can articulate where she thinks her opponents go wrong. Verdict: she should maintain belief but with slightly reduced confidence; it isn't full akrasia, because peer disagreement distributed across a field doesn't automatically defeat a first-order argument she can still defend.

Case 4: A scientist runs a pre-registered study and gets p = 0.03. She knows her lab has a culture of p-hacking and selective reporting, but her protocol was locked in advance and her analysis plan was fixed. Verdict: she should treat the result as valid and not discount it; the causal pathway that produces false positives in her lab (post-hoc flexibility) doesn't reach this particular study.

Case 5: A decision-maker recognizes she exhibits the sunk-cost fallacy in other domains. In this specific business decision, she feels she genuinely weighed future costs against future benefits, with no emotional attachment to past investment. But she also knows she can't introspectively verify that her reasoning is untainted. Verdict: she should distrust her first-order judgment and act against it; recognizing membership in a biased class is strong higher-order evidence when she lacks an independent audit trail showing this instance is different.

My tentative principle—call it the audit-block view—is that higher-order evidence defeats a first-order belief only when two conditions hold: (i) the error source lies in a reasoning step the agent cannot independently audit or correct, and (ii) the agent lacks specific, non-circular evidence that this particular instance is an exception. The strength of defeat scales with the specificity of the causal pathway from the error source to the belief-formation process.

Does this principle actually deliver all five verdicts? If not, which clause needs repair, and which of my verdicts should I overturn to keep the principle simple? Also, apply it to a case I haven't listed: a chess grandmaster in severe time pressure knows that grandmasters blunder in time trouble about forty percent of the time, but she has studied this exact endgame for hours and can trace a clear line to the winning move. Should she trust her analysis or defer to the base rate?
