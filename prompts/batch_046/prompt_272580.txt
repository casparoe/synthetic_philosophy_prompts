My study group has constructed an argument that takes the familiar inefficacy objection—applied together to climate emissions, meat consumption, and democratic voting—and extends it into a reductio of collective causation itself. The reasoning proceeds in eight steps. First, in each domain the harm or result is genuinely collective: climate damage requires billions of emissions, factory-farmed suffering requires mass demand, and election outcomes require mass voting, with no single act being sufficient. Second, for any particular emission, purchase, or vote, the aggregate outcome occurs whether or not that specific act takes place, since the remaining acts are sufficient to produce it. Third, if an outcome occurs regardless of whether A happens, then A is causally redundant with respect to that outcome. Fourth, causal redundancy entails that A is not a link in the causal chain producing the outcome, because a chain requires that each link be counterfactually necessary for the result. Fifth, therefore none of the individual actions composing the aggregate are causally linked to the collective result. Sixth, but the collective result just is the product of those individual causal links arranged together; if none of the links exist, there is no chain and hence no collective result. Seventh, it follows that climate damage, factory-farmed suffering, and election outcomes are not actually caused by anyone, despite being real phenomena. Eighth, if there is no causal link between individuals and these outcomes, then moral obligations regarding them are obligations with respect to causally orphaned events, which renders the entire framework of collective moral responsibility incoherent rather than merely excusing particular agents.

We reject the conclusion—climate damage clearly is caused, and we want to preserve the intuition that an individual can wrongfully contribute. Which step should we surrender? Please explain why that step seemed defensible, and then price the main alternatives: giving up the counterfactual necessity condition on causation, giving up the aggregation premise that collective results are composed of individual causal links, or giving up the bridge from causal contribution to moral responsibility—indicating what each choice forces its defender to accept about collective action, overdetermination, or the basis of moral evaluation.
