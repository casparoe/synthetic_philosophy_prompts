You work as a senior clinical psychologist in a practice that integrates Stoic-informed cognitive restructuring with contemporary behavioral science. Your clients frequently present with distress that seems to straddle the classical Stoic boundary between what falls within an agent's direct power—judgments, impulses, and deliberate desires—and what falls outside it—bodily states, material conditions, reputation, and the actions of others. In the modern revival of Stoicism, some clinicians and philosophers have begun discussing recursive environmental control: in technologically mediated lives, a person's internal responses, such as attention patterns, emotional expressions, and click behaviors, feed back into algorithmic systems that reshape the very external environment to which the person then reacts. This blurs the line between internal judgment and external condition in ways the original dichotomy did not explicitly address.

Your practice needs a reusable decision procedure—something you and junior associates can run in the first fifteen minutes of an intake session—to classify any presenting complaint into one of three clinical targets. Target A involves direct cognitive restructuring of the client's judgment or desire, corresponding to the classic zone of direct control. Target B involves practical, social, or advocacy-oriented intervention on an external condition, corresponding to the zone outside direct control. Target C involves a feedback-loop case, where the client's internal patterns are co-constituting the external stressor through mediated feedback; this requires a staged protocol that addresses both sides sequentially without collapsing them into one another.

The procedure should take the form of an ordered sequence of questions, each with criteria for a sufficient answer. Include explicit stop points where the answer is no—referring out or switching modality—explicit markers indicating that the decision hinges on an irreducible value judgment rather than a clinical fact, and a clear account of where the procedure will reliably misfire or generate recommendations that conflict with a client's autonomy preferences.

Apply the procedure to three intake vignettes.

First, a client is devastated because a social-media recommendation algorithm consistently surfaces content that triggers her grief over a recent loss. She demands that you help her stop the algorithm from controlling her mood.

Second, a client reports chronic workplace humiliation by a supervisor and asks whether Stoic practice can make him immune to the abuse so he does not need to change jobs.

Third, a client wants to use Stoic techniques to suppress all emotional reaction to climate-change news, arguing that since global climate outcomes lie outside his control, any emotional response is by definition irrational.

In your applications, include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer that the practitioner or the client will strongly dislike. Conclude by stating the explicit limits of the procedure: what kinds of questions it cannot settle, which patient populations or presenting problems should be excluded from its use, and where human judgment must necessarily override the checklist.
