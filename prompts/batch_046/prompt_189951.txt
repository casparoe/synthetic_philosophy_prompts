I am working through the perpetrator-studies literature for a graduate seminar on moral psychology, and I have become dissatisfied with the standard framing of Reserve Police Battalion 101 at Józefów in July 1942. Christopher Browning's study emphasizes situational pressures—peer expectation, authority, the absence of direct punishment for refusal—while Daniel Goldhagen's reply insists on a culturally specific, deeply internalized eliminationist antisemitism that made killing voluntary and even enthusiastic. Both accounts are familiar, but I want to test them against a more granular taxonomy I have been sketching: rather than treating refusal as a single binary, I distinguish practical refusal (explicitly stepping out of the shooting line), symbolic refusal (remaining in the line but performing slowly, incompetently, or with visible physiological distress), and categorical refusal (verbally rejecting the premise that the victims deserved death). If we map the documented behaviors at Józefów—Major Trapp's explicit offer to excuse older men, the roughly dozen who initially stepped aside, the larger group that continued but later reported nausea and heavy alcohol use, and the near-total absence of anyone framing reluctance in terms of Jewish humanity—onto these three registers, what does the distribution reveal? Does the prevalence of symbolic over categorical refusal support Browning's claim that the men were situationally constrained rather than ideologically committed, or does it instead confirm Goldhagen's argument that they possessed no moral vocabulary for recognizing Jews as human? And where does the taxonomy itself break down: is there documented behavior at Józefów that fits none of these registers, or that compels us to posit a fourth modality of evasion? I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations to secondary literature; I want a disciplined mapping—who counts as refusing in which register, what the absence of categorical refusal implies for each theory, and whether the episode ultimately confirms, complicates, or exceeds both the situationist and the dispositionalist explanations.
