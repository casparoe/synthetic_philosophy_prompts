I run a 90-minute workshop for about twelve advanced undergraduates or early graduate students with passing familiarity with democratic theory but no specialist background in institutional design. I want them to feel—through rules and payoffs rather than through framing or lecture—a specific structural tension in legislative selection: career politicians operate under an asymmetric incentive in which omission (letting a long-horizon public system decay through inaction) is electorally invisible and safe, while visible intervention risks blame if it fails; single-term citizens selected by sortition, stripped of re-election incentives and bound by strict post-service employment restrictions, can correct that omission bias, yet the same rotational design and term limits erase institutional memory and expose them to different pathologies such as over-reliance on permanent staff or inability to learn from past errors. Design a runnable, rule-based simulation or structured role-play divided into three 20-minute rounds. The group of twelve should be split into two cohorts—six “career” legislators with multi-round tenure, campaign-reputation scores, and re-election thresholds, and six “sortition” legislators serving exactly one round with no return, no campaign score, and a binding post-service restriction—tasked with stewarding a shared resource or infrastructure system that degrades invisibly unless actively maintained, where outcomes unfold with a one-round delay. The mechanics, not the facilitator’s commentary, should make the dynamics palpable: career members should discover that passive inaction preserves their reputation while risky maintenance exposes them to visible, attributable failure; sortition members should discover they can authorize bold action but lack continuity to correct missteps, verify staff reports, or build on prior rounds. Include precise rules, role descriptions, payoff structures, and any materials needed. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments from each round onto the mechanisms of omission bias, institutional amnesia, and bureaucratic capture; predicts what will predictably go wrong in execution (for example, career members colluding to blame staff for decay, or sortition members misreading hidden degradation signals because they inherit no records); states frankly which feature of real-world democratic design the simulation cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply in discussion; and admits what the exercise necessarily oversimplifies. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Finally, invite pushback: if the premise that this tension can be made palpable in ninety minutes without collapsing democratic theory into caricature is confused, or if the task as posed asks for something this format cannot deliver well, say so plainly instead of complying politely. Keep everything self-contained for a respondent without web access.
