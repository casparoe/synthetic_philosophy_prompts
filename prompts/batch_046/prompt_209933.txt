Question: Machiavelli argues that fortune governs about half our actions. Explain his distinction between fortune and virtù, and assess whether he thinks a ruler can ever be fully secure against misfortune.

Student answer:
Machiavelli treats fortune as an unpredictable external force, comparing it to a river that floods unless you build dykes in calm weather. Virtù is not Christian moral virtue but practical skill — energy, cunning and foresight. In The Prince he says fortune controls about half of what happens to us, leaving the rest to our free will. To cope, a ruler must be both lion and fox, using force and deception as needed. The key point is that virtù requires an unchanging, rock-like constancy; if a ruler maintains this steadfast nature regardless of circumstances, he can withstand fortune like a rock withstands water. In The Discourses Machiavelli claims that well-ordered republican institutions can actually remove fortune from politics altogether, leaving only errors of human judgement. Therefore, with enough personal and institutional virtù, complete security is achievable.
