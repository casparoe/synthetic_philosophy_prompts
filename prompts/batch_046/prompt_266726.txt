In a seminar on moral revolutions—specifically the debate over whether norms like dueling and slavery collapsed through moral argument, economic interest, or shifts in honor—my colleague keeps deploying the line “honor killed dueling; reason never did” to dismiss any claim that abolitionist or anti-dueling arguments had independent causal or normative force. She treats the slogan as if it establishes that moral philosophy is epiphenomenal to historical change. Before our next session I want a precise disambiguation I can bring to the table.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that slogan might express? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but questionable, or false; and specify which versions actually license her dismissive move against the relevance of moral argument. In particular, separate causal claims (about what psychologically or socially moved agents) from conceptual claims (about what counts as a reason for abandoning a practice) from normative claims (about what ought to have driven the change). Then address two intermediate possibilities that rarely come up in our discussions: first, that moral arguments operate only when they are translated into honor-terms, so that “reason” never acts naked but always wears honor’s clothing; and second, that honor-shifts themselves embody implicit practical reasoning, so that calling the change “honor-driven” already smuggles in a rational structure. Say for each intermediate reading whether it saves the slogan, collapses it into something banal, or reveals a false dichotomy. Present the menu so that I can point to exactly which entry she is relying on when she uses the line to end debate.
