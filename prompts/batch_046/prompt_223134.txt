I write about science and ideas for a magazine audience, and I am trying to make sense of a long-standing deadlock in Holocaust historiography that seems impervious to new archives. Christopher Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 argues that the perpetrators were largely ordinary men swept up in situational dynamics—peer pressure, bureaucratic distancing, and the incremental normalization of killing—while Daniel Goldhagen contends that a deep-seated, specifically German eliminationist antisemitism made them willing executioners who required little external coercion. Both camps accept the same core behavioral evidence—high rates of voluntary participation, minimal punishment for refusal, and the absence of explicit selection for ideological fervor—yet they construct incompatible moral-psychological portraits from it. I want you to diagnose why this disagreement has stalled for so long.
