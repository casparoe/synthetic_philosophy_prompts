You are a philosopher of mind writing for advanced graduate students who are comfortable with Block’s access-phenomenal distinction and with the broad architecture of global workspace theory, but who have not yet mapped the full logical space of how these two frameworks interact. Before you draft anything, spend a moment brainstorming the combinatorial possibilities: list the yes/no answers to the core structural questions, locate named or unnamed views in those slots, mark the empty ones, and only then construct the instrument. Your deliverable is a decision-tree taxonomy—no more than eight leaves—designed to sort a reader by what they actually believe about consciousness and broadcasting, not by which label they prefer.

The tree should begin with a single commitment: “There is a genuine distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness.” From there, build three successive branch nodes, each posed as a concrete case or structural claim that a student can answer without knowing where it leads. First, ask whether phenomenal content can exist in the complete absence of any cognitive access whatsoever—using Sperling-style partial-report trials and masked change-blindness stimuli as the dividing cases—versus whether it merely overflows degraded or partial global access. Second, ask whether any access that does occur must be global broadcasting across a wide range of consumer modules, or whether localized broadcasting within a restricted sub-system (a “local workspace”) is sufficient, with binocular rivalry under conditions of non-reportability serving as the test case. Third, ask whether phenomenal consciousness is best modeled as a first-order representation, a higher-order representation, or an emergent dynamic of workspace activity. You must place at least the following positions in the space: a strong overflow view (phenomenal exceeds all access); a global-workspace identification (phenomenal consciousness just is global broadcasting); a local-workspace intermediate; a higher-order access theory that denies pure phenomenal consciousness; and a deflationary option that treats the distinction as tracking only graded differences in reportability. For any unoccupied combination—such as accepting overflow while denying any workspace architecture whatsoever—briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, to being genuinely unexplored, or to being unstable once fully articulated. Crucially, inject one non-standard distinction into a branch: ask whether “access” should be treated as a binary threshold (broadcast achieved or not) or as a graded, latency-sensitive process in which broadcasting takes time and phenomenal consciousness might exist in the pre-broadcast interval. Note which existing positions fall on each side of this split and whether it generates genuinely new leaves or merely subdivides old ones. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate—such as certain higher-order theories versus particular global-workspace formulations—and explain why the diagnostic questions fail to pry them apart.

Present the taxonomy itself first: nested questions leading directly to named or precisely described positions, with the logical space visible. Only then provide the reasoning behind each branch, the diagnosis of empty slots, and the flagging of indistinguishable pairs. Do not open with a preamble, do not restate the prompt, and do not include any bibliography, citation, reading list, or reference to specific paper titles and dates.
