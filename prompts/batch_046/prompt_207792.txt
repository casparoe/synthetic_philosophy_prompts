I have been reading some popular essays on the ethics of abortion and have found myself caught between two arguments that seem to point in different directions, yet I cannot see clearly how they relate. One is Judith Thomson's well-known thought experiment in which a person is kidnapped and hooked up to a famous violinist who needs the use of their body for nine months to survive; the idea, as I understand it, is that even if the violinist has a right to life, that right does not automatically oblige the other person to remain physically connected for the entire duration. The other is the claim, often linked to Don Marquis, that abortion is gravely wrong because it deprives a foetus of a future like ours—something that, if left unharmed, would go on to enjoy experiences, relationships and projects comparable to those of any adult.

I would like to compare these two lines of reasoning, but not in the usual way. Instead of asking whether bodily autonomy trumps the value of future life, I want to examine whether either argument can survive a particular challenge: the idea that moral status might not be an on-off switch but rather accumulates gradually, like a curve that is shallow in the earliest weeks, steepens around sentience or viability, and only reaches full weight at birth. Does Thomson's reasoning about the right to withdraw support depend on the assumption that the being being supported already possesses full moral status? Or does her argument work just as well if the being in question has only partial or minimal status? And on the other side, does Marquis's appeal to a future like ours require that future to be either wholly present or wholly absent, or can it accommodate the notion that destroying an early embryo removes only a tiny fraction of future value, whereas destroying a foetus near term removes a far larger fraction?

There is also a scaling question that keeps bothering me. In Thomson's scenario, our intuitions about whether one must stay connected seem to shift with the length of time involved—nine minutes feels clearly permissible to disconnect, nine months feels genuinely ambiguous, and nine years feels like a major moral burden. I wonder whether Marquis's argument is sensitive to scale in the same way. If the future that is lost is long and rich, the wrongness seems severe; but if the potential future consists of only a few days or weeks of experience, does the wrongness shrink proportionally? I am not looking for a list of sources or a history of the debate—simply a careful comparison of these two arguments, with particular attention to whether they are structurally binary or can be reshaped to fit this kind of gradualist, scaling intuition without losing their force.
