I'm writing a feature on how epistemic modesty is being stress-tested in high-stakes expert forecasting, and I witnessed a sharp disagreement at a recent interdisciplinary workshop that I think captures a deeper conceptual tension. Two researchers—call them Dr. A and Dr. B—were debating how a climate-modeling panel should calibrate its confidence in predicting a specific nonlinear tipping-point cascade by 2040. Each panelist has direct access to her own inside reasoning: the physical mechanisms she has modeled, the sensitivity analyses she has run, and the anomalies she has personally flagged. At the same time, the panel knows the outside-view record: over the past four decades, expert groups forecasting geophysical nonlinearities have systematically underestimated transition speeds by a factor of roughly two, and individual experts rarely revise their own confidence downward when shown that base rate.

Dr. A argued that genuine epistemic modesty requires treating the inside reasoning as largely epistemically inert—she should weight the outside-view base rate heavily, perhaps even treating her own mechanistic understanding as noise—because she possesses no privileged, verifiable access to whether she is one of the rare experts who avoids the historical bias. Dr. B responded with three distinct moves. First, he said that if every expert collapsed her reasoning into the base rate, forecasting could never improve; modesty would harden into a self-fulfilling paralysis that prevents the reference class itself from being updated. Second, he claimed that selecting an aggregation rule—how much to weight inside versus outside evidence—is a second-order methodological choice that should itself be guided by the best available reasoning, including an assessment of whether the historical reference class is too heterogeneous to apply. Third, he charged that Dr. A's position secretly depends on an inside-view judgment about her own likely position in the distribution—specifically, that she is probably not the outlier—which is itself an unmodest first-order belief and therefore self-undermining.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move: rate Dr. A's central claim and each of Dr. B's three counter-moves on a spectrum from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then address what both appear to be missing: is there a principled distinction between applying epistemic modesty to belief content versus applying it to belief-formation procedures, and can that distinction resolve when the outside view should dominate as a prior rather than merely constrain confidence at the margins? I am particularly interested in whether either party has conflated rational deference to a track record with an illegitimate surrender of reasoning autonomy.
