I am redesigning an undergraduate module on philosophy of science, and a recent classroom failure has made the topic personally urgent: I presented Einstein's elevator and Schrödinger's cat as stripped-down logical arguments, and my students treated them as puzzles with correct answers rather than as exercises in extending conceptual imagination. I now want to teach Flatland by Edwin Abbott Abbott—not merely as an analogy for dimensional reasoning, but as a full narrative thought experiment—asking whether its fictional form is essential to its epistemic function. Read the novel through the debate over how scientific thought experiments yield knowledge: do they work by revealing internal contradictions in a conceptual scheme, by functioning as mental models that train intuition, or by isolating variables in a counterfactual scenario? Focus especially on A. Square's resistance to the Sphere's testimony and on the social satire that frames geometric discovery as a threat to established order. Does the narrative unreliability and the protagonist's gradual, reluctant belief-revision illuminate the psychological dynamics by which scientists come to accept counterintuitive results? Or does the allegorical dimension—Abbott's critique of Victorian hierarchy—contaminate the thought experiment with values that empirical reasoning ought to exclude? I am looking for a reading that takes both the philosophy of science and the novel seriously: one that does not reduce Flatland to an illustration of dimensional reasoning, but that also does not distort epistemology to accommodate the plot. Where does the work genuinely complicate the standard philosophical account—perhaps by suggesting that some scientific knowledge is partly knowledge about our own cognitive and social resistance to novelty? And where does the philosophical framework expose limits in what the novel can claim to demonstrate?
