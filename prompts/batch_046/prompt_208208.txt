I was arguing with a friend late into the night about whether Zhuangzi's reaction to his wife's death — drumming on a tub and singing, treating her passing as a transformation in the larger flow rather than as a loss — offers real consolation or whether it secretly undermines the very particularity that makes love and grief intelligible. She insists that if death is only a shift in the configuration of qi, then what we mourn is never a person but merely a temporary arrangement, and that Zhuangzi's framework dissolves the boundary between self and world so thoroughly that there is no "you" left to miss. I think there is something more subtle going on: that the denial of a fixed self applies to the griever as well, and that the song is not indifference but a different mode of holding the other.

I'd like a poem that stages this tension structurally. Write it as a pantoum, using the form's repeating lines — which return in altered positions — to mirror the idea that identity is not a stable substrate but a pattern that reappears in new relations. Specify at least two refrains: one that names the tub or the song, and one that names the boundary between the self and the ten thousand things. Crucially, arrange the repetition so that each recurrence strips away a layer of personal reference; by the final stanza, the "I" should have dispersed into the form itself, leaving the reader uncertain whether the voice is Zhuangzi's, the wife's, or the process of transformation speaking through both. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the Zhuangzi recognizes the view — the refusal to treat death as harm to a persisting subject, the idea of entering a different state within an ongoing transformation — but it should not quote or paraphrase any canonical passage verbatim. Do not resolve the tension; write it as if to close a seminar on comparative philosophy, where the goal is not to celebrate the view but to ask whether it can retain the particularity it claims to transcend.
