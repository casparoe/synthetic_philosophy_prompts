I've been arguing with a friend in my comparative politcs reading group about whether constitutional monarchy is just a museum piece or actually does philosophical work, and I'm stuck. In the UK, Scandinavia, and Japan you've got this odd institutional design where a hereditary monarch formally appoints the prime minister, gives assent to every statute, and in Denmark and Norway still heads the state church — but hasn't refused a bill since 1708, never picks a PM against the election result, and in Japan the Emperor performs formal state functions literally on cabinet advice with no independent initiative. Meanwhile democratic sovereignty is supposed to sit with the people or with parliament. So there's a tension: the practice keeps someone born into the job as the formal apex of authority, but strips them of every substantive choice, which looks like it violates democratic equality unless there's some hidden theory licensing the split.

What I'm trying to figure out is what philosophical commitments this pattern actually presupposes. Does it require Bagehot's old distinction between dignified and efficient parts of government — the crown supplies continuity and awe, ministers supply actual decision — or is that just one compatible story? Could a pure popular-sovereignty view accommodate a hereditary symbol that holds formal veto powers it never uses, or does the practice quietly assume that legitimacy needs a non-elected anchor separate from partisan politics? And there's the religious dimension: in Norway the monarch is literally head of the Church of Norway despite being a constitutional figurehead with no legislative role. Does that presuppose a particular view about the separation of spiritual and temporal authority, or about representation?

Can you reconstruct the implicit theory of sovereignty, representation, and value that makes this design coherent? I want you to distinguish what the practice strictly needs — what would have to change if the underlying view were dropped — from what just fits neatly with it. Also, is there an internal inconsistency between treating the monarch as above politics in one context and as a state employee funded by taxpayers in another?
