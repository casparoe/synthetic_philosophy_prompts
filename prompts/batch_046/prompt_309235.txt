Here is an argument I have drafted for a graduate seminar. I now find it suspicious because it seems to prove far more than its premises warrant—perhaps that no necessary being can be genuinely atemporal, or that every attempt to combine contingency and kalam reasoning collapses into an infinite regress of creators. I want you to tell me precisely which premise or inferential step ought to be rejected, explain why that step initially looks compelling, and price the cost of giving it up.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Every being whose non-existence is conceivable without contradiction is contingent, and every contingent being requires a fully sufficient explanation for its existence that lies outside itself. (This is the contingency version of the cosmological argument.)

(2) If an explanation is itself contingent, it requires a further explanation; hence either there is an infinite explanatory regress, or the chain terminates in a being that is not contingent.

(3) An actually infinite explanatory regress is impossible, by reasoning analogous to the kalam argument: in such a regress no member possesses an independent ground, so nothing in the chain is ever genuinely explained.

(4) Therefore there exists at least one necessary being, N, which is its own explanation and the ground of all contingent beings.

(5) Now apply the kalam principle directly to N: if N begins to exist, it requires a cause. But N is necessary, so it does not begin. Hence N is eternal—either timeless or everlasting.

(6) If N is timeless (entirely outside time), then its causal relation to the temporal world must be symmetric: an atemporal cause cannot stand ‘before’ its effect in any temporal sense, nor can it change its relation to the world from absence to presence, since change requires time.

(7) But the world does have a beginning (as argued by kalam reasoning applied to temporal succession). So N must stand in an asymmetric relation to the world: present in causing the beginning, yet not causing an eternal world.

(8) That asymmetry requires an explanation. Since N is timeless and unchanging, the explanation cannot lie within N. It must lie either in the world itself (which is circular, since the world is the effect) or in a further being, M, which determines why N exercises creative power at this point rather than eternally.

(9) By (1)–(4), M must itself be either contingent (requiring further explanation) or necessary. If necessary, then M is also timeless, and the same asymmetry problem applies to M’s relation to N. If contingent, it requires a ground, and we are in an infinite regress—contradicting (3).

(10) Therefore, accepting both the contingency version (with explanatory termination) and the kalam version (with temporal initiation) forces us into an infinite regress of necessary creators, each explaining why the prior one exercised creative power asymmetrically.

(11) But (3) forbids infinite regresses. Hence we derive a contradiction from premises (1)–(5).

(12) The contradiction shows that one of these premises must be abandoned. If we preserve (1) and (4)—the claim that contingency demands termination in a necessary ground—then we must deny either the kalam principle in (5) or the claim that asymmetry requires explanation in (8).

Please analyse this argument with particular attention to whether the move from (6) to (8) commits a modal scope fallacy or conflates de dicto and de re necessity.
