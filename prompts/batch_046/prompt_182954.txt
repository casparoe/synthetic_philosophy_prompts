Write the full specification of desiderata that a theory of self-deception must meet in order to count as adequate, with particular attention to whether the phenomenon is genuinely possible rather than reducible to ordinary error or strategic self-manipulation. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure—such as the need to explain denial of addiction, refusal to acknowledge relational betrayal, and systematic overestimation of one's own motives—without collapsing self-deception into either wishful thinking or dissociative partitioning. Include the constraint that the theory must accommodate register-based partitioning: the agent assigns contradictory contents different weights in practical versus theoretical reasoning rather than storing them in isolated mental modules, and must explain how the false belief can guide action while the true belief remains accessible for other inferences. Distinguish non-negotiable conditions—e.g., that the account not invoke a homuncular deceiver, that it preserve enough rational unity to keep self-deception distinguishable from clinical dissociation, and that it not make belief-formation intentionally controllable—from camp-specific preferences, such as whether the suppressed belief must be genuinely unconscious or whether explicit motivational causation is required. Then state whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, especially the combination of avoiding a divided self and avoiding direct intentional belief-manipulation. Finally, briefly score how intentionalist, deflationary, and modular-partition accounts fare against the list, keeping the weight on the criteria rather than on the scorecard.
