The expanding-circle framework treats moral progress primarily as the widening of direct moral consideration to groups previously excluded. Yet this framing leaves unresolved whether progress also demands a deepening of obligations to those already within the circle; whether there is a principled stopping point before universal inclusion (for instance, with artificial agents, ecological systems, or distant future beings); and whether institutional design should prioritize extending membership or strengthening protection for current members. A further tension—rarely examined—is whether “circle contraction,” the deliberate narrowing of obligations to preserve core commitments, can ever be morally justified rather than merely a failure of progress.

Assume a research program with limited funding and a five-year horizon aims to take the expanding-circle model seriously for both normative theory and institutional design. Rank the following four lines of work by importance and tractability, and defend the ordering with explicit criteria: (a) formalizing non-collapsing criteria for moral standing that avoid reducing membership solely to sentience-based thresholds; (b) empirical investigation of whether moral-circle shifts are driven more by institutional pressure or by cultural narrative, and which lever is more durable; (c) normative analysis of whether and when circle contraction is permissible, and what safeguards would distinguish legitimate boundary-drawing from rationalized exclusion; (d) practical frameworks for allocating scarce moral and political resources between deepening obligations inside the existing circle and extending them outward.

Your answer should identify the single crux—conceptual, empirical, or normative—whose resolution would most change how effort is divided among the other three lines. Explain what evidence or argument would genuinely shift your own ranking.
