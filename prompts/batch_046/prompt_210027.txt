As a novelist who often constructs narratives around impossible ethical choices—scenes in which a character must violate one solemn vow to honor another—I find myself repeatedly returning to the philosophical debate over whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible. For decades, the same structural positions have circulated: one camp maintains that morality, like logic, must be consistent and therefore can never genuinely command contradictory actions, while another camp, drawing on the phenomenology of tragic choice, insists that some conflicts leave unavoidable moral residue—guilt, regret, or what is sometimes called agent-regret—that no resolution can fully dissolve. Despite extensive discussion, neither side has yielded significant ground. Rather than asking you to adjudicate the dispute, I want a diagnosis of why it has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is.

In particular, please examine whether the impasse is substantive, verbal, or structural. Is there a hidden ambiguity in the term "genuine"—does it denote logical irreconcilability within the normative system itself, or does it denote practical unavoidability accompanied by lasting moral cost? Or do the sides implicitly operate with different models of normative coherence: one treating conflict as evidence of a system failure that must be eliminated, the other treating conflict as evidence of moral richness or structural overdetermination, in which obligations can genuinely pull in opposing directions without either being canceled? I want you to defend your diagnosis by referring to what each side actually claims about moral residue, practical reason, and the conditions for coherence, not by surveying named positions or texts.

Then, given that diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress. Would it require a conceptual distinction that disentangles logical contradiction from practical overdetermination? A formal argument demonstrating that one model of coherence collapses into self-undermining requirements? Or should the question be abandoned in favor of a different inquiry—perhaps about how agents ought to live with unavoidable loss rather than about whether morality permits contradiction?

Finally, please separate clearly in your response what is settled among philosophers who know this topic—such as the formal structure of conflicting obligations and the distinction between overriding and outweighing—from what remains genuinely contested, including the interpretation of moral residue and the proper criteria for normative coherence. Do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
