I'm a backend dev with basically no philosphy background, but I got into this weird argument with my roommate about why cities rename some streets but leave up statues of literally the same people. I read about this municipal heritage board policy — I think a few european cities are trying it? — where they don't just vote "bad guy" yes or no. Instead they run every monument through two filters. First, was the harm "structural" (like being part of an economy that exploited people) versus "agentic" (the person personally designed or enforced it). Second, there's this "moral depreication schedule," like accounting, where the moral weight of an act decays over generations unless the monument is actively used to celebrate that harm today. So a statue of an 18th-century merchant who traded in enslaved people stays up because his harm was structural and depreciated over 200 years, but a street named after a 1950s mayor who signed segregation laws gets renamed because it's agentic and hasn't depreciated yet. They also treat the medium differently: street names are "functional infrastructure" so renaming is just administrative cleanup, but statues are "symbolic capital" so removing one counts as erasing history.

What I can't figure out is what view of moral responsibility or collective memory this whole setup actually presupposses. Does it only hang together if you believe that only direct intentions make someone blameworthy across time? Or is there a hidden theory about public space — that functional things shouldn't carry moral weight but symbolic things should? And is this even consistent with itself? They keep a statue that symbolizes a harmful system but rename a street that points to the exact same system. Could the same outcomes be licensed by a totally different philosophy, like a reparative-justice view that cares about present harm to descendants, or is the practice locked into one specific view? I also want to know where it's underdetermined: are there parts of the policy that don't actually need this depreciation idea, or could you swap in another theory and still get the same renames and removals?

Finally, if we replaced their implicit theory with a straightforward one — like, if you caused serious harm, you don't get public honors, full stop, regardless of time, structure, or whether the harm was personal — what exactly would have to change? Would they have to tear down half the statues in the city, or just apply the rule consistently across streets and statues? Don't just list philosophers; I want the actual commitments reconstructed, and flag the cases where the practice doesn't actually force one answer.
