A neurologist acquaintance recently discussed a patient who, after sustaining a localized cortical injury, reports vivid burning sensations in his left hand upon exposure to moderate heat. He describes the quality with precision, assigns high ratings on standardized intensity scales, and can reliably distinguish the sensation from other tactile stimuli. Nevertheless, he does not withdraw his hand, shows no signs of emotional distress, actively refuses analgesic treatment, and states that he has no desire for the sensation to stop; he behaves, in every observable respect, as though nothing adverse is occurring. My acquaintance argues that in the absence of suffering, protective behavior, or any request for relief, there is no ethical urgency to suppress the sensation, and that describing it as harmful would confuse a raw sensory report with a genuinely bad experience. I feel there is still something wrong with allowing a destructive bodily signal to persist unopposed simply because the patient is indifferent to it, but I cannot articulate what makes the state bad if neither its felt unpleasantness nor its motivational force is present. Is my acquaintance correct that the absence of aversion removes the harm, or does the sensation constitute a genuine injury to the patient regardless of his reaction, and what would justify treating it as such?
