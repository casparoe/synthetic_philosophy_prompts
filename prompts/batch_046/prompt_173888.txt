I am a retired physicist who, late in life, has begun reading philosophy with the habits of an empirical scientist intact, and I have come to hold the following view about promissory obligation: a promise binds not because the utterance carries an intrinsic normative charge, nor because it is a bare social convention like driving on the right, but because it initiates an emergent, dissipative normative structure—analogous to a non-equilibrium steady state in statistical mechanics—that requires continuous social energy expenditure (reputation tracking, anticipatory emotional sanction, reciprocal expectation) to maintain its coherence. On this account, the "ought" of keeping one's word is the macroscopic manifestation of a local stability condition in cooperative interaction; if the maintenance costs (social visibility, future interaction probability, emotional investment) fall below a critical threshold, the binding force does not weaken linearly but collapses discontinuously, much like a phase transition. This explains both the phenomenology of guilt and the cross-cultural prevalence of promise-keeping without invoking a sui generis moral fact.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which observations, arguments, formal results, or conceptual distinctions would count against it, sorted by how decisive they would be? In particular: are there well-documented cases in which promises retain full binding force under conditions of perfect anonymity and zero repetition—where the dissipative structure has no substrate to maintain? Could neurobiological or cross-cultural evidence show that the psychological weight of promise-breaking is invariant to social-cost parameters, undermining the claim that the norm is sustained by external energy flow? Would formal results in evolutionary game theory—showing that stable cooperation requires no explicit commitment mechanism at all—render the dissipative-structure account redundant rather than explanatory? I also want to know what defenders of this view, including myself, should concede as genuinely damaging: for instance, if the emotional response to breaking a promise proves to be driven primarily by an intrinsic reward mechanism rather than by anticipated social cost, does that collapse the account into something closer to internalism?

Please distinguish evidence that would be merely awkward—requiring qualification—from evidence that would be fatal. Say whether the view makes any distinctive predictions that rival accounts (contractualist, trust-based, or virtue-theoretic) do not, and whether it risks being insulated into unfalsifiability by retreating to the claim that "bindingness is present exactly when the dissipative conditions are satisfied." If it has been so qualified, is that a legitimate precision or a defect for a view of this kind? Finally, please write your answer in American English.
