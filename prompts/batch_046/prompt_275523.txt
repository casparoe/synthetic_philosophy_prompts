Consider the following exam question and a student’s response. The question asks whether Quine’s naturalism and his criterion of ontological commitment can be extended—via an analog of the Quine-Putnam indispensability argument—to defend the ontological standing of moral properties.

Question: Quine’s criterion of ontological commitment binds a theory only to those entities that must be values of bound variables in its regimented formulation, and his naturalism treats mature empirical science as the final arbiter of what exists. Some philosophers have proposed that moral categories might earn ontological admission in the same way mathematical entities do: if normative distinctions are indispensable to our best social-scientific explanations of coordination, conflict, and institutional behavior, then Quine’s framework should recognize them. Evaluate this proposal. In particular, address (a) whether Quine’s rejection of the analytic-synthetic distinction removes any principled boundary between factual and normative revision; (b) whether his holism about belief applies with uniform strength and in the same manner to logical, empirical, and moral statements; and (c) whether the parallel with mathematical entities holds, given Quine’s treatment of abstract objects and his pragmatic attitude toward ontology.

Student’s answer: Quine’s naturalism treats all cognition as continuous with empirical science, so his ontological-commitment criterion applies directly and exclusively to entities quantified over in mature physics and biology. Moral properties, not being indispensable to these core disciplines, are therefore excluded from the ontology. This exclusion is reinforced by Quine’s holism: since every belief—including logical, mathematical, and normative ones—is in principle revisable, moral beliefs are revisable in exactly the same way and to the same degree as hypotheses about elementary particles. Consequently, normative claims enjoy no special epistemic shelter; they must be treated as provisional conjectures subject to direct empirical falsification. The Quine-Putnam argument succeeds for numbers because they are literally embedded in physical equations, whereas moral values never appear in such formalisms, so the analogy collapses entirely. Moreover, because Quine rejects the analytic-synthetic distinction, he eliminates any sharp separation between fact and value, which entails that normative discourse is not an autonomous domain but merely a pragmatic extension of scientific reasoning. From all this it follows that Quine is best interpreted as a moral error theorist: moral language pretends to denote entities that his ontology denies. At the same time, this does not make him a logical-positivist emotivist, since Quine allows that normative language can still serve practical coordinating functions within the web of belief, provided we do not mistake its utility for literal quantification over real properties.
