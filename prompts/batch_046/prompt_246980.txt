I am preparing the syllabus for an interdisciplinary seminar that bridges Victorian evolutionary biology with modern game-theoretic models of cooperation, and I have become preoccupied with a counterfactual that I hope you can help me think through. Charles Darwin died decades before the prisoner's dilemma was formally articulated, and long before evolutionary biologists began using one-shot and iterated versions of the game to study the origins of altruism. Suppose Darwin were presented with the contemporary landscape: not only the basic payoff structure of mutual cooperation versus defection, but also the finding that stable cooperation can emerge in large, anonymous populations through mechanisms such as direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity mediated by reputation, spatial or network reciprocity, and multilevel selection. Given his documented reluctance to invoke selection above the level of the individual organism—except perhaps in his occasional remarks on the social instincts of ants and bees—how do you think he would have assessed the explanatory value of these mechanisms for the cooperative phenomena he observed in nature?

To complicate the counterfactual further, consider a relatively niche result from synthetic evolutionary biology: in engineered microbial consortia that exchange essential metabolites, mutant "cheater" strains that withhold contribution can proliferate initially, yet often drive the local population to collapse so quickly that defection becomes self-undermining even within a single interaction round, since the cheaters destroy the shared resource pool on which they depend. Would Darwin likely have interpreted this rapid population-level collapse as a vindication of group selection that he largely resisted, or rather as an extreme, emergent consequence of relentless individual-level competition?
