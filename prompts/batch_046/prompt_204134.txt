I serve on an interdisciplinary clinical review board, and a recent case—where an experienced nurse’s direct observation of a patient’s deterioration was initially dismissed in favor of a senior physician’s theoretical model—has made me wonder whether remedying testimonial injustice can ever avoid generating a symmetrical wrong. I have sketched an argument that seems to show the remedy is self-defeating, and I want it pressure-tested. Here are the steps.

(1) Testimonial injustice is wrong precisely because it assigns a speaker less credibility than they merit, where the deficit is causally sustained by prejudice tied to the speaker’s social identity rather than to their epistemic relation to the claim.
(2) Correcting such an injustice requires restoring the speaker’s credibility to its objectively merited level.
(3) In any collective deliberative context—such as a board weighing competing clinical interpretations—credibility functions as a positional good: attention, deference, and decision-weight are finite, so improving speaker A’s rank necessarily lowers the relative rank of at least one competitor B.
(4) If B belongs to the group that previously enjoyed the inverse prejudice (the privilege corresponding to A’s marginalization), then lowering B’s relative credibility, even to B’s true merit, employs B’s group membership as the operative basis for the reduction.
(5) Employing group membership to lower credibility is structurally identical to the wrong in (1): it is testimonial injustice directed at B.
(6) Therefore, correcting testimonial injustice for A necessarily involves committing testimonial injustice against B.
(7) But if an action necessarily involves committing the very wrong it seeks to remedy, that action is self-defeating and ought not to be performed.
(8) So we ought not correct testimonial injustice.

This strikes me as suspicious—surely we should still correct the injustice—but I cannot locate the slip. I would most like to preserve (1) and (7), though I am open to sacrificing either if the cost is lower elsewhere. Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what do the main alternatives—those who deny that credibility is a positional good, those who distinguish corrective lowering from prejudicial lowering, and those who reject the symmetry claim—each have to pay to reject it? Finally, please say what would change your mind: which specific argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where is your own reasoning here most likely to fail?
