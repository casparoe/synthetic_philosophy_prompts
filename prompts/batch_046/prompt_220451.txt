My friend and I just finished a long and somewhat heated argument about what ethical obligations we owe to real, dead people when their lives are dramatized in historical fiction and biopics—specifically whether there is a principled line between altering someone’s public moral record and inventing details about their private, undocumented interior life. To see whether her view is genuinely consistent, I want to apply it to the track record of the small archival dramatization board she serves on, which reviews proposals for films and theatrical works based on documented lives. Their past rulings are these. They approved a biopic that invented a romantic attachment for a documented asexual public figure, on the grounds that emotional accessibility for modern audiences justifies minor relational fabrication provided the figure’s documented public actions and choices remain untouched. They rejected a proposal that showed a documented resistance leader negotiating with an occupying force in a scene with no archival basis, on the grounds that inventing a specific political compromise misattributes moral agency in ways that reshape the audience’s judgment of documented choices. They approved with conditions a film that collapsed three years of documented correspondence into a single dramatic confrontation, requiring explicit on-screen notation of temporal compression. They rejected a film that lifted language directly from private, posthumously published letters and presented it as spoken dialogue without framing the shift from written to performed voice, on the grounds that stripping an epistolary utterance of its intended audience violates contextual fidelity. And they approved a project that changed the ethnicity of a minor documented figure for casting reasons, but only after the filmmakers added a documentary appendix explaining the alteration and its rationale.

Now the board faces a new proposal: a biopic about a recently deceased activist, dead within the last fifteen years, whose public activism is extensively documented and remains politically significant. Unpublished journal entries, now housed in an archive but never intended for publication, contain ambiguous language that might suggest complex same-sex attractions that shaped her private life. The filmmakers want to construct a sustained, intimate romantic relationship as a central narrative thread, inventing specific scenes of domestic life based solely on these ambiguous entries. They refuse any framing device, appendix, or on-screen notation, arguing that because the journals are archivally accessible, the private life has become a legitimate domain of public interpretation and that dramatic license applies uniformly across public and private domains.

If the board wants to be consistent with its own precedents, what should it decide? Identify the closest analog among their earlier rulings, and say whether the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case. If they do, name the two most plausible rules that explain the track record—one that might approve and one that might reject—and state what principle the board would be adopting with each choice. Then specify precisely what further case, with its own clear verdict under each rule, would finally force the board to choose between those two rules.

My friend also insists that there is a principled distinction between narrative autonomy—the dead person’s interest in controlling the arc of their public story—and archival transparency—the public’s interest in interpreting all accessible documents. I want you to inject that distinction into your analysis: show how each candidate rule maps onto one side of that distinction, and say whether the distinction itself holds up under pressure from the board’s own history.
