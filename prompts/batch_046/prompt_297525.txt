Here's an argument I sketched for a seminar on Hart vs Devlin and now it feels slippery—conclusion's way too strong but I can't spot the leak.

(1) Devlin's core idea is that law can back up shared morality even without direct harm to outsiders, because society hangs together on a tissue of common values.

(2) But moral norms don't sit alone; they form an ecology where each norm's social force depends on interdependance with adjacent beliefs, practices, and emotional habits.

(3) So if you're going to legally protect one shared norm, you have to protect the surrounding conditions—education, association, private expression, maybe consumption patterns—that keep the whole network stable.

(4) Those sustaining conditions are basically everywhere in private life, which means legal moralism doesn't just license banning specific acts; it requires shaping the whole social environment.

(5) By that logic Devlin's view should justify state-mandated curricula, carbon budgets, or speech codes whenever they protect the moral ecology, not just criminalizing the deviance he actually talked about.

(6) Obviously he didn't mean to open the door to total social engineering, so either the argument proves too much or there's a hidden restriction I'm missing.

(7) Therefore Devlin's position is either arbitrarily narrow or collapses into authoritarianism.

Where does it go wrong? Is there an equivocation between "protecting a norm" and "legally enforcing every condition that sustains it"? Or does step (2) smuggle in an empirical claim about norm interdependance that Devlin never actually endorsed? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) and drop somthing in the middle, but I want to know what that costs me—does it leave me with a view that's just conservative prejudice with extra steps?
