Since taking on leadership of a small research team earlier this year, I have been forced to decide whether to interpret my colleagues’ conduct—and my own—as the expression of stable moral traits, or as behavior so thoroughly governed by circumstance that talk of “character” is largely retrospective storytelling. I want to test a tentative principle I have sketched, which introduces a distinction between what I call trait-carrying situations and trait-revealing situations. In a trait-carrying situation, the social or professional role itself supplies strong norms that would produce the behavior in almost anyone occupying the role; in a trait-revealing situation, the norms are silent, permissive, or even opposed, so that the conduct must come from the agent’s own disposition rather than from role pressure.

My tentative principle is this: a person genuinely possesses a virtue only if it manifests in trait-revealing situations; consistent manifestation in trait-carrying situations alone is insufficient evidence, though a clear failure in a carrying situation is strong evidence of absence. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, mostly coherent but containing one pair I find genuinely hard to reconcile.

First, a senior researcher admits a statistical error in her own published paper, knowing that a correction may damage her reputation, and she does so without being prompted. I judge that she possesses genuine intellectual integrity; concealment was easy and unrewarded, so this is a revealing situation. Second, the same researcher is unfailingly courteous during formal peer review, where civility is explicitly expected, but is dismissive and abrupt with administrative staff who have no influence over her career. I judge that she lacks genuine respect for persons; the courtesy is role-performance, and the dismissal occurs in a revealing situation where kindness was entirely optional. Third, I recently lost my temper with a contractor after three nights of poor sleep and sudden news of a family illness, though I am ordinarily patient. I judge that I remain genuinely patient; extreme sleep deprivation combined with emotional shock temporarily removed my agency, so this is an excusing condition rather than a revealing situation. Fourth, a colleague donates anonymously to emergency relief every year regardless of his financial circumstances, but he never participates in visible local campaigns where recognition is public. I judge that he possesses generosity; he seeks it out in revealing situations and avoids the social pressure of carrying ones. Fifth, a surgeon remains calm and decisive during emergency operations—high-stakes, highly scripted roles—but is visibly anxious and indecisive at casual social gatherings and in non-clinical staff meetings. By my principle, she lacks the general virtue of composure; she possesses only professional competence within a carrying situation. Yet I feel strongly that she is genuinely courageous in the operating theater, not merely performing a role.

Sixth, a friend refuses to join in gossip about a mutual acquaintance whether the target is present, whether the group is intimate or large, and whether joining in would earn her immediate social approval. I judge that she possesses loyalty or fairness; the refusal is uniform across both carrying and revealing conditions. Seventh, the hard pair: I want to maintain that a single act in an extreme, unrehearsed situation—pulling a stranger from a burning vehicle—reveals true courage more clearly than years of calm routine behavior. But if I accept that extreme unrehearsed situations reveal deep traits, then the surgeon’s calm in the operating theater, which is also extreme and unrehearsed in its particular demands, should reveal true composure, directly contradicting my verdict in the fifth case. So either extreme unrehearsed situations always reveal character, in which case I must overturn my verdict about the surgeon, or they do not, in which case I should be skeptical of using the burning-vehicle scenario to establish courage at all.

Please state the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that best captures these verdicts. For each candidate, specify precisely which clause is motivated by which case, state its prediction for a case I have not listed—namely, a teacher who is consistently kind to students during formally observed evaluation periods but is observed to be indifferent to them in unobserved hallway interactions—and honestly identify which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate. Finally, say whether my carrying/revealing distinction can be repaired by adding conditions—perhaps concerning the preservation of agency, or the agent’s own reflective endorsement—without becoming ad hoc, or whether the tension between the surgeon case and the burning-vehicle case forces me to abandon one of those verdicts entirely.
