Below are notes for a short essay on whether moral status comes in degrees, using insects and thermostats as boundary cases. The core claim is that gradation should be tracked along three axes—valenced experience, temporal integration of that experience, and social recognizability—rather than along a single “consciousness” threshold. On this view, an insect possesses low-grade moral status because it likely has brief, unintegrated valenced states; a thermostat possesses none, despite feedback loops that structurally resemble the predictive dynamics of conscious systems. To prevent functional mimicry from inflating status, I propose a “mimicry penalty”: when a non-biological system replicates the architecture of a conscious process without underlying phenomenology, we should not only deny it status but also reduce our epistemic confidence in attributing that architecture to nearby biological cases. I then ask whether this penalty is ad hoc, whether the three-axis framework avoids collapsing into panpsychism at the bottom, and whether social recognizability should count as constitutive or merely evidential. Please comment on the coherence of the argument, the fairness of the thermostat comparison, and whether the “mimicry penalty” solves more problems than it creates. In particular, point out any place where the distinction between structural resemblance and phenomenological presence risks becoming question-begging.
