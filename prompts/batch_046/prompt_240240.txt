I'm trying to settle my own view on when an atrocity should be named genocide, working from concrete judgments rather than from legal texts. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: Systematic, state-coordinated killing of an ethnic or religious minority with explicit bureaucratic intent to destroy the group as such. Verdict: genocide; the naming is correct regardless of diplomatic fallout.

Case 2: Decentralized mass killings of the same kind of ethnic group during a civil conflict, where local militias drive much of the violence and central-government intent is ambiguous, yet the targeting pattern is unmistakable. Verdict: still genocide; perfect bureaucratic centralization is not required.

Case 3: A state-engineered famine that disproportionately kills members of a specific national peasantry, where internal documents show awareness of the demographic impact, but officials frame the policy as economic restructuring and the group boundary overlaps with class. Verdict: should be named genocide, though I recognize this depends on accepting an intersectional group definition.

Case 4: Mass extermination of people defined solely by political opposition—no ethnic, religious, or national marker—where the death toll exceeds that in Case 3. Verdict: not genocide; it is an atrocity and a crime against humanity, but the protected-group criterion is essential, and I want to keep that boundary firm.

Case 5: The systematic destruction of a pre-existing indigenous community's cultural institutions, forced assimilation of children, and suppression of language, with relatively low direct killing. Verdict: genocide, because cultural destruction counts; yet this creates tension with Case 4, since the political category in Case 4 feels more "constructed" than some bureaucratic categories used to define ethnic groups, and I am unsure why construction should matter in one place but not the other.

The case I keep returning to: a regime invents an administrative category—"Class-VII registrants"—purely to round people up and kill or forcibly assimilate them, with no prior social identity attached to the label. Is this genocide?

Is there a single principle—perhaps about the reality or pre-existence of the group, the nature of intent, or the politics of naming—that captures Cases 1–5? If not, which verdict should I overturn? And what does the best principle predict for the invented-administrative-category case?
