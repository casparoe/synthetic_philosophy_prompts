I am a high school student working on an independent philosophy project, and I have come to defend a modified version of the procreation asymmetry that I call generational replacement ethics. The core claim is that there is a structural asymmetry between the wrongness of bringing people into conditions where severe suffering is probable, and the absence of any corresponding moral failure in failing to bring people into conditions of probable flourishing. I extend this with an added epistemic condition—predictive opacity—which holds that assessments of a potential person’s net welfare are necessarily speculative to a degree that makes positive obligations to reproduce incoherent, even though negative obligations to avoid foreseeable harm remain fully intact. I apply this framework not only to individual parents but to collective institutions that shape the conditions into which future citizens are born.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest assessment of its exposure, not a list of standard objections I have already considered. Which specific intuitions—perhaps about the intrinsic value of existence, about collective duties of social continuity, or about the moral significance of potential lives—would serve as decisive defeaters if they turned out to be reliable? Which results in welfare economics, decision theory, or risk modeling could undermine either the asymmetry itself or the claim about predictive opacity? Are there empirical developments—say, in intergenerational risk assessment or in methods for forecasting long-term welfare outcomes—that could shrink the epistemic gap to practical irrelevance?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of this view would concede it as genuinely counting against them, or whether they would dismiss it as presupposing a rival framework. I am particularly interested in whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability: if every apparent counterexample is absorbed by appealing to predictive opacity, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a fatal weakness for a view meant to guide institutional policy? Finally, if the position survives these tests, does that survival preserve any real prescriptive force for collective decision-making, or does it collapse into a purely negative prohibition with no constructive content?
