I teach an upper-division seminar in philosophy of history and social ontology—eighteen students, seventy-five minutes, with only an introductory philosophy background assumed. I want an in-class simulation that makes participants feel the conceptual pressure of path dependence not as a historical anecdote but as a live institutional problem, before any standard labels are introduced. Specifically, they should experience how locally rational sequential choices accumulate into a structure that feels inevitable in retrospect, and they should confront the ambiguity between weak path dependence—the path genuinely mattered, but alternative branches were available—and strong path dependence—once taken, the path permanently forecloses alternatives in a way that makes counterfactual migration conceptually impossible, not merely costly.

Design a structured, round-based activity in which small groups construct an institutional protocol—say, a collective grading rubric, a deliberation procedure, or a resource-allocation rule—across four decision rounds. In each round, present a choice that is individually defensible but asymmetrically constrains future options (for example, adopting a transparency rule that later makes compromise impossible, or accepting a minority-veto mechanism that protects dissent but paralyzes reform). Use non-monetary stakes—reputation tokens, veto chips, or collective credit—to motivate engagement without real money. At the end of round three, interrupt with a counterfactual rewind: show each group a snapshot of the institutional state they would have reached had they diverged at round one, and require them to vote on whether they can migrate to that branch or whether it is now conceptually inaccessible given the commitments they have made. Arrange the rules so that migration feels psychologically costly—sunk institutional investment, broken procedural trust, illegitimacy—even when technically permitted, and so that groups split on whether the alternative was ever genuinely live.

Then provide a debrief of about fifteen minutes that maps specific moments in the simulation onto weak versus strong path dependence; onto the problem of analogical reasoning in history (why invoking Munich, Vietnam, or the Thucydides trap to interpret their own simulation misleads); and onto the retrospective fallacy of reading inevitability back into contingency. Predict what will go wrong the first time—groups will likely treat the rewind as a simple do-over rather than a counterfactual assessment, or will conflate procedural fairness with historical necessity—and say how to correct this in the moment without breaking the experiential frame. Be explicit about which feature of genuine historical path dependence the simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the irreversible flow of actual time and the absence of any observer-independent branching tree—and specify how you, as facilitator, must supply that limitation through framing rather than through the rules.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke highly specialized theoretical apparatus that depends on precise scholarly attribution; rely instead on the conceptual distinction between weak and strong path dependence and the well-known pitfalls of historical analogy, applied here through a novel institutional-design game with a counterfactual-rewind mechanism.
