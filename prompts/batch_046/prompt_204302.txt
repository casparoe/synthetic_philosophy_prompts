As a doctoral student in philosophy of mind working on the architecture of self-consciousness, I have been re-examining the classic split-brain experiments—particularly the cases in which the right hemisphere directs a left-hand response to a visual cue presented in the left visual field, and the language-dominant left hemisphere is then asked to justify the choice verbally. I am struck by a recurring structural feature that seems more specific than the standard “interpreter module” account of confabulation. In these episodes, the patient does not merely invent a plausible reason for an action they did not consciously initiate; rather, the verbal report appears to retroactively collapse the temporal order between decision and explanation. The patient reports that they “always intended” the match made by the left hand, and when the discrepancy is gently pressed, they deny that there was ever a moment of ambiguity, division, or felt alienness in the hand’s movement. The phenomenological gap between the right-hemisphere-driven selection and the left-hemisphere-produced narrative is not just covered over—it seems to be erased from accessible memory, so that the explanation is experienced not as post-hoc but as the original motive, and the possibility of hemispheric dissociation is actively rejected rather than ignored. This strikes me as distinct from ordinary rationalization, from anosognosia for motor deficit, and from the “choice blindness” documented in intact subjects, because what fails here is not awareness of the choice itself but awareness that the reason came second and originated elsewhere. Does this precise configuration—this temporal and agential restructuring coupled with a denial of internal division—have an established name in the neuropsychological or philosophical literature? If it is treated as a subtype of left-hemisphere interpreter confabulation, what distinguishes it from the broader class? And if no precise term exists, how should its boundaries be drawn against related phenomena such as the illusion of conscious will or source-monitoring errors?

Please say clearly where your reasoning is most vulnerable—whether in the empirical characterization of these cases, in the distinction from choice blindness, or in the taxonomy of confabulation—and specify what evidence or argument would cause you to revise your verdict, for instance if the retroactive restructuring I describe turns out to be an artifact of the questioning protocol rather than a stable feature of split-brain self-report.
