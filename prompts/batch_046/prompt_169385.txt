I am an American high school junior writing during my semester abroad at a Gymnasium in Bavaria, and I need help thinking through a conflict I cannot resolve with ordinary advice. Here, an unwritten but rigorously observed norm—often captured by the word Ordnung—treats compliance with explicit rules as a collective moral duty rather than as mere obedience. When I recently witnessed a classmate commit a minor institutional infraction and chose not to report him, my host family interpreted my silence not as kindness but as a failure of basic civic character; they explained that trust depends on everyone enforcing the same visible standards, not on private judgments about which rules matter. Back in my American school, the moral framework I was taught treats individual conscience as prior to rules: we are encouraged to ask whether a regulation serves justice or loyalty in a particular case, and blind adherence is regarded as ethically dangerous rather than virtuous.

Because of this contrast, I have been asked to help draft a student honor code for a transatlantic exchange program, and I am trying to determine which of these folk-moral structures should govern it. I want to know whether there is a philosophically serious defense of the German view—one that grounds social cooperation in universal, transparent rule-following without reducing that view to authoritarianism or suppressing individual moral agency. I also want to understand whether the American emphasis on personal conscience can survive the objection that it makes shared norms arbitrary, turning institutional rules into optional suggestions that collapse whenever loyalty or sympathy demands it. Which framework offers the more coherent account of how moral community and mutual trust are actually sustained, and what would a code have to contain in order to respect both without falling into contradiction?
