As a novelist constructing the moral architecture of a novel that turns on extremity and speech, I have adopted what I call the frame-boundary view regarding the duty of truthful speech in the classic case of the murderer at the door. I take the Kantian prohibition on lying to be correctly articulated in its rigor, but I do not defend it through consequentialist exception; instead, I propose a scope condition. The categorical duty not to utter falsehood applies only within a shared moral frame constituted by mutual recognition among rational agents. When an interlocutor manifests, by imminent and destructive intent, that he has exited that frame—seeking not dialogue but annihilation—he no longer stands within the boundary to which the duty extends. The false statement given to misdirect him is therefore not a permitted exception carved out of an intact rule, but a speech-act occurring outside the rule’s domain, analogous to addressing someone in a language they have forfeited membership in. I am aware that this risks sounding like a distinction without a difference, yet I maintain it avoids both consequentialist contamination and the rigid universalism that refuses all falsehood regardless of relational context. What I need is a rigorous map of what would genuinely count against this position, not a list of authorities. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones: formal arguments showing that scope conditions on categorical duties inevitably collapse into ordinary exceptions; psychological or anthropological findings about whether moral agents actually recognize such frame-exit conditions in extremis; historical or legal cases where analogous boundary arguments have licensed abuse; and any result in moral philosophy demonstrating that a duty cannot carry domain restrictions without sacrificing its categorical character. Rank these by how fatal they are, and include an honest assessment of whether this framing can be qualified into unfalsifiability—whether any counterexample can be absorbed by redrawing the boundary—and whether such insulation is a vice for a normative view or an acceptable feature. Finally, indicate which objections a committed defender of the frame-boundary view would themselves concede as damaging, and which they would dismiss as missing the point entirely.
