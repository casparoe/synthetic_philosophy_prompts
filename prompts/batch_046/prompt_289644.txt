I am preparing the final session of my graduate seminar on infinite ethics and wish to close with a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, rhyming ABA with two refrains that alternate and finally converge in the closing couplet. The poem should take as its specific subject the impossibility that arises when one attempts to rank worlds containing infinitely many lives: the principle that every life counts equally (anonymity) and the principle that a world in which every person is unambiguously better off is strictly better (Pareto dominance) can both be honored, but only at the cost of abandoning any continuous representation of the ordering. In other words, there can be no real-valued social welfare function that respects both conditions; arbitrarily small changes in the distribution of welfare across an infinite population can reverse the ranking. I want the two refrains to embody these two principles, returning in the tercets like bargaining positions that never yield. The turn—around the fourth or fifth tercet—should be the moment the reader realizes that what must be surrendered is not one of the two principles but continuity itself, leaving the ordering radically discontinuous. The rhyme scheme should carry the A-rhyme through the refrains so that their sonic persistence mirrors their logical persistence. In the final quatrain, let the two refrains stand side by side without resolution, leaving open exactly which commitment we are least willing to pay. It should be accurate enough that a specialist in population ethics recognizes the standard impossibility result—where anonymity and Pareto jointly force the abandonment of a continuous ordering—rather than a generic meditation on infinity, and it should function as a poem in its own right for a reader unfamiliar with the literature. Please do not resolve the tension with a sermon about intervention or a shrug about the circle of life; leave it where our discussion ended, with the choice of what to give up still unmade.
