As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I am trying to work out the logical structure of authority between an earlier and a later self in the context of medical advance directives. I have concrete intuitions about several cases, but they seem to contain a tension that I cannot resolve, and I want to know whether there is a single coherent principle that captures them, which parts of my pattern are mistakes, and what the principle predicts for an unlisted scenario.

Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: A healthy 55-year-old executes a directive refusing mechanical ventilation should he ever enter a permanent vegetative state with no awareness. Years later he is in precisely that condition. Verdict: Honor the directive; do not ventilate.

Case 2: A healthy 70-year-old executes a directive refusing a feeding tube should she develop severe dementia and fail to recognize her family members. Later she is severely demented, shows no recognition, but is apparently content—she smiles, accepts pureed food, and exhibits no distress. Verdict: Honor the directive; do not place the feeding tube if she stops swallowing.

Case 3: A healthy 60-year-old executes a directive refusing antibiotics for pneumonia should he ever lose what he calls rational agency—defined as the capacity to reason about his long-term values and life plan. Later he has moderate dementia: he cannot reason about long-term values, yet he clearly recognizes his spouse, requests specific music, and states, "I want to stay here." He then contracts pneumonia. Verdict: Treat the pneumonia; the current expressed desire to live should override the earlier refusal.

Case 4: A healthy 65-year-old executes a directive refusing all life-extending treatment should she become, in her words, "no longer the same person." She later suffers a severe stroke that eliminates long-term memory, removes all recognition of others, and produces a temperament radically different from her prior calm and generous character—she is now persistently irritable and fearful. She develops a treatable infection. Verdict: Honor the directive; withhold life-extending treatment.

Case 5: A healthy 50-year-old executes a directive with two clauses: first, if she has Alzheimer's and asks for her deceased mother, caregivers should not correct her or treat her for depression, allowing her to live in that belief; second, she should receive standard comfort care for physical pain. Later she has Alzheimer's, asks for her mother daily, and is comforted by being told her mother is "on her way." She also develops a painful but easily treatable urinary infection. Verdict: Follow the emotional-care clause—do not correct her—but also treat the infection; the directive's permission about belief does not extend to allowing physical suffering.

The tension I see is between Case 2 and Case 3. In Case 2 the cognitive loss is more total—no recognition, no reasoning—yet I want to honor the earlier refusal. In Case 3 the loss is partial—some recognition, some present-tense preferences remain—yet I want to override the earlier refusal. So my pattern seems inverted: the more of the prior self that remains present, the more willing I am to disregard that prior self's instructions. That strikes me as probably wrong, but it is what my intuitions actually deliver.

Please provide the following. First, state one principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible; state each clause precisely and identify which case motivates it. Second, apply the principle to a case I have not listed: a patient in a minimally conscious state who occasionally tracks moving objects with her eyes but has never expressed a preference, and who has a directive refusing all medical intervention. What does the principle say? Third, be explicit about which of my verdicts—especially Case 3—the principle cannot accommodate without revision, and whether you think that revision is warranted. I am not asking for a survey of named theories or a bibliography; I want only the logical structure of the authority relation and an honest accounting of the trade-offs.
