i've been arguing with a friend about whether anti-corruption laws just export western norms, and he keeps falling back on this analogy: clientelism is basically kinship writ large. the patron is like an elder brother or father, the clients are dependants, the jobs and favors are like birthday gifts or dowry help, and an outsider calling it bribery is like telling a family that sharing groceries counts as corruption. he uses this to say that western 'corruption' frameworks are ethnocentric because they mistake reciprocal obligation for crime.

can you audit the analogy properly? first, the correspondence table — what exactly plays the role of the family boundary, the patron, the client, the vote or contract, and the gift? then the breaks: kinship usually has exit costs and emotional bonds that political clientelism doesn't, and family gifts don't typically come with explicit quid-pro-quo expectations of the kind you see in vote-buying. but here's where i get stuck — in some contexts people invoke 'philotimo' or 'utang na loob', these unquantifiable debts of honor that aren't contractual, to argue the exchange can't be priced like a bribe. does injecting that idea actually save the analogy, or does it just relocate the problem? and finally, does his conclusion — that western standards are ethnocentric — survive once the disanalogies are laid out, or was the family framing carrying the whole argument?
