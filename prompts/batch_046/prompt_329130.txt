Write a dialogue between a philosopher of history who treats "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" as institutional kinds—categories made real through 19th-century university curricula, museum taxonomies, and disciplinary boundaries—and a philosopher who treats them as approximate natural kinds tracking genuine discontinuities in epistemic practice. The institutionalist should argue that enactment does not entail unreality, drawing analogies to money or citizenship, and should point to the "long 12th century" thesis and the continuity of alchemical practice into chemistry as cases where the tripartite scheme imposes breaks that practice did not have. The naturalist should concede boundary fuzziness but insist that some clusterings of methods, institutions, and problems persist independently of our labels, and should press whether the institutionalist's view collapses all taxonomy into arbitrary convention. Each should respond directly to the other's analogies rather than restate her opening position, and the exchange should conclude with both identifying the single premise—about the ontology of social kinds, or about the detectability of epistemic regimes—whose failure would force her to switch sides.
