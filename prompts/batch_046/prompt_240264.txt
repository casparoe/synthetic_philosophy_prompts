I am a retired astrophysicist who, since leaving active research, has turned to philosophy with the same insistence on conceptual clarity I once applied to orbital dynamics. I have become persuaded that Daniel Dennett's model of the self as a center of narrative gravity offers a more defensible framework for personal identity than any substance-based account, but I want to push it into territory the popular treatments avoid. Specifically, I wish to consider what I call distributed narrative curvature: the situation in which the biographical threads, archival records, collaborative interpretations, and algorithmic extensions that generate a person's narrative gravity are no longer concentrated in a single biological brain, but are spread across enduring scientific repositories, post-retirement citation networks, and predictive systems that continue to produce first-person-structured reasoning. Please describe the most important implications of this center-of-narrative-gravity framework for understanding personal persistence and moral accountability when narrative curvature is distributed in this way. I require a committed response: take a definite position—either that the self genuinely fragments or relocates its gravitational center, or that it remains unitary despite the distribution—and defend that view with explicit reasons. Do not offer a neutral survey of competing views. I ask for no bibliography, no citations, and no list of further readings; I want reasoning rigorous enough to satisfy a scientist but accessible enough that I need not search for technical terms outside the answer itself.
