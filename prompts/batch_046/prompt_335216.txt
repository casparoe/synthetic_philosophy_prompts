I have recently been asked to advise on the ethics curriculum for an officer-training program, and I am working through a specific defense of the moral equality of combatants: the equality is not a brute moral fact but derives from a reciprocal, risk-bearing relation among those who actually face lethal harm, which means it should hold only where combatants on both sides are genuinely exposed to comparable danger and have not been coerced into the role. I want an honest assessment of what would genuinely defeat this position rather than merely complicate it. Could an asymmetry in risk exposure—say, between remote drone operators and front-line infantry—already break the reciprocity without undermining the broader equality thesis? Could evidence that widespread conscription removes voluntary entry vitiate the contract for one side but not the other? And is there a conceptual result—perhaps from contractualist ethics or the philosophy of imposed risk—that shows this reciprocity framing collapses back into Walzer’s ignorance-based account or into a simple denial of equality altogether? Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, say what defenders of this view should themselves concede as damaging, and judge whether the position is well insulated or qualified into emptiness. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that summarizes its degree of vulnerability in one sentence.
