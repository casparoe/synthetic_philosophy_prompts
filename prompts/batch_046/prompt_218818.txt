I was talking with my study group about Aristotles sea battle problem and we ended up with this argument that feels like it should be wrong but I cant find the leak. Heres the chain my friend laid out:

(1) If there is going to be a sea battle tomorrow, then the statement "a battle will happen tomorrow" was true yesterday.
(2) Whatever was true yesterday is now unalterable, since you cant change the past.
(3) So if there will be a battle, it is now unalterable that there will be one — in other words, its necessary.
(4) The same reasoning applies if there wont be a battle: then its now necessary that there wont be.
(5) But contingency just means that neither outcome is necessary.
(6) So there cant be any future contingencies at all; every event is either necessary or impossible.

The conclusion wipes out free will and planning, which seems like way too much. Id rather keep (2) — the past is fixed — and I also want to keep (5), since thats just what contingency is. So which step do I actually have to reject, (1) or (3)? Explain why the rejected one looked plausible at first. And whats the price? Does giving it up force me into saying future statements lack bivalence, or can I save bivalence with a branching-time picture where one path is actual but not predetermined?
