Consider the stronger version of the institutional critique of effective altruism: its measurability bias does not simply neglect structural or systemic goods that resist quantification, but actively transforms the ontology of the problems it addresses—turning educational flourishing into test-score gains and political representation into voter-registration tallies, not as imperfect proxies but as conceptually distinct targets. Assume this claim is taken seriously by critics and resisted by practitioners of measurability-driven allocation. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this stronger claim to be wrong? Identify which premises—about conceptual transformation under institutional incentives, about the irreducibility of certain goods, or about the separability of measurement error from ontological distortion—would need to fail, and specify what observations, institutional case studies, or conceptual distinctions would count as decisive against it rather than merely showing it is incomplete. Say also what defenders of the practice could themselves concede without abandoning their core commitment to quantitative evaluation, and whether the critique risks anything substantial or has been insulated by qualifications that make it unfalsifiable.
