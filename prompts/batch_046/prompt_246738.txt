In a recent interdisciplinary colloquium I chaired, a colleague summarized the contemporary debate over "Western values" as follows. The phrase, she argued, denotes a genuinely coherent axiological cluster built on three commitments: methodological individualism in moral reasoning, a procedural rather than substantive conception of justice, and an institutionalized epistemic norm of fallibilism. She further maintained that this cluster is historically specific to post-Reformation European political theology, yet in modern human-rights discourse it is routinely universalized in ways that conceal its particularity. Crucially, she claimed that these three commitments are mutually reinforcing only within an ontological framework that treats collective moral agency as a category error; consequently, she concluded, the coherence of the category is precisely what renders it an instrument of normative imperialism whenever it is applied across cultures, rather than a neutral descriptive tool.

Please audit this summary for fairness. Specifically: which elements accurately reflect the philosophical and sociological literature on whether "Western values" form a coherent category and on what that category includes; where does the summary conflate a descriptive claim about historical origin with a normative claim about universalizability; is the inference from the three commitments to the rejection of collective moral agency genuinely supported, or does it import an unstated premise; and does the conclusion that conceptual coherence necessarily entails cultural imperialism follow from the premises, or does it depend on a hidden assumption about the relationship between conceptual clarity and normative particularity? I am less interested in a simple verdict than in the precise location of the missteps and in whether a corrected version of the argument could still reach a similar conclusion.
