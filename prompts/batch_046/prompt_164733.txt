Please explain the conceptual architecture of Maimonides’ concessional account of animal sacrifice in the Guide for the Perplexed, with particular attention to the tension between the historical contingency of the command—instituted, as he argues, as an accommodation to widespread idolatrous practices involving sacrificial rites—and the eternal, rational orientation of the Law toward immaterial, intellectual worship. Rather than summarizing the concession thesis, articulate the precise philosophical problem that arises when a normative divine command is grounded in a pedagogical compromise with human weakness. Specifically, consider whether this concession undermines the authority of the command itself, or whether it can be reconstructed as a form of staged withdrawal: a framework in which the material form of animal sacrifice is deliberately retained not for its intrinsic value, but precisely as a necessary focal object that must remain present in order to be gradually emptied of literal significance and redirected toward contemplative devotion. Distinguish this reading from a straightforward evolutionary or progressive narrative of religious development, and clarify the conceptual role of the animal body within it—not as a mere disposable instrument, but as an indispensable material anchor whose presence enables its own transcendence. I want a rigorous conceptual analysis of the internal logic of this position, not a historical survey of medieval reception or modern commentary.
