(2) Take saving a drowning child: the right-making reason is that an innocent person is in grave danger. (3) Human psychology never permits bare moral properties to cause behavior without mediation; something psychological—either a representational belief or an affective state—must always be interposed. (4) If belief mediates, the immediate causal factor is the belief-state representing that someone is drowning, not the danger itself; the agent is moved by their representation, not by R. (5) If affect mediates, the immediate cause is the feeling-state of distress triggered by the scene, not the other person's distress; again, a psychological proxy stands in for R. (6) So in every actual case, the agent is causally moved by a psychological proxy, never by the bare right-making reason R itself. (9) Yet we routinely and reliably distinguish the genuinely good agent from the merely prudent or accidental one, which presupposes that moral worth is real and detectable. (10) So (1) must be false: being moved by the bare reason is not necessary for moral worth.
