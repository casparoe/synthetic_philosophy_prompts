In debates over collective-risk governance—particularly around technologies with irreversible, globally distributed effects—I keep encountering the formulation: "If anyone can take the action, everyone bears the cost." The phrase is usually offered as a summary of the unilateralist's curse, and in my last discussion it was deployed to argue that international coordination mechanisms are structurally futile: since a single actor can always proceed alone, collective agreements are irrelevant by definition. I want the slogan taken apart into its possible precise meanings. Separate the claim that unilateral action is individually rational from the claim that collective decision rules are powerless, from the claim that outcomes are necessarily Pareto-inferior to coordinated ones, and from any hidden premises about the irreversibility or observability of the action. For each reading, say whether it is a tautology, a substantive thesis about strategic structure, or an empirical claim that could be false. Then tell me which of these readings the "coordination is futile" argument actually needs, whether that reading is defensible, and which other readings are being smuggled in by the slogan's ambiguity. I am not asking for a literature survey or citations—just a menu of interpretations with verdicts on which ones do real argumentative work.
