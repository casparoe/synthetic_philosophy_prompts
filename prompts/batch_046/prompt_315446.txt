I've been designing a graduate seminar on comparative moral sociology, and I've hit a conceptual snag that I hope you can help me think through. We are comparing three cultural frameworks—honor cultures, dignity cultures, and what sociologists have called victimhood cultures—not descriptively, but normatively: is any of them morally superior? I want to avoid a simple overall verdict, so I'd like you to rank the three across four specific dimensions. The first is resilience against institutional corrosion, by which I mean the tendency of formal rules to be captured by informal status games that reward public humiliation rather than procedural fairness. The second is capacity for moral repair after asymmetric harm—cases where one party holds far more power to define the narrative of injury and the other lacks reciprocal standing. The third is scalability to large, anonymous societies in which face-to-face reputation mechanisms no longer function. The fourth, which I'm particularly interested in, is compatibility with negative reciprocity norms: social rules that mandate proportional withdrawal of cooperation rather than either direct retaliation or unconditional forgiveness. For each dimension, place the three cultures in tiers—top, middle, bottom—and provide a brief justification.

Then, if forced to aggregate these rankings into an overall judgment of moral superiority, which framework prevails? More importantly, does the aggregation itself feel legitimate, or does one dimension dominate in a way that reveals a hidden bias in how we weigh moral cultures? I am not looking for a bibliography or references to particular ethnographies; I want the reasoning to stand on its own, since the point is to test whether these frameworks can be compared conceptually without collapsing into either relativism or an unexamined preference for modern liberal norms.
