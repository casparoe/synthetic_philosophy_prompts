I'm running a grad seminar on practical reason and one of my students—bright kid, but way too into metaethics—dropped this bomb on me after Tuesday's session. She's trying to show that indirect normativity collapses unless you buy what she calls the reflective inheritence condition. Here's her chain, more or less verbatim from my notes: (1) If indirect normativity holds, then what I ought to do is fixed by what I'd endorse after fully idealized deliberation—full info, coherence, no framing bias, the usual package. (2) But for that endorsement to actually bind me, rather than just describing some counterfactual agent, there has to be a transmission principle—her reflective inheritence condition—which requires that my actual cognative architechture can realize the reasoning path leading to the norm without structural rupture. (3) Any real human mind has bounded rationality, affective noise, and context dependence, so the reasoning path is always disrupted; there's always rupture. (4) So the inheritence condition is never satisfied, and indirect norms never actually reach the agent. (5) That makes indirect normativity just a theory about what imaginary agents should do, not about me.

(6) But the whole point was to explain obligations that outstrip my actual reasoning—like why I shouldn't dump carbon even if I never model climate dynamics. (7) Therefore either we reject the inheritence condition, or we admit indirect normativity fails its own purpose. She really wants to keep (2) because she thinks without it we're just doing direct rationalism with extra steps. I feel like there's an equivocation between 'disrupted reasoning' and 'failure of transmission' somewhere around (2)-(3), but I can't pin it down. Which premise do you reject, and what's the cost?
