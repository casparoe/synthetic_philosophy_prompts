In a recent philosophy-of-science discussion, a colleague dismissed my worry that predictive success does not guarantee literal truth about unobservable entities by saying that “truth is what works.” She seemed to be using the slogan to block any inference from practical success to ontological commitment, yet the phrase was doing heavy argumentative labor without its precise content being specified. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that “truth is what works” could be expressing, treating them as a menu rather than selecting one? For each reading, specify: (1) whether it functions as a semantic claim about the meaning of “true,” an epistemic claim about our criterion for truth, a metaphysical claim about what truth consists in, or a normative claim about what we ought to call true; (2) whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false; (3) whether Charles Peirce, William James, or neither could plausibly endorse it, with brief reference to their actual positions—say, James’s appeal to “cash value” and the “will to believe” versus Peirce’s fallibilist, long-run convergence view—without treating this as a request for textual citations; and (4) whether that particular reading actually supports my colleague’s move of dismissing correspondence-based realism about theoretical entities. I am especially interested in whether there is a defensible intermediate reading—perhaps one restricted to empirical beliefs and compatible with a minimalist or deflationary account of truth—that avoids both the radical metaphysical claim that truth just is practical success and the trivial claim that utility is merely one indicator among others. Please also indicate which readings risk collapsing pragmatism into verificationism or instrumentalism in ways Peirce explicitly rejected, and whether any version can accommodate normative or mathematical truths without treating them as “working” in the same predictive, technological sense.
