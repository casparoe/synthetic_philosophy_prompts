I am preparing a position paper for a philosophy of mind seminar and want a consistency check before I defend the package. My current view has six parts. First, I accept illusionism in the robust form: there are no phenomenal properties or qualia, and conscious experience is exhaustively constituted by representational states carrying particular functional and intentional contents. Second, I take this to dissolve the hard problem—once we have explained the representational architecture and its self-monitoring capacities, there is no remaining ontological mystery about why those processes are accompanied by subjective feel. Third, I hold that introspection is not direct acquaintance but a representational mechanism that systematically mischaracterizes its targets, presenting functional and representational properties as intrinsic, non-representational phenomenal ones. Fourth, despite this pervasive mischaracterization, I maintain that first-person authority is largely preserved, because the underlying representational content is still accurately tracked even when its ontological status is misdescribed. Fifth, I think phenomenal concepts—the ones we use when we think about experience from the inside—are empty in reference yet indispensable in practice, serving what I call a phenomenal framing function: they index representational states to a specific spatiotemporal perspective in a way that standard representational vocabulary cannot, which makes them necessary for flexible first-person planning and social coordination. Sixth, I am committed to the methodological claim that empirical consciousness research should therefore abandon any search for neural correlates of phenomenal properties and should instead map the architecture of these self-modeling representational systems, treating folk reports of what it is like as data about framing mechanisms rather than as ontological evidence.

My concern is that these commitments may not hang together. In particular, (2) and (5) seem to pull in opposite directions: if the hard problem is fully dissolved by explaining representation, why do we need a special class of indispensable but empty concepts to perform cognitive work that should already be covered by the representational account? There also appears to be a tension between (3) and (4): if introspection systematically mischaracterizes the ontology of every conscious state, on what basis can first-person reports be authoritative about anything beyond the misrepresentation itself? And there may be a conflict between (5) and (6): if phenomenal framing is genuinely indispensable for cognition, then eliminating phenomenal concepts from scientific description might not be a mere methodological option but an impossibility, which seems at odds with treating them as empty. Please audit this package. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, indicate which ones dissolve on closer inspection, specify the least costly revision and what that revision costs, and do not signal in advance which commitment you think I should abandon.
