I am designing an assignment for my philosophy of biology seminar that asks students to think through multi-level selection not as a settled taxonomy but as a live ontological problem. To that end, I would like you to write a short story—roughly 500 to 800 words—centered on a fictional marine biologist in the late 1970s, Dr. Elena Vasquez, who is stationed at a remote Pacific research outpost studying a colonial hydrozoan that exhibits a puzzling form of programmed necrosis. In this organism, individual polyps die off in precise geometric patterns that do not correlate with genetic relatedness or Hamiltonian kin-selection coefficients; instead, the sacrifices stabilize the colony’s structural matrix and, unexpectedly, improve the reproductive conditions for unrelated photosynthetic symbionts living inside the colony. Vasquez must wrestle with whether the phenomenon is best explained by selection at the level of genes, of individual polyps, of the colony as a superorganism, or of some broader ecological assemblage that includes the symbionts.

Please embed the conceptual tension directly into the narrative architecture rather than resolving it through expository dialogue. You might render it through the physical layout of the colony—perhaps Vasquez notices that the dead polyps form a lattice that mirrors neither the genetic map nor the individual boundaries—through fragments of her field journal that record measurements without interpretation, or through a brief, unresolved exchange with a colleague who insists on a strictly genic reductionism. The story should avoid both mystical holism and triumphalist reductionism; its tone should be contemplative, empirically precise, and philosophically unsettled. Most importantly, it should leave the reader with the impression that identifying the correct unit of selection is not merely an empirical bookkeeping task but raises deeper metaphysical questions about what constitutes an individual in nature. Do not include citations, bibliographic references, or appeals to specific historical papers; the conceptual machinery—replicator selection, vehicle selection, and the problem of ecological dependency—should be accessible through the narrative itself.
