As a science journalist who covers institutional climate governance, I sit on the advisory policy board of the Eastern Seaboard Research Consortium, and we must render an actionable recommendation on a pilot grant that has become philosophically fraught. The municipality of Riverside has proposed a $2.4 million program to test what its application calls a progressive contraction tariff: a hybrid instrument that pairs a declining carbon-intensity tax with binding local-output contraction targets drawn from degrowth economics. The proposal contains an opt-in clause allowing individual districts within Riverside to unilaterally adopt stricter contraction caps and claim corresponding rebate premiums, without coordinating with neighboring districts or with the broader regional regulatory framework that our consortium helps administer.

We are divided over what this clause does to collective rationality. Some board members treat unilateral district-level escalation as a necessary form of decentralized moral leadership, particularly given the inadequacy of national carbon pricing and the urgency of contraction pathways. Others warn that the structure invites a collective-action failure structurally analogous to the unilateralist's curse: each district, acting on its own accurate assessment of climate risk, may choose caps that are locally defensible but collectively destabilize regional supply networks, displace emissions to non-participating zones, and erode the economic viability of the pilot—thereby harming the communities it intends to protect. Our task is not to settle climate economics in the abstract, but to decide, with a clear bottom line, whether to approve the grant outright, reject it, or approve it only under conditions that restrict, restructure, or prohibit the unilateral opt-in mechanism.

I am asking for a formal memo addressed to the board recommending one of those three outcomes. The memo should specify the criteria reviewers could apply to analogous future proposals, give an honest and complete account of the strongest consideration favoring the option you do not recommend, and state explicitly what governing principle—regarding collective coordination, unilateral moral agency, or institutional risk—we would be adopting with our decision. Please treat the case as concrete: the districts are real administrative units within the pilot, the rebates are real budget lines, and our endorsement will determine whether the program proceeds.
