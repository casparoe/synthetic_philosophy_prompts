Consider the institutional practice maintained by many large public art museums: their permanent galleries and acquisition budgets are directed almost exclusively toward works that art historians classify as formally innovative or conceptually significant—pieces that often depend on specialized contextual knowledge to be interpreted—while empirical visitor studies repeatedly show that these same works generate very little spontaneous emotional engagement or comprehension among non-specialist audiences. At the same time, these institutions run extensive education and outreach departments whose explicit mission is to bridge that gap, treating emotional accessibility and immediate understanding as pedagogical objectives to be achieved through labels, tours, and programming rather than as artistic deficiencies of the works themselves. This bifurcation seems stable and widely accepted, yet I find it philosophically puzzling. What theory of artistic value does this practice presuppose? Does it assume that merit is an intrinsic, reception-independent property of the object—something like formal significance—while treating affective transmission as an external, curatorial problem? Or can it be read as presupposing a two-level account in which artistic value and communicative efficacy are entirely separate dimensions?
