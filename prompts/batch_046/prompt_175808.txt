I'm preparing a presentation for my high school philosophy seminar on whether King Lear can be read through Confucian role ethics rather than through the usual Western framework of individual autonomy versus tyranny. Most interpretations treat Lear's demand for verbal proofs of love as an act of ego, and Cordelia's refusal as modern authenticity resisting flattery. I want to examine the division-of-the-kingdom scene through zhengming—the rectification of names—asking whether Lear fails not merely as a father but as a ruler who severs the substance of the five relationships while keeping their titles. Goneril and Regan perform the rhetoric of xiao without its practice; Cordelia's silence might then be read not as liberal self-assertion but as a refusal to participate in a role that has become hollow. Does the tragedy show that moral and political collapse begins when roles are reduced to speech acts? And do the storm scenes signal a breakdown of cosmic order linked to this role failure, or only Lear's private madness? I need a reading that takes both Shakespeare's dramatic architecture and Confucian ethics seriously—one that says where the play illuminates the idea that authority must be embodied rather than declared, where it resists being reduced to a Confucian parable, and what an honest critic must concede about forcing the text in this direction.
