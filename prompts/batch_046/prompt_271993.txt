Exam question: Explain Hume's argument that beauty is not a quality in objects but a sentiment in the perceiver, as developed in his essay on the standard of taste. Show how Hume attempts to preserve normative judgment—distinguishing correct from incorrect taste—through the figure of the qualified critic. Then apply this framework to the following case: an international conservatory trains listeners from diverse cultural backgrounds to experience a specific harmonic interval, the minor sixth, as beautiful, achieving near-uniform agreement regardless of prior musical exposure. Does Hume's model allow us to regard this training as an improvement of taste, or does it reduce aesthetic judgment to institutional conditioning? Be precise about whether Hume's appeal to shared human nature can accommodate such engineered convergence.

Student answer: Hume argues that beauty is not an objective property of things but rather a feeling produced in the mind of the perceiver, which makes aesthetic judgment fundamentally subjective. To prevent this subjectivism from collapsing into complete relativism, Hume introduces the figure of the true judge—someone with refined delicacy, extensive practice in comparison, and freedom from prejudice. These qualified critics provide a standard not because they access an objective feature of the artwork, but because their sentiments are more authoritative, reflecting the proper functioning of human faculties. Applying this to the conservatory case, if the training develops natural capacities for discrimination, it might seem like improvement; but because Hume denies that beauty resides in objects, he must deny that any institutional framework can produce genuine improvement, since improvement requires a standard external to the institution that his subjectivism cannot provide. Therefore, the near-uniform agreement achieved by the conservatory is not evidence of refined taste but of conditioning that undermines Hume's appeal to shared human nature. Ultimately, the convergence of qualified judges shows that aesthetic value, though not metaphysically objective, is normatively binding. The true judge adopts a general point of view that transcends private feeling, allowing aesthetic judgment to function analogously to moral judgment, where sympathy and shared sentiment establish normative standards.
