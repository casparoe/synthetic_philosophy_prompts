You are a senior philosopher of social recognition and an experienced convener of interdisciplinary workshops, with deep familiarity with Hegel's master-slave dialectic and its applications to institutional dynamics. Before drafting the speech, please think carefully and outline your strategy: identify the three structural moves of the dialectic—the life-and-death struggle for pure recognition, the collapse of asymmetry into dependency, and the slave's acquisition of self-certainty through transformative labor—and decide precisely how each move will shape the welcome, particularly in justifying a rejection of the "charity model" of institutional recognition in favor of what we are calling an "inverted lordship" framework, in which bodies that claim to bestow credentials are actually sustained by the unrecognized epistemic and affective labor of those they evaluate.

Then write polished opening remarks (approximately 700 words) for the first session of a workshop titled "Asymmetrical Recognition: Hegel, Labor, and Institutional Practice." The audience is split: roughly half are specialists in 19th-century philosophy who will catch misreadings of the dialectic, and half are sociologists, labor organizers, and accreditation practitioners who need the machinery made concrete without being patronized. The philosophical idea must do real structural work, not decorative work: use the dialectic to establish the ground rules for the session by showing that recognition is never a one-way gift from the powerful to the powerless, that the party appearing to hold "mastery" over recognition is structurally dependent on the labor and self-certainty of the "slave," and that any presentation treating accreditation, peer review, or credentialing as mere benevolence misses the core insight. Make this tangible for practitioners—refer to the hidden labor of maintaining evaluative standards, the affective cost of being evaluated, and the dependency of accrediting bodies on the very researchers they rank—while maintaining the conceptual precision the philosophers expect. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not quote directly from Hegel's Phenomenology; paraphrase his argument entirely in your own terms. The tone should be welcoming, rigorous, and genuinely inclusive of both halves of the room.
