I am writing a novel about a senior archivist at a national cultural institution that operates under what its ethics manual calls a bifurcated loyalty protocol. The protocol formally separates two registers of professional conduct: in the advocacy register, curators are expected to maintain deep, unquantified commitments to particular artists and collections—what the manual, borrowing from moral philosophy, terms ground attachments—which justify passionate, non-transferable support; in the judgment register, those same curators sit on acquisition and funding panels where they must evaluate candidates through anonymized rubrics and are explicitly forbidden from invoking any personal connection or particular commitment. The architecture seems designed to honor both partiality and impartiality by assigning each to its own sphere. I am struck by how closely this mirrors Bernard Williams's discussion in "Persons, Character, and Morality" of the rescuer who saves his wife: Williams writes that if the rescuer justifies his choice by appealing to a moral principle permitting preference for one's spouse, he has had "one thought too many." The institutional protocol appears to institutionalize exactly that division—treating personal loyalty as legitimate in advocacy but as a corruption in judgment. What philosophical commitments about integrity, moral responsibility, and the value of partiality does this bifurcated architecture presuppose? Does it depend on the view that ground projects are legitimate only when quarantined from public reasoning, or can it avoid the implication that our deepest commitments are morally irrelevant whenever universal criteria apply? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in the separation, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and say what would have to change in the protocol—both structurally and conceptually—if one took seriously Williams's suggestion that demanding universal justification for one's most defining attachments is not a safeguard of integrity but its dissolution.
