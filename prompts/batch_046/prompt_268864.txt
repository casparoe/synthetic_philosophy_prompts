I'm preparing a conceptual taxonomy for a graduate seminar on categorization and form. The topic is what makes every token of the lowercase letter 'a' an instance of that letter across radically different typefaces—serif, sans-serif, blackletter, handwritten, and stylized logotypes—using the Letter Spirit framework as one reference point but not as the only one. I need a branching tree that sorts positions by concrete commitments rather than by name. The root should ask whether every valid 'a' shares at least one geometric feature with every other valid 'a', regardless of style. From there, the tree should branch through the following commitments: whether category membership is graded or all-or-nothing; whether morphological roles (such as a bowl, stem, counter, or ascender) are necessary but variably realized; whether family resemblance without shared necessary conditions is sufficient; whether functional legibility in context overrides form; and whether historical descent from canonical exemplars fixes membership independently of present form.

Each leaf must name a position (e.g., essentialist, prototype-theory, family-resemblance, structural-decomposition / Letter Spirit, functionalist, institutional-chain) or be marked 'unnamed,' and must state in one sentence that view's most awkward commitment—particularly regarding edge cases: a single-story glyph readable only as a 'q' in isolation but decoded as 'a' in context; an 'a' composed of four disconnected strokes with no enclosed counter; a corporate logomark that is legible only to trained users; and a blackletter form whose bowl is open at the bottom.

Please construct the tree so that a reader can navigate it by answering yes/no or either/or questions without knowing the philosophical labels in advance. Then, after placing each named position in its logical cell, identify any combination of answers that is currently unoccupied and diagnose it briefly: is it incoherent, clearly unattractive, or simply unexplored? Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your branching logic cannot actually separate, and explain which diagnostic question would be needed to split them.

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Avoid framing questions that require knowing a specific theory's name to answer.
