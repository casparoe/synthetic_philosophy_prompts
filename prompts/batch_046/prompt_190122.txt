At a gallery symposium, Maya and Julian argued over a conservation accident: a preservation process accidentally created a second painting that is a perfect molecular duplicate of an existing original. The original still exists; both now share identical visible properties and neither was made to deceive, but they have different causal histories. Maya said that since both objects afford exactly the same experience and neither carries deceptive intent, destroying one would not diminish total aesthetic value; value lies in present properties and the experience they generate, not in hidden causal chains. Julian replied that this ignores the relational character of value: the original bears a specific historical connection to its artist, and that connection is a distinct good. Even if observers can never tell them apart, destroying the original eliminates that particular causal link, reducing overall value. Maya countered that grounding value in permanently unobservable, undetectable properties is metaphysically extravagant; if no possible observer could distinguish the objects, the causal link does no practical or aesthetic work and should not count in the value calculus. Julian responded that value routinely depends on facts we cannot observe — the value of a particular friendship persists even if an indistinguishable twin existed — and that art is similar: provenance matters even when hidden, which is exactly why a revealed forgery loses value despite looking unchanged. Maya then said the friendship analogy fails because friendship value is constituted by interaction history with a specific individual, whereas aesthetic value resides in form and publicly accessible history; if the history is permanently hidden and plays no role in experience, treating origin as essential is just origin essentialism. Julian concluded that origin is never irrelevant — it is what separates a masterpiece from a perfect accident, and denying that collapses the distinction between art and replica.
