You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in distributive ethics, with particular expertise in prioritarian weighting, the leveling-down problem, and relational dimensions of welfare. Before answering, spend a brief moment brainstorming the main competing interpretations—pure weighted-sum prioritarianism, prioritarianism with an anti-harm side-constraint, and status-sensitive or relational prioritarianism—that could generate the pattern below.

Professor L has issued a consistent sequence of judgments on distributive scenarios over the past decade. The track record is as follows.

Case 1: A tax scheme takes 4 welfare units from each person at level 85 and transfers 3.5 units to each person at level 20; the remainder is lost to administrative cost. L approves, holding that the concave-weighted benefit to the worst-off exceeds the weighted loss at the top.

Case 2: A scheme takes 10 units from everyone above level 80 and destroys them, leaving all other levels unchanged. L rejects this as leveling-down without prioritarian justification.

Case 3: A scheme takes 8 units from each person at level 90 and gives 9 units to each person at level 25, but a disrupted social network accidentally generates a new cohort at level 15. L approves, arguing that the net weighted improvement across the bottom two groups dominates the loss at the top, even though a new worst-off group emerges.

Case 4: A scheme gives one person at level 10 a 40-unit boost to 50, funded by taking 0.5 units from each of 200 people at level 95, reducing them to 94.5. L approves without reservation.

Case 5: A scheme imposes a welfare ceiling of 75 by banning luxury goods that have no direct resource cost but confer status-based psychological benefits. Those originally above 75 fall to 75; those below 30 gain 4 psychological points because the visible gap shrinks, though their material conditions are unchanged. L rejects the cap, stating that harming the well-off for purely relational equality violates prioritarian logic unless the worst-off receive a direct welfare improvement, not merely a comparative one.

Case 6: A scheme redistributes resources so that everyone moves closer to the mean: the group at 20 gains 5, the group at 50 gains 2, and the group at 90 loses 20. L approves, but explicitly conditions approval on the surplus being transferred, not destroyed.

Now consider Case 7, the new proposal. A universal welfare-transparency law mandates real-time public display of every citizen's precise welfare score. No material resources are redistributed. Those originally above the median (around 60) suffer a 6-point psychological welfare loss from privacy erosion and comparison anxiety, falling to approximately 54. Those originally below the median suffer no material change, but gain a 6-point psychological welfare boost from reduced stigma and targeted social support enabled by the transparency, rising to approximately 36 for the worst-off at 30. Crucially, the worst-off remain materially at 30; only psychological and relational dimensions shift, and the absolute resource pool is untouched.

Predict Professor L's verdict on Case 7. Identify which of the six precedents is the closest analog, and explain whether the track record genuinely underdetermines the outcome. If it does, state precisely what principle L would be adopting with each possible choice—approval versus rejection. Then propose a hypothetical Case 8 that would best distinguish the competing rules you identify.

In your response, state clearly how confident you are in your prediction, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; reason entirely from the internal logic of the pattern.
