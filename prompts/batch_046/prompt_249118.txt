I'm working on a story about medieval philosophy sneeking into modern brain science, and I keep comeing back to Avicenna's flying man. Here's an argument my editor says proves way too much, but I can't find the leak. (1) The flying man is suspended in air with no sensory input and no awareness of his body, yet he still knows he exists, so self-knowledge doesn't depend on bodily mediation. (2) If awareness of the self is immediate and non-inferential in this way, then awareness of any mental state belonging to that self should also be immediate — there's no bodily filiter that could hide a mental state from its owner. (3) So if I have an implicit bias, a subliminal perception, or any other so-called unconscious mental state, I must in fact be immediately aware of having it; otherwise it wouldn't genuinely be mine. (4) But psychology and neuroscience depend on mental states we don't have access to. (5) So either Avicenna's point fails, or unconscious mental states aren't really mental, or neuroscience is measuring something that doesn't belong to the self. I'd rather save (1) — it still feels true that I don't need my eyes open to know I'm here — but then I'm stuck denying implicit bias exists, which sounds nuts. Where's the hidden slide? Is it an equivocation between "the self" and "all mental contents," or does (2) smuggle in a transparency thesis that Avicenna never claimed? I'd also like whoever answers to flag where their reasoning is shakiest and tell me exactly what kind of finding or counter-argument would make them give up (1) instead.
