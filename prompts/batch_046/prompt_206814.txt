I am a doctoral student in comparative philosophy working on non-absolutist epistemologies in institutional contexts, and I am exploring whether Jain anekāntavāda—specifically the pairing of naya-vāda (standpoint theory) with syādvāda (seven-fold conditional predication, saptabhaṅgī)—can serve as an analytic lens for modern constitutional adjudication that must navigate irreconcilably plural historical narratives.

Consider the Supreme Court of India’s 2019 judgment in the Ayodhya title dispute. The Court was required to adjudicate between claims grounded in archaeological stratigraphy, continuous religious practice, documentary title, and the legal status of the 1992 demolition, all within a constitutional order that formally prohibits the absolutisation of any single religious narrative. The judgment ultimately awarded the disputed site to a Hindu trust for temple construction, granted five acres elsewhere for a mosque, and insisted that the allocation rested on legal rather than theological grounds.

Analyse this episode through the lens of anekāntavāda, but do not merely observe that “all sides held partial truths.” I want a disciplined conceptual mapping that runs in both directions.

First, identify the distinct naya-s (standpoints) that the judgment actually engaged—archaeological, historical-textual, devotional-occupational, and constitutional-legal—and specify, for each, what facet of the disputed reality it genuinely captured and what it necessarily occluded.

Second, examine whether the Court’s reasoning approximates syādvāda, in the sense that it predicates properties of the site conditionally (for instance, “from the standpoint of documentary title …”; “from the standpoint of uninterrupted worship …”), or whether it collapses these qualified predications into a single, unconditioned dispositive conclusion that amounts to dṛṣṭivāda—the fallacy of treating one adopted standpoint as exhaustive.

Third, consider specifically the institutional risk of naya-ābhāsa: the appearance of multi-perspectival reasoning that in fact smuggles in one dominant naya under the guise of procedural neutrality. Does the judgment’s separation of “faith” from “law” function as a genuine syādvādic qualification, or as an ābhāsa—a simulacrum of pluralism that masks an absolutist allocation?

Finally, what does the episode reveal about the structural limits of applying anekāntavāda to constitutional adjudication? If saptabhaṅgī requires that every predication be held conditionally and simultaneously, can a court—which must issue a single, binding, binary order—ever genuinely instantiate many-sided truth, or does the institutional necessity of award versus non-award necessarily violate the logic of anekānta?

Please keep the analysis tightly focused on the judgment’s reasoning and on the theoretical framework; I am not seeking a bibliography or further reading, but a rigorous conceptual examination of whether this institutional episode confirms, strains, or reveals a deep structural incompatibility with the Jain framework.
