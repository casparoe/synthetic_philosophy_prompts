As a novelist, I am drawn to the boundary between circumstance and authorship, particularly when the circumstance is not human. Consider the well-known episode of the 1815 Tambora eruption and the subsequent 1816 climate anomaly often called the Year Without a Summer: the volcanic winter confined Mary Shelley, Lord Byron, Percy Shelley, and Claire Clairmont at the Villa Diodati, where Byron proposed a ghost-story competition that yielded Frankenstein. Analyze this episode through the conceptual lens that treats extreme climate and volcanic events as historical actors—entities capable of a form of non-human agency—while also holding in view the peril of environmental determinism. Be precise about the kind of agency at stake: does the eruption exercise what might be termed negative agency, operating not through positive intervention but through the removal of ordinary possibilities—travel, outdoor sociability, the rhythms of a normal summer—thereby structuring the conditions under which human choice occurred? Or does any ascription of agency to Tambora collapse into determinism, erasing the deliberate aesthetic judgments, literary inheritances, and interpersonal dynamics that actually shaped the novel? Map the theory onto the case with discipline: identify who or what occupies the role of agent, what counts as the decision-point or action, and which features of the episode—confinement, competition, genre expectation, individual temperament—the framing explains and which it misdescribes. Then reverse the direction: what does the specificity of Shelley's artistic choices, and the fact that not every confined writer in 1816 produced a comparable work, show about the limits of treating volcanoes and climate anomalies as co-authors of culture? Say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and consider whether there is a middle path that grants structural causal weight to the eruption without attributing to it anything like intentional authorship.
