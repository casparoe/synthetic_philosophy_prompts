I work in logistics, not philosophy, but I have spent a lot of evenings trying to make my moral views consistent, and I would like a careful audit before I share them with anyone. Here is where I have landed.

(1) I think supererogation is genuinely real, not merely "very hard duty." Saving a stranger from serious harm at real risk to yourself is morally excellent, but failing to do so is not blameworthy. There is a real line between "required" and "above and beyond."

(2) I think moral reasoning works like weighing items in a budget. Every consideration has a weight, reasons can be compared across categories, and the right choice is the one that balances those weights correctly. There is no separate "supererogatory box" in this accounting — just very large positive entries.

(3) I reject any "moral ceiling." Once you have fulfilled your obligations, the reasons to do further good do not shrink or disappear. They remain just as strong as they were before.

(4) I accept "ought implies can" strictly, but I apply it at two levels: you cannot be obligated to do what you literally cannot do, yet you can also fall short of an admirable ideal without fault even when you do have the capacity.

(5) I believe moral capacity should be treated as a renewable but depletable resource. You should not systematically spend it down on supererogatory acts if that predictably erodes your ability to meet ordinary obligations — to family, work, or community — in the near future.

(6) At the same time, I think that if a specific supererogatory opportunity is right in front of you, and the cost is within your present means, then appealing to some vague future depletion is usually rationalization. The reserve argument only blocks action when the harm to future obligations is direct and foreseeable.

I am concerned that (2) and (3) together make it hard to maintain (1): if reasons stay strong and can be weighed against one another, then saving the stranger looks like it carries overwhelming weight — stronger than many clear obligations — so why is it not required? I am also worried that (5) conflicts with (3): if reasons never weaken, why should my personal depletion matter? Meanwhile, I wonder whether (4) and (1) are just restating the same point in different language, and if so, I should drop one. Please go through these commitments pair by pair. Tell me which tensions are real and which dissolve on inspection, rank the real ones by severity, and for each say exactly what I would have to revise and what that revision costs me. Do not just flag every possible friction; discriminate sharply, and be specific about which commitment is doing the work in each conflict.
