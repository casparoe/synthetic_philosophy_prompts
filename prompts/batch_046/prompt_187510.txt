I am a doctoral student working on the moral ontology of democratic participation, and I should be grateful for help in interpreting a difficult passage in John Stuart Mill’s Considerations on Representative Government. In the discussion concerning the mode of voting, Mill advances the view that the franchise is not to be conceived as a private right—analogous, as he puts it, to the choice of a trade or the disposal of property—but rather as the discharge of a public trust, a fiduciary duty in which the voter is bound to consider the general welfare rather than his own particular interest. To treat the ballot as an instrument of personal gratification, he suggests, is fundamentally to misunderstand its nature.

Yet in the very same discussion Mill appears to allow that, in circumstances where no candidate adequately represents the voter’s convictions, one may legitimately support the least objectionable option available. This looks like a straightforward lesser-evil calculus: the voter selects not the candidate who best expresses his judgment of the common good, but the one who will do the least harm, even though this selection seems to carry no positive expressive content.

My difficulty is conceptual rather than exegetical. If voting is genuinely an expressive act that manifests the voter’s judgment about the public good, then strategic selection of the least harmful candidate appears to evacuate that expressive content, reducing the ballot to a purely instrumental preference ordering. I am trying to determine whether Mill’s framework can accommodate both commitments without contradiction—specifically, whether there is room for what I am inclined to call a disjunctive duty of voting, according to which the fiduciary obligation can be satisfied either by giving direct expression to one’s political judgment or, under conditions of severe moral constraint, by minimising foreseeable harm. Does Mill intend a distinction between the internal morality of the voter and the external mechanics of election, or does the introduction of lesser-evil reasoning necessarily corrupt the fiduciary character he assigns to the vote?

I am not seeking bibliographical guidance or references to modern literature; I want to understand the internal architecture of Mill’s position. Could you assist me in working through this tension? I should prefer the reply in British English, and I should particularly value an analysis that remains close to Mill’s own conceptual vocabulary rather than importing anachronistic decision-theoretic terminology.
