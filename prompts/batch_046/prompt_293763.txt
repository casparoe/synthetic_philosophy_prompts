I've been working through Hume's 1757 essay 'Of the Standard of Taste' and I'm stuck on the logical structure of a passage near the opening. Hume writes: 'We are apt to call barbarous whatever departs widely from our own taste and apprehension: But soon find the epithet of reproach retorted on us. And the highest arrogance and self conceit is at last startled, on observing an equal assurance on all sides, and scruples, amidst such a contest of sentiments, to pronounce positively in its own favour.' I can see he's describing how mutual accusation undermines aesthetic dogmatism, but I want to be precise about what he thinks the symmetry establishes. Is the point that disagreement reveals an absence of objective standards, or merely that confidence should be suspended? Does Hume's subsequent appeal to 'delicacy of taste' resolve the tension by identifying a genuine standard, or does it relocate the arrogance he just diagnosed? Please explain the move in the passage and how it bears on that distinction. Keep your answer to one paragraph, not exceeding 180 words.
