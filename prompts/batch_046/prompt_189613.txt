In philosophy of action and decision theory, Buridan's ass is often compared to a physical control system with hysteresis—say, a thermostat with separate heating and cooling set-points separated by a dead band—so that even when an input sits exactly at the midpoint, a hidden asymmetry (previous state, structural bias, or noise) resolves the tie. Proponents use this to argue that perfect symmetry is physically and psychologically impossible, and therefore that the paradox of choice without preference is only an artifact of an unrealistic model, not a genuine challenge to rational agency. Audit the analogy: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the donkey, the two bales of hay, the starvation, the hysteresis loop, and the hidden tie-breaking mechanism onto one another; then identify where the mapping breaks, especially whether mental deliberation can possess the same kind of hidden-state resolution as a mechanical switch, and whether importing physical symmetry-breaking quietly substitutes causal mechanisms for normative reasons. Finally, determine whether the conclusion—that Buridan's problem dissolves once we recognize unavoidable asymmetry—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along.
