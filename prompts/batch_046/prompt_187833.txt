In debates over whether institutions, geography, or culture explain why some nations are rich, a common framing treats institutions as software running on geographic hardware: the physical substrate—climate, resources, location—is fixed like silicon, while rules and norms are rewritable code that determines economic performance. Proponents use this to argue that institutional reform is the decisive lever, since hardware is difficult to change and software can be patched. Lay out the full correspondence table—what plays the role of the kernel, the drivers, legacy dependencies, version updates, system crashes, and user input—then identify precisely where the mapping breaks: can institutions migrate or be imported without altering the geographic substrate; does geographic transformation (deforestation, climate shift, resource depletion) behave like a hardware modification rather than a constant; and where, if anywhere, does culture sit in the stack? Separate the disanalogies that undermine the institutionalist conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and say whether the conclusion—that institutions dominate because code is easier to rewrite than chips are to redesign—survives once those breaks are visible, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. If a different analogy better captures the interaction without smuggling in that premise, suggest what it is and why.
