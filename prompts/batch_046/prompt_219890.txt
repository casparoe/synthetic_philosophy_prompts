Write an essay on the problem of justifying deduction as dramatized in Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise. The dialogue is standardly read as generating an infinite regress: if every application of an inference rule requires that rule to be added as an explicit premise, then no deduction can ever be completed. Consider, however, a less-discussed variant of the Tortoise's challenge. Rather than demanding that Achilles add modus ponens as a premise, suppose the Tortoise grants that rules may operate implicitly, but insists that their implicit status is legitimate only if they function as defeasible defaults rather than absolute constraints. He then asks Achilles to justify why modus ponens applies non-defeasibly in this particular case, rather than being overridden by contextual considerations or by alternative logics that preserve the premises yet block the conclusion. Achilles replies that the non-defeasible character of the rule is constitutive of logical form itself, not an empirical claim requiring separate defense. The Tortoise responds that this sounds like a meta-premise about the rule, which either requires its own justification—reviving the regress at the meta-level—or is merely a stipulation that masks circularity. Your task is to weigh whether this reframed challenge succeeds in showing that the justification of deduction cannot escape circularity even by appealing to implicit or constitutive status. Must the defender of deduction either accept an explicit, unjustified stopping point, or concede that logical rules stand on the same epistemic footing as empirical premises? Or is there a coherent middle path—perhaps through pragmatic, normative, or competence-based accounts—that dissolves the regress without begging the question? Reach and defend a specific conclusion, explaining which interpretations of Carroll's dialogue it supports and which it forces you to reject.
