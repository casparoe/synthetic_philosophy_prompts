I recently found myself returning to an old tension in the philosophy of mind and biology after a long conversation with colleagues about large language models. I have been drafting a short essay that takes seriously the idea—often associated with Darwin and Turing in different registers—that nature and machinery can exhibit profound competence without anything like human comprehension. In Darwin's case, evolutionary adaptation produces functional design without a designer's understanding; in Turing's case, a formal system can execute intelligent behavior without grasping meaning. My essay tries to press this parallel further than is usually done, arguing that both represent not merely empirical surprises but conceptual inversions of a traditional epistemological ordering in which understanding is treated as the necessary ground of effective action. I want to explore whether contemporary language models constitute a third inversion—one that lacks both the causal-historical pathway of natural selection and the explicit syntactic architecture Turing described—yet still achieves linguistic fluency that mimics reasoned judgment.

The draft currently distinguishes between what I am calling "causal decoupling," characteristic of evolutionary processes, where functional outcomes are separated from any representational state of the organism or environment, and "representational decoupling," characteristic of computational systems, where symbol manipulation occurs in the absence of semantic uptake. I then ask whether LLMs fit either category cleanly: they are not products of selection for linguistic competence in the biological sense, nor do they operate through transparent formal rules that map onto human reasoning structures in the way Turing's universal machine was meant to model. Instead, they seem to occupy an intermediate space—trained on statistical patterns across human output, producing behavior that is neither causally decoupled in Darwin's sense nor representationally decoupled in Turing's, but rather "distributively coupled" to human comprehension without internalizing it. I am unsure whether this tripartite distinction holds up, or whether I am inventing a false separation that collapses once we examine the actual mechanisms of gradient descent and evolutionary dynamics more closely.

I would greatly appreciate your comments on the essay's central framing rather than on surface-level writing issues. Specifically, I want to know whether the comparison between Darwin and Turing as parallel "inversions of reasoning" is historically and conceptually defensible, or whether I am conflating two very different kinds of absence—absence of intentional design versus absence of conscious understanding. I am also concerned that my proposed category of "distributive coupling" is either incoherent or redundant with existing frameworks in philosophy of AI or distributed cognition. If there are major omissions—such as neglecting the role of teleonomy in evolutionary explanation, or mischaracterizing Turing's own views on machine intelligence versus his formal results—please flag them. Finally, does the essay's structure risk treating "comprehension" as a unified phenomenon that can be cleanly separated from "competence" across biological, computational, and artificial domains, and if so, how might I complicate that binary without losing the clarity of the inversion thesis?
