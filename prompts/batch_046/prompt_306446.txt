Argue that the standard defense of term limits as a remedy for career-politician pathologies fails because it addresses only the symptom—long tenure—while leaving intact the underlying structural feature: the transformation of legislative service into a career trajectory with its own internal goods and reputation markets. Develop the stronger claim that replacing election-based career tracks with sortition—random selection of citizens for single, non-renewable terms—does not merely reduce corruption or increase descriptive representation, but eliminates the principal-agent divergence that arises when a representative's long-run career interests systematically decouple from the electorate's adaptive policy needs. In particular, address the objection that term limits alone could achieve the same decoupling by showing that shortened horizons under term limits actually accelerate short-term extraction and revolving-door dynamics, whereas sortition preserves epistemic diversity without creating any careerist incentive structure to be exploited. Conclude by specifying one institutional design feature—such as a mandatory cooling-off period coupled with random selection—that would be necessary for this argument to hold, and explain why its absence undermines even well-intentioned rotation schemes.
