I'm currently developing a novel that interweaves four historical moments of collective defeat—the American Confederacy, Masada, the Kosovo Field, and the Dunkirk evacuation—not as decorative backdrop, but as living structures of memory that continue to shape present political imagination. In my preparatory reading, I've become convinced that scholarship on national myths treats these cases too symmetrically, as if all defeated causes follow the same arc from trauma to monument. But I suspect there is an under-examined distinction between what we might call terminal defeat myths, which seal the past as irrecoverable loss, and generative defeat myths, which preserve the cause as an unfinished moral obligation—often turning on whether the narrative emphasizes voluntary sacrifice over external betrayal, and whether the lost territory remains symbolically present rather than permanently ceded. Before I commit my narrative architecture to this distinction, I want to understand which open conceptual questions in the study of lost causes actually matter most for modeling how these memories remain politically combustible rather than fossilized.

Specifically, among the unresolved problems—whether mythic persistence depends on an active territorial claim versus diasporic displacement, whether honorable defeat requires a specific phenomenology of choice in the moment of loss, how homeland and diaspora communities maintain these structures differently, or a framing I have not yet considered—which should I treat as upstream of the others? If I have limited conceptual space in the novel's framework—say, three years of research compressed into a single comparative logic—which two or three questions deserve priority because resolving them would restructure how I understand the remaining cases? Please defend the ordering with explicit criteria: importance for explaining why some lost causes remain live political forces while others become sealed history, tractability for a writer without institutional access to classified archives or hyper-specialized historiography, dependency—how many other questions hinge on this one being settled first—and which of these are genuinely neglected in the existing comparative literature rather than already well-covered. I am not asking for a bibliography, a literature survey, or citation lists; I want a defended agenda for conceptual work that could actually be pursued without web-dependent verification and then integrated into fiction. Which answers, if they turned out differently, would force me to rebuild the entire comparative architecture rather than merely adjust one chapter?
