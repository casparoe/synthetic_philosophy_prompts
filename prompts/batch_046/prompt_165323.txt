I'm taking an advanced seminar on moral psychology and the architecture of practical reason, and we've spent several sessions on Bernard Williams's objection to impartialist morality—particularly the idea that there are choices in which inserting a certain kind of moral calculation does not merely add information but structurally corrupts the decision from within. In our discussions the point tends to collapse into a vague preference for loyalty over utility, so I want an in-class experience that makes the corruption feel like an internal fracture rather than an external cost.

Please design an 80-minute, in-person classroom activity for about 14 upper-level undergraduates or beginning graduate students. Frame it around a collective "continuity project"—for example, maintaining an irreplaceable multi-generational archive, ecological observation, or craft lineage that requires sustained identity and commitment across time. Divide participants into rotating roles: "continuers," embedded in the project's internal norms and temporal rhythms, and "interrupters," who receive periodic, externally generated moral alerts demanding that specific continuers abandon the work to address urgent but non-unique crises (rescue efforts, advocacy campaigns, or distributive obligations where others could also act). Arrange the rules so that each interrupter must decide not merely whether to help, but whether their specific presence is morally optimal—a calculation that, if performed, gradually fragments the project's coherence through what we might call interruption accounting, even when each individual choice seems defensible. Use non-monetary stakes: accumulated coherence tokens, narrative continuity markers, and a collective mapping board. Run three rounds representing 15-year spans, with escalating ambiguity about whether the crisis genuinely requires that particular agent. The debrief should map the moments of calculation onto the distinction between impartial moral demands and ground projects that constitute a life, noting specifically where participants felt the corruption was internal to their reasoning rather than externally imposed. Include an honest note on which feature of the integrity objection—most likely the phenomenological weight of mortality and irreplaceability—the activity cannot reproduce, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run (for instance, participants treating coherence tokens as fungible utility rather than as narrative identity). Also inject a niche framing extension: ask participants to consider whether moral obligations can be amortized across a life without destroying the project, and whether such amortization itself constitutes the alienating step.

Present the rules, roles, round-by-round timeline, debrief questions, predicted failure modes, and the honest reproduction limit clearly. End with a single line beginning Core tension: that names the specific feature of Williams's view the activity is designed to make participants feel.
