I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun to read philosophy seriously, and I have volunteered to lead a single session for a small circle of fellow retired scientists and engineers who are working through elementary issues in epistemology and the philosophy of science. I do not want you to explain logical omniscience to me; I want you to design the pedagogical architecture for the session. The audience reasons formally without difficulty, but none have encountered modal logic, and they will enter the room assuming that “knowing” is essentially a matter of correct information being stored and accessible. Please lay out the exact order in which the components must be introduced and why—what must be in place before the problem can even be articulated, and where the dependency chain is most fragile. Specify the one carrying example or case that should do the structural work for the whole session, and explain precisely why the familiar detective-who-fails-to-add-two-and-two illustration is inadequate for this particular group. Map the misconceptions they are likely to generate or arrive with—perhaps that omniscience is merely a psychological failure of attention, or that it can be dissolved by appealing to computational limits—and indicate the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted rather than corrected after the fact. Include one diagnostic question I can pose at the end that will reveal whether they have understood that the difficulty is internal to the formal model of knowledge rather than a contingent limitation of human cognition. State clearly what must be left out at this level and why. Finally, please indicate where your proposed teaching plan is most vulnerable: which argument, alternative framing, or contested interpretation of the problem would cause you to reorder the sequence or replace the carrying example, and where the reasoning behind your misconception map is most likely to fail.
