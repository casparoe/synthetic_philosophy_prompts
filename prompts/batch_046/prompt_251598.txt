I'm working through a seminar on democratic failure in classical Athens, and I'm struck by how differently the assembly behaves in two well-known crises: the Mytilene debate of 427 BCE, where the Athenians vote to massacre the male population of a rebellious ally, then reverse the decision within a day; and the Sicilian Expedition of 415 BCE, where they vote to launch a massive invasion despite serious objections, and then refuse to recall the fleet even as disaster unfolds. Both involve the same democratic body facing high-stakes choices under pressure, yet one produces a rapid reversal and the other a fatal commitment. I want to understand this not just as history but as a problem in collective practical reasoning.

Analyze these two episodes through the lens of democratic epistemics — specifically, the claim that assemblies correct errors not primarily by aggregating individual beliefs but by shifting the emotional and normative register through which the same evidence is interpreted. I want you to apply a distinction that doesn't usually appear in the literature: between what we might call informational reversal (the assembly changes course because it acquires genuinely new facts about the world) and register reversal (the assembly changes course because a speaker reframes the existing facts through a different affective or moral lens, without altering the strategic calculus). In the Mytilene case, Cleon's original argument and Diodotus's rebuttal apparently rest on the same strategic facts — the risk of future rebellions, the value of terror — but they invoke different emotional registers: punitive rage versus calculated mercy. In the Sicilian case, Nicias's warnings about risk and Alcibiades's promises of glory similarly share the same strategic information but frame it through fear versus ambition. Use this distinction to map exactly who plays which role in each debate, what counts as the evidence being shared, and whether the reversal in Mytilene counts as an informational update or a register shift.

Does the fact that the reversal happened only when the proposed action was execution — an irreversible harm — suggest that democratic bodies have an asymmetrical capacity to correct decisions about moral horror versus strategic overreach? Or does the comparison reveal that register reversal is only available when there is a competing speaker with enough rhetorical power to trigger the shift, which was present in Mytilene but absent or suppressed in Sicily? Finally, say whether this pattern supports the idea that democratic epistemics should be evaluated differently for different types of action, or whether it undermines the claim altogether by showing that the assembly's corrections track emotional salience rather than epistemic quality.
