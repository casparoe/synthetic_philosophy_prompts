I am mediating a dispute on a community memorial committee following a local tragedy. One faction wants the commemorative inscription to describe the deceased as "robbed of years," treating premature death as an objective harm to the dead themselves—specifically, a deprivation of goods they would have experienced had they lived. The opposing faction insists on language like "freed from suffering," arguing that once consciousness has ceased there is no longer any subject to bear the harm; they say treating death as bad for the dead is like mourning that a demolished statue feels nothing, which confuses absence with injury. To complicate matters, one member has introduced a symmetry objection: we do not regard it as a misfortune for any of us that we were not born in 1850 and thus missed out on two centuries of potential life, so why should missing out on future life after death be any different? The deprivationists reply that pre-birth absence lacks a prior subject to be deprived, whereas post-death absence removes goods from an existing subject. I need to understand the philosophical architecture of this disagreement—particularly whether the symmetry challenge succeeds, whether the "no subject" argument collapses the distinction between harm and absence, and whether there is a coherent middle position that allows us to honor grief without attributing harm to a non-existent subject. How should I evaluate these competing pictures of whether non-existence can wrong the person who no longer exists?
