As a novelist who constructs fictional worlds governed by internal grammars that must remain interpretable by a reader who lacks direct access to my private intentions, I have been drawn to a sharpened version of Wittgenstein's private language argument. Rather than treating mental rules as either present or absent, consider a distinction between a storage architecture—where a putative private language user retrieves fixed internal instructions as though pulling files from an archive—and a generative architecture, where the user dynamically reconstructs normative constraints in real time without relying on any prior repository. Critics contend that Wittgenstein's argument demolishes the storage architecture but leaves the generative one intact, since without a hidden code to misremember there can be no systematic error of retrieval, only an ongoing practice of self-correction that need not be shared to remain consistent. Defenders respond that even generative reconstruction presupposes a criterion of correctness that cannot be self-authorized: if every reconstructed constraint automatically feels right, then the distinction between correct application and mere inclination collapses, and the concept of a rule dissolves.

Examine in particular whether the generative model implicitly imports a hidden communal standard through the back door of temporal consistency—whether tracking one's own past reconstructions requires a stability that only shared practice can underwrite. Consider also whether a novelist's practical intuition—that a narrative's internal logic must survive independent interpretation by strangers—offers analogical support for either side of the dispute. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: does the private language argument survive intact, require substantive revision, or fail altogether once this architectural distinction is fully in view?
