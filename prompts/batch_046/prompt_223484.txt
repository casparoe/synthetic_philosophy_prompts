I've been trying to work out whether insects matter morally, and I keep walking myself into the same wall.

(1) Even if there's only a one-in-a-thousand chance that any particular insect feels anything, there are so many alive at once—perhaps ten quintillion—that the expected number of sentient insects is enormous, far exceeding all humans and other mammals combined.
(2) If being able to feel is what gives a creature moral weight, then insects collectively should matter far more than we do.
(3) That would mean ordinary actions—walking on grass, buying food grown with pesticides, driving—probably cause more suffering than saving a human child prevents, and I should restructure my life around avoiding them.
(4) But that conclusion feels impossible to accept; I don't actually believe I should stop living to avoid stepping on ants.
(5) So either the reasoning is broken, or I'm just too comfortable to face the truth.

The step that traps me is between (2) and (3). It seems I'm treating each insect as holding a tiny "moral lottery ticket"—mostly blank, but with a minute chance of being a full moral subject. Multiply enough tickets and I get a near-certain moral catastrophe. But maybe that's the wrong way to use probability: perhaps uncertainty should only tell me how careful to be, not let me add up half-subjects into a giant sum. If I reject that aggregation, though, do I have to reject all moral reasoning about large numbers? Or can I keep the math and just deny that it applies this way?

Which step should I actually give up—(1)'s multiplication, (2)'s move from sentience to weight, (3)'s comparison with human life, or the hidden assumption that probabilities aggregate linearly across beings? And what does each rejection cost?
