I am a third-year undergraduate writing a term paper for my philosophy of mathematics seminar on whether actual infinities can be coherently maintained. A classmate—call him M—sketched the following argument, which he thinks shows that accepting Hilbert's hotel reasoning forces one into finitism and thereby collapses Cantor's cardinal hierarchy. Each premise strikes me as locally plausible, but I cannot accept that standard mathematics is self-refuting in this precise way. I want to know exactly which step fails and what giving it up costs.

If M's framing rests on a confusion—say, if he is treating membership-relation invariance as analogous to room-occupancy, or smuggling in an undefended potentialist metaphysics—please say so plainly rather than politely repairing the argument for him.

Here is M's argument, with steps numbered:

(1) Hilbert's hotel shows that a completed, actually infinite collection of occupied rooms can accommodate one more guest: shifting the occupant of room n into room n+1 for every n frees room 1, with no occupant left without a room because there is no "last" room.

(2) This violates Euclid's principle (the whole is greater than the part) only for actual infinities; for any finite N, shifting N occupants necessarily leaves the occupant of room N stranded.

(3) So the coherence of the shift depends precisely on the absence of a boundary—on the claim that the infinite procedure is already completed, with no terminal stage that a merely potential, step-by-step process eventually reaches.

(4) Cantor's power-set theorem establishes that for any set S, there is no surjection from S onto P(S). The standard proof constructs a diagonal subset d that differs from every subset in any proposed enumeration.

(5) Now apply the hotel reasoning not to elements of S, but to the subsets in an enumeration of P(S). Suppose we have listed some subsets s₁, s₂, s₃, ... . The list is "full" in the sense that every listed subset is accounted for, but Cantor shows it misses d.

(6) If S is actually infinite, then by (1)-(3) being "full" is never a permanent obstacle: just as we can always shift guests to free a room, we can always extend a partial enumeration of subsets by one more entry—reindexing the existing ones—to accommodate d.

(7) More generally, one can define a "manager's induction" on subset-rank: at each finite stage k we have enumerated k subsets; since there is no highest finite k, the induction never terminates in failure, and the limit stage should therefore contain every subset, including d.

(8) Hence, for any infinite S, there should be no principled barrier to completing the enumeration of P(S). But this directly contradicts (4).

(9) The contradiction dissolves only if we deny that S is ever actually infinite—treating infinite talk as describing only potential, open-ended procedures that never reach a completed totality. Thus accepting Hilbert's reasoning commits us to finitism and undermines the cardinal hierarchy (e.g., |P(ℕ)| > |ℕ|).

M concludes that standard set theory is unstable: it uses actual infinities to derive Cantor's results, then uses those same infinities in Hilbert-style reasoning to show that the results cannot hold.

I do not think M is right, but I also do not think he is obviously confused. I would most like to keep (1) and (4) intact. I am open to giving up either (6) or (7), but I want to understand the price of each: does rejecting (6) require abandoning the analogy between cardinal addition and subset accommodation, and does rejecting (7) require denying that induction can be extended to limit stages in this context? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
