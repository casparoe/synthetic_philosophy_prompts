In a scene I am drafting, two interlocutors—an art historian defending disjunctivism and a cognitive scientist—debate the argument from illusion using a holographic installation that projects a vase at one viewing angle and empty space at another. The historian argues that veridical seeing and hallucination share no common mental state: they are disjuncts—either one genuinely perceives the vase or one undergoes a mere seeming-to-see—so the argument from illusion fails by assuming a shared phenomenal substrate that never exists. The scientist replies that neuroimaging reveals identical cortical activation for both conditions, implying a shared representational mechanism that the disjunctivist must dismiss as phenomenologically irrelevant. The historian counters that neural overlap is not experiential overlap, comparing shared paper and ink to different novels, and accuses the scientist of a category error: treating the physical medium as identical to the perceptual content. The scientist then advances a less common objection: if disjunctivism is combined with phenomenal externalism—the view that experience includes its actual object—then in illusion the object is absent, leaving not a false representation but a mental relation with no relatum, which collapses illusion into an incoherent void rather than a genuine experience. The historian responds that this misinterprets the disjunctive account: the illusory state is not a void but a distinct experiential kind whose proper object is the seeming itself, a genuine mental event that requires no external vase to be fully constituted. Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: assess the strength of each of the four moves, indicate where either party assumes what they need to prove, and say whether there is a stable middle ground—perhaps a relational view that preserves disjunctive phenomenology without committing to phenomenal externalism—or whether the framing itself forces an unnecessary choice between representationalism and direct realism.
