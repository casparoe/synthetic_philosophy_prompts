In a graduate seminar I have been convening on the ontology of affect, the long-running dispute between judgmentalists, perceptualists, and embodied-feeling theorists has settled into a stalemate that feels structurally odd. Every participant now concedes that emotional episodes involve bodily perturbation, that they can be directed at absent or abstract objects, and that they sometimes arise without occurrent conceptual articulation; nevertheless, each camp still insists that only its framework captures the intentionality of grief, indignation, or awe. The disagreement is no longer driven by competing phenomenological reports or by disputed neuroscientific findings—those have largely converged—but by a deeper conflict over what it means for a state to be "about" something. Is this primarily a verbal disagreement, turning on whether "judgment" requires explicit propositional form or whether "perception" can be satisfied by non-conceptual affective framing? Or is there a substantive divergence about whether intentional directedness can be constituted by evaluative orientation without representational content? Might the sides in fact be answering different explanatory questions—one concerning the conditions under which an emotion targets a particular object, another concerning the norms that render it rational or fitting—while treating both under the single heading "the nature of emotion"? Given that diagnosis, what kind of intervention would actually constitute progress: a formal model of non-propositional intentionality, an empirical design that dissociates bodily arousal from evaluative salience, or a reconceptualization that treats "emotion" as a heterogeneous family with no unitary underlying nature? And if the stalemate persists despite agreement on the phenomena, should we read it as evidence that the original question was ill-posed, or merely as a sign that the conceptual tools required to answer it do not yet exist?
