In a postgraduate seminar on the politics of naming atrocities, a fellow student kept invoking the line "genocide is the crime of crimes" to dismiss any attempt to analyse whether a particular episode of mass ethnic violence should be classified as genocide, crimes against humanity, or war crimes, arguing that the distinction was morally obscene once the scale was acknowledged. I would like you to disambiguate the slogan: lay out the distinct precise claims it might be making—about legal hierarchy, about unique moral gravity, about epistemic or rhetorical priority, about the triggering of specific obligations such as the duty to prevent, and about the symbolic politics of naming itself. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or conceptually confused, and indicate which readings, if any, actually justify refusing to engage with the taxonomic question rather than merely answering it. I am particularly interested in whether any reading collapses into the claim that legal categorisation is irrelevant to moral response, and whether that collapse is itself defensible. Please state how confident you are in this disambiguation and single out the point on which you are least certain.
