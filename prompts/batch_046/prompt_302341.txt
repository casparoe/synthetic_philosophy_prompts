I work in distributed systems, not philosophy, but I have been trying to think through mind uploading as if it were a live migration problem: the gradual replacement of biological neural substrate with synthetic equivalents over time, rather than a static snapshot copied to new hardware. I want the conceptual specification first, before any engineering feasibility discussion. Please write the full list of desiderata that a philosophical theory of gradual mind replacement must satisfy to count as adequate—not merely technically plausible, but conceptually coherent.

For each requirement, give the underlying case or consideration that motivates it: for example, why intermediate hybrid states (part biological, part synthetic tissue operating together) pose a distinct problem; why the distinction between a live migration and a backup copy is conceptually loaded rather than cosmetic; why substrate independence needs explicit defense rather than assumption; and why error, noise, and state divergence matter given that a synthetic replacement will not be a bit-perfect mirror. Explicitly mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable—failure on these means the theory does not address the problem at all—versus which are preferences of particular camps (for instance, demands for phenomenal continuity versus functional equivalence, or for strict numerical personal identity versus psychological continuity).

Make sure your list covers at least these problem areas:
- Partial or incremental replacement: the "rolling upgrade" scenario in which some percentage of neural tissue is synthetic and the rest remains biological, with no sharp cutoff, and what the theory says about mental status during the transition.
- The copy-versus-move distinction: whether uploading creates a new instance while the original biological system continues intact, and whether that outcome affects claims about survival or identity.
- Substrate and mental properties: whether the theory must defend substrate independence, may allow substrate-sensitive properties, and what conceptual or empirical pressure should decide between these options.
- Error tolerance and state divergence: how much deviation between original and replacement is compatible with preserving "the same mind," whether perfect fidelity is required, and whether divergence accumulates dangerously over gradual stages.
- Temporal dynamics and interruption: whether the theory treats the mind as a static data structure that can be paused and resumed, or requires continuous dynamical flow, and what that implies for any interruption during migration.

Then, identify whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. For instance, examine whether demanding strict numerical identity of the person across substrate change, combined with substrate independence and genuinely gradual replacement, produces a contradiction or an implausible result. If there is a tension, spell out the exact conflict and which desideratum must be relaxed.

Finally, indicate which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular informal views people reach for in casual discussion about "downloading consciousness to the cloud." Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring named theories, though brief notes on how common intuitions fare against the list are welcome. I do not need citations, bibliographies, references to specific texts, or lists of further reading; I need the conceptual architecture.
