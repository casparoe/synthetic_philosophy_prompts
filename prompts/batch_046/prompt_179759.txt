Consider the following argument about moral sainthood, which seems to show that the very idea of scalar moral improvement makes ordinary moral decency impossible. (1) A moral saint is an agent who always selects, from among the options actually available to her, the one with the greatest expected moral value, given her information and resources. (2) Moral improvement is continuous: for any agent who is not a saint, there exists a well-ordered sequence of available choices that gradually approaches the saintly optimum, without any discontinuous jumps. (3) It follows that for any actual non-saint agent at any moment, there is a specific "next best" choice—one closer to the optimum than her actual choice, yet still within her power. (4) If she does not take that choice, she is voluntarily maintaining a measurable gap between her actual moral state and the optimal state defined in (1). (5) Maintaining that gap for the sake of a non-moral good—rest, artistic work, or intimate attachment—therefore treats the non-moral good as outweighing the moral cost of remaining suboptimal. (6) But if non-moral goods can never outweigh moral costs, then every failure to take the next available step is wrong. (7) So if (1) through (3) are correct, then anyone who is not currently living as a moral saint is perpetually and severely blameworthy: there is no legitimate "good enough," only a continuous wrong of not taking the very next step. (8) This collapses the distinction between heroic virtue and ordinary decency, and makes every personal project that is not maximally altruistic morally criminal. (9) Therefore one of the premises must be rejected. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (2), since without a coherent optimum and continuous gradation the notion of moral progress seems unintelligible. Where exactly does the argument slip? Is there a hidden equivocation—say, between "suboptimal relative to an ideal" and "wrong relative to a duty"—that allows me to keep the structure but block the conclusion? If the flaw is in (6), what does accepting that non-moral goods can outweigh moral costs cost me: does it reduce the saint ideal to one preference among others? If the flaw is in (4), do I lose the normative grip of the ideal altogether? And if the flaw is in (5), how do I distinguish permissible trade-offs from mere rationalization?
