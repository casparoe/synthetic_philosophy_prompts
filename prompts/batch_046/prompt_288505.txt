I am developing an essay on Moritz Schlick's thesis that human life acquires meaning primarily through activities pursued for their own sake—activities he characterizes as play—rather than through the fulfillment of external goals or instrumental labor. Rather than offering a standard exposition, I wish to test a particular reinterpretation. I propose that Schlick's argument implicitly relies on a distinction between the structural frame of an activity, which may be teleological and serve an external purpose, and the experiential mode of engagement, which may be present-focused and autotelic. On this reading, even commitments that are structurally goal-oriented—such as long-term caregiving, institutional maintenance, or sustained collaborative work—can carry Schlickian meaning if they are enacted in a playful mode, provided that the agent is not treating them merely as means to a further end.

The essay would unfold in three stages. First, it reconstructs Schlick's claim that purpose-driven existence tends toward emptiness or infinite regress once a final goal is achieved, leaving only self-justifying activity as a source of value. Second, it introduces the frame/mode distinction and examines whether Schlick's sharp opposition between work and play survives this analysis, or whether it collapses into a merely psychological observation about attention and affect. Third, it raises an objection drawn from the ethics of care: if meaningfulness depends solely on internal mode rather than structural conditions, then even exploitative or repetitive labor could be redeemed by adopting a playful attitude, which seems normatively unacceptable. The essay tentatively responds that Schlick's view is not purely descriptive but contains a normative institutional claim—namely, that meaningful life requires social arrangements that protect domains of play from total colonization by instrumental demands, rather than merely encouraging individuals to reframe labor psychologically.

I would welcome critical comments on three specific points. First, is this frame/mode reading textually defensible given Schlick's emphasis on the objective character of activities rather than on subjective experience? Second, does the care-ethics objection genuinely undermine the reinterpretation, or does it largely restate Schlick's original concern in different vocabulary? Third, is the move from logical-positivist value theory to a normative claim about institutional protection coherent, or does it represent an unjustified leap that the essay must either defend or abandon? I am particularly interested in whether this trajectory makes the argument more relevant to contemporary discussions of work and meaning, or whether it distorts Schlick's core position beyond recognition.
