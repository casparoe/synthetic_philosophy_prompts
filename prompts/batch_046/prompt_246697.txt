Read Camus's The Stranger through the lens of absurdism and contemporary accounts of meaning in life, focusing on Meursault's trial and his final acceptance of the world's gentle indifference. First, explain how the novel frames Meursault's refusal to perform grief or remorse—not as moral failure, but as an honest confrontation with the absence of inherent meaning. Then assess his life against two broad contemporary frameworks: subjective-satisfaction theories, on which meaning requires finding one's own life fulfilling, and objective-significance theories, on which it requires engagement with goods that matter independently of one's attitudes. Does Meursault fail on both counts, or does his refusal to manufacture false significance amount to a kind of negative meaning—a meaningful rejection of the narratives society demands? Finally, examine the prosecutor's insistence that Meursault's life must fit an intelligible moral story. Is this insistence a hidden desideratum that contemporary theories of meaning often smuggle in—namely, that a meaningful life must be narratively coherent to others? Offer a reading faithful to both the novel's scenes and the philosophical distinctions, and note where the text resists being reduced to either framework.
