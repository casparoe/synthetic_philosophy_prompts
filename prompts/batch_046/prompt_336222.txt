Recently I have begun working with an online editorial collective that publishes anonymously, and in our internal debates about whether anonymity dissolves moral responsibility a colleague invoked the Kierkegaardian slogan that the crowd is untruth to suggest that any discourse mediated by an undifferentiated mass necessarily lacks accountability and therefore any claim arising from it can be dismissed without individual rebuttal. I am trying to understand what precise philosophical claims are hiding inside that phrase. Could you lay out the distinct theses it might express, ranging from the trivial observation that groups sometimes make poor decisions, through the claim that anonymity eliminates individual authorship, to the stronger metaphysical or epistemological positions about whether truth requires a singular passionately committed individual and whether the public as such is a fiction that cannot bear responsibility? For each reading please indicate whether it is a platitude, a defensible but substantive claim about social epistemology, or an overstatement that does not actually follow from Kierkegaard's critique of the press and leveling; and tell me specifically which of these readings, if any, would actually support my colleague's move of dismissing an argument solely because it emerges from a crowd-like or algorithmically aggregated context rather than from a named individual. I should add that I am particularly interested in whether there is a niche interpretation that connects Kierkegaard's notion of leveling—where quantitative aggregation replaces qualitative distinction—to contemporary dynamics of engagement metrics and visibility algorithms that treat every interaction as interchangeable; if that connection is philosophically legitimate or an anachronistic stretch please say so, and do all of this in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write in a careful letter.
