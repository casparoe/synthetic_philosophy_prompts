After a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend last night about rescue ethics, I have tried to set down precisely where I stand on whether the numbers should count. I would like an audit of whether these commitments hang together, or whether I am quietly helping myself to incompatible motivations. Here is the package:

(1) I accept something close to Taurek's view: in a direct rescue choice between one stranger and five strangers—no special ties, no prior obligations—the mere fact that there are five does not generate a stronger reason to save them. Each potential victim's claim is equally weighty and non-commensurable; adding further people introduces parallel claims that do not accumulate or multiply.

(2) I also think the separateness of persons is a genuine constraint: I ought not to instrumentalise one individual as a mere resource for the benefit of others. That is why I judge pushing the fat man off the bridge to stop the trolley impermissible, even though five lives hang in the balance.

(3) At the same time, I think the killing versus letting-die distinction is largely a red herring. What explains my judgement in (2) is not the causal mode of death, but the use of the fat man's body without consent. Consequently, if I redirect an existing external threat away from five people and towards one—without using anyone's body as a tool—this is permissible, though I maintain the permissibility does not depend on counting heads.

(4) I hold that moral verdicts should be framing-invariant: describing my situation as 'saving one' rather than 'failing to save five' should not alter what is permissible, because the underlying option set and the claims at stake are identical.

(5) I think that if Group B comprises everyone in Group A plus four additional people, I ought to save Group B. This is not because four extra lives tip the scale; it is because saving Group B involves no abandonment of anyone that saving Group A would have rescued. The choice dominates in a purely structural sense.

(6) Yet in distributive allocation—say, dividing a scarce life-saving medicine—I judge that if five people each require one unit and one person requires all five units, I should distribute one unit to each of the five. Here I seem to be honouring five separate, parallel claims, but the outcome tracks the cardinality of claimants in a way that feels uncomfortably close to the additive aggregation I reject in (1).

Please examine whether this is a coherent package. I am particularly unsure whether (3) can be sustained without smuggling in the very aggregation that (1) denies, and whether (6) collapses into the numerical counting I claim to reject.
