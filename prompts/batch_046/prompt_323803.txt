I've been trying to understand why the discursive dilemma literature never actually resolves anything. You know the setup: a group votes on premises A and B, and also on the conclusion C that follows from them, and majority rules can make them accept all three or reject the logical link, depending on whether you aggregate premises first or conclusions first. List and Pettit turned this into a big deal about democratic reasoning, and since then people have proposed all these aggregation rules — premise-based, conclusion-based, some weighted schemes. But reading the recent papers, it feels like everyone is replaying the same moves: either you defend premise-based aggregation as rational and bite the bullet on the democratic weirdness, or you defend conclusion-based as democratic and bite the bullet on logical incoherence. I'm starting to think the stall isn't about the formal results — those are pretty clear — but about whether 'group belief' is even the kind of thing that needs to be logically consistent, or if its just a bookkeeping device for tracking individual opinions. Maybe one camp is doing normative political philosophy (what should a democracy do?) and the other is doing social epistemology (what should we collectively accept as true?), and they use the same words but ask different questions. Or maybe there's a hidden disagreement about whether rationality for groups is just summed individual rationality, or something emergent.
