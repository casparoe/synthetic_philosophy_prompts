I am taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of language and recently read a study guide circulated by a classmate that attempts to synthesize Austin and Searle for contemporary issues. The guide summarizes their view as follows: Austin initially proposed that performative utterances constitute a special subclass of speech that does things rather than describes them, but he eventually abandoned this binary in favor of a unified account in which every utterance possesses both a locutionary and an illocutionary dimension, with the latter being primary. According to the summary, Searle then refined this framework by arguing that illocutionary force is determined entirely by the speaker's private mental intention, so that conventional rules and institutional contexts only govern perlocutionary effects — that is, what the audience actually does in response — rather than the act performed in saying something. The guide further claims that Austin's felicity conditions are essentially Gricean conversational maxims applied to institutional settings, and that because large language models can generate syntactically well-formed sentences like 'I promise' or 'I apologize' with high statistical coherence, they perform genuine illocutionary acts provided the human interlocutor treats them sincerely. It concludes that the fundamental weakness of speech-act theory is that it overstates speaker authority, since digital communication demonstrates that performative force can emerge purely from audience uptake without any underlying speaker commitment or conventional authorization.

Parts of this sound familiar, but other parts seem to misrepresent distinctions I recall from our readings. I am particularly unsure about the claim that Searle reduces conventions to perlocutionary effects, about the identification of felicity conditions with Gricean maxims, and about whether audience uptake alone can generate illocutionary force in the absence of speaker intention and institutional backing. Could you provide a careful audit? Please distinguish what is broadly accurate, what is a subtle distortion or conflation of two separate claims, what involves using a technical term in its everyday sense, and whether the guide's conclusion about LLMs and digital communication follows from a corrected version of Austin's and Searle's actual positions. I would also appreciate clarification on whether Austin truly 'abandoned' the performative-constative distinction or merely reconceptualized it, and whether the summary's treatment of speaker intention captures Searle's view or collapses it into a form of psychologism he would reject.
