In the working group I coordinate on post-conflict communities, the debate between defenders of what they call mnemonic sovereignty—the claim that a group's lived narrative has authoritative standing over its past even when it conflicts with archives—and defenders of documentary traceability—the claim that obligations to the dead depend on verifiable events rather than felt ones—has been frozen for years. Both sides grant the same archival findings and the same survivor accounts; both agree that suffering matters and that records can be partial; neither has moved. Is the stall a hidden empirical disagreement about how collective identity is formed, a normative clash over who holds epistemic authority, or a verbal confusion in which one side asks what must be acknowledged and the other asks what actually occurred, both under the heading of truth? Or is the disagreement bedrock, resting on intuitions about moral continuity that neither side can justify to the other? Give a committed diagnosis—substantive, verbal, or structurally misaligned—and defend which of the following is the appropriate response: a conceptual distinction that separates the two questions, an institutional division of labor that assigns each domain its own authority, or an acceptance that the domains are incommensurable and progress should not be expected.
