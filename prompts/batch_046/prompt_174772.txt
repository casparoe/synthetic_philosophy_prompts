I'm a software engineer without any formal philosophy training, but a recent conversation about predictive processing and consciousness got me stuck on a specific perceptual illusion called color phi: when two differently colored dots are flashed in rapid succession, observers report seeing a single dot move smoothly through an intermediate color that was never actually displayed. During that discussion, someone introduced a distinction between "Orwellian" and "Stalinesque" revisions of experience. In the Orwellian version, you first have one conscious experience and then your brain rewrites it afterward to match new information; in the Stalinesque version, the original experience never existed at all and is replaced entirely by the reconstructed narrative, like a figure airbrushed from a photograph.

From this framing, my friend constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to swallow the very phenomenon it starts with. Here is the reasoning as I understand it, laid out step by step:

(1) In color phi, the perceived intermediate color and motion trajectory depend entirely on the second dot, which has not yet arrived when the first dot is registered.
(2) So whatever experience accompanies the first flash must remain open to being overwritten — or never fully finalized — until the second flash arrives.
(3) If information at time t+1 can determine the content of experience at time t, then the "present" of consciousness is never really present; it is always under retrospective revision.
(4) Applying the Orwell/Stalin distinction: if the revision is Stalinesque, there was never a raw experience of separate static flashes in the first place — only the smooth-motion narrative ever existed in consciousness.
(5) Generalizing from this case to all perception, our brains constantly run predictive interpolation, functionally similar to frame-generation algorithms that fill gaps between rendered frames based on what comes next.
(6) Therefore, all conscious experience is constructed retrospectively from predictive models, with no unmediated, real-time "now."
(7) But if consciousness is always retrospective, then there is never a present subject available to do the revising — the reviser itself is always one frame behind, revising an experience that belonged to a past self.
(8) So any retrospective model of consciousness eliminates the very conscious agent it was meant to explain.

He insists that this proves retrospective models are self-defeating, but I feel like something slipped between the video-interpolation analogy in step (5) and the conclusion about the missing self in step (7). If I want to preserve the idea that perception involves genuine retrospective construction without falling into the self-elimination trap, which premise or inference should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Must I then accept that there really is an unedited, present-moment experience after all? Please answer in American English.
