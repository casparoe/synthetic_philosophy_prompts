Write a villanelle of exactly nineteen lines, in the traditional form with two refrains and the rhyme pattern A1bA2, abA1, abA2, abA1, abA2, abA1A2. The poem should take as its subject the conceptual problem of comparative moral evaluation in infinite ethics—specifically, the idea that if moral patients are distributed without bound across space or time, every available action produces outcomes with the same infinite cardinality of good and harm, so standard aggregative consequentialism collapses into indifferent totals and can no longer guide choice. Rather than treating this as science fiction, render it as a pressure-test for the structure of moral reasons. Choose one refrain to embody the cyclical, never-closing recurrence of obligation (an “infinite debt” that cannot be discharged by any act), and choose a second refrain to state the failure of quantitative comparison (the idea that no sum exceeds infinity, so no option is better). Use the poem’s turn in the final quatrain to shift from the paralysis of aggregation to the survival of non-aggregative, deontic constraints—obligations whose force does not scale with the number of affected beings—so that the rhyme scheme’s closure on the paired refrains registers not resolution but a changed orientation toward the same unending demand. The language should be precise enough that a philosopher familiar with the aggregation problem and infinite-population ethics recognizes the conceptual architecture, yet it should function first as a poem, with the refrains and rhyme carrying the philosophical weight rather than merely decorating an argument.
