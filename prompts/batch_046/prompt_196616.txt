I've been chewing on the Meno problem for a bit — you know, why is knowledge actually better than just having a true belief? — and I'm drafting some notes for a blog post but I'm stuck in the weeds. Most discussions I see just say knowledge involves justification or that it's more stable, but I keep thinking the interesting part is about counterfactual resilience. Like, if you know something, you wouldn't have believed it in nearby worlds wheather it's false, whereas true belief could just be lucky.

So I sketched out this comparison: two people bet on an election outcome. One actually understands the polling model (knowledge), the other just guessed right (true belief). Only the first one updates correctly when conditions shift slightly. I'm trying to use this to argue that knowledge has practical value because it tracks the right modal structure, not just the actual world. But then I started wondering if I'm secretly just describing safety conditions from epistemology without realizing it, or if I'm mixing up epistemic value with practical utility.

Also, I threw in a half-baked analogy to the unilateralist's curse — the knowledgeable person can act alone with confidence, the lucky one shouldn't — but I think that might be a stretch. Anyway, I'd love some comments on wheather this framing is coherent, if I'm misreading Plato's point (I haven't re-read the Meno since college), and wheather the prediction-market angle actually illuminates the value problem or just makes it more confusing. I probably made some obvious mistake somewhere, so feel free to point it out.
