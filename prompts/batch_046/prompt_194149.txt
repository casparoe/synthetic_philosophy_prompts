I am preparing a long-form feature on how philosophers of epistemology have borrowed from engineering to frame peer disagreement. A recurring analogy—common in both academic papers and popular explainers—treats rational agents as nodes in a distributed calibration network, like temperature sensors spread through a building that report slightly different values. On this picture, conciliationism looks like the standard engineering fix: feed all readings into a Kalman filter and update your state estimate toward the weighted mean, treating divergence as noise to be smoothed out. Steadfastness, by contrast, is likened to a node that detects an outlier and discounts it, preserving its own reading on the ground that it knows its own calibration history. The analogy is frequently invoked to suggest that conciliationism is the rational default—merely the belief-level equivalent of good sensor fusion—while steadfastness is the exceptional posture that needs special justification, like proving your own sensor is more reliable than the network's aggregate.

How far does this actually hold? I want an explicit correspondence table—what in the disagreement case plays the role of the sensor node, the calibration standard, the measurement noise, the central processor or filter, the outlier-detection algorithm, and the "true temperature" being estimated. Then the disanalogies: where does the mapping fracture, particularly around the fact that beliefs are often categorical rather than cardinal, that agents lack a shared ground truth against which to calibrate, that averaging two metaphysical or moral commitments produces not a middle value but an incoherent hybrid, and that the engineering model presupposes interchangeable nodes with common design specs, whereas peerhood is precisely the contested boundary that determines who belongs in the network in the first place. Separate the breaks that matter for licensing conciliationism from the ones that are merely decorative. Does the conclusion—that conciliationism is the default and steadfastness the deviation—survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the normative weight all along? And if the engineering frame is misleading, what is a better analogy—perhaps one drawn from jurisprudence, from distributed scientific collaboration, or from something else—that captures peer disagreement without smuggling in the assumption that there is a single correct belief-state toward which all nodes should converge?
