Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain—on the problem of defining life by necessary and sufficient conditions, using borderline entities that sit between metabolism and replication. The two refrains must be fixed exactly: Refrain A, “We draw a line, but life is not confined” (lines 1, 6, 12, and 18); and Refrain B, “We name a case, yet nothing is defined” (lines 3, 9, 15, and 19). Maintain the traditional villanelle rhyme scheme: rhyme A carried by the refrains and by the first and third lines of each tercet (and the first line of the quatrain), using words in the family of confined/defined/mind/find; rhyme B carried by the middle line of each tercet and by line 17, using words in the family of cell/tell/well. In each tercet, the non-refrain lines should present one specific borderline case—first a synthetic minimal cell stripped of ribosomes and unable to translate proteins or reproduce outside a host; then a virus that evolves and replicates but possesses no metabolism and no self-maintained boundary; then a prion that propagates folded structure without nucleic acids or cellular organization—so that the stanza tests whether Refrain A (autonomy and boundary maintenance) or Refrain B (evolutionary continuity) succeeds. The formal repetition should do the conceptual work: because the refrains never alter, the poem should show that both criteria keep returning unchanged even as the cases undermine them, and in the final quatrain, where both refrains stand side by side without synthesis or revision, the reader should feel the actual philosophical deadlock—neither criterion can be abandoned, neither can claim the category alone. Keep the biological descriptions accurate enough that someone familiar with the debate recognizes the issues (host dependence for translation, absence of autonomous metabolism, replication without genetic material), but do not name specific authors, texts, or theories; do not resolve the tension; and leave the category of “life” open at the poem’s end.
