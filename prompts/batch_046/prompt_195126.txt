I came across a summary of transcendental arguments in an unnamed philosophy blog—likely written by someone with graduate training in epistemology—whose author tried to distinguish the tradition's core move from common misunderstandings. The summary ran roughly as follows. A transcendental argument begins with an undeniable premise about our cognitive situation—say, that I can distinguish my own mental states from other things, or that my experiences appear ordered in time—and asks what conditions must obtain for that premise to be true. The canonical case is Strawson's argument that self-consciousness requires an objective spatiotemporal world: if I could not differentiate my mental states from states of an external world, I could not attribute mental states to myself at all, so objectivity is a necessary condition of self-awareness. The summary noted that critics, particularly Stroud, have objected that this merely shifts the burden: rather than proving realism, it defines self-consciousness in terms that already presuppose realism, so a skeptic can reject the conclusion by rejecting the loaded description of the premise. The author then proposed that transcendental arguments are best read not as proofs but as diagnoses: they show that skeptical denial is unstable because it relies on capacities—like self-reference or temporal ordering—that it simultaneously undermines, without establishing positive metaphysical claims. The summary added that this diagnostic framing explains why transcendental arguments have largely fallen out of favor in contemporary analytic metaphysics, since modern philosophers prefer direct arguments or conceptual analysis that does not invoke conditions of possibility. Finally, it distinguished between capacity-based transcendental arguments, which derive conditions from the exercise of a specific cognitive capacity, and framework-based ones, which derive conditions from the structure of an entire conceptual scheme, and suggested that only the former remain defensible today.
