I am preparing an advanced seminar on Buddhist metaphysics, and during a recent discussion with colleagues I found myself returning to the charioteer analogy as it appears in older English renderings of the Milindapañha. In the passage, King Milinda presses the monk Nāgasena on whether he is the same person who was once an infant, and Nāgasena answers by comparing a person to a chariot: neither the pole, the axle, nor the wheels taken individually is the chariot, yet when arranged together they receive that conventional designation. The analogy clearly denies any enduring substrate beneath the five aggregates, but the text also maintains that the psychophysical stream carries karmic fruits forward in a manner that is not arbitrarily distributed across lives.

What I find conceptually slippery, and what I hope you can help me clarify, is the precise dependence relation the passage intends between the aggregates and the person. The person is certainly compositionally dependent on the aggregates—there is no chariot without its parts—but does the text also commit to an explanatory dependence, such that all first-personal discourse could in principle be translated without remainder into third-personal descriptions of impermanent mental and physical events? My intuition is that the charioteer analogy may conflate these two forms of dependence in ways that modern analytic metaphysics could disentangle. Could you help me understand whether the Milindapañha is best read as advancing a strict reductionist account of personal identity, or whether it preserves a non-reductionist but still anatta-compatible framework for diachronic moral agency? I would especially value your thoughts on whether distinguishing compositional from explanatory dependence alters how we should understand the Buddhist persistence conditions for karmic responsibility without smuggling back in a permanent self.
