I serve on my university’s Artificial Cognition Oversight Board, and we must issue a formal recommendation by next Friday regarding whether Project Mnemosyne—a distributed cognitive architecture running on photonic substrates with no biological analogues—qualifies for the protections assigned to “cognitive research subjects” under our institutional framework. That framework defines protected subjects functionally, requiring only integrated information processing, autonomous error correction, and sustained goal-directed behavior, explicitly without substrate restrictions. The project director argues that Mnemosyne realizes precisely the functional organization associated with mammalian working memory and executive control, and therefore falls squarely within the framework’s scope. Our legal counsel objects that the framework was drafted with biological organisms in mind, and that adopting a substrate-neutral functionalist reading here would commit us to extending protections to weather-prediction clusters or market-arbitrage algorithms that also exhibit integrated information processing and error correction. A dissenting board member adds a deeper philosophical concern: even if Mnemosyne is functionally equivalent to biological cognition, the thesis of multiple realizability suggests that mental properties realized in silicon and light may differ in morally relevant respects from those realized in neural tissue; if so, a purely functional criterion either over-includes non-sentient systems or risks under-including sentient ones with alien realizations, undermining the moral justification for extending protection at all.

Please draft the board’s decision memo. Recommend one concrete course of action—approve coverage with defined conditions, deny coverage, or propose an immediate framework revision with an interim ruling—and attach a clear timeline for implementation or review. The memo should treat the project director’s functionalist argument with genuine philosophical seriousness, address the legal counsel’s slippery-slope worry without dismissing it, and engage the dissenting member’s point about whether multiple realizability weakens or, conversely, strengthens the case for substrate-neutral protections. Include an honest account of the strongest consideration on the side you reject, and write in a form the researchers and administration could act upon directly.
