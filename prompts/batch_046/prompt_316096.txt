Some regional public-procurement systems employ a bidding protocol structurally identical to the dollar auction: participants pay a non-refundable entry fee, then make successive incremental offers that become binding commitments regardless of victory; the runner-up must pay their final bid in full, and any withdrawal forfeits all prior payments, which are pooled and redistributed to remaining bidders as continuation bonuses. Administrators defend the rule not on efficiency grounds but as a seriousness filter that selects only for agents willing to tolerate escalating, unrecoverable losses. What philosophical account of practical rationality does this design presuppose? Does it treat sunk costs as carrying normative weight—something like a deontic obligation to honor prior investments—or does it merely exploit a descriptive psychological tendency toward escalation? Can the protocol be justified under a view of rationality that denies sunk costs any binding force, perhaps by interpreting the forfeiture rules as credible pre-commitment devices rather than moral duties? Reconstruct the implicit theory of commitment and rationality embedded in the practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely fits it, and say what structural changes would be necessary if the underlying view were replaced by one on which past expenditures carry no rational or moral weight at all.
