I am a doctoral student in ancient history, and the distinction between multi-causal explanation and genuine causal overdetermination has become personally urgent: a reviewer of my chapter on the late Republic argued that I was merely stacking contributing factors rather than showing that the outcome was redundantly caused, and I realized I have never had to articulate the conceptual architecture to anyone else. Next week I must lead a single ninety-minute seminar for second-year undergraduates on overdetermination in historical explanation. The students will arrive equating “many causes” with “overdetermined” and assuming that an event with numerous contributing factors was therefore inevitable. I want them to leave able to distinguish a multi-causal explanation—where each factor is a necessary part of one sufficient set—from convergent overdetermination—where independent sufficient sets exist—and, specifically, to grasp a third form I am calling sequential backup overdetermination, in which the removal of one cause does not prevent the outcome because a structurally analogous cause is already queued in the historical sequence.

Plan the explanatory arc: state what conceptual dependency must be secured before the distinction can even register (I suspect the logic of sufficient sets must precede any talk of redundancy), identify the one concrete historical scenario that should carry the session—I am considering the crisis of the Roman Republic from 49 to 31 BCE, but I want your judgment on whether it actually displays the right structural features or should be replaced—and specify exactly what that example must exhibit and what it must avoid exhibiting. Then map the misconceptions in the order they will arise: the inevitability confusion, the dilution error in which students declare everything overdetermined, and the particular mistake of treating institutional decay and personal ambition as separate sufficient sets when they may in fact be interlocked necessary conditions within a single set. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off.

Include one end-of-session classification check—a single comparative case they must sort—that will reveal whether they have internalized the structure rather than memorized my examples. State explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level: I do not want the session to drift into philosophy-of-science debates about probabilistic causation or counterfactual-dependence theories, and I want you to name the boundary line openly. Finally, give me a committed recommendation: tell me definitively whether to use the Roman Republic case or replace it, state the principle that governs your choice, identify the closest analog that would expose whether my “sequential backup” category is genuinely distinct from standard convergent overdetermination, and specify the further case that would settle the matter. Take a definite position with reasons; do not lay out options for me to choose between.
