In my philosophy of science elective we have been discussing Otto Neurath’s ISOTYPE project and his claim that a universal picture language could replace the ambiguities of verbal scientific communication. Our seminar has split over a question that no one has been able to move past: is a pictorial symbol genuinely autonomous, or does every image secretly depend on a linguistic “legend” or conventional protocol that undermines its universality? One side holds that geometric shapes, proportional figures, and stylized icons carry meaning directly through visual resemblance and quantitative scaling, so that a reader without shared spoken language can still grasp a climate or economic chart. The opposing side replies that even Neurath’s own symbols—the stylized worker, the factory, the arrow of correlation—require prior cultural training and verbal glosses to distinguish representation from metaphor and correlation from causation, which means ISOTYPE is not an alternative to language but only a compressed, visually organized form of it.

What makes the stall so frustrating is that both sides accept the same historical facts about ISOTYPE’s design and its failure to achieve universal adoption, and neither considers the other empirically mistaken; yet after months of discussion neither has budged. My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden factual dispute waiting for archival discovery, but a conceptual disagreement disguised as an empirical one—specifically, that the two camps operate with different criteria for what counts as “understanding” a symbol, with one demanding immediate perceptual recognition and the other demanding inferential articulation within a broader conceptual scheme. Still, I am unsure whether the impasse is actually about epistemology, a verbal disagreement about the word “language,” or a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether cognition can ever be genuinely non-propositional. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is—substantive, verbal, or a case of answering different questions under one heading—and tell me whether any conceptual distinction, perhaps between what we might call “iconic syntax” and “iconic semantics,” or between protocol-dependence at the level of acquisition versus at the level of justification, could actually break it? Or is the question itself ill-posed because it presupposes a false dichotomy between pure seeing and pure saying, such that no further argument should be expected to resolve it?
