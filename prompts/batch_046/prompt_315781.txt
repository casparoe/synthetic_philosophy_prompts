I am working through Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England and have encountered a well-known but puzzling passage, which I will present here in paraphrase rather than verbatim quotation. Blackstone writes that the law of nature is coeval with mankind and superior to every other law, binding in all places and at all times; that human laws contrary to it possess no validity whatsoever; and yet that human laws that are valid derive all their force and authority, whether immediately or mediately, from this original source. At the same time, he insists that when human laws do conflict with natural law, private subjects ought nevertheless to obey them, because the public disorder that would arise from private disobedience is a greater evil than the harm of submitting to an unjust statute.

My difficulty is conceptual rather than historical. The conventional reading places Blackstone firmly on the natural-law side of the Hart-Fuller debate, and indeed his language about derivation and validity sounds closer to Fuller's internal morality of law than to Hart's separation thesis. Yet his explicit concession that unjust statutes remain binding on private persons seems to echo Hart's claim that law and morality are conceptually distinct.

What I want to understand is whether these two commitments can be reconciled through a structural rather than a purely moral reading—specifically, whether Blackstone implicitly treats the relation between natural law and positive law as a kind of geometric projection. On this view, positive law is a distorted image of the natural-law original, but for the image to count as law at all it must preserve certain invariant properties—promulgation, generality, non-contradictoriness, and prospectivity—without which the projection collapses into mere arbitrary command or violence. If so, Fuller's eight procedural principles might be reconstructed not as external moral conditions imposed upon law, but as the necessary invariants that allow the projection to hold. Meanwhile, Blackstone's claim that we must obey even unjust statutes might then be read not as a proto-positivist separation of law and morality, but as an acknowledgment that the projection can suffer content-distortion while still preserving its structural invariants, and that private disobedience threatens those very invariants—stability, predictability, and congruence between declared rule and official action—rather than merely producing pragmatic chaos.

Could you help me understand whether the paraphrased passage supports this structural or geometric reading, or whether it ultimately forces us back toward the standard interpretation? I am especially interested in whether Blackstone's appeal to the mischief of private disobedience can be interpreted as protecting the invariant properties of the legal system itself, and whether this makes him closer to Fuller, closer to Hart, or situated in a conceptual space that neither framework fully captures.
