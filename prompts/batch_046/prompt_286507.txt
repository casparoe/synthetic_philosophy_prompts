I have been reflecting on why the familiar dispute between brain death and circulatory death, for all its clinical importance, often obscures a deeper conceptual problem: both sides typically assume that selecting the correct biological criterion will settle what it means for the organism to be dead. But I am interested in a tension that survives even when we adopt a single, unified framework. Consider a view that defines death strictly as the irreversible loss of integrated organismal function—the capacity of the body to maintain homeostasis and coordinate its parts as a unified living system—while simultaneously allowing that, for legal and moral purposes, death may be declared whenever there is irreversible loss of cortical integration (the neural substrate of consciousness, memory, and cognition), even if brainstem reflexes and spontaneous respiration persist. Give me a concrete, detailed example of the conceptual issue this generates: specify a precise patient state—perhaps a prolonged disorder of consciousness with intact brainstem activity—where applying this unified framework consistently yields contradictory judgments about whether the patient is alive or dead. One judgment should treat the patient as dead because the integrative capacity that constitutes personhood has been permanently lost; the other should treat the identical patient as alive because the biological organism continues to function as an integrated homeostatic unit.

Then explain why this contradiction cannot be dissolved by simply redefining "integration" so that it applies only to the organismal level or only to the cortical level without abandoning the very reason for adopting a unified framework in the first place. In other words, show that the tension is not a misapplication but an internal fracture: the framework was designed to capture both biological life and personal existence under one concept, yet the patient state forces them apart. Finally, clarify why this is a distinct conceptual problem from the usual brain-death versus heart-death controversy—specifically, how it turns on a disagreement about which level of biological organization bears the property of being alive, rather than on which physiological system fails first.
