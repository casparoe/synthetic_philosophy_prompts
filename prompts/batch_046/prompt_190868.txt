I'm in a seminar on nationalism and historical memory, and we're working through Hobsbawm's and Anderson's frameworks for how modern nations construct continuity with a past that was often discontinuous. I'd like to apply this to a literary case that seems to perform exactly what they describe, but also to complicate it in ways the standard political examples don't.

Read Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings—not only the narrative proper but its framing apparatus, including the prologue's claim that the story is translated from the Red Book of Westmarch and the appendices with their family trees, calendars, and linguistic notes—through the lens of invented tradition and imagined community. The Shire presents itself as an ancient, stable, agrarian society with an unbroken past, yet its collective identity is shaped by recent trauma (the occupation under Sharkey and the subsequent Scouring) and by archival practices that retroactively establish continuity. How does the novel's own philological framing function as an invented tradition that naturalizes the Shire's national identity? Does Tolkien endorse this construction as benign collective memory, or does the Scouring scene expose it as a reactive invention in Hobsbawm's sense—an attempt to restore coherence after rupture?

At the same time, consider whether the free peoples of Middle-earth—Rohan, Gondor, the Elves—constitute imagined communities sustained by shared symbols, standardized languages, and a sense of simultaneous anonymity rather than face-to-face interaction across vast distances. Where does the work resist being read this way? For instance, does the Elves' deep, lived memory of ages past undermine Anderson's emphasis on the specifically modern character of the nation-form? And what does the novel add to the philosophical debate about whether invented traditions are always instruments of domination, or whether they can also serve as acts of collective healing?

Please stay close to major scenes and framing devices—the Shire's reckoning, the Scouring, the prologue's editorial fiction—rather than relying on minor appendices I could not check. I want a conceptual reading faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the novel to an illustration of Hobsbawm and Anderson, nor should it distort their concepts to fit the plot. I'm preparing this for an essay, so I would value your judgment on where the philosophical lens genuinely illuminates the text and where an honest critic has to say that the work pushes back.
