I'm preparing a seminar on distributive justice, and a discussion with colleagues has left me unsure whether Rawls's separateness of persons objection retains any distinctive force once we move past classical utilitarianism to modern hybrid frameworks—particularly those that preserve aggregation but weight individual welfare by prior moral standing or relational equality rather than by simple summation. One interlocutor proposed that the objection actually bundles two separable claims: distributive separateness, the normative thesis that benefits and burdens should not be summed across distinct persons, and experiential separateness, the metaphysical thesis that first-personal access to harm and pleasure is bounded in such a way that one person's suffering cannot be genuinely "offset" by another's gain. I want to know your credence that the Rawlsian objection succeeds specifically against these egalitarian-utilitarian hybrids—those that reject pure maximization but still aggregate weighted values across individuals—rather than collapsing into a general anti-aggregative intuition that also undermines prioritarianism or democratic bargaining.

Please decompose your answer into the following terms, each with a rough number and one line of justification: first, the probability that distributive separateness is a coherent and non-circular normative principle rather than a restatement of anti-utilitarian intuition; second, conditional on its coherence, the probability that modern weighted-aggregation theories violate it in a way that contractualist or prioritarian alternatives avoid; third, the probability that experiential separateness, if true, actually lends independent support to distributive separateness rather than being compatible with constrained aggregation; then combine these into an overall credence that the objection genuinely undermines the hybrid views, showing briefly how the terms interact. Finally, identify which term is the weakest link in your derivation and specify what kind of argument—perhaps concerning the structure of interpersonal welfare comparisons or the metaphysics of first-personal boundaries—would most move that number.
