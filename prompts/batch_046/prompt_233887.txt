In the Everettian literature on quantum mechanics, the problem of recovering the Born rule from deterministic branching has persisted for decades without clear resolution. On one side, decision-theoretic derivations—most prominently developed by Wallace—treat an agent’s preferences over branching outcomes as constrained by rationality axioms, from which weights proportional to the squared amplitudes are said to follow; critics reply that this either smuggles in probabilistic assumptions through the back door of utility theory or fails to address why nature should care about an agent’s rational preferences at all. On the other side, self-locating or epistemic accounts—associated with Saunders and Vaidman—hold that a pre-measurement observer should distribute credence over post-branching copies according to the same squared-amplitude measure because of a principle of indifference applied across centered worlds; critics respond that without a well-defined measure over uncountably branching worlds this collapses into an arbitrary counting problem, and that “not knowing which copy one is” presupposes the very probabilistic structure it aims to derive. Meanwhile, symmetry-based arguments such as Zurek’s envariance claim to ground the weights in physical invariance properties of the global state, yet they face the objection that invariance under swapping subsystems does not by itself generate a normative or epistemic reason to weight outcomes rather than merely count them. Despite agreement on the formal Schrödinger dynamics and on the experimental statistics that need explaining, neither camp has convinced the other, and the objections appear to regenerate in slightly modified form with each new derivation.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a formal or empirical disagreement—everyone accepts the same unitary evolution and the same statistical predictions—but a disagreement about what kind of concept “probability” is being asked to do in a universe where every outcome is actually realized. The decision-theoretic camp seems to be asking a normative question about how a rational agent ought to weight future experiences, while the epistemic camp asks a metaphysical-cum-epistemological question about what a subject should believe given self-location in a branching structure, and the envariance camp asks a physical question about which symmetries objectively privilege certain weights. These may be three different questions being conducted under the single heading “the Born rule problem,” with each side treating the others’ answers as irrelevant because they address the wrong kind of explanandum.

Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether branches have ontological weights independent of observers, or about whether rational belief revision requires a unique “actual” world to serve as an evidential anchor? Are the sides divided by bedrock intuitions about whether determinism is compatible with genuine uncertainty, or by a verbal ambiguity in “probability” that makes them talk past one another? More specifically, could the stalemate be driven by an unstated disagreement over whether the multiverse contains a discrete or continuous ontology of worlds, such that “counting branches” and “weighting by amplitude” are not competing derivations but incompatible metaphysical frameworks that neither side has made explicit?

Given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress? Would it take a formal result showing that any rational preference structure over branching futures necessarily yields the Born weights without circularity; a conceptual distinction that separates normative decision weights from epistemic credences in a branching setting; an empirical test—perhaps involving decoherence timescales or quantum computing architectures—that distinguishes weighting-from-counting at the physical level; or is the disagreement so deeply rooted in competing standards of explanation that no single result could resolve it? And if progress is possible, which of the three camps—decision-theoretic, epistemic, or symmetry-based—would be forced to revise its core commitments by that result, and which could absorb it without damage?
