In a discussion about whether a split-brain patient or a hypothetical inter-cerebral interface could host a single distributed self, a participant kept invoking Hofstadter’s idea that the self is a strange loop—a self-referential pattern rather than a material object—and summarized it with the line: 'The loop is the self, not the skull.' Separate the precise claims hiding in that sentence. Is it a metaphysical thesis about personal identity, an epistemic claim about self-knowledge, a semantic thesis about the reference of 'I', or merely a rejection of brain-bound essentialism? Say which readings are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false. Then tell me which reading the speaker actually needed to support distributed first-person reference—the claim that 'I' could be partly instantiated in one brain and partly in another—and whether that reading survives once we distinguish continuity of pattern from phenomenological unity.
