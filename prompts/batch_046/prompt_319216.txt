After a long argument with a friend about whether the Knobe asymmetry reveals anything about intentional action or merely tracks moral blame, I have tried to fix my own commitments in writing. Here they are: (1) The harm/help asymmetry is robust, cross-culturally stable, and not a linguistic framing artefact. (2) It shows that normative evaluation—specifically, judging an outcome good or bad—is partly constitutive of attributions of intentionality, not merely a post-hoc bias. (3) I also take the pattern to extend to amoral cases, such as breaking versus repairing a vase, where explicit moral evaluation seems absent; I treat these extensions as genuine data, not noise. (4) From this I infer that folk psychology contains no single unified concept of intentional action, only family-resemblance patterns that shift with contextual salience. (5) I hold further that for pure omissions the pattern reverses: failing to prevent harm is judged intentional, whilst failing to provide help is judged unintentional. (6) Despite (4), I wish to defend experimental philosophy’s vignette method: surveying these patterns is legitimate because they are stable enough across populations to be systematically mapped, even if there is no essence to capture.
