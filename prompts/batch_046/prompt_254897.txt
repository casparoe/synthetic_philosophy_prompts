Consider the institutional arrangement maintained by Switzerland and, in related but distinct forms, by Finland and Sweden: a defence posture built on universal or selective conscription, armed neutrality or military non-alignment, and the principle that territorial defence is a shared civic burden rather than the exclusive domain of a professional caste—whether expressed through Switzerland’s militia-based neutrality or through Nordic doctrines of total defence. This is not merely a strategic allocation of military labour; it embeds a particular relation between the citizen, the state, and the use of force. What philosophical commitments about citizenship, moral obligation, and collective security does this practice presuppose? Does it require a republican conception of civic virtue, in which the duty to bear arms is partly constitutive of political membership? Or can it be reconstructed on purely instrumental, liberal-contractual grounds—as an efficient distribution of defence labour that respects individual rights? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and obligation, distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly requires and what merely accompanies it, and ask whether the same neutrality policy could survive intact if the conscription element were replaced by a fully professional, volunteer-based model. If that substitution occurred, which underlying philosophical assumptions about the state-citizen relationship would necessarily collapse, and which might remain untouched?
