I was discussing with colleagues in a seminar on modern intellectual history whether the Enlightenment should be treated chiefly as the source of modern progress—scientific rationality, rights discourse, democratic governance—or as the source of modern domination, particularly through its conceptual entanglement with colonial administration, racial taxonomy, and bureaucratic rationalization. In these exchanges I keep noticing a specific argumentative pattern that I lack a name for. Critics often build what I think of as guilty lineages: they trace a direct conceptual path from an Enlightenment commitment—say, universal stage-theories of civilization or the classification of human capacities by reason—to a harmful institution, treating the institution as the logical working-out of the idea itself. Defenders, in response, build innocent lineages: they trace the same institution to a selective omission or distortion of Enlightenment commitments—ignoring anti-imperial currents, or suppressing cosmopolitan strands—so that harm is always explained by what was left out, never by what was present. Both sides accept genealogical reasoning—ideas are judged by tracing their institutional descendants—but apply it with opposite directional biases: critics treat conceptual presence as sufficient causation, defenders treat conceptual absence as sufficient exculpation. This feels related to the no true Scotsman fallacy and to debates about internal versus external critique, but neither captures the specific asymmetry in how lineage-tracing is deployed. Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy of history or intellectual historiography? If there is none, what distinguishes it from ordinary cherry-picking, and what boundary conditions should govern its fair use?
