I am a control-systems engineer familiar with state-estimation, sensor fusion, feedback loops, and open-loop observers, but I have no training in philosophy. Restate the core epiphenomenalist thesis — that phenomenal consciousness exerts no causal influence on physical events — together with the paradox of phenomenal judgment, namely that subjects appear capable of forming accurate beliefs about the qualitative character of their own experiences despite those experiences being causally inert, using only the vocabulary of my discipline: state vectors, read-only registers, observer dynamics, signal-to-noise ratios, feedforward predictions, and closed-loop versus open-loop architectures.

Be explicit in your mapping: identify what corresponds to a phenomenal property, to the physical brain-state, and to the act of judgment or report; show precisely where causal closure is preserved; and indicate whether the judgment is modelled as an internal observation, a predictive reconstruction, or an externally triggered report.

Then provide the residue report. State which elements of the philosophical position translate without distortion, which can only be carried over by smuggling in assumptions that lack engineering counterparts (for example, first-person authority, intrinsic qualitative character, or the idea that judgment requires direct causal interaction with its object), and which disappear entirely. In particular, address whether the paradox reduces to a familiar engineering situation — such as monitoring a side-channel that does not influence the controller's dynamics — or whether there remains a substantive philosophical problem that no state-space representation can capture. Conclude with an assessment of whether the residue indicates a limitation in the control-theoretic vocabulary or reveals that the original philosophical claim was less coherent than it appeared.
