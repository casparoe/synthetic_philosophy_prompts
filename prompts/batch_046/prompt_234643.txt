After a long argument with a friend about whether phenomenal consciousness could be causally inert, I keep returning to a particular configuration that seems sharper than the standard paradox of phenomenal judgment. Suppose we grant that brain states cause all our reports about experience, while the experiences themselves cause nothing physical. The usual worry is that we could not know about consciousness if it never causes our beliefs. But imagine something more specific: the physical system generating reports is not merely uncaused by consciousness, but structurally biased so that absence judgments are impossible. Whenever no phenomenal state is present, the brain still produces the report that there is something it is like; and whenever a phenomenal state is present, the brain sometimes suppresses the report, yet the suppression mechanism is itself part of the same physical chain that receives no corrective feedback from the missing experience. The result is not mere ignorance of consciousness, but a systematically false confidence that experience is always present and always correctly reported, with no internal channel through which the absence of experience could ever correct the report. This feels distinct from the usual zombie-world or self-stultification problems, because the error is hidden by the architecture of the correlation itself rather than by a simple gap between world and belief. Is there an established name for this specific failure mode—where epiphenomenalism combines with a one-way correlation bias to make phenomenal absence permanently undetectable to the very system that reports it? If there is no established term, can you distinguish it precisely from the standard paradox of phenomenal judgment and from variants of the zombie argument, and propose what boundaries would keep it from collapsing into ordinary skepticism about other minds?
