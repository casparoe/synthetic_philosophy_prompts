Consider the following argument, developed for a seminar on constructivism, which seems to show that intuitionistic mathematics collapses into contradiction unless it abandons the interpretation that distinguishes it from classical logic. (1) In the BHK interpretation, to assert ∀x∈D.P(x) over a constructively given domain D is to possess a uniform method that, given any element of D, produces a proof of P for that element. (2) Define a domain C of controlled choice sequences: each s∈C is an infinite binary sequence generated by a deterministic algorithm that outputs 0 or 1 at stage n according to whether the sequence has already contained a 0 at an even index; once a 0 has appeared at an even index, all later outputs are 0, otherwise the algorithm consults a constructible but unbounded reservoir of conditions to decide. (3) By the intuitionistic continuity principle, any total function f:C→{0,1} is continuous, hence its value on s is determined by some finite initial segment of s. (4) Define Q(s) to hold just in case the first occurrence of 0 in s, if there is one, occurs at an even index. (5) For any particular s, one can constructively determine whether Q(s) holds by running the generating algorithm, since the rule is fully explicit and deterministic. (6) Nevertheless, for any finite initial segment compatible with both an even-first-0 extension and an odd-first-0 extension, continuity prevents any intuitionistically acceptable procedure from deciding Q(s) without further examination. (7) Therefore no uniform, continuous decision procedure exists for Q across all of C, even though Q is well-defined for each s individually. (8) This implies that the statement ∀s∈C (Q(s)∨¬Q(s)) cannot be constructively asserted, despite our ability to verify Q(s) or its negation case by case. (9) But if universal quantification over a constructively given domain can fail in this way—each instance constructible, the uniform proof impossible—then the BHK reading of the universal quantifier collapses, since it requires exactly such a uniform method. (10) The only escape is to reject the continuity principle, yet that principle is derivable from the BHK interpretations of implication and negation. (11) So intuitionism either harbors an internal contradiction or surrenders the epistemological core that justifies rejecting the law of excluded middle in the first place.

Which step should be rejected? I am inclined to keep (1) and (3), since they capture what is distinctive about the intuitionistic position, and I would rather not concede (10) if it means abandoning BHK altogether. But I want to know precisely where the slide occurs—whether in the move from individual constructibility to uniform failure in (6)–(8), in the assumption that Q is genuinely well-defined across all of C in (4)–(5), or elsewhere—and what each rejection costs for the coherence of constructivism.
