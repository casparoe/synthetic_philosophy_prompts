I've spent some time trying to understand, without any formal philosophical training, whether the person in another possible world who shares my entire past and remembers my childhood is literally me or only a perfect stand-in. I understand the rough division: on one view you can exist across worlds with full identity intact, while on the other—associated with David Lewis—each world contains only its own inhabitants, and cross-world similarity is managed through counterpart relations rather than shared identity. What I keep returning to is a specific tension that feels sharper than the usual debates about origins or psychological continuity. Imagine a very nearby world where, at one branching point, my counterpart—someone with my exact history up to that moment—makes a moral choice I find genuinely abhorrent. If counterpart theory is right, that act belongs to a different person, yet the continuity is so tight that dismissing it as "not me at all" feels like an evasion rather than a solution. If transworld identity is right, then I am in some meaningful sense the agent of that act elsewhere, which seems to make my moral standing depend on possibilities I never actualized. Write an essay weighing counterpart theory against transworld identity, but do so specifically through what I will call modal moral residue: the intuition that our closest possible-world variants may leave an ethical trace on us even if they are numerically distinct. Reach a definite conclusion—can counterpart theory accommodate this residue without collapsing into transworld identity, or does the feeling that our counterparts are "ours" in some loaded sense actually force us to accept identity across worlds? Defend your verdict with reasons, and do not end on a non-committal note.
