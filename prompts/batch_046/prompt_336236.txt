I'm preparing a philosophical essay on comparative historical explanation and have been discussing with colleagues whether the classic Needham-style framing—why modern industrialization occurred in Britain rather than in China—is conceptually coherent or whether it collapses into an ill-formed comparison. I'd like you to take on the role of an expert philosopher of history and comparative sociology. Defend instead a modular complementarity framework: rather than asking why one nation succeeded and the other failed, we should ask which specific institutional-technological modules (for instance, coal-geography-state-capacity linkages or patent-market feedback mechanisms) achieved cross-scale complementarity within one global network and failed to do so in another. Explain how this reframing addresses standard objections—such as China's internal heterogeneity, Britain's colonial extraction, or the problem of anachronistic economic categories—without reducing the comparison to mere particularism or making it unfalsifiable.

Please think step by step: first, clarify the logical structure of contrastive historical explanation; second, diagnose the unitary trajectory assumption and why it misleads; third, present the modular complementarity alternative and show how it preserves comparative rigor; fourth, respond to at least one serious counter-argument (for example, that modular decomposition imports presentist concepts). Write formally and precisely, as if for a philosophy of science audience.
