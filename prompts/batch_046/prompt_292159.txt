Two colleagues in a history-of-science seminar have been arguing about whether non-human forces—volcanic winters and epidemic pathogens—can properly be called historical actors, without falling into environmental determinism. They agree that human institutions matter, but disagree about ontology and explanatory weight.

(1) The first speaker argued that the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora should not be relegated to background context for European political history in 1816. By triggering catastrophic harvest failures and forcing state-level responses—grain tariffs, poor-relief restructuring, and internal migration controls—the eruption initiated causal chains that reshaped institutional possibilities in ways not reducible to human choice. She proposed labeling such forces structural actors: entities that lack intentions but nevertheless constrain and enable human action across ecological and political scales.

(2) The second speaker objected that actor implies at least minimal agency—goal-directed responsiveness to conditions—and that applying it to a volcano is a category error. He warned that this vocabulary invites the very determinism both reject: if historians explain 1816 unrest primarily by pointing to weather, they displace the diplomatic and institutional failures visible at the Congress of Vienna. He granted climate is a powerful cause, but insisted that cause and actor must remain distinct.

(3) She replied by distinguishing three levels of causal involvement: intentional agents (humans), responsive agents (pathogens that evolve under selective pressure from human countermeasures), and structural actors (geophysical events that reshape possibility spaces without responding). She noted that Yersinia pestis during the Black Death altered transmission dynamics in response to quarantine practices, thereby meeting a responsiveness criterion, whereas Tambora remains a structural actor. In either case, she said, recognizing non-human causal weight situates human agency within embedded systems rather than erasing it.

(4) He countered that her taxonomy is a relabeling exercise. If structural actor means only significant cause, then solar radiation and gravity also qualify, and the term adds nothing. He proposed a stricter historiographical test: an entity counts as an actor only if scholars can meaningfully debate its strategies or counter-strategies relative to human ones. By that test, pathogens barely qualify and volcanoes do not. He added that the real peril is not vocabulary but proportional explanation—assigning Tambora primary causal weight for political unrest systematically understates institutional failure.

(5) She conceded that proportionality is central, but argued that his stricter test is anthropocentric: it defines agency solely by analogy to human intentionality. She introduced what she called ontological scaling—the idea that agency operates differently at ecological, institutional, and individual scales, and that refusing to recognize non-intentional forms at larger scales systematically understates how societies are embedded in geophysical and biological systems. She asked whether his framework can account for path dependency induced by exogenous shock without reducing such dependency to a footnote in diplomatic history.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state who is right about the core question—whether non-human forces can be historical actors without collapsing into determinism—and identify what both parties are missing, particularly regarding how explanatory weight should be distributed across human institutions and non-human forces when they interact in a single causal chain. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; work only with the arguments as presented.
