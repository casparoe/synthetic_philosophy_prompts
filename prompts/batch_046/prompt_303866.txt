After a long argument with a friend last night — she kept insisting that to call something a reason just is to presuppose someone for whom it counts — I am defending in tomorrow's normativity seminar the view that reasons can be genuinely free-floating: normative forces that obtain without being addressed to, or possessed by, any reasoning agent. I do not mean this as metaphor or heuristic; I mean that the vector of a reason (what it directs toward) can be separated from its origin (a reasoner), and that this separation is not a reification error but an ontological possibility with concrete bite for institutional design. The audience is mostly internalists in the Williams and Korsgaard traditions, for whom reasons must be anchored in an agent's motivational set or practical identity; there are also two moral realists who accept objective reasons but still tie them to actual or possible agents, and one AI-alignment researcher who wants to know whether a distributed system can be guided by reasons none of its nodes hold. I need a speaker's prep sheet, not a defense: list the objections I will actually get, ordered by damage and by who raises them, phrased in the vocabulary each camp uses. For each, tell me what to concede, what to hold, and exactly where my reply is softest. Pay special attention to the charge that free-floating rationales collapse either into empty formalism or into covert anthropomorphism, and include the objection I am hoping nobody asks — likely the one about whether a malfunctioning thermostat then has reasons to cool the room.
