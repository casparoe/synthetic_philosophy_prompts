In my metaphysics seminar we have been working through the classic statue-and-clay problem, but I want to push the discussion into a scenario that standard textbooks do not cover, without asking you to cite specific authors. Write a dialogue between two equally sharp graduate students—one defending a causal-continuity view of material constitution, the other defending a form-structural pluralist view—set during an office-hour discussion after class. The case they must work through is this: an artist sculpts a human figure in clay, then produces a perfect digital scan at sub-millimeter resolution, destroys the original clay, and later prints an exact replica using chemically identical clay arranged by an automated printer. The causal-continuity student should argue that the printed object is not the original statue, because the spatiotemporal path of the particular clay is broken, even though the macroscopic form and chemical composition are preserved; the form-structural student should argue that constitution is a relation between a form and whatever matter appropriately realizes it, not a historical chain linking specific bits of clay, so that if the form is exactly realized in suitable matter the statue survives. Require real back-and-forth: the continuity student should press the point that without causal linkage we cannot distinguish this case from two simultaneous independent prints made from the same digital file, and should ask whether the pluralist is thereby committed to there being two statues; the structuralist should reply by distinguishing token-form realization from file-identity and should challenge whether causal continuity can account for ordinary gradual part-replacement—say, a conservator replacing half the clay over years—without secretly smuggling in form-conditions. Have them stay in the weeds: discuss whether the digital scan itself functions as an intermediate constituent, whether modal properties such as being the artist’s original work depend on causal history or on formal continuity, and whether either view can accommodate a hybrid restoration in which half the original clay is gradually replaced by printer-deposited clay during a live repair. Do not have either side become a strawman, do not let them talk past each other, do not invoke bibliographies or named theories, and do not declare a winner at the end. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a university assignment.
