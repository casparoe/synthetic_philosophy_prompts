Me and two devs I work with got into it over lunch about whether historians can ever actually escape being presentist — judging the past by todays standards. I do software, not philosophy, so I'm well out of my depth, but they asked me to ask someone smart to referee. No bibliography needed, just reasoning.

(1) Jake said it's literally impossible. Picking what counts as a 'significant' event always uses values you have now. We study abolition because modern people care about freedom, not because 19th-century papers treated it as the big story.

(2) Priya said you can practise 'archival neutrality' — describe past actions using only the categories and moral language the people then actually used, and keep your modern judgement in a seperate mental column. She reckoned this lets you understand them on their own terms without smuggling in your views.

(3) Jake said that's just procedural theatre. Translation always drags in present meanings, and deciding which archives to open is already value-laden. He said 'archival neutrality' hides bias behind a checklist and calls it objectivity.

(4) Priya came back with a concrete case: writing about 18th-century workhouses. You can record that inmates were set to hard labour for moral correction using their own terms, without calling it institutional cruelty. The evaluation is suspended, not hidden.

(5) Jake countered that even framing it as 'hard labour for moral correction' treats the place as a penal-work system rather than, say, a religious charity — and that framing comes from modern sociology, not 1780. So the description is already contaminated before you even reach judgement.

Can you go through moves 1 to 5 and rate each from clearly wrong to clearly right? Also say who's missing what overall, and whether there's any principled line between describing and judging or if Jake's right that it's all one slippery mess. Don't need citations or a reading list.
