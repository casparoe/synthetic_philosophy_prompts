Consider the following argument drawn from Rousseau’s letter to d’Alembert attacking theatrical spectacles. Rousseau writes that the theater corrupts not by showing immoral examples but by restructuring the spectator’s relation to action itself: the audience learns to experience moral judgment and compassion as passive, aesthetic pleasures rather than as obligations that should issue in conduct. A spectator can weep for a fictional sufferer, feel a refined sentiment of virtue, and leave the playhouse unchanged—indeed, satisfied—because the emotional reward of sympathy has been detached from any real burden of intervention. I am struggling to understand whether this corruption should be read as mainly individual, a kind of moral laziness, or as structurally political. In particular, Rousseau elsewhere holds that legitimate sovereignty expresses the general will, a collective orientation toward the common good that binds citizens through self-legislated obligation. Could theatrical identification therefore operate as a counterfeit general will: a shared emotional convergence of an audience that mimics the unity of a sovereign people while lacking the binding, legislative, and universal character of true political will? Or does the comparison collapse because theatrical sympathy is always particular—directed at this character in this situation—whereas the general will is necessarily abstract and applies to all citizens equally? I want help seeing exactly how the passage bears on this distinction, and whether Rousseau’s own reasoning gives resources for deciding between these readings, or whether the two arguments remain independent.
