I am preparing an essay for an advanced seminar in moral psychology and would like critical feedback on both the accuracy of my framing and the philosophical interest of the central move. Rather than summarizing the familiar situationist critique—associated with thinkers like Gilbert Harman and John Doris—I want to develop a stronger, less commonly articulated extension that I am tentatively calling institutional fragmentation of character. The core claim is that the problem is not merely that global personality traits fail to predict behavior across contexts, but that the practical demands of modern institutional life systematically require contradictory emotional and cognitive dispositions within what virtue ethics treats as a single, unified character. For example, the emotional suppression and rapid threat-assessment that battlefield endurance demands appear structurally incompatible with the emotional attunement and deliberative patience that bureaucratic dissent or institutional whistleblowing requires. If virtues are genuinely skills developed through sustained practice, as some neo-Aristotelians maintain, then cultivating excellence in one institutional domain may actively undermine one's capacity for virtue in another—not through weakness of will, but through genuine skill-incompatibility at the level of affective and cognitive habituation. I then ask whether virtue ethics can survive by localizing virtues to specific practices without collapsing practical reasoning into domain-relative fragments, or whether this forces an unpalatable choice between abandoning the traditional unity of the virtues and accepting that modern moral agents are necessarily fragmented in ways that make global character assessment not just empirically unreliable but conceptually incoherent.

Please comment specifically on three things. First, does this argument misrepresent the empirical literature on personality consistency, or does it genuinely extend the situationist point rather than merely restating it in institutional language? Second, are there replies available to virtue ethicists—perhaps drawing on the idea that practical wisdom coordinates local skills, or on the notion that practice-relative excellence need not entail global fragmentation—that I have underestimated or overlooked? Third, is the framing philosophically interesting, or does it risk conflating psychological description with normative evaluation in a way that makes the argument circular? I am especially concerned that I might be smuggling normative conclusions about modern institutional life into what purports to be an empirical extension of situationism, and I would value concrete suggestions for how to keep the descriptive and normative strands analytically distinct without sacrificing the critical force of the essay.
