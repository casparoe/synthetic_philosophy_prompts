I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. In debates about reproductive ethics and climate obligations, I keep encountering a one-liner that is treated as if it settles deep objections: “If a life is worth living, then bringing that person into existence can’t be wronging them.” People invoke it to dismiss worries about having children despite foreseeable hardship, or about selecting embryos in ways that determine which particular individual comes to be. I want to understand exactly what precise claim this slogan could be expressing, because on its face it sounds tautological, yet it functions as a substantive moral answer.

Please disambiguate the distinct theses hiding in that sentence. Specifically, lay out and evaluate the following readings:

- A comparative-harm reading: the claim is that existence is not worse for the person than non-existence.
- An identity-dependence reading: the claim is that under a different choice the same individual would not exist, so there is no stable subject who can be harmed.
- A threshold-welfare reading: the claim is that crossing some “life worth living” boundary nullifies any personal grievance, regardless of comparison.
- An impersonal-evaluation reading: the claim is that the world is improved by this life’s presence, even if no one can personally complain.

For each reading, state whether it is trivially true, genuinely substantive, or false; whether it actually resolves the reproductive or climate case; and whether it smuggles in hidden assumptions about modal identity or counterfactual comparison. I want a committed response—do not merely present a menu and leave the choice to me. Take definite positions on which readings are defensible, which confuse metaphysical identity with moral evaluation, and which one best captures what people actually mean when they use the line. Give your reasons.

Also, I find myself reaching for an analogy from distributed systems: if a server node is instantiated only under a particular network partition, we do not say the partition “harmed” the node by bringing it online with limited connectivity. Does that structural parallel illuminate the philosophical point, or does it distort it by treating persons as instantiated objects? Please address that analogy directly, say whether it holds or breaks down, and explain why.
