Two colleagues of mine—both teaching in medical ethics though neither is a practising clinician—have been arguing over a hypothetical case they devised for a graduate seminar. The patient has suffered catastrophic brain injury with confirmed absence of all brainstem reflexes, yet is maintained on veno-arterial ECMO: spontaneous cardiac activity has ceased entirely, but the machine perfuses the body with oxygenated blood, so the torso remains warm and certain spinal reflexes persist. She opened by saying that if we adopt a circulatory-respiratory definition of death, this patient is already dead, since the heart is not functioning autonomously; but she noted that applying this criterion here produces a strange result, because the body never cools, never develops rigor mortis, and is being actively sustained in a way that makes the usual biological signs of death absent. He replied that the circulatory criterion was formulated for clinical contexts without artificial life support, and that extending it to ECMO yields the absurd conclusion that a warm, perfused body is legally a corpse—an outcome that undermines the social purpose of death determination, namely to provide an unambiguous point at which burial, inheritance, and withdrawal of treatment become permissible without moral ambiguity. She countered that this is precisely why the whole-brain death criterion was developed: to recognise that the organism as an integrated whole has lost its unifying function, even if individual subsystems can be maintained mechanically, and she insisted that integration is an empirical biological concept rather than a normative one. He then argued that if integration is truly the decisive factor, consistency demands that anyone on a ventilator with no cerebral function but with a spontaneously beating heart should also count as dead, which would reduce brain-dead organ donors to cadavers; he claimed this reveals that advocates of brain death are not merely describing biology but are smuggling in a normative judgement about which forms of severe disability amount to the loss of personhood. She rejected this, saying that artificial circulation should not be allowed to override the loss of the brain’s coordinating role, and that the burden lies on him to explain why maintaining blood flow by machine should preserve the status of a living organism when the source of integration is irreversibly gone.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess whether her initial application of the circulatory criterion to the ECMO case is correct or misconceived; evaluate his appeal to the social and legal function of death determination; consider her defence of organismic integration as the proper biological foundation; and judge his charge that brain-death reasoning covertly depends on normative commitments about personhood. In each case, indicate whether the move is clearly mistaken, partly sound, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Finally, say what both parties appear to be missing—whether it is the distinction between death as a biological event and death as a legal status, the jurisdictional variation in how these criteria are actually applied, or some other conceptual point that would dissolve part of their disagreement without requiring either to abandon their core position.
