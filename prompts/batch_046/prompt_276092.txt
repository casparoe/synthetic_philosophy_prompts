I've been thinking about choices where different goods don't seem to sit on the same scale—where you can't just add up points and see which option wins. Here are my gut verdicts on specific cases, and I want to know whether they hang together.

First, career and calling. I could become a physician or a poet; both paths are genuinely open to me, both align with deep parts of who I am, and neither seems "better" in any metric I trust. If I choose poetry, I don't think I made a mistake; if I choose medicine, I don't think I betrayed my true self. The choice is hard, but it doesn't feel like I'm failing at reasoning. In fact, if I flipped a coin between them, that wouldn't strike me as irrational—just as good a method as agonizing for years. So my verdict is: these values are incommensurable, and arbitrary selection is permitted.

Second, a broken promise versus a prevented harm. I promised to meet a friend, but on the way I can stop someone from being badly hurt. I break the promise without hesitation. Here there is clearly a right answer, and the harm to my friend from my absence is outweighed, not merely traded.

Third, my life's work versus a stranger's life. I'm near finishing a symphony that I believe has real artistic value. A stranger needs a bone-marrow donation only I can provide; giving it saves them but destroys my ability to finish the work (the treatment carries a risk to my hands and cognition). I think I should donate and save them. But—and this is crucial—the loss of the symphony isn't "canceled out" by the saved life. There's what I'd call a value leakage: the world is permanently poorer for the unfinished work, and that loss remains real even though I did the right thing. The choice isn't just weighing two quantities.

Fourth, a rescue with unequal social value. A fire traps two people: a surgeon who saves hundreds of lives yearly, and an unknown child. I can save only one. I think I should save the child. To choose the surgeon because of her social utility feels wrong, like treating the child as less worthy of life. But I also feel that losing the surgeon is a greater tragedy for the world, and my refusal to weigh that feels like a kind of willful blindness, not moral clarity. So I have both judgments at once, and they pull in opposite directions.

Fifth, the coin-flip problem that ties back to the first case. If medicine and poetry are genuinely incommensurable, then flipping a coin between them is rational. But if I'm in the fire and flip a coin between the surgeon and the child, that feels not just wrong but monstrous—like abdicating moral responsibility. So either incommensurability doesn't apply to life-and-death rescue, or my comfort with coin-flipping for careers is wrong. I can't reconcile these easily.

Sixth, scaling and protected domains. I'd pay a hundred dollars to save a statistical life somewhere far away. I'd probably pay a thousand. At some point—maybe a million, maybe when it requires giving up my own capacity to live a meaningful life—I think I'm no longer required to pay, even though the ratio of cost to lives saved might be identical. There's a threshold where my own life-project becomes protected, not just "more valuable."

I'm trying to find the principle behind all this. Maybe there's a distinction between choices within a domain of value and choices across domains? Maybe there's a rule about when arbitrary choice is permitted, and a separate rule about moral remainder—this value leakage I mentioned? Or maybe my verdict in the surgeon case has to go, and social utility should count after all?

Please state the principle, or a small set of competing principles, as precisely as you can. For each clause, tell me which case motivates it. Then apply your account to two cases I haven't listed: what should I do if I must destroy one masterpiece to save one life, and what should I do if I must choose between being a present parent and being a devoted monk, with no middle path? Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot save—especially the tension between allowing coin-flips for careers and forbidding them for rescue, and the tension in the surgeon-versus-child case. If you need to drop one of my judgments to keep the account coherent, say which one and why.
