For an upper-level graduate seminar in epistemology, draft a 90-minute final-exam essay question on the Meno problem—specifically why knowledge is better than true belief—that students cannot answer by reciting Plato’s dialogue or standard textbook summaries. To prevent pure regurgitation, embed the following constructed comparison: an artificial system generates true beliefs about distant astronomical phenomena through pure statistical correlation with no representational understanding, justification, or causal connection to the facts, while a human astronomer acquires the very same beliefs solely through expert testimony, never having examined the raw data herself. The question should require students to test whether the usual value-explanations—stability under counterfactual perturbation, action-guidingness, agent-credit, and resistance to Gettier-style luck—actually distinguish knowledge from mere true belief across both cases, or whether they either inadvertently grant the system knowledge or deny the human’s. Provide: (1) the essay prompt, clearly framing the scenario and specifying that students must evaluate at least three distinct value-accounts against both cases; (2) a model answer of roughly 800 words that applies these accounts systematically and reaches a defended conclusion about whether any succeeds without overgeneralizing; (3) a marking scheme totaling 25 marks, divided into five must-hit points, each stating its point value, the specific analytical move required, a one-line description of the typical failure mode, and a brief note on what separates an A-level execution (e.g., recognizing an ambiguity in “stability” between modal and epistemic readings) from a B-level execution (e.g., applying the account correctly to one case but not noticing the parallel failure in the other). The prompt must not request citations, bibliographies, or references to specific scholars by name, though well-known positions may be invoked if they serve the analysis.
