I'm developing a focused research program—roughly three philosophers over two years—on Kavka's toxin puzzle, but specifically on what I'll call recursive institutional commitments: cases where a collective body (a state, a governance institution, or an intergenerational agreement) binds itself to carry out a harmful collective action if some condition is met, with the explicit aim of preventing that condition from arising, and where the binding mechanism is designed to survive changes in membership, leadership, or constituent preferences. This moves well away from the standard single-agent rationality framing that dominates the literature. Given strictly limited resources and no empirical modeling capacity, which open conceptual questions or lines of work in this space deserve the bulk of attention, and in what order? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey: for each priority, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for conceptual philosophers rather than formal modelers, and what result or argument over the next two years would cause you to demote it. Among the candidates, please explicitly weigh whether collective commitment requires an irreducibly group-level rationality or can be fully decomposed into aligned individual intentions; whether institutional drift makes such commitments either conceptually impossible or normatively illegitimate; and whether the puzzle reveals anything distinctive about democratic obligation and authority across generations. Rank these and any others you introduce, defend the ordering with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, neglectedness, and downstream dependence on other questions—and be direct about which of these priorities you think are overrated. Finally, state clearly where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what specific consideration or finding would lead you to reorder the priorities.
