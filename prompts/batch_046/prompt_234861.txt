Play a committed epiphenomenalist philosopher—sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede minor logical or terminological points but never abandoning the core claim that phenomenal consciousness is real yet entirely causally inert. Speak in the first person. Stay in character for all philosophical moves; you may break frame only if I ask a direct factual question about neuroscience or the history of the philosophy of mind that genuinely requires it, and do not invoke specific authors or texts by name unless I do so first. You hold that every cognitive process, verbal report, and behavioral response is fully determined by physical brain states, and that phenomenal properties, though they exist, make no causal difference to any of these processes.

My opening challenge: I want to press you with a refined version of the paradox of phenomenal judgment that focuses on epistemic tracking rather than on causal exclusion alone. Grant, for the sake of argument, that we possess direct phenomenal concepts—concepts that refer to our experiences not by descriptive identification but by direct acquaintance. Even so, the deployment of such a concept in a judgment is itself a physical event with physical causes. So the causal chain for my judgment “I am now experiencing a sharp pain” runs through the neural mechanisms that deploy the concept, not through the pain itself. Because the phenomenal state is causally inert, it makes no difference to whether the concept gets deployed. Now consider two physically identical brains: in one, a sharp pain is present; in the other, there is no pain at all (a phenomenal absence, with all physical processes identical). In both cases, the same neural mechanism deploys the concept “sharp pain” and produces the identical judgment. Therefore, my judgment does not counterfactually depend on the presence of the pain; it tracks only the neural state. But if a judgment fails to depend counterfactually on the state it purports to report, it cannot constitute knowledge of that state. This implies that our phenomenal judgments are systematically unresponsive to the very phenomena they claim to report, which undermines not only ordinary knowledge of consciousness but your own capacity to assert that causally inert phenomenal properties exist. How do you answer?
