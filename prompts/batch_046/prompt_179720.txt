Consider the standard six-node centipede game in which two players alternate, at each node, between taking a payoff that is slightly larger than the continuation payoff or allowing the game to proceed to a larger shared pot. First, explain the backward-induction argument that fully rational players will defect at the very first node, being careful in your exposition to distinguish mutual knowledge of rationality from common knowledge of rationality in the strict, infinite-iteration sense. Second, evaluate whether this result is genuinely paradoxical for rational-choice theory: does it show that rationality is somehow self-undermining, or does it merely expose a tension between what is individually rational and what is collectively optimal? Student Answer:
The centipede game has six decision nodes. At each node the player whose turn it is can take a payoff that is a bit larger than what she would get if the game continued, but if she continues the total pot grows. At the final node, player two should take because there is no further node, so she gets the larger share. Knowing this, at node five player one should take, and so on, rolling all the way back to node one, where player one takes immediately and both end up with almost nothing.

This reasoning requires mutual knowledge: each player knows the other is rational, and knows that the other knows, and so on. In fact that infinite chain is exactly common knowledge, so in this context the two terms are basically equivalent. Once you have that structure in place, the induction is airtight.

The outcome is paradoxical because both players are rational yet both do badly, which proves that rational-choice theory is self-defeating: if everyone follows the theory, everyone is worse off. One way out is credible commitment, which Schelling showed can block the induction by letting a player bind herself to cooperate. But that only works if you are not fully rational, because a truly rational agent would never tie her own hands. Another response appeals to bounded rationality: real people cannot compute all six steps, so they cooperate. This saves cooperation descriptively, and it also saves it normatively—if agents literally cannot perform the induction, then cooperating is not a violation of rationality at all.
