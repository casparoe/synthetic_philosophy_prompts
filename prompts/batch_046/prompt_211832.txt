I sit on the Clinical Consciousness Assessment Board at a university-affiliated medical center, and we must issue a written opinion before Thursday's meeting on whether to incorporate Integrated Information Theory's integrated-information value (phi) into our protocol for assessing residual consciousness in patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness.

The immediate case: a 34-year-old patient, six months post-traumatic brain injury, exhibits no consistent behavioral markers of awareness. A new high-density EEG protocol, however, yielded an anomalously high phi score by integrating the patient's cortical signals through an external computational module. The family is requesting continued life support, arguing that this measurement reveals a conscious substrate invisible to behavioral assessment and that withdrawal would therefore be premature. The attending neurology team responds that phi lacks clinical validation for individual prognostication and should not override established behavioral and neuroimaging criteria.

What makes this a philosophical rather than merely technical dispute is a feature of IIT itself that several board members have raised. The protocol couples the patient's brain with the external integration device, and under IIT's exclusion postulate only the maximally irreducible complex within a boundary is conscious. If the coupled brain-device system achieves a higher phi value than the brain alone, the theory's own logic might locate consciousness in the coupled system rather than in the patient—undermining the family's rationale for maintaining support specifically for the patient. We need a recommendation that takes both the scientific promise and this conceptual coupling problem seriously, treating the family's position with respect rather than dismissing it as scientifically naive.

Please draft the board's opinion with the following sections in this order:

1. Executive Recommendation (about 100 words): a clear bottom line—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—stating any concrete conditions and a specific time frame for review or implementation.

2. The Case in Brief (about 150 words): summarize the patient's clinical status, the family's argument, and explain why this is not simply a disagreement about data quality but a case in which the theoretical framework being invoked may not apply in the way its proponents assume.

3. The Argument for Including the Measure (about 200 words): present the strongest version of the family's and supporting clinicians' position, including why a measure that captures neural integration rather than observable behavior might matter for ethical decision-making, without caricaturing it.

4. The Strongest Counter-Argument: The Coupled-System Problem (about 250 words): explain the exclusion-postulate objection honestly—how coupling the brain to an external computation module might shift the maximal complex, and why this creates a conceptual, not merely empirical, barrier to using phi to justify patient-specific interventions.

5. Recommended Action, Conditions, and Time Frame (about 200 words): state exactly what the board should do (for example, reject incorporation pending specified validation steps; approve only for research contexts with defined safeguards; or approve with restrictions on how the measure may be cited in clinical decisions), including when the decision should be revisited.

6. Assessment of the Losing Side (about 150 words): identify the strongest consideration that the recommended course of action fails to honor—whether the risk of false negatives in behavioral assessment, the moral urgency of not dismissing a possible conscious substrate, or the theoretical possibility that boundary conditions can solve the exclusion problem—and explain why it does not overturn the recommendation despite its weight.

The opinion should read as a formal institutional document: precise, actionable, respectful of the philosophical stakes, and free of bibliographic appeals or citation requests.
