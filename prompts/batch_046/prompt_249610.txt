I have never studied philosophy formally, but after a long conversation with friends about why a twenty-pound note has value and whether race works in a similar way, I have become fixated on a distinction that I cannot quite shake. It seems to me that money only functions because each of us, at the moment of exchange, actively renews our belief in it; racial categories, by contrast, seem to operate in the background, sustained not by any explicit agreement but by the quiet repetition of ordinary behaviour. I am trying to understand whether both are 'socially constructed' in the same sense, or whether one is a fiction we perform knowingly and the other a fiction we perform without noticing.

I would like you to write a villanelle — nineteen lines arranged in five tercets and a closing quatrain, using two refrains that rhyme with each other and recur in the traditional pattern so that they are compelled to share the same space at the end — that takes this specific tension as its conceptual backbone. One refrain should evoke the explicit, almost contractual renewal that keeps currency alive; the other should evoke the unnoticed, habitual repetition that keeps a social kind in place. The turning point of the poem, whether at the volta or in the final quatrain where the refrains are forced together, should expose the fragility common to both: the banknote reduced to paper, the category revealed as maintained rather than discovered. The poem should not resolve into either celebration or despair, but should leave the tension open, as my friends and I left it. Please write in British English, with accurate verse form, and without citing particular scholars or texts, since I am attempting to work out the idea for myself rather than reproduce an argument I have read elsewhere.
