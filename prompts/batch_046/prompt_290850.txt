I've been revisiting the debate between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on whether a deterministic universe can preserve the kind of moral responsibility that justifies retributive punishment. Dennett's compatibilist strategy is to redefine freedom as rational self-control—the absence of coercion combined with responsiveness to reasons—thereby saving moral agency without requiring libertarian metaphysics. Caruso, by contrast, argues that determinism undermines the basic desert claim that offenders morally deserve suffering in proportion to their fault, and he urges us to replace retribution with a public-health quarantine model focused solely on risk and rehabilitation. Most discussions treat this as an abstract metaphysical disagreement, but I want to see what happens when an institution actually tries to build a sentencing framework that honors both commitments simultaneously, because the tension becomes institutional rather than merely conceptual.

Write a deadpan parody of an internal government white paper—approximately 700 words, formatted with numbered sections, subsections, and bullet points—titled "Unified Framework for Desert-Neutral Retributive Practice: Integrating Compatibilist Capacity Audits with Quarantine Protocols." The voice should be that of a mid-level policy analyst who sincerely believes she has synthesized Dennett and Caruso. The document must propose concrete sentencing mechanisms that assess offenders for Dennett-style rational capacities—absence of coercion, responsiveness to reasons, and the kind of "elbow room" that makes choice meaningful—while insisting, per Caruso, that no punishment is ever administered because anyone deserves it, but only for "community value-expression" and "future risk containment." The satire should arise from the relentless, straight-faced effort to hold both views at once: invent bureaucratic metrics such as "Culpability-Neutral Retribution Coefficients" or "Elbow-Room-Adjusted Quarantine Durations," and show how a sentencing board might calibrate prison terms to an offender's demonstrated rational control without ever admitting that this calibration tracks moral blameworthiness.

The piece should be readable to someone familiar with the free-will literature—Dennett's defense of a freedom "worth wanting," Caruso's skepticism about just deserts—without requiring citations, footnotes, or a bibliography; do not include any reference list. The internal logic should remain consistent: the analyst never collapses into pure compatibilism or pure skepticism, but instead produces increasingly baroque institutional rituals that expose the instability of the hybrid view. End with a brief "Implementation Timeline" proposing pilot programs in jurisdictions that have "opted out of metaphysics," maintaining the same bureaucratic tone throughout. The humor should come from exaggerating real features of both positions rather than inventing strawmen, so that a specialist recognizes the parody as conceptually fair.
