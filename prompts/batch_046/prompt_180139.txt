Since my father's recent illness and the unequal suffering I have witnessed around me, I have been trying to reconstruct karma not as a metaphysical mechanism but as a coherent theory of moral desert. Below are my intuitive verdicts on specific cases; I want to know whether they point to a single principle, or whether consistency requires me to abandon some of them.

Case 1: A corporate executive who systematically defrauds retirees lives comfortably into old age and dies without visible reversal. Verdict: If karma is a law of desert confined to one life, it plainly fails here; I reject invoking hidden past lives as an ad hoc rescue.

Case 2: A child born with a severe congenital disorder who experiences only pain and dies young, having done nothing to warrant it. Verdict: This suffering is undeserved; any explanation that traces it to the child's prior moral actions strikes me as morally unacceptable.

Case 3: A person who spends decades anonymously caring for strangers, then dies in poverty and obscurity, never praised or rewarded. Verdict: The absence of visible reward does not trouble me—karma need not be theatrical. But the presence of genuinely undeserved suffering in Case 2 does.

Case 4: Two drivers cause identical harm: one was reckless and intended the risk; the other was careful but struck by sudden mechanical failure. Both suffer the same injuries. Verdict: The reckless driver deserves harm; the careful one does not. So karma, if it operates at all, must be sensitive to intention or mental state rather than to bare outcome.

Case 5: A region overexploits its water supply across generations; the descendants, who did not choose the policy, later suffer drought and famine. Verdict: There is a causal chain that feels like collective moral accounting, yet I resist saying the descendants deserve their suffering, since they are not the responsible agents.

Case 6—the one I keep revising: A dedicated nurse who has spent years treating victims of violence is herself injured as an innocent bystander during an assault on a patient. One part of me wants to say her prior virtue should shield her, or that her suffering somehow balances a cosmic debt; another part insists that karma cannot function as a transferable credit system and that her harm is purely undeserved. I genuinely do not know which impulse to trust.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes karmic desert from causal consequence, or that restricts karma to present mental formations rather than to inter-life accounting—that captures Cases 1 through 5 and resolves Case 6? State it clearly, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed (for instance, a person who genuinely reforms after causing serious harm and then suffers an entirely random misfortune), and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without revision.

Please separate what is broadly settled in philosophical and comparative studies of karma from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding the necessity of rebirth, the coherence of collective karma, and whether karma presupposes a substantive self that persists across lives—and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
