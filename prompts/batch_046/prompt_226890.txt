Consider an applied-ethics advisory panel that has issued the following four non-binding guidance rulings on individual obligations in contexts of collective harm. First, regarding national democratic elections with tens of millions of voters, the panel held that an individual has no moral obligation to vote specifically for climate-mitigation policies, because the expected causal impact of a single ballot on the outcome is negligible. Second, regarding the consumption of factory-farmed meat in global markets of billions, the panel held that individuals do have an obligation to reduce or eliminate such consumption, despite the fact that any single withdrawal has no measurable effect on aggregate production. Third, regarding voluntary carbon-offset purchases intended to neutralize personal air travel, the panel held that there is no obligation to buy offsets, on the grounds that symbolic compensation does not discharge direct obligations and the individual transaction is not causally decisive. Fourth, regarding a local energy cooperative of roughly two hundred households that can fund a community solar array only if membership exceeds a specific viability threshold, the panel held that there is an obligation to join precisely when one's participation is necessary to reach that threshold.

The panel now faces a new case. A decentralized verification network for carbon-credit registries invites individuals to run lightweight validation nodes on personal routers; each node consumes negligible electricity and bandwidth; the network operates fully regardless of any single withdrawal; participation is opt-in, yields a minor reputational credential, and lends institutional legitimacy to carbon-offset markets that the panel has previously treated with skepticism. The technical function—improving registry transparency—is genuinely valuable, but critics contend that widespread participation greenwashes continued fossil-fuel dependence by making offsets appear more credible than they are. The panel must advise whether an environmentally concerned individual has an obligation to participate, an obligation to abstain, or no obligation either way.

Your task: (i) identify at least two distinct overarching principles—such as marginal causal efficacy, direct non-complicity, fair-share collective duty, or institutional legitimation—that are each consistent with all four prior rulings, explaining briefly how each principle accommodates Ruling 2 despite Ruling 1; (ii) predict or recommend the verdict that best coheres with the panel's track record, naming the closest analog among the four rulings and stating honestly whether the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case; (iii) for each competing principle, state exactly what it predicts about participation in the router-node network and what auxiliary assumption it needs to reach that prediction; (iv) say what principle the panel would be adopting if it chose participation, and what different principle it would be adopting if it chose abstention or neutrality; (v) specify one further realistic, non-science-fictional hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the two leading principles.
