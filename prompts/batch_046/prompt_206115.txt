I've been mulling over prioritarianism — the view that helping the worst off counts for more — and that leveling-down objection people keep going on about. The usual story is it's daft to make everyone equally miserable just to get equality. But I had this other thought: what if the moral weight of someones wellbeing depends not just on how badly off they are, but on the spread around them? Like, if theres massive inequality, the extra happiness of the well-off gets 'diluted' or made less urgent because the gap makes their advantage kind of noisy? So maybe if you level down a bit — lower the ceiling without touching the floor — the wellbeing that's left carries more total moral weight, even untill you realise the rich are worse off in absolute terms.

Write an essay on whether this 'spread-sensitive' version can dodge the leveling-down objection, or if it's just the same problem wearing fancier clothes. Look at whether reducing variance can ever justify dragging the best down, and whether that ends up being a hidden argument for equality after all. Don't end on a shrug — pick a side and justify it. Also, if the spread idea collapses, is there a cleaner way to think about moral urgency that avoids both equality worship and this dilution worry?
