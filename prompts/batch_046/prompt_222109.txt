I have been reflecting on diachronic identity in cases of total compositional turnover. Take a decentralized governance protocol that runs continuously for fifteen years: every validator node is eventually replaced, the codebase is fully rewritten through successive upgrades, and all founding participants have exited, yet an unbroken chain of consensus-derived state transitions connects the present system to its original genesis configuration. On my understanding, endurantism must deny that the present protocol is identical to the original, because endurantism requires a persisting object to retain some essential, unchanged core across time, and here no specific node, code module, or founding document survives intact. Perdurantism, by contrast, can treat the protocol as a four-dimensional process composed of successive temporal stages, but this seems to collapse the distinction between genuine persistence and mere causal succession—if being part of a spatiotemporally continuous "worm" is sufficient, then any arbitrarily carved sequence of causally linked events could count as a single enduring object. Given this tension, is there a version of endurantism—perhaps one that appeals to functional or organizational continuity rather than to the retention of essential parts—that can accommodate such total turnover without smuggling in perdurantist resources, or does this case genuinely show that only four-dimensionalism can make sense of persistence through complete replacement?
