I am preparing a discussion of Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart (1958) for a philosophy seminar, and I want to read the novel through the lens of Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood, but with a framing that avoids the usual reduction of Okonkwo's tragedy to individualism versus community. Specifically, I am working with the distinction—common in recent African philosophy but rarely applied to literature—between Ubuntu as an ethical maxim of mutual care and Ubuntu as an ontological thesis that personhood is constituted, not merely supported, by relational recognition. Read the novel's major turning points—Okonkwo's exile to Mbanta, his participation in Ikemefuna's ritual death, the burning of the church, and his suicide—asking whether the text treats relational rupture as the loss of moral standing or as the collapse of a self that had no pre-social substrate. Pay particular attention to Okonkwo's interior monologues, which often seem to presuppose an autonomous, self-evaluating agent: does the narrative ultimately vindicate that presupposition, or does it expose it as a modernist fantasy? Consider also the novel's ending, in which a District Commissioner contemplates reducing Okonkwo's life to a single paragraph in an ethnographic study: does this moment dramatize the violence of treating a relationally constituted life as an isolable object? I want a reading that takes both Achebe's narrative craft and the philosophical distinction seriously—one that says where the idea of ontological relationality illuminates scenes that a purely literary reading misses, and where the novel's complexity forces the philosophy to become more precise, especially regarding whether recognition can be withdrawn without annihilating the person recognized.
