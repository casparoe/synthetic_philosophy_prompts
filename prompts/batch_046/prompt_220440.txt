In my interdisciplinary reading group on Buddhist metaphysics and analytic philosophy of personal identity, a debate about anatta and moral responsibility has stalled despite extensive discussion. The reductionist camp argues that because the self is only a stream of skandhas with no underlying substrate, talk of the same person over time is a pragmatic fiction; consequently, retributive moral attitudes are conceptually misplaced, since there is no enduring subject to hold accountable. The conventionalist camp responds that conventional designation, prajnapti, is not arbitrary but functionally anchored: social and karmic practices depend on tracking a continuous stream, and this tracking is sufficient for a non-substantialist yet robust personal identity that can genuinely bear moral and soteriological consequences. Both sides accept the Abhidharmic analysis of aggregates and both reject a Cartesian ego; they also agree that suffering is undesirable and that karma operates causally. Yet after a year of meetings, neither side has conceded ground.

Recently, the discussion shifted to what one member called the indexical residue problem. Even if identity is conventionally constructed, there appears to be an irreducible first-person indexical—the direct this-ness of present awareness—that is not identical to any particular skandha and does not travel with psychological continuity in the way memory or intention does. The reductionists treat this residue as a linguistic artifact of reflexive grammar with no metaphysical weight; the conventionalists treat it as the functional core that prevents conventional designation from being purely arbitrary, since the indexical anchors the stream from within. The debate has stalled again over whether this residue represents a hidden empirical claim about the phenomenology of deep meditative states, in which autobiographical memory is suspended but reflexive awareness persists, a difference in standards of evidence about what counts as explanatory adequacy, or a bedrock intuition about the non-transferability of first-person perspective that neither side can argue for.

Given your diagnosis—is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or ill-posed? Do the sides answer different questions under the single heading of personal identity? Is there a hidden formal or phenomenological question doing the work? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a phenomenological distinction, a formal model of conventional tracking, a shift in the framing question, or an argument that no further progress should be expected?
