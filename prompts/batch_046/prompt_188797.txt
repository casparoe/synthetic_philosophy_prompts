I was discussing charity obligations with a friend who reads philosophy blogs, and they summarized Peter Singer's well-known "drowning child" thought experiment for me. According to them, Singer asks us to imagine walking past a shallow pond where a child is drowning; since saving her only costs muddy shoes, we clearly must wade in. From this single case, my friend says Singer derives a sweeping principle: physical distance is morally irrelevant, so anyone with disposable income has an ongoing, strict duty to donate almost everything beyond basic survival to prevent distant suffering, with no real difference between rescuing the nearby child and funding malaria nets abroad. The summary also claims that Singer explicitly denies the existence of supererogatory actions—good deeds that go beyond the call of duty—arguing that if you can prevent serious harm at trivial cost to yourself, failing to act is morally equivalent to letting the child drown. Finally, my friend added that this means buying non-essential goods is not just imprudent but actively immoral, on a par with walking past the pond to preserve clean shoes.

I have no formal training in ethics, but something about this feels like it jumps from a compelling intuition to an overwhelming conclusion, and I am especially unsure about the part involving supererogation, since that sounds like a technical idea I have only seen mentioned in passing. Could you go through the summary point by point and tell me what Singer actually holds, what is a distortion or an import from later effective-altruist writing, and whether the denial of supererogation genuinely follows from the pond scenario? In particular, I want to know if the argument secretly depends on a controversial view about our obligations to strangers that the summary takes for granted, or whether there is a more modest reading of the example that stops well short of saying I must give away nearly all my income. I am trying to decide whether my friend has captured Singer's view or turned a specific argument about rescue into a general moral demand that Singer himself might resist.
