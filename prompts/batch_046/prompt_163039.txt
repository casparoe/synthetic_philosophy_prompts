I'm working through an argument in my intergenerational ethics seminar that seems to show climate-policy choices are morally indifferent, and I'm struggling to locate the precise error. Here is the reasoning, laid out step by step:

(1) A policy choice is morally wrong in a person-affecting sense only if it wrongs at least one specific, identifiable individual who actually exists in the world that results from that choice.
(2) If policy A produces future population P and policy B produces a non-overlapping future population Q—because altered economic conditions, migration patterns, and reproductive timelines yield different sets of people—then choosing A does not wrong anyone in Q (they never exist under A), and choosing B does not wrong anyone in P (they never exist under B).
(3) Therefore, whenever a choice determines who will come to exist, no person-affecting wrong can be done to the future people who result.
(4) Aggressive solar-radiation management and deep decarbonization will generate radically different demographic trajectories over the next two centuries: the former preserves fossil-fuel economies and particular urbanization patterns, while the latter reshapes energy infrastructure, labor migration, and family-formation timelines.
(5) Even if the management pathway produces widespread but sub-lethal respiratory harm from stratospheric aerosol fallout, the affected individuals are not wronged by that pathway, because under decarbonization they would never have been born.
(6) By parallel reasoning, if decarbonization produces energy-poverty-related morbidity among a different cohort, those individuals are not wronged either.
(7) Hence there is no person-affecting moral reason to prefer decarbonization to management; any reason must be impersonal, such as maximizing aggregate well-being across possible populations.
(8) But this proves too much: it implies that we could deliberately select a pathway that predictably generates severe, widespread suffering—provided no one's life falls below the threshold of being worth living—without wronging anyone, which conflicts with the intuition that deliberately imposing such suffering on future generations is gravely wrong.
(9) So one of the premises must be abandoned.

The instructor wants us to identify the weakest step, explain why it initially looks defensible, and then spell out what giving it up costs for the three standard positions in this debate—the person-affecting restriction, the impersonal comparative view, and the contractualist account that treats future people as having claims regardless of modal origin. I am particularly reluctant to surrender premise (1), since it captures the intuition that morality must be answerable to actual people. Yet I also worry that rejecting (4) requires an implausible claim about demographic determinism being false. Which step should I reject, and what does each of the three positions have to pay?
