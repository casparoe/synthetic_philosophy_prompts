I am serving as a TA for an upper-level seminar on applied moral philosophy, and I need to evaluate a student response to one of our exam questions. The course unit covered suffering-focused ethics, including the distinction between weak negative utilitarianism—where suffering carries greater moral weight than happiness but need not be lexically prior—and strong negative utilitarianism, where any quantity of suffering outweighs any quantity of happiness. We also discussed the asymmetry argument regarding the harm of suffering versus the absence of corresponding deprivation in non-existence, along with the scenario sometimes called the benevolent world exploder.

Question presented to students:
Some philosophers argue that the asymmetry between suffering and happiness—specifically, that the presence of suffering is always bad and its absence always good, while the presence of happiness is good but its absence is not bad—directly entails strong negative utilitarianism and, consequently, the conclusion that a world with zero sentient beings is preferable to any world containing both happiness and suffering. Evaluate this inference. Your answer should: (a) distinguish weak from strong negative utilitarianism; (b) determine whether the asymmetry argument actually licenses the move from weak to strong NU; (c) identify any unstated premises required to reach the extinction conclusion; and (d) explain whether a weak negative utilitarian could consistently reject the world-exploder conclusion without abandoning the asymmetry intuition.

Student answer:
The inference is essentially valid, though it requires one explicit premise about moral permissibility. The asymmetry shows that suffering has a moral status that happiness lacks: suffering is always a harm, and avoiding it is always a benefit, whereas happiness is a benefit but its absence is not a deprivation. This means happiness carries no compensatory weight. If suffering exists in a population, happiness within that same population does not offset it, because if those happy individuals had never existed, they would not have been harmed by missing that happiness—exactly the point of the asymmetry. Therefore, any world containing suffering is worse than a world without sentient beings, regardless of how much happiness is present. This is strong negative utilitarianism: suffering dominates happiness lexically. The weak position tries to maintain that happiness still counts positively, but this is inconsistent with the asymmetry—if happiness truly mattered, then failing to create happy people would be a harm, which the asymmetry denies. So weak NU collapses into strong NU. The world-exploder conclusion then follows: since any actual world will contain some suffering, eliminating all sentient beings removes all suffering and sacrifices nothing of moral value, because happiness was never a moral gain. The only unstated premise is that active extinction is permissible, but the anti-natalist logic extends naturally to existing beings—if it is wrong to create beings who will suffer, it is wrong to preserve them. Thus the argument holds, and the weak NU objection fails.
