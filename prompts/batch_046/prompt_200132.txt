I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop on collective cognition and distributed consciousness. The audience will be divided roughly evenly between analytic philosophers of mind—several of whom specialize in panpsychism and the combination problem—and cognitive scientists who study group-level information integration but have limited patience for ungrounded metaphysics. I need the remarks to run about eight minutes, to be formally polished but not excessively academic in tone, and to do genuine structural work rather than serve as decorative framing.

Specifically, I want the speech to be organized around a conceptual distinction I have been developing in my own research: the difference between aggregate phenomenology, in which macro-subjects are composed of micro-subjects that share homogeneous qualitative characters so that the whole is merely a summed or amplified version of its parts, and synthetic phenomenology, in which micro-subjects bear radically heterogeneous qualitative characters that combine to produce macro-subjects with emergent phenomenal structures not present in any constituent—analogous to the way primary color qualities yield novel hues that are not properties of any single wavelength. I want to use this distinction to frame the workshop’s central question: when an empirical system—whether a collaborative research team, a densely coupled neural assembly, or an artificial distributed network—exhibits highly integrated information processing, should we understand it as an aggregate of individual conscious perspectives, as a genuinely synthetic collective subject, or as falling outside both categories entirely?

The speech must be fully accessible to the scientists without condescending to the philosophers, and it must avoid any bibliography, reading list, citation, or appeal to authority by name. It should not ask the audience to commit to panpsychism; rather, it should use the combination problem—particularly the challenge of explaining how heterogeneous micro-qualities could bond into unified macro-experience—to show that the metaphysical question of macro-subjectivity and the empirical study of collective cognition are structurally parallel inquiries. Please write these opening remarks so that the aggregate-versus-synthetic framing genuinely constrains what can and cannot be said about collective minds during the rest of the workshop.
