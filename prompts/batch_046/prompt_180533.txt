I'm grading final exam answers for my metaphysics seminar. The exam asked students to consider a modified statue-and-lump scenario: the statue is formed from a self-healing ceramic in which every constituent particle is gradually replaced over fifty years, with no discontinuity in shape or artistic properties. The specific question was whether the standard modal-property argument for non-identity—the statue could not survive being flattened, whereas the material aggregate could—remains sound in this case, and whether continuous replacement undermines or strengthens the reasoning.

Below is one student's answer. Please assign it a letter grade and provide specific, point-by-point comments justifying the evaluation. Focus on the logical structure, the handling of temporal parts or aggregates over time, and any misapplication of constitution theories.

Student answer:
"The modal argument still holds. The statue—call it Form—can survive gradual replacement but cannot survive flattening. The current ceramic aggregate—Matter—can survive flattening, since its particles remain, but it cannot survive complete replacement, since then it is numerically a different aggregate. By Leibniz's law, Form is not identical to Matter. This result supports Baker's constitution view: the statue is constituted by different aggregates at different times, but is never identical to any of them. The error in ordinary thinking is mereological essentialism—the belief that an object must keep the same parts. Baker rejects this, and the continuous-replacement case confirms that the statue persists despite part-change, which is exactly what constitution without identity predicts. Thus the modified case strengthens, rather than weakens, the non-identity claim."
