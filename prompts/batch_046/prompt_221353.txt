In discussions of social choice theory one often hears Sen's liberal paradox treated as structurally identical to Arrow's impossibility result: both begin with apparently uncontroversial conditions—universal domain, Pareto efficiency, and minimal liberty in Sen's framework; unrestricted domain, transitivity, and Pareto in Arrow's—only to show that they cannot all be jointly satisfied, and the comparison is used to license the conclusion that liberal rights, like rationality conditions, are expendable constraints that must be dropped if we want any coherent collective decision procedure. I want an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly—what plays the role of Arrow's unrestricted domain, transitivity, and independence of irrelevant alternatives within Sen's conditions—then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not: in particular, does the conflict in Sen's case arise from the internal logic of preference aggregation, or does it depend on a prior normative commitment to individual sovereignty over specific choice domains? To push beyond the standard two-person, single-decision formulation, consider an extension with three agents, each assigned a nested hierarchy of liberal domains—say, primary dietary choice, then meal timing, then kitchen access—where exercising a lower-level right can constrain the higher-level options available to others. Does the paradox still arise in this nested structure, does it multiply across levels, or does it dissolve? And once these structural differences and the nested-domain case are fully on the table, does the conclusion that liberalism must be abandoned for the sake of coherence survive, or was it being carried by the Arrow analogy all along?
