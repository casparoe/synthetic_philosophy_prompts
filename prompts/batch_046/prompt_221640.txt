I have recently begun advising on the design of anonymous peer-review platforms, and this has made Glaucon’s challenge unexpectedly personal: I observe people altering their conduct precisely when they believe it is untraceable, yet the philosophical debate about whether one ought to be moral without the ring of Gyges seems entirely stalled. For generations, responses have recycled the same moves — either morality is shown to be instrumentally rational through repeated interaction or reputation mechanisms, or it is defended as constitutive of the agent’s own flourishing, with the ring dismissed as psychologically unrealistic. Critics reply that these answers either smuggle in an implicit observer or redefine invisibility away. Is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of rationality, a verbal dispute about whether ‘ought’ is internal or external, or two distinct questions — one predictive, about game-theoretic behaviour, one normative, about the authority of moral reasons — pursued under a single heading? I also want to press a refinement: rather than treating the ring as pure third-person invisibility, we might model it as asymmetric epistemic invisibility, where the agent knows she is unobserved, retains full self-knowledge, and can still anticipate future exposure. If this framing is coherent, does it relocate the debate from rational choice to moral psychology? Please diagnose the disagreement using what the principal sides actually argue, not as a survey of positions, and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about behaviour under verified anonymity, or a demonstration that the original question is ill-posed because it conflates motivation with justification.
