Write an essay on the following interpretive controversy in Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education. Whitehead's well-known account describes learning as moving through three rhythmic stages—romance, precision, and generalization—and insists that education must be oriented toward disciplined wisdom rather than mere utility or vocational training. A recent unnamed interpretation proposes that Whitehead's rhythm actually contains a hidden fourth stage, recursive dissolution, in which the generalizations achieved in the third stage are deliberately destabilized so that the learner can re-enter romance without sacrificing technical competence. According to this reading, the missing stage explains why modern STEM curricula fail in Whiteheadian terms: they arrest development at precision and never reach either true generalization or this necessary destabilization. The interpreter further claims that this resolves an apparent tension between Whitehead's anti-utilitarian rhetoric and his willingness to accommodate specialized scientific instruction.

Your task is to assess whether this four-stage framework is a legitimate development of Whitehead's position or a distortion that imports later process-relational pedagogy into his work. Specifically, examine whether Whitehead's own discussions of educational rhythm, the discipline of study, and the proper place of technical knowledge support the idea of intentional destabilization as part of the cycle, or whether the interpreter has conflated Whitehead's model with John Dewey's notion of reconstructing experience. Reach and defend a definite verdict: is recursive dissolution a faithful extension of Whitehead's aims, and does it genuinely illuminate the conflict between his vision and contemporary competency-based assessment? Do not conclude with an uncommitted survey of interpretations; take a clear position, explain what is at stake in choosing one reading over the other, and argue for your conclusion.
