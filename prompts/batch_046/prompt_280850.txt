I'm presenting a seminar paper next Thursday to a colloquium on critical heritage studies and postcolonial legal theory. The audience is precisely this mix: about ten heritage scholars who treat physical retention of looted artifacts as an extension of colonial violence regardless of governance structures; two international lawyers whose work centers on the 1970 UNESCO Convention and who are skeptical of ontological arguments in property disputes; and one museum director who has publicly endorsed full physical restitution for Benin Bronzes but worries that complex joint-ownership schemes create bureaucratic cover for delay. My thesis: for the Benin Bronzes currently held in smaller German university collections—not the headline national museums—the morally appropriate framework is not binary restitution versus retention, but what I term distributed custodianship. Under this model, Nigerian institutions would hold binding veto authority over display, research access, and deaccession decisions, while the objects remain in European climate-controlled storage; I defend this not on pragmatic grounds but on an ontological claim that these specific bronze plaques have acquired a genuinely plural, multi-sited belonging through 130 years of dispersed scholarly interpretation and material interaction, so that removing them entirely to a single site would constitute not restoration but a different kind of epistemic loss.
