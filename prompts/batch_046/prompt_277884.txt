I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on democratic ethics and moral agency in politics, and next Thursday I am presenting my working paper, "Distributed Dirt: Reconceiving the Problem of Dirty Hands for Administrative Democracies," to a philosophy of politics reading group. The thesis I defend is that the canonical dirty-hands framework—where a singular political agent knowingly violates ordinary morality for the public good—misdiagnoses the problem in modern bureaucratic states. Instead, I propose what I call an institutional diffusion thesis: morally compromising outcomes in contemporary governance typically result not from one dramatic choice but from distributed budgetary, regulatory, and committee-level compromises, such that the dirt is structurally dispersed across institutional nodes. From this I derive a redesign principle: democratic accountability mechanisms should target what I term moral load-bearing nodes—the specific procedural junctures where moral costs are aggregated—rather than holding individual officeholders symbolically responsible.

The audience is precisely composed as follows: two senior scholars who work on collective responsibility and institutional ethics (they will be sympathetic to structural explanations but will worry that I am dissolving individual agency into organizational flow); three political theorists who are broadly Walzerian in orientation and treat dirty hands as an irreducible feature of singular political judgment; one applied normative ethicist who is deeply skeptical of any argument that seems to let individuals off the hook by appealing to complexity; and one advanced graduate student writing on democratic legitimacy, who tends to ask whether my argument undermines the very possibility of authoritative political decision. They care about preserving robust individual moral agency, they are familiar with the classic Weberian formulation, and they will likely treat my injection of organizational-design language as either a category mistake or a convenient escape route. Please prepare me for the Q&A by drafting the objections I will actually face from this specific group—not generic philosophy-of-science objections, but the ones shaped by their commitments—ordered by how damaging they are likely to be and how likely they are to arise. For each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary and conceptual frame of the person who would raise it, and pair it with a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, followed by a frank note on where that reply is weakest or concedes too much. I also want to know what I should concede up front in my opening remarks to defuse the worst attacks, which reply has to land for the proposal to survive, and—crucially—which question I am secretly hoping nobody asks, with a brief strategy for handling it if it comes. Finally, identify the weakest point in my own institutional-diffusion position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook.
