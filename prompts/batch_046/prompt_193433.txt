Consider the following reconstruction of an argument drawn from William James's The Principles of Psychology (1890), in the portions treating voluntary action and the feeling of effort. James observes that conscious experience never contains an event that directly produces muscular contraction; rather, what we experience is a feeling of consent or effort that accompanies movement already being prepared by lower neural centers. He suggests that consciousness is not the efficient cause of voluntary action in the manner of a physical push, but operates instead like a selective gate that can permit or block tendencies that are otherwise automatic. The mental decision, on this view, is not the origin of the action but a high-level veto or consent exercised over pre-existing neural preparations.

I am trying to understand the exact logical structure of this position and how it relates to the modern neuroscience of voluntary action following Libet's measurements of the readiness potential. Two points confuse me.

First, James describes the mental and neural processes in ways that seem to pull in opposite directions. At times he writes as though mental consent and neural preparation are two descriptions of a single event; at other times he speaks as if consciousness genuinely interferes with an independent neural stream, which sounds like interactionist dualism. Does his view commit him to epiphenomenalism about mental causation, or to a non-physical cause acting on physical processes? Or can it be read consistently as a high-level functional description of neural selection, without requiring that consciousness be reducible to those mechanisms?

Second, Libet's experiments are standardly read as showing that the brain initiates movement before conscious awareness, leaving consciousness only with a late veto just prior to execution. More recent discussion, however, raises doubts about whether the readiness potential is specific to the particular movement being performed, proposing instead that it may reflect a generic state of motor readiness rather than a deterministic precursor to a specific action. If the readiness potential is indeed generic in this way, does that strengthen James's framework—because it shows consciousness selecting among prepared possibilities rather than initiating from scratch—or does it weaken the analogy, because there is no specific tendency being vetoed?

Please help me work through these questions. I am particularly interested in whether James's framework can accommodate the idea that any veto itself is implemented by neural mechanisms, thereby avoiding non-physical intervention, and in whether a generic-readiness-potential interpretation undermines the parallel between James's gatekeeper model and Libet's conscious veto.
