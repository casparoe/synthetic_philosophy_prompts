I direct a philosophy-of-physics workshop for ten to twelve advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students, meeting once for seventy-five minutes. Design a structured role-play that forces participants to feel the ethical pull of taking distributed phenomenal properties seriously at the fundamental level, without naming panpsychism, panprotopsychism, or any canonical figure until the debrief.

The scenario: participants sit on a fictional “Fundamental Research Ethics Board” reviewing a proposal to run a “deep-field coherence protocol,” a hypothetical high-energy procedure that, according to one interpretive framework supplied only partway through, temporarily suppresses local phenomenal integration in substrate-level physical structures below the Planck scale. Give them rules, assigned roles (principal investigator, risk assessor, public representative, metaphysical-neutral chair), and a decision procedure they must complete in writing: harm identification, scale estimation, benefit weighing, and a binding vote with public justification.

Arrange the incentives and information flow so that they first apply conventional bio-centric harm-benefit analysis, arrive at either reckless permissiveness or paralytic prohibition depending on an unstated premise about where phenomenal integration can occur, and only then receive the suppressed premise—namely, that minimal experiential properties are not emergent but are distributed across the substrate being manipulated. The deliberation should stall precisely at the combination problem and the asymmetry of suffering attribution.

In your design, specify the physical materials (brief role cards, a one-page “experimental protocol” summary with ambiguous language, a hidden “metaphysical premise” envelope), the timeline minute-by-minute, and the payoff scheme: social stakes through mandatory public defense of votes, not monetary rewards. Then provide the debrief: map each deliberative crisis onto the aggregation problem, the boundary problem for phenomenal subjects, and the quantitative incommensurability of micro-scale suffering. Be explicit about which feature of the ethical challenge—specifically, any actual quantitative weighting of distributed micro-experience—this simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Also predict what will go wrong the first time (for instance, participants treating substrate-level fields as merely metaphorical, or the chair collapsing the ambiguity too early) and say how to handle it. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the prompt should stand alone for a model without web access.
