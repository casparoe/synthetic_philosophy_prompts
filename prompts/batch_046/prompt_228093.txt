Write a poem in three sections corresponding to the historiographical labels "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution," structured so that the period boundaries are visibly constructed rather than discovered. The first and third sections must be in rhyming couplets using the same rhyme scheme (AABBCC...), spoken in the voice of a retrospective classifier—first a humanist framing the preceding era as an unlit interval, then a modern historian framing a discrete rupture in practices of validation—while the middle section is in unrhymed free verse spoken from within the period that can define itself only by negation (not scholastic, not dark, not yet modern). Choose one material object or practice—something like an astrolabe, an alchemical furnace, or a particular mode of textual commentary—that appears in all three sections but is assigned incompatible predicates: in the first it belongs to an unbroken tradition, in the second it signals a rebirth, in the third it marks an epistemic break. Let this repetition-with-variation ask whether the thing itself changed or only the frame around it changed. The philosophical point should be precise, not a generic "history is subjective" sentiment: render the specific problem that "Renaissance" and "Scientific Revolution" may function as retrospective jurisdiction, drawing borders that make continuous change legible as discrete regimes, while "Middle Ages" is defined purely relationally as the shadow between two invented lights. End without resolving whether societies genuinely fail or merely change; let the form—especially the return of rhyme in the third section after the unrhymed center—do the work of showing that the center collapses without its framing couplets. Do not cite specific historians or works; keep the argument conceptual and structural, accurate enough that a historian of science or philosopher of history would recognize the problem of periodization rather than a vague meditation on time.
