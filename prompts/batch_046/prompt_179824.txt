I am studying a particularly dense passage from David Hume's Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, in which he analyses the origin of our idea of necessary connection. To paraphrase the argument: when we observe constant conjunctions—for instance, one billiard ball striking another and motion being communicated—we never perceive any power, force, or metaphysical bond within the objects themselves. All that is presented to the senses is the mere succession of events. Through repeated exposure to this pattern, the mind acquires a custom or habit; upon the appearance of the cause, it anticipates the effect. The internal feeling of determination or expectation—this mental transition from one idea to another—is, on Hume's account, the sole impression from which we copy our notion of necessity. There is no corresponding external impression that reveals an objective link in nature.

I should like help in understanding precisely how this bears upon contemporary debates between Humeanism and anti-Humeanism regarding laws of nature. To frame my difficulty, I wish to distinguish between what I shall call thin regularity and thick regularity. By thin regularity I mean a pattern that is merely observed to hold, carrying no implicit commitment about what must occur in counterfactual or unobserved circumstances. By thick regularity I mean a pattern treated as genuinely law-like precisely because it supports modal claims—claims about what would happen were conditions altered—and thereby possesses what I term modal weight.

Given Hume's insistence that the idea of necessity derives entirely from an internal sentiment of expectation rather than from any observed feature of the world, does his argument undermine only those anti-Humean positions that postulate objective governing relations—such as metaphysically primitive universals or essential dispositions—by demonstrating that we lack any sensory basis for them? Or does it also create a problem for the Humean herself, who seeks to distinguish laws from accidental generalisations without appealing to an observed modal bond? Please explain the precise work that the internal impression is meant to perform in Hume's account, and whether that work can be extended—without violating his empiricist constraints—to justify treating certain regularities as thick rather than merely thin.
