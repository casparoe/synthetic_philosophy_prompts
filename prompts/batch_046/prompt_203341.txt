I am delivering the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop titled "The Architecture of Hypotheticals," co-hosted by a philosophy department and a cognitive-psychology laboratory. The audience is split evenly between analytic metaphysicians and philosophers of mind—several of whom have published on personal identity and modal intuition, and who will catch every understated reference—alongside empirical researchers who study reasoning biases, framing effects, and the illusion of explanatory depth. I want a formal address of about eight minutes that treats a specific, non-canonical intuition pump as its organizing spine: the "convergent divergence" scenario, in which two individuals share an identical autobiographical narrative from birth through age thirty, then experience a single day of radically different events, after which their memories and behavioral dispositions largely reconverge except for one engineered asymmetry—only one retains a faint, inexplicable sense of having "missed something" without being able to articulate what. The speech should do real structural work rather than use the case as decoration. First, it should show the philosophers why this pump makes continuity of psychological relations feel like an obvious criterion for personal identity over time, when in fact the asymmetry has been calibrated to exploit the source-monitoring bias and create an artificial aura of explanatory depth. Second, it should show the psychologists why philosophers treat that engineered feeling as metaphysical data rather than cognitive artifact, thereby revealing the methodological tension that justifies the workshop's central ground rule: no speaker may invoke a hypothetical without first naming the specific bias it triggers or suppresses. The address should avoid generic welcoming language; it should open with the pump itself, move through the tension between the two halves of the room, and close by framing the rule not as an external constraint but as a logical consequence of taking intuition pumps seriously. The tone must be precise enough that the philosophers do not feel the psychology is being caricatured, yet transparent enough that the psychologists are not being lectured about their own field from a distance.
