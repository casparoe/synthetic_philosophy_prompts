I'm preparing a presentation for a graduate seminar on existentialist ontology, and I've constructed an argument that uses Sartre's own concepts—radical freedom, the Look, and the coefficient of adversity—to reach a conclusion I find unacceptable but can't locate the error in. Here is the chain as I have it, with each step numbered:

(1) For Sartre, consciousness is radically free: in every situation, however physically or socially constrained, I retain the absolute ontological capacity to choose my stance toward that situation—my transcendence can never be annihilated.
(2) This means freedom is not merely one capacity among others but the defining structure of being-for-itself; I am never identical with my facticity (my body, past, or social role).
(3) The coefficient of adversity applies here: an obstacle only counts as an obstacle relative to a freely chosen project. A rock is an obstacle to the climber but not to the geologist, and this adversarial quality is conferred by the project rather than intrinsic to the object.
(4) The Look (le regard) objectifies me: another consciousness sees me as an in-itself, and this seems to constrain my freedom by fixing me in a social identity.
(5) But if freedom is genuinely inescapable and total, then my being-objectified is itself something I freely transcend or consent to; otherwise I would be genuinely determined by the Other, contradicting (1).
(6) By the same reasoning, applying the coefficient of adversity to social structures, racism, poverty, or imprisonment only have adversarial force relative to projects I have freely chosen.
(7) Therefore, every experience of being oppressed, shamed, or socially determined is ultimately self-imposed: I freely choose the projects that make these structures adverse to me.
(8) This collapses Sartre's social philosophy—his critiques of anti-Semitism, colonialism, and class oppression—into the claim that victims freely choose their victimhood.
(9) So either (1) is false and freedom is genuinely limited by structures, or Sartre's framework for social critique is incoherent.

I want to keep (1) and Sartre's social critique intact, but I feel the slide happens somewhere between (4) and (7), possibly involving an equivocation between choosing my attitude and choosing the situation itself. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, if I deny (5) or (6), can I still maintain that freedom is "radical," or do I have to accept that social facticity genuinely limits transcendence in ways that are not self-chosen? And does this damage Sartre's account of bad faith, since bad faith requires that I can deny a freedom I genuinely possess?
