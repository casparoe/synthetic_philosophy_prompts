As I have recently been appointed to the ethics committee of a medical foundation that must choose between sustaining palliative hospice programmes and funding a high-risk gene-therapy trial—where either decision abandons a vulnerable cohort—I find myself genuinely torn between two obligations that seem to remain fully in force regardless of my choice. In reading the philosophical debate about whether moral dilemmas are genuinely possible, I have encountered the argument that if an agent truly ought to do A and truly ought to do B, yet A and B are incompatible, then the agent is necessarily culpable either way; since such structural culpability appears intolerable, many philosophers conclude that every apparent dilemma must be resolvable in principle. It strikes me, however, that this objection presupposes a univocal conception of moral obligation and neglects a pluralist view on which obligations are indexed to distinct institutional roles—my duty to present patients arising from my trustee role in hospice care, and my duty to future patients arising from my oversight role in research funding. If obligations are thus role-relative and operate in separate normative registers, then the two oughts do not generate a single contradictory command, and the structural-culpability argument collapses. Given this role-indexed pluralism, are not genuine moral dilemmas not only possible but structurally inevitable for anyone occupying multiple ethical roles? Or am I conflating the distribution of obligations across roles with the failure to resolve an all-things-considered conflict? Please present your assessment in British English, and clarify whether my reasoning contains a subtle misunderstanding of the anti-dilemma position.
