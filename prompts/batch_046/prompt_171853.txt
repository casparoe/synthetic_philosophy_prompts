Design a 90-minute seminar activity for twelve postgraduate philosophy of science students that stages the problem of simultaneous invention—taking Newton and Leibniz, and Darwin and Wallace, as background cases—and asks whether discoveries are historically inevitable and whether priority disputes matter if they are. Your design must inject a specific, somewhat niche conceptual twist: rather than treating convergence on a result as uniform, the activity should separate structural inevitability (that a given law or formalism must emerge from the available data) from representational contingency (that the notation, explanatory framing, or conceptual architecture through which the result is expressed is not fixed, and that different formulations open different future research paths). The deliverable should be a fully specified, runnable exercise.

Specify the room layout, physical materials (for example, indexed card sets representing a shared empirical dataset, coloured tokens for reputation points, and hidden worksheet templates), and the rules for two parallel "laboratories" of six students each. Both laboratories must work independently from identical initial data to derive an underlying pattern, using different representational constraints assigned in secret (one group must frame the result geometrically, the other symbolically or mechanistically). Only after both groups have committed to a formal statement should they reveal their derivations to the full seminar. At that point, introduce a negotiation phase in which the groups must allocate collective "priority credit" using the token economy. Design the payoff scheme—with no real money—so that total reputation points depend both on correct convergence and on the perceived downstream fertility of the representational form chosen, but with a hidden second-round task (a predictive extension of the original dataset) that rewards one framing more than the other. The rules should be arranged so that participants first experience the convergence as inevitable, then discover that their representational commitments have locked them into incompatible predictions, forcing them to feel the tension between Mertonian universalism and the reward system of science before either concept is named.

Include a debrief that maps each phase of the activity onto the distinction between structural inevitability and representational contingency, and that explicitly connects the students' negotiation behaviour to the philosophical question of whether it matters who made the discovery when the discovery was unavoidable. State clearly what will predictably go wrong on the first run—for instance, groups withholding intermediate steps to protect priority, or students conflating the derivation of the law with the choice of representation—and describe exactly how you will intervene. Be frank about which feature of the actual historical priority disputes the simulation cannot reproduce, and identify what you, as facilitator, must supply verbally to bridge the gap.

Write in British English. Take a committed position: design both the payoff scheme and the debrief to defend the view that representational contingency genuinely undermines the claim that simultaneous discovery makes priority morally irrelevant; justify this design choice with explicit reasoning, and do not present the opposing universalist position neutrally. Conclude with a brief reflective note identifying where your design takes liberties with the historical record of calculus or evolutionary theory, or where it relies on a contested philosophical premise about the ontology of scientific discovery.
