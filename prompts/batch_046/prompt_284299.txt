I am applying for a three-month interdisciplinary workshop fellowship to convene philosophers of mind and behavioral biologists around a single conceptual problem: can an ant colony be said to possess a mind, and if so, what kind? I want to avoid the standard framing that pits functionalist emergence against phenomenological skepticism about a missing subject of experience, because both sides treat integration as a continuous variable rather than a structural one. Instead, I propose to use Douglas Hofstadter's ant-colony dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach — the exchange centered on Aunt Hillary and the emergence of colony-level cognition — not as a whimsical illustration but as a rigorous benchmark. The dialogue suggests that what makes Aunt Hillary a candidate mind is not merely distributed computation but a specific recursive capacity: the colony can represent its own organizational state to itself, including states that are not currently actual. The research question, then, is whether real ant colonies instantiate this counterfactual self-modeling or whether they only exhibit reactive, parallel distributed processing without a unified representational frame. Existing positions leave this open because ethologists document impressive collective decision-making but rarely ask whether the colony represents alternatives to its present configuration, and philosophers debate collective intentionality without specifying whether it requires counterfactual representation or only coordinated action. My approach will be conceptual analysis paired with a critical review of existing descriptions of colony behavior, focusing specifically on decision-making under perturbation: does the colony behave as if it holds a model of what it would be like with a missing caste or altered pheromone topology, or does it merely adjust local rules? I choose this over empirical neuro-ethology because the question is about the conditions for mindhood, not about measuring neural analogs, and over purely literary interpretation of Hofstadter's text because the dialogue needs to be tested against actual colony dynamics rather than treated as fiction. A successful result would be a precise, non-scalar criterion that distinguishes collective cognition from collective mind — one that identifies counterfactual self-representation as the decisive feature without requiring a single physical locus of consciousness — together with an argument about whether ant colonies meet it. Failure would look like an analysis that collapses back into either unqualified functionalism or an unargued denial of collective subjectivity, or one that cannot specify what empirical or conceptual evidence would settle the matter. Given three months, the deliverable should be a focused framework paper of approximately thirty pages suitable for a philosophy of biology workshop, with all bibliographic details to be supplied separately; the scope deliberately excludes original field experiments and exhaustive citation surveys. Write the proposal in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or lists, stating the precise question, why current answers are insufficient, the method and its advantages, the form of a result, the conditions for failure, and an honest assessment of what three months can and cannot achieve.
