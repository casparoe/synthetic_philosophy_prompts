I have just come away from a long, exhausting argument with a friend about whether death can be bad for the person who dies. My friend kept pressing an analogy he treated as decisive for the Epicurean position: being dead is like being inside a sealed, lightless, soundproof chamber that contains no occupant. Just as we do not say the empty chamber is suffering, missing out, or being wronged, he argued, we should not say the dead person is harmed, since harm requires a present subject and death eliminates the subject entirely. I countered with a different image — that death is more like having one's passport permanently revoked for a journey that was already mapped out and paid for: the traveler feels nothing, yet goods specifically indexed to her are canceled. He replied that this second picture cheats by retaining a holder who can be wronged, whereas death removes the holder altogether. I want you to audit both analogies precisely: map the correspondences explicitly (what in the source domain stands for the dead person, for the goods of life, for the absence of experience, and for the relation of harm), locate exactly where each mapping fractures — especially regarding the persistence or annihilation of a subject, the temporal asymmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence, and whether counterfactual goods can be ascribed to an entity that no longer exists — and determine what each analogy silently imports about whether value requires a present experiencer. Finally, assess whether either analogy can genuinely carry its intended conclusion — the Epicurean denial that death is a harm, or the deprivationist claim that it is — or whether both beg the question once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy avoids these pitfalls, propose it.
