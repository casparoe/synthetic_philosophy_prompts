At a seminar on moral philosophy, two participants—Dr. Elena Voss and Professor James Okonkwo—debated Augustine’s absolute prohibition on lying, using as their focal case a physician who, when asked directly about a terminal prognosis, answers, “Many patients with this condition live meaningful years.” The sentence is true in general but deliberately avoids confirming the grave specific prognosis the physician knows applies to this patient; she never utters anything she believes false.

Voss argued that Augustine’s analysis in his treatise on lying is narrowly focused on mendacium: the explicit utterance of a falsehood with the intent to deceive. She noted that Augustine examines whether one may lie to save a life or to protect virtue, and in every case rejects the lie because it violates the purpose of language—to express the speaker’s mind. From this, Voss concluded that Augustine’s prohibition applies only when the speaker’s internal belief contradicts the literal content of the utterance. In the medical case, the physician’s statement aligns with her belief, and she has not violated the signification of her words. Voss acknowledged that Augustine rejects even “joking lies” when harmless, but she pointed out that this still concerns utterances intended to be taken as false within a playful frame, not strategic omissions in serious contexts. Therefore, she maintained, Augustine would classify the physician’s response as perhaps imprudent or unkind, but not as lying, since there is no contradiction between mind and speech.

Okonkwo replied that Voss was treating Augustine’s argument as a legalistic rule about false sentences rather than as a structural principle about human communication. He emphasized that Augustine holds language to be an instrument of truth whose proper use is to make one’s mind known to another; consequently, any intentional use of speech that creates a systematic divergence between the listener’s formed belief and reality—whether through explicit falsehood or through calibrated omission that exploits the listener’s expectation of completeness—violates the same moral structure. Okonkwo noted that Augustine’s rejection of deceptive concealment when one is bound to speak supports extending the prohibition beyond literal falsehood. In the medical scenario, the physician answers a direct question in a way designed to produce a false overall impression: she intends the patient to understand the prognosis as less severe than she knows it to be. That intent to deceive about the state of affairs, Okonkwo argued, is precisely the corrupt will that Augustine condemns.

Voss countered that Okonkwo was importing modern categories—“calibrated omission” and “expectation of completeness”—that Augustine never articulates. She observed that Augustine allows figurative and metaphorical language in which the literal content is false, because in those cases the speaker does not intend the listener to adopt the literal meaning as their belief. This demonstrates, she said, that Augustine cares about the communicative contract and the speaker’s intent to deceive about belief-states, not about whether the listener ends up with an accurate picture of reality. The physician is not operating within a shared metaphorical frame; she is being evasive. But evasion, Voss insisted, is not the same as lying for Augustine, because the speaker’s words still express a true belief, and the listener’s misunderstanding stems from inference, not from false signification.

Okonkwo responded that the figurative exception actually strengthens his case: in metaphor, the listener is not deceived about the speaker’s belief, whereas in strategic omission, the listener is deceived about both the speaker’s belief and the objective situation. The physician’s statement is not a metaphor; it is a partial truth deployed to mask a fuller truth. If Augustine’s prohibition is meant to protect the integrity of the speaker’s mind and the trust of the community, then allowing such omissions creates a loophole that renders the prohibition toothless—one could always lie by selecting true sentences that together construct a false narrative. Okonkwo concluded that Augustine’s logic, consistently applied, condemns the physician’s response as a form of mendacium, even if the literal sentences are true.

Voss then added that Okonkwo’s standard would collapse the distinction between lying and many ordinary social practices—polite deflections, diplomatic language, and pedagogical simplification—making Augustine’s ethics impossibly demanding. She argued that Augustine was writing against specific abuses, such as lying for religious conversion or to protect criminals, not against all forms of selective disclosure. If Okonkwo’s extension were correct, Augustine would have condemned not only explicit falsehoods but also every incomplete explanation, which is not borne out by the treatise’s examples.
