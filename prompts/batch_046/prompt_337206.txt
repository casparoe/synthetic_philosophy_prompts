Many jurisdictions determine eligibility for disability accommodations and benefits not by underlying diagnosis but by functional assessment—whether a person can perform specific work-related or life activities—regardless of whether the limitation stems from a neurological condition, a psychological state, or a socially constructed environmental barrier. What philosophical commitments about the nature of disability, mental states, and moral status does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it commit the law to a functionalist view of impairment—defining disability by its causal-functional role rather than by its biological substrate—and does it implicitly rely on multiple realizability, treating neurologically, psychologically, and socially mediated limitations as equivalent realizations of the same functional deficit? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and ask whether it remains coherent with other legal practices that still treat psychiatric and physical conditions differently in insurance coverage and criminal-responsibility contexts. Finally, describe what a disability-determination system built on a purely etiological rather than functional criterion would have to change, and whether such a system could still honor the intuition that the same limitation deserves the same accommodation regardless of its origin.
