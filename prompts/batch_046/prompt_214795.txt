I am a novelist engaged with long-form fiction in which characters enter binding commitments—contracts, vows, or self-imposed rules—that they anticipate breaking should the conditions ever be tested. In exploring the conceptual architecture of such moves, I have become absorbed by Kavka's toxin puzzle and the philosophical disputes it has provoked since its initial formulation. I do not seek a summary of the literature; rather, I wish to understand which open questions, unresolved implications, and lines of inquiry in this area genuinely deserve sustained attention, and which are, in your judgement, overrated, merely derivative, or irrelevant to the depiction of human agency.

Please consider, in particular, three clusters. First, does the puzzle expose a deep incoherence in the very concept of rational intention, or does the paradox dissolve once we draw a rigorous distinction between an intention formed for instrumental reasons and one formed for its own sake? Second, to what extent does the resolution of the puzzle carry substantial weight for the choice between causal and evidential decision theory, or is that connection largely decorative—an application that flatters the theory without altering it? Third, and more speculatively, might there be an unexplored line of work—perhaps concerning collective or distributed agency, the phenomenology of narrative precommitment, or the scaling of rationality conditions across group protagonists—that could reframe the puzzle so that it speaks not only to analytic philosophy of action but to artistic practice?

Defend a prioritisation. Which of these clusters, or others you identify, ought to occupy the centre of future philosophical effort, and why? Specify the criteria you are employing—tractability within a reasonable span of years, the number of downstream debates that depend upon the answer, relevance to actual human deliberation, or relevance to narrative construction—and explain the trade-offs among them. Which implications carry real weight for a writer attempting to render credible the psychology of commitment and betrayal, and which do not follow at all? Deliver a defended agenda, not a survey, and certainly not a reading list.
