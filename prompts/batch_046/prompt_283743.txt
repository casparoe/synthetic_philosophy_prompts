As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I have been tracking the renewed skepticism about phenomenal consciousness that moves beyond mere mystery-mongering to outright conceptual denial. The argument—associated with Dennett's original quining strategy and recently sharpened by Chase and Sanborn—holds that qualia are not just elusive but incoherent as a theoretical posit: once we scrutinize the supposed properties of subjective experience, such as intrinsicness, ineffability, and direct acquaintance, they dissolve into functional or representational descriptions, leaving no genuine residue. For a feature I am writing on how classical empiricism might engage with this, I am trying to reconstruct the likely stance of David Hume. Given his reduction of the self to a bundle of perceptions, his copy principle demanding that every idea trace back to an impression, and his well-known suspicion of metaphysical entities that outstrip observable experience, how do you think Hume would have adjudicated the claim that qualia are an incoherent posit?

More specifically, I want you to predict not only whether he would have endorsed the quining move, but also how he would have handled a somewhat niche modern counter-argument: the phenomenal concepts strategy, which contends that we possess irreducible first-person concepts for our experiences—concepts that are not derivable from physical descriptions yet remain fully compatible with a physicalist ontology. Would Hume have treated these special concepts as legitimate impression-derived ideas, or as empty fictions generated by the imagination's tendency to reify patterns of association? Please reconstruct his probable reasoning step by step, indicating where he might see the incoherence charge as a natural extension of his own empiricism, and where he might resist it—perhaps by arguing that the vivacity and immediacy of sensory impressions provide an evidential status that purely functional accounts fail to capture.

Finally, please address whether Hume would have recognized a distinction between epistemic access and ontological commitment in this debate—specifically, whether conceding that we have a distinctive first-person mode of knowing our experiences necessarily commits us to non-physical properties—or whether he would have collapsed that distinction and treated any irreducible conceptual access as evidence of a corresponding metaphysical entity. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want a rigorous conceptual reconstruction of his likely position, grounded in the logic of his system rather than in anachronistic attribution.
