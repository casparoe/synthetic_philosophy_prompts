I've come around to a kind of inverted Strawsonain view: I think reactive attitudes don't ground moral responsibility through expressing quality of will, but only work when backed by what I'd call a recognitional stance — you have to already be treating the other as inside the moral community before resentment or gratitude can do normative work. So responsibility isn't constituted by the attitudes themselves, but by whether that prior stance is justified; if it's not, the attitudes are just emotional noise. What would count against this? Not the usual free-will debates, but the real defeaters: cases where people clearly direct reactive attitudes at outsiders (politicians they don't know, maybe even AI), and whether that proves the recognitional backing isn't actually necessary. Also, do Strawson's vicarious attitudes need the same backing, or do they work differently? And does this accidentally collapse moral blame into a kind of institutional membership test, which feels wrong? Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender of this inverted view should bite the bullet on, and be honest about whether the position is well-insulated.
