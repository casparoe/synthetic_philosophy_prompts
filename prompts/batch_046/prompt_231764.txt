I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy and decision theory working on action-guidance under deep uncertainty, and I am trying to build a diagnostic taxonomy of positions on consequentialist cluelessness that moves past the standard literature framing. I want an instrument that sorts views by the substantive claims they make rather than by the labels philosophers attach to themselves, so that someone could use it without prior knowledge of the debate's cast of characters.

Please construct a branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with named or described leaves—organized around three successive diagnostic questions.

First branch: Identify the primary locus of the cluelessness problem. Is it (A) epistemic, meaning our contingent lack of predictive knowledge about specific long-range causal chains; (B) structural, meaning features of complex adaptive systems—sensitivity to initial conditions, emergent macro-effects, or feedback loops—that render long-range prediction in principle unreliable for bounded agents like us regardless of data; or (C) normative, meaning fundamental uncertainty about the correct axiological metric (welfare, preference-satisfaction, capabilities, relational goods, etc.) that propagates downward through every outcome evaluation?

Second branch, applied within each first-branch option: Does the identified cluelessness defeat the action-guiding function of consequentialism altogether, or does it merely complicate application? Here distinguish defeatist positions, on which the theory is rendered practically inert, irrational, or self-undermining for agents in our epistemic position, from revisionist positions, on which consequentialism survives but requires supplementation, transformation, or restriction.

Third branch, for revisionists only: What is the operative normative modification? Include at least the following options, allowing for combinations where coherent: (i) subjective expected-value reasoning with explicit credence distributions; (ii) robustness or maximin reasoning across a set of plausible worlds; (iii) satisficing or "good-enough" threshold reasoning that abandons optimization; (iv) institutional or rule-consequentialist delegation that moves evaluation from acts to policies or social structures; and (v) meta-ethical retreat to a non-consequentialist framework—such as virtue ethics, contractualism, or rights-based deontology—that nevertheless retains some consequentialist intuitions at the structural level.

For each leaf, provide: (a) a concise descriptive label (e.g., "structural-defeatist-abandonment," "normative-revisionist-satisficing," etc.); (b) one sentence stating its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment; and (c) a brief note on whether the combination is occupied in the philosophical literature, unoccupied but coherent, or apparently incoherent, with a one-sentence diagnosis for any gap (neglect, unattractiveness, or logical tension).

Additionally, flag explicitly any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate—where two different answer paths collapse into the same practical recommendation or theoretical commitment on closer inspection—explaining precisely where the diagnostic distinction breaks down.

Every node must be phrased as a substantive claim about the nature of uncertainty or decision-making, not as "do you agree with Author X?" The intended audience is philosophers of ethics and formal decision theorists who need a classification instrument for seminar discussion, not a literature review or bibliography. Do not include reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific page numbers.
