At a small philosophy-of-economics reading group, two regular members—call them Priya and Elias—got into an extended disagreement about whether Oskar Lange’s competitive-market socialism actually answers the knowledge-based objections raised by Hayek and Mises, or whether it quietly abandons the very mechanism those objections target. I was there but stayed out of it, and I did not take sides. They went through several rounds, and now they want an outside adjudication. Please go through their exchange point by point: say who makes the stronger move at each stage, identify any hidden premises (for instance, whether an analogy to modern distributed optimization is fair to Lange’s boards, or whether entrepreneurial discovery strictly requires private property rather than just hard budget constraints), and then say who is more right overall. Also note what both are missing—perhaps something about how later participatory or computer-assisted variants of market socialism alter the picture, or how Hayek’s later work on cultural evolution complicates a simple “information-processing” framing of his critique.

Here is how the exchange went.

Priya opened by arguing that Lange’s model solves the socialist calculation problem because it retains a price mechanism for both consumer goods and capital goods. Central boards adjust prices by trial and error until supply equals demand, and labor and consumer goods remain in genuine markets. She said Hayek’s well-known objection—that socialist planners lack the dispersed local knowledge embedded in market prices—only strikes a fully centralized command economy where every allocation decision is made top-down. In Lange’s scheme, prices still emerge from interaction with supply and demand; the board functions as an accounting device, not an omniscient planner. So, she concluded, Hayek attacked a strawman.

Elias replied that this misses the epistemic heart of Hayek’s argument. The capitalist price mechanism does not merely record supply and demand; it communicates tacit, contextual knowledge—what Hayek described as knowledge of particular local circumstances that never becomes fully explicit—through profit and loss signals that have real bite only when capital is privately owned and genuinely at risk. Lange’s boards can adjust prices algorithmically, but they manage social capital under soft budget constraints. There is no entrepreneurial mechanism in which competing actors risk their own resources and discover genuinely new combinations; errors are socialized rather than borne by the discoverer. So Lange has not solved the knowledge problem—he has replaced distributed discovery with a centralized feedback loop.

Priya countered that this overstates the uniqueness of private risk. Lange allows labor mobility and consumer sovereignty, and the board’s trial-and-error adjustments are functionally analogous to market clearing. She then introduced a modern comparison: distributed optimization systems, such as iterative machine-learning feedback loops, show that globally efficient allocations can be approximated without every node owning the resources it adjusts. If algorithmic adjustment works in other complex domains, why should it be impossible for socialist boards?

Elias said the comparison actually reveals the weakness. Machine-learning optimization operates within a fixed objective function and a bounded parameter space defined in advance. Real economic discovery, by contrast, involves redefining the problem itself—creating new goods, new preferences, and new technological combinations that no preset algorithm can anticipate. Hayek’s challenge is not about computing an equilibrium given fixed ends; it is about open-ended, qualitative transformation of economic possibilities. By treating Lange’s board as an optimization algorithm, Priya shows that the model can handle static allocation but not the dynamic discovery that private property and hard budget constraints make possible. Elias added that Mises’s original argument was not merely about information processing but about the impossibility of rational economic calculation without a market for capital goods priced through actual exchange of private titles. Lange supplies accounting prices, but not prices formed through genuine exchange of property rights; without those rights, there is no rational basis for comparing the opportunity costs of capital across alternative uses.

Priya responded that this sets an impossible standard. No system—capitalist or socialist—guarantees open-ended discovery. Capitalist firms themselves plan internally; the market only sets external constraints. Lange’s boards face analogous external constraints through consumer and labor markets, so the boundary between market and plan is far blurrier than Elias admits. She added that if Mises’s argument depends on a metaphysical commitment to private property as the sole source of economic rationality, then it is a normative claim disguised as an economic one.

Elias concluded that a blurry boundary is not an unreal distinction. Capitalist firms do plan, but they do so within a price system that ultimately tests their plans against the risk of failure. Lange’s boards never face that ultimate test in the same way, because they cannot go bankrupt; their errors are distributed across society. So while Lange may solve the static allocation problem that Mises framed, he does not solve the dynamic knowledge problem that Hayek emphasized. The real disagreement, Elias said, is whether economic rationality requires distributed ownership with hard budget constraints, or whether algorithmic adjustment with social ownership can genuinely replicate the informational function of the market.
