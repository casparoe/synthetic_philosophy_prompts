I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about whether "collapse" denotes a real kind of historical event or is merely a retrospective narrative we impose on complex institutional drift. We kept returning to three cases: the Late Bronze Age fragmentation of interdependent palace economies; the Maya Terminal Classic transition, in which southern lowland dynastic centers largely ceased monumental construction and royal governance while populations and cultural practices persisted and relocated; and Rapa Nui, where the standard internal ecocide narrative competes with accounts stressing post-contact demographic shock and internal social restructuring. My friend insists that in none of these are we observing a society's failure, only its reconfiguration into a different organizational form, and that treating them as instances of a single phenomenon called "collapse" commits a category error: it assumes that the state apparatus, the cultural repertoire, and the demographic population are a single unit that can live or die together, when in fact they decouple. I am not fully persuaded — I think there is a meaningful difference between transformation and termination — but I am unsettled enough that I need whatever I say tomorrow to be conceptually precise.
