I am preparing a graduate seminar on the intersection of philosophy of physics and philosophy of religion, and the topic has taken on personal urgency after a close friend—who had long admired Einstein's scientific humanism—recently expressed despair that determinism must annihilate moral agency. I want to understand whether Einstein's own worldview actually collapses under that tension, or whether there is a coherent, if underappreciated, synthesis in his scattered writings on determinism, quantum mechanics, and what he called cosmic religious feeling. You should respond as a philosopher of science with particular expertise in early-twentieth-century physics and philosophical theology; please work solely from your training knowledge, do not search for sources, and think carefully before answering, first outlining the four central tensions I identify below.

The tensions are: (1) Einstein's commitment to a deterministic physics, including his well-known skepticism toward quantum indeterminacy as a feature rather than a failure of theory; (2) his description of a cosmic religious sentiment—an awe before the rational harmony of nature that explicitly rejects a personal deity and prayer; (3) his occasional suggestions that belief in human freedom, though scientifically unfounded, is pragmatically indispensable for ethical life; and (4) a synthetic interpretive framework I will call modal-compatibilist cosmic determinism, which holds that free will is not ontologically present in human agents but is modally necessary in every world in which rational moral agents exist to discover deterministic laws, thereby allowing Einstein to maintain both strict physical determinism and a normative space for moral responsibility without reducing freedom to mere illusion.

Please rank the following five interpretive positions on Einstein's stance toward free will and religion from most to least defensible as an integrated reading of his overall philosophy. Assign each a tier—S, A, B, C, or D—and a numerical coherence score from 1 to 10, where 10 indicates that the interpretation resolves all four tensions with minimal ad hoc supplementation. The positions are: (1) the hard Spinozan determinist reading, which denies free will entirely, treats moral responsibility as a socially useful fiction, and interprets cosmic religion as purely aesthetic awe at necessary law; (2) the quantum-compatibilist misreading, which takes Einstein's rejection of quantum randomness as leaving ontological room for human-level indeterminacy and severs cosmic religion from determinism; (3) the ethical-illusion compatibilist reading, which accepts determinism but insists that the illusion of free will is indispensable for ethics, without explaining how that illusion coheres with cosmic awe; (4) the epistemic-modesty reading, which treats Einstein's determinism as methodological—physics seeks deterministic laws, but he remains metaphysically agnostic about freedom—while cosmic religion responds to the partial success of that method; and (5) the modal-compatibilist cosmic determinism outlined above, which imports modal logic to preserve freedom as a normative-modal condition rather than an ontological one. After presenting your tier list with scores, please justify the top and bottom ranks, and specifically evaluate whether position 5 succeeds where the others fail, or whether it imposes a logical apparatus that Einstein's texts never support.
