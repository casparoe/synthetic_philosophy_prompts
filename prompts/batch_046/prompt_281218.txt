Consider the institutional practice governing biographical film and television production. For living subjects, and frequently for the recently deceased whose estates hold proprietary rights, producers seek legal clearances, life-rights agreements, or estate permissions; yet for figures dead for centuries—monarchs, philosophers, scientists, or revolutionaries—no comparable consent mechanism exists, and dramatists freely alter motives, dialogue, and chronology. At the same time, these productions routinely employ historical consultants, advertise claims of authenticity, and defend alterations by appealing to narrative economy rather than to any moral licence. The practice therefore treats temporal distance and the presence of a legal estate as morally decisive, while treating representational accuracy as a uniform professional virtue regardless of whose practical identity is being reconstructed.

What implicit theory of posthumous moral standing, representational harm, and the obligations owed to the dead does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the view: does it hold that wrongs to the dead are necessarily mediated through harm to living descendants, audiences, or cultural memory; or that moral standing expires once no legal person remains to represent it? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely coheres with it—could a producer who believes the dead retain non-derivative interests in how their lives are narrated still operate comfortably within current industry norms, or does the practice implicitly rule that out? Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories—whether that artistic licence always outweighs dignitary concerns, or that historical truth is owed solely to viewers—would all license the same clearance asymmetry. Finally, describe what a production framework built on the view that representational fidelity is a direct duty to the dead themselves, rather than merely an epistemic service to audiences, would have to change in its clearance protocols, consulting norms, and marketing claims.

Please separate what is settled about posthumous harm and representational ethics from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and legal theorists who specialise in this area, and do not present any contested view—such as the claim that the dead literally cannot be wronged, or that dramatic licence nullifies all dignitary obligations—as though it were the consensus.
