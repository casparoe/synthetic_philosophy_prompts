I have recently been appointed to an advisory committee for a regional emergency-response authority, and our charter contains a curious provision. Under ordinary conditions, every expenditure requires a deliberative vote with published reasoning; but once a crisis is declared, the chair may disburse funds unilaterally—exercising what is essentially coercive fiscal power—provided that within forty-eight hours she files a written justification framing the act as serving justice or the common good. The force is immediate and unchecked; the justice is retrospective and discursive. This arrangement strikes me as philosophically loaded. It seems to presuppose that justice and force are not only distinct but sequential: power acts first, and legitimacy is stitched on afterward. But is that separation necessary? Could the same protocol be read instead as claiming that true justice just is the rational reconstruction of necessary power, so that there is no real gap? Reconstruct the implicit theory—perhaps in the vein of Pascal—about the justice-force relation that our charter presupposes. Say whether the practice strictly requires that theory or is compatible with rivals, whether its parts are consistent, and what exactly in our crisis rules would have to change if we adopted a view on which justice and force must coincide in the very same act rather than in a two-step sequence.
