I've come round to a kind of hybrid take on the procreation asymmetry — not just the usual 'no harm to people who don't exist yet' line, but that reproductive choices open what I'd call live causal pathways with weak promissory weight. Once a pathway is active (say, conception's happened and development is underway) there's a duty not to break it without good reason, but before that pathway exists there's literally zero obligation to initiate one. So it dodges Benatar's asymmetry problems without collapsing into a totalist 'reproduce as much as possible' view. Tell me what'd count against it — not just the non-identity problem, but which concrete cases (embryo selection, climate-related reproductive decisions) make the pathway-duty look ad hoc, whether it clashes with bodily autonomy in ways it's defenders have to concede, and if any formal result in population ethics or decision theory shows pathway-dependence can't ground moral asymmetry. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say whether the view is well-insulated or not, and lead with that bottom line. Keep it under 350 words — one paragraph's fine if structured.
