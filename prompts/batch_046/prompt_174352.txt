I was in a philosophy seminar last week working through Karl Jaspers’s analysis in Die Schuldfrage—specifically his separation of criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—when two members, Rosa and Daniel, began an extended disagreement about whether his framework applies to present-day democratic citizens who had no personal role in historical state crimes. They asked me to referee, and I want to understand the logic of each move precisely before weighing in.

Rosa argued that Jaspers is explicit: political guilt attaches to citizens of a state that committed crimes, regardless of personal innocence, because citizens share responsibility for the state they inhabit and must accept institutional consequences—reparations, reform, historical reckoning—even if they bear no criminal or moral guilt individually. She applied this to a present-day case: if a democratic nation discovers that its predecessor regime systematically expropriated land from an indigenous population, current citizens should accept political guilt in the form of collective reparative obligations, precisely because they live within the territorial and legal continuity of that state and continuously reproduce its institutions through voting, taxation, and civic participation.

Daniel replied that Rosa collapses a distinction Jaspers insisted upon. Political guilt, he said, applies to citizens who actually lived under and supported the regime, not to generations born decades later into a democratic system with no meaningful continuity of political will. For Daniel, extending political guilt across generations transforms it into an inherited stain, which Jaspers rejected—moral and metaphysical guilt are personal and non-transferable, and political guilt requires actual membership in the culpable polity. He added that if institutional continuity alone generates guilt, then every modern citizen inherits an infinite regress of obligations, making democratic self-government impossible because citizens are permanently burdened by decisions they never authorized.

Rosa countered that Daniel reads Jaspers too individualistically. Institutional continuity is not mere legal inheritance but active maintenance: present citizens vote, pay taxes, and benefit from property regimes and boundaries established by past crimes. Political guilt, on her reading, is not about personal psychology but about objective membership in a collective agent that persists through time. She conceded that moral guilt remains strictly personal, but insisted that refusing to acknowledge political guilt is a form of evasion—treating the state as a temporary association rather than a historical body that citizens continuously reproduce.

Daniel responded that Rosa’s “objective membership” standard proves too much. By her logic, anyone who benefits from any historical injustice—through global supply chains, colonial borders, or accumulated capital—would bear political guilt for events they never influenced. He argued that Jaspers designed his categories precisely to prevent this inflation: criminal guilt belongs to perpetrators, moral guilt to those who failed to resist, metaphysical guilt expresses human solidarity in suffering, but political guilt must be tied to actual civic responsibility within the guilty state, not to passive structural benefit. Otherwise, he said, the concept loses its normative force and becomes mere rhetorical guilt-tripping.

Please go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate: which arguments soundly apply Jaspers’s framework, which misread it, and where the real philosophical tension lies. In particular, say whether political guilt can be transmitted across generations without becoming either moral guilt or an empty collective label, and identify what both Rosa and Daniel miss—whether in the logic of collective responsibility or in Jaspers’s own distinctions. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the reasoning itself.
