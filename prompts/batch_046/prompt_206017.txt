I've been in this philosphy of memory seminar and we've been stuck for weeks on whether societies actually have a moral duty to remember past wrongs or whether there's a real, non-evil case for collective forgetting. One camp keeps pushing the Margalit-style view that remembering is part of solidarity with victims — if the community lets the atrocity fade, they dissolve the moral relation to those who suffered, not just the historical record. The other camp draws on Rieff to say that modern remembrance culture is mostly therapeutic performance, and that forcing a society to keep reliving trauma actually blocks any real moral repair or social renewal. What's frustrating is that everyone in the room agrees on the basic historical facts, everyone agrees that state-imposed amnesia is wrong, and yet the argument hasn't moved an inch in a month. Part of the problem might be that I've started using this idea of "mnemonic triage" — deliberately letting peripheral details fade so the core moral claim survives without the archival noise — and people either think thats just forced forgetting with extra steps or they think its the only honest option, with no middle ground.

My working theory is that this isn't really a moral disagreement at all but a disagreement about what kind of social bond we're talking about: is collective identity held together by shared narrative continuity across time, or by shared present commitment that doesn't need to constantly reference the past? But I'm not convinced thats right either — it might just be bedrock intuitions about whether the past should weigh on the present, dressed up in fancy language. So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement verbal — are the two sides using "remembering" in different senses, like propositional recall versus ritual reenactment, or "forgetting" as erasure versus letting go? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, maybe about whether societies that practice selective fading actually recover social trust faster? Or are the sides just answering different questions — one about the ethics of the victim's relation, one about the ethics of the survivor's future? And given whatever diagnosis you give, is there anything that could actually end the stall — a conceptual distinction, a comparative study we'd need, or is it just a standoff we should accept? I don't want a survey of the literature; I want an honest read on what kind of disagreement this actually is.
