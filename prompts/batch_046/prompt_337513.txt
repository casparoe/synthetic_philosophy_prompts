I serve on the editorial board of an interdisciplinary journal that receives submissions spanning neuroscience, physics, and philosophy of mind, and I have noticed a recurring problem: authors frequently claim that one phenomenon is “fundamental to” or “grounds” another without clarifying whether they mean metaphysical grounding, causal dependence, mere correlation, or ontological reduction. I need a reusable decision procedure—essentially a checklist with ordered tests in flowchart form—that a non-specialist reviewer can apply in about twenty minutes to determine whether a submitted paper’s central claim involves genuine metaphysical grounding rather than one of these nearby relations. The procedure should be derived from the core distinctions in contemporary metaphysical grounding theory, particularly the non-causal and typically irreflexive character of grounding, the asymmetry of fundamentality, and the difference between grounding and supervenience or reduction, but it must be usable by someone without formal training in metaphysics. It must explicitly flag the judgment calls that cannot be eliminated, note the points where the procedure is likely to misfire—such as with emergentist frameworks or downward-causation arguments—and state clearly what the checklist cannot adjudicate.

Please apply the procedure to three hypothetical submission abstracts. First, a claim that conscious experience is fundamentally grounded in integrated information, where the argument rests primarily on mathematical isomorphism between information metrics and phenomenological structure. Second, a claim that macroeconomic market patterns are grounded in individual preference orderings, but where the authors allow that market-level dynamics causally feed back to reshape those very preferences. Third, a claim that quantum coherence phenomena in cellular microtubules ground macroscopic biological organization, and where the authors invoke what I will call “recursive fundamentality”: a situation in which the putative ground and the grounded are described at such different levels of abstraction that each can be framed as prior depending on the explanatory context, creating a circularity risk that is not straightforward logical circularity. Include at least one case where your procedure yields a verdict that the authors would reject, and explain why the procedure still returns that answer despite their objections. Finally, specify the boundaries of the tool: what kinds of interdisciplinary claims fall entirely outside its scope, and what a reviewer should do when the checklist returns an ambiguous result that requires escalation rather than a binary decision.
