Design a runnable 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level seminar of approximately 14 students in comparative philosophy or philosophy of language. The aim is to make participants feel the logical pressure behind the Mingjia (School of Names) argument—associated with the dialogue tradition linked to Gongsun Long—that a white horse is not a horse, not as verbal sophistry but as a rigorous consequence of separating form (xing) from color (se).

Before any facilitator explanation, the rules should compel students to discover the coordination failure themselves. Structure the session as a classification dispute with assigned roles and small stakes. Divide the group into three factions: four Name-Masters (Mingjia logicians) who must enforce a strict decompositional rule—every entity card must be sorted into exactly one category corresponding to a single atomic property (form, color, material, function); four Common-Sense Interlocutors who defend ordinary groupings, such as the claim that white horses belong with other horses; and the remaining six students serve as Arbiters who adjudicate disputes using only the rules given, not intuition.

Provide each faction with identical decks of 24 cards describing entities (for example, a white horse, a black horse, a white marble, a wooden horse statue, a pale cloud). The Name-Masters must place each card in one and only one bin. The Interlocutors may challenge placements. Introduce a small-stakes mechanism—call it a disputation fee—where each failed defense costs the challenging faction one token from a shared pool of ten; if the pool empties, the Interlocutors concede. The critical constraint, which should feel artificial and frustrating, is that compound descriptions are not permitted as category labels: the Name-Masters must split them, placing form-cards in the form-bin and color-cards in the color-bin, so that no single card can occupy both bins simultaneously.

Timeline: 10 minutes for rules and role assignment; 30 minutes for the first sorting round and initial disputes; 15 minutes for a reversal in which the Arbiters must apply the same rules to their own intuitions about membership; 25 minutes for structured defense and cross-examination; 10 minutes for the facilitator to reveal that the activity has reproduced the logical skeleton of the White Horse argument.

In the debrief, map specific moments onto the argument’s structure: the separation of form and color corresponds to the first-round splitting; the interlocutor’s objection that this excludes the obvious corresponds to the Interlocutors’ token loss; the problem of compound names corresponds to the prohibition on compound category labels. State explicitly which feature of the Mingjia position—its metaphysical commitment that universals for form and color exist independently in a shared ontological space—cannot be reproduced by card-sorting and must be supplied by the facilitator. Also state what will predictably go wrong: students will try to smuggle compound categories back in through metaphor or functional resemblance; advise the facilitator to allow one such smuggling attempt, then expose its cost in tokens, before returning to strict rules. Be frank that the activity captures the logical pressure but not the historical disputation context of the Warring States period, and that the disputation fee is an artificial injection designed to make the cost of logical consistency visceral.

Keep the prompt self-contained and meaningful without web access. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to obscure secondary texts. Do not include verbatim quotations from the White Horse Dialogue or from classical commentaries; paraphrase all claims. The prompt should be formal and polished.
