I was recently appointed trustee of a family charitable foundation whose charter contains a self-amending clause allowing the board to revise any provision, including that clause itself. The document’s counsel assured me this is boilerplate, but examining the logic has led me to an argument that seems to show all such institutions are authority-empty. (1) For a rule R to genuinely bind an agent A, A must not be simultaneously authorized by R itself to nullify R. (2) A self-amending clause S explicitly authorizes the board to replace S with an unamendable rule, or with no rule at all—thereby nullifying S. (3) So under S, the board is authorized to destroy the very source of its own authority. (4) Therefore S does not genuinely bind the board; it grants a revocable permission disguised as an obligation. (5) If S lacks binding force, then no norms enacted under S can bind either, since their authority derives entirely from S. (6) Hence any institution—my foundation, a constitutional order, a cooperative charter—that incorporates S contains no genuinely authoritative norms. (7) Yet courts, members, and participants consistently treat such institutions as fully binding. (8) So either self-amending institutions are systematically deluded about their authority, or one of my premises is wrong. The conclusion feels intolerable, but I cannot see the slip. Which step should I reject—(1), (4), or (5)—and what does each rejection cost? In particular, if I keep (1) and deny (5), must I accept that derivative norms carry some non-derivative, perhaps social or contractual, source of authority? And if I reject (1), do I have to concede that genuine authority is compatible with the power to destroy one’s own source of authority?
