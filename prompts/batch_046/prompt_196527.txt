I work as a software engineer for an international logistics nonprofit that operates a real-time alert platform; we have no in-house philosophy staff. Our system receives approximately two hundred reports per day flagging potential rescue or harm-prevention scenarios—capsized vessels, preventable disease clusters, infrastructure failures—across multiple regions. Leadership wants to operationalize the principle commonly associated with Peter Singer's drowning-child argument, namely that if an agent can prevent something severely harmful without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, then the agent is required to do so. I need this translated into a reusable decision procedure that my engineering team can implement as a rules engine: an ordered sequence of questions, with explicit criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, designated stop points where the output is do not escalate, designated hand-off points where the issue is one of values rather than verifiable fact and requires human sign-off, and clear markings of which steps are irreducible judgment calls. The procedure must be executable within ten minutes per alert by staff without philosophical training, must specify who approves ambiguous cases, and must accommodate high-frequency, high-stakes decisions.

Please design this procedure. Then apply it step-by-step to three cases: first, an alert that thirty people are aboard a capsizing vessel near one of our ships, where immediate deployment for rescue delays delivery of temperature-sensitive vaccines; second, an alert describing a low-cost vaccination campaign in a remote region that would require diverting funds from a multi-year education program; and third, an alert in which a staff member's immediate family is in the affected zone, but the calculated intervention cost exceeds the comparable-sacrifice threshold, so the procedure recommends against prioritization—an outcome I expect to reject. For each case, trace the procedure's path, identify where it misfires or conflicts with intuition, and note exactly what the procedure leaves unresolved. Finally, specify the hard boundary conditions the procedure must accommodate—ambiguous harm severity, unverified causal efficacy, and contested opportunity-cost metrics—and state clearly what this framework cannot do on its own.
