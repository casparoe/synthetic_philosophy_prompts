The sorites paradox has resisted resolution for over two millennia despite intense analysis: epistemicists maintain that predicates like "heap" have sharp boundaries we are merely ignorant of; supervaluationists deny any such boundary while preserving classical logic for clear cases; and degree theorists assign intermediate truth-values that appear to violate bivalence in a way that feels stipulative rather than explanatory. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement has stalled not because of a hidden logical or empirical fact, but because the parties are answering different questions under the same name—some asking how language ought to be regimented, others asking whether the world contains vague properties, others asking how human cognition processes category boundaries—while treating shared intuitions about grains of sand as if they were common data. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement about semantics, ontology, or logic that I am glossing over? I want a structural diagnosis supported by what each side actually commits to: do they differ about facts, about standards of explanatory adequacy, about which intuitions are authoritative, or about the very purpose of philosophical analysis? Are they addressing distinct problems—linguistic tolerance, metaphysical determinacy, cognitive architecture—that have been conflated? And what, on your diagnosis, would actually count as progress? Would it require a conceptual distinction—perhaps between tolerance principles applied to collective nouns versus gradable adjectives, or between failure of transitivity in an ordering relation versus failure of predicate application? Could an empirical result about categorization mechanisms break the deadlock, or a formal result about continuous spaces and non-transitive relations? Or does the persistence of the paradox indicate that the question is ill-posed, and that progress means abandoning the framing altogether?
