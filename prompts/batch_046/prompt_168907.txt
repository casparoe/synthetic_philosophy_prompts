I have been formalizing Kierkegaard's diagnosis of the press and the public—particularly the idea that mass dissemination creates an abstract phantom, the public, which flattens qualitative distinctions into quantitative circulation and dissolves individual responsibility into anonymity. More recently I have been wondering whether the architecture of digital platforms—what some call context collapse, where algorithmic distribution assembles heterogeneous audiences who share no common responsibility—intensifies or transforms this problem, and whether extending his critique to democratic deliberation in such conditions overreaches. Here is the chain I constructed:

(1) For Kierkegaard, ethical-religious communication requires direct address: speaker and listener must stand in a particular, responsible relation, not mediated by an anonymous collective.
(2) The press, and by extension any institution of mass dissemination, structurally eliminates direct address. It speaks to an indefinite audience; no one is specifically called, and no one is specifically accountable.
(3) If a mode of discourse structurally eliminates direct responsibility, then it is incompatible with the ethical-religious task of becoming a single individual.
(4) Modern democratic deliberation depends precisely on such mediated, mass-scale discourse—shared information, public opinion, collective decision-making.
(5) Therefore democratic deliberation is structurally incompatible with the ethical-religious task.
(6) But Kierkegaard treats this task as unconditional: it applies in every sphere of life, with no exemptions for politics.
(7) Hence anyone who takes him seriously must either withdraw entirely from democratic life or live in perpetual ethical failure.
(8) Yet Kierkegaard does not advocate withdrawal; he writes for a public, employs the press, and treats inwardness as compatible with external social roles.
(9) So the argument must fail somewhere. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (6), but I am uncertain whether to reject (3) by allowing mediated discourse a legitimate subordinate place, or to reject (5) by denying that democracy necessarily requires the kind of leveling anonymity he condemns.

Where exactly does the reasoning slip? Is there a subtle scope shift—perhaps direct address is required only for religious communication, not for political coordination—or does (5) rest on a false premise about democratic dependence on mass anonymity? And if I keep (1) and reject (5), what does that commit me to about the possibility of democratic citizenship without leveling?

One further request: if this framing rests on a confusion—say, if I have conflated the religious category of the single individual with a general moral psychology, or if the task as posed cannot be answered well without misrepresenting his texts—please say so plainly rather than accommodating the premise politely. I would rather have the confusion named than papered over.
