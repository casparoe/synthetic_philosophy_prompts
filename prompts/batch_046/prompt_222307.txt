I have been tracking the judgments of a philosopher of artificial cognition—call them R—on whether particular systems are agents, simulators, or neither, and I am trying to reverse-engineer the implicit taxonomy. So far R has said: AlphaGo, with its value network and Monte Carlo tree search integrated into a single reward-optimizing loop, is an agent; a large language model running in a chat interface is a simulator; a distributed swarm of simple robots that collectively solves logistics problems with no individual representational states is neither, but rather emergent computation lacking both agential and mimetic structure; a human with complete episodic amnesia who still acquires procedural skills and pursues immediate needs is an agent; a predictive-coding model of visual cortex running in isolation with no motor output is a simulator, running perceptual inference without action selection; and a brain-computer interface that translates motor-cortex signals into a robotic arm reaching for objects, even when the user reports no conscious awareness of the arm, is an agent, because the integrated brain-interface-arm system realizes coherent goal-directed behavior.

R has never stated the principle explicitly. Now consider a new case: a multimodal foundation model embedded in a humanoid robot with continuous proprioceptive and visual feedback. It adapts its movements to obstacles and grasps objects in real time, yet it possesses no explicit reward function, no long-term planning module, and no persistent self-model across power cycles; it was trained entirely on next-token prediction over interleaved text, image, and proprioceptive sequences, and its apparent goals—reaching, avoiding—are only interpretable post-hoc by observers.

Predict R’s verdict—agent, simulator, or something else—then do the philosophical work: identify at least two distinct principles that jointly fit all six prior verdicts, say what each predicts for this embodied model, give your best guess for R’s actual implicit rule with reasons, and specify the further case that would best discriminate between the competing principles. I am less interested in a bibliographic survey than in the conceptual architecture: in particular, address whether the distinction turns on teleofunctional integration versus shallow behavioral mimicry, on the presence of an explicit world-model coupled to action selection, or on some other axis entirely, and note where R’s precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer.
