I've been roped into giving the opening remarks at a workshop next Thursday — the theme is historical fiction and biopics, specifically what do we owe the real dead when we dramatise them? The audience is going to be awkward: a few historians, the director of a recent film about a civil-rights organiser who died in 1978, and two of his grandchildren who are still furious about one scene. I want the speech to turn on a slightly niche framing I'm calling the asymmetry of retroactive framing — the idea that the dead can't correct the narrative arc we lock them into, so even factually accurate choices become an unauthorised framing of their agency. This needs to do real structural work, not just decoration: it should explain why I can't offer simple comfort like 'he'd be proud' without betraying the idea, and why I can't condemn the director without ignoring that all history is selection. Half the room won't be philosophers, so it needs to land without jargon, but the experts should feel it's doing actual philosophical labour. Please write the speech in American English — the venue is in Chicago — and don't include any bibliography or citations; I just need the text. I definately want to avoid falling back on Epicurean-style claims that death is nothing to us, since this organiser was religious and that framing wouldn't honour the family on this occassion.
