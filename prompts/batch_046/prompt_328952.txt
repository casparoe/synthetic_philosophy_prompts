You are serving as the senior philosophical advisor to the Municipal Heritage and Civic Memory Commission. The Commission must vote next Thursday on whether to adopt Resolution 14-B, which would formally designate the city’s historic central plaza as a site of “transgenerational institutional guilt” and mandate an annual ritual of civic acknowledgment. The resolution draws on conceptual distinctions between criminal liability, political responsibility, moral guilt, and metaphysical guilt—frameworks associated with Karl Jaspers’s post-war analysis—though it applies them to a collective body that persists across generations through institutional continuity rather than biological descent. Proponents argue that without such a designation, the city treats historical injustice as a closed archive rather than an open wound in its civic identity, and that the ritual can be structured to honor victims without assigning personal blame to present residents. Opponents, including several commissioners with backgrounds in legal philosophy and moral psychology, contend that guilt is essentially non-transferable, that attributing moral guilt to present citizens who did not participate in the original acts violates basic conditions of agency and culpability, and that the proposed ritual risks substituting symbolic performance for material repair while coercing emotional assent from citizens who bear no direct responsibility.

Please draft the short written opinion you will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. Your memo should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—including a specific time frame for implementation, revision, or sunset review. It should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side rather than dismissing it. In particular, address whether an institution can bear a form of guilt that is neither purely criminal nor purely metaphorical, what conditions of collective agency must hold for that attribution to be coherent, and whether the proposed ritual can be designed so that it distinguishes historical accountability from inherited personal blame. Think carefully about the dependency relations among these concepts: what must be true about institutional identity over time for the resolution to make sense, and where does that leave individual citizens who are members but not authors of the institution? Before writing, briefly brainstorm the conceptual architecture—how the standard categories map onto institutional life, where they break down when applied across generations, and what a modified framework might look like for this specific civic context. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for circulation to a diverse commission including historians, legal scholars, and community representatives. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
