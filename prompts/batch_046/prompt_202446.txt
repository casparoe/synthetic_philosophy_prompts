I cover science and forecasting for a general-interest publication, and I need a reusable editorial protocol I can run in about thirty minutes before filing. The problem is this: I regularly report on scientific projections—climate-model outputs, epidemiological forecasts, risk assessments—whose evidentiary basis will shift within months or years, and I want my present reporting stance (whether to present a claim as credible, hedged, or deferred) to be disciplined by Bas van Fraassen’s reflection principle, which holds that a rational agent’s current beliefs about a proposition should align with her beliefs about what she will come to believe. I want this principle translated into an ordered decision procedure—a flowchart in words—with explicit stopping conditions: where the answer is “do not present as established,” where it is “present with calibrated hedging,” and where it is “present as a serious claim requiring no special caveat.”

The procedure must incorporate a distinction that standard expositions usually collapse: evidential revision (my future belief changes because I receive new data, corrected analysis, or improved models) versus institutional revision (my future belief changes because of disciplinary politics, funding shifts, or social pressure within the expert community). Only evidential revision is governed by reflection in its standard form; institutional revision should trigger a separate calibration step. Treat this evidential-institutional filter as central.

For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what requires a quantitative or qualitative judgment call (and flag it explicitly as such), and what expertise is assumed. I am a generalist science journalist, not a statistician; where the procedure demands numerical reasoning, it must say so honestly and indicate when I should consult an editor or methodologist rather than guess. The protocol should also specify where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or framing rather than belief calibration.

Deliver the procedure first. Then apply it to three cases:

1. A climate-model projection that global mean temperature will reach a specific threshold by 2050, with the modeling group acknowledging that next year’s satellite parameter updates will likely narrow or shift the confidence interval.
2. A prominent AI-risk assessment predicting a 15% probability of catastrophic misalignment by 2040, where peer review is expected to correct methodological overreach downward—yet applying the reflection principle together with the evidential-institutional filter produces a reporting stance that forces stronger present endorsement than my personal skepticism allows. Identify the exact node where the procedure overrules my intuition, and mark it.
3. A sociological forecast that public trust in scientific institutions will decline sharply over the next decade, where the anticipated revision is driven not by new survey data but by anticipated institutional backlash against the forecasters themselves. The procedure should stop with a clear hand-off—indicating that belief-stance calibration is blocked and the framing decision belongs to editorial values rather than epistemic reflection.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which classes of claims it should never be applied to, which cognitive biases it will not eliminate, and which nodes require mandatory sign-off by an editor rather than the journalist running the protocol alone. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; I have no access to external sources and need a procedure I can execute from internal reasoning alone.
