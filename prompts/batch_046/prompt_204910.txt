I have been trying to formalize an objection I encountered in a discussion about whether culture can genuinely cause economic change, using Weber's well-known thesis about the Protestant ethic as my central case. The reasoning seems to force a dilemma: if culture is a cause, then cultural explanation proves too much and becomes unfalsifiable; if it is not, then Weber is wrong. I cannot locate the slip. Here is the chain, step by step:

(1) For a cultural factor C to count as a genuine cause of an economic outcome O, C must contribute explanatory power that material conditions M do not already provide.

(2) If C contributes explanatory power that M does not, then there must be at least one counterfactual situation in which M is held constant, C is absent, and O does not occur.

(3) Weber's thesis claims precisely this kind of independence: he argues that Calvinist ascetic values accelerated the development of capitalism in parts of Northern Europe where the underlying material conditions—market structures, legal frameworks, labor availability—were broadly comparable to regions where capitalism developed more slowly.

(4) But historical comparison shows that these material conditions were never actually constant; differences in inheritance law, urban guild organization, and trade access always accompanied the religious differences.

(5) Therefore we never have the clean counterfactual required by (2), so we can never empirically establish that C made an independent contribution.

(6) If we nevertheless allow culture to be cited as a cause whenever it correlates with an outcome, then any economic change can be explained by pointing to some accompanying cultural difference.

(7) That renders cultural explanation unfalsifiable and therefore empty as a scientific claim.

(8) To preserve the scientific status of sociology, we must deny that culture ever makes an independent contribution; it is always only a reflection or reinforcement of material conditions.

(9) But then Weber's claim that the Protestant ethic was an independent cause of capitalism, not merely its byproduct, collapses.

(10) So the argument forces a choice: either cultural explanation is always redundant, or it is always available and therefore useless.

If I have to choose which premises to preserve, I would prefer to keep (1) and (3), because I think explanations should do real work and I do not want to dismiss Weber outright. But I worry that accepting both commits me to (8) and therefore to rejecting Weber anyway. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
