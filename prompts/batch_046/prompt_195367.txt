Read Mozart's Don Giovanni through Bernard Williams's philosophy of opera—above all his claim that opera constructs a self-contained world in which modern moral judgment is suspended by musical identification, and his warning that treating works as ethical case studies distorts both the art and the philosophy. Focus on the cemetery scene with the Commendatore and the finale: does Don Giovanni's refusal to repent, followed by his descent and the survivors' closing sextet, represent the opera finally imposing the moral verdict that Williams says it should resist, or does the music—the D-minor terror, the abrupt shift to the major-mode epilogue—keep that judgment in suspension? I want a reading faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce the opera to an illustration of Williams's ideas, and one that does not twist his resistance to moralism into an excuse to ignore the work's own dramatic logic. End by saying what the opera adds to the philosophy of moral luck—particularly the question of whether a life can be judged by a single, unrepeated moment—that philosophical argument alone cannot capture.
