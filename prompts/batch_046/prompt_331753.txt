I'm preparing a consistency audit for a seminar on the ethics of armed conflict, and I want to know whether my current package of views is jointly tenable before I defend it. Here is my profile:

(1) I accept the moral equality of combatants in its traditional form: soldiers fighting for a just cause and soldiers fighting for an unjust cause possess identical moral permissions to kill opposing combatants and identical rights not to be killed, with the objective justice of the cause making no direct moral difference to these permissions and rights.

(2) I think defensive killing is governed by a liability-based account: a person forfeits their right not to be killed only if they are morally responsible—through culpable action or omission—for posing an unjust threat.

(3) I hold that soldiers on the unjust side typically pose an unjust threat objectively, but they very often lack culpability because they are non-culpably ignorant of the factual circumstances that make the war unjust, or because they are subject to severe coercion or institutional deception; therefore, they usually do not forfeit their rights.

(4) I hold that soldiers on the just side do not pose any unjust threat and therefore retain their full rights not to be killed, regardless of whether they are conscripted or voluntary.

(5) I think that the combatant/non-combatant distinction should be maintained as a basic legal and moral rule, and that it ought not be collapsed into an individual liability analysis—even if such an analysis would sometimes justify targeting non-combatant architects of aggression or protect excused conscripts.

(6) I think that a soldier's personal belief about the justice of the war is neither necessary nor sufficient for their moral permission to fight; instead, institutional authorization and the division of moral labor between political decision-makers and soldiers do the normative work.

(7) I draw a sharp distinction between factual ignorance, which can excuse unjust soldiers, and normative ignorance about the principles of just war, which cannot excuse them, because I take those normative principles to be accessible to practical reason regardless of institutional deception.

Please examine these commitments for tension. Which pairs pull against each other genuinely, which tensions are only apparent on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision for each genuine conflict? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each say specifically what would have to give. Be explicit about whether (1) can survive alongside (2) through (4), whether (5) is consistent with (2) given (6) and (7), and identify the single revision that would clean up the most tension if I had to choose one. Deliver this as an audit of my position set, not as a general lecture on just war theory; I am not signaling which commitment I am prepared to abandon.
