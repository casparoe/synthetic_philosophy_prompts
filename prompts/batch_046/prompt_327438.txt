I'm writing up my position for a philosophy of science seminar on determinism, chaos, and predictability, and I want to check whether the package I'm defending actually coheres or whether I'm smuggling in contradictions. Here is where I've landed: (1) I accept metaphysical determinism for classical physical systems—every state is fixed by prior states plus dynamical laws, with no branching futures. (2) I take chaotic dynamics to be deterministic through and through; long-range unpredictability is purely epistemic, arising from exponential divergence of trajectories and the physical impossibility of infinite-precision measurement, not from any ontological openness in the dynamics themselves. (3) I defend a sharp modal distinction between predictability-in-principle and predictability-in-practice, and I think chaos only undermines the latter—there remains a genuine counterfactual sense in which the future state is fixed and computable, even if no embedded agent can perform the computation. (4) At the same time, I hold that if computing a deterministic system's global evolution requires infinite precision in initial conditions such that no physically realizable mechanism could ever execute it, then applying the determinism label globally is largely a metaphysical decoration: it correctly describes local law-governance but fails to ground what I call predictive sovereignty over the system's trajectory. (5) I reject quantum indeterminacy as a source of meaningful freedom or openness—randomness is not agency, and it does not rescue any interesting form of control. (6) Finally, I think chaos offers a structurally useful phenomenological model for human deliberation: the lived experience of openness and multiple live possibilities is real and analogizes well to chaotic divergence, even though the underlying neurophysical dynamics, if deterministic, remain fixed.

Go through these commitments and audit them for consistency. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which ones only appear to conflict, and how severe is each tension? In particular, I am worried that (3) and (4) pull in opposite directions—does my defense of the in-principle distinction commit me to denying that chaos undermines global determinism, or can (3) and (4) be reconciled without abandoning either? I also want to know whether (6) creates a real problem for (1) and (2), or whether that tension is merely apparent and dissolves once we separate phenomenological modeling from metaphysical commitment. Order the real tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and exactly what it costs me in terms of the other commitments. If consistency requires me to drop one of these, tell me which one and why; but I should flag that I am reluctant to give up determinism or the purely epistemic reading of chaos unless they genuinely cannot coexist with (4).
