In particular, I am examining Daniel Dennett's claim—often illustrated with minimal computational examples—that intentionality is always derived, since even human cognition is ultimately the product of evolutionary 'design' that bestows meaning from the outside. I want to contrast this with John Searle's stricter dichotomy between original intentionality, which he takes to be an intrinsic biological feature of certain organisms, and derived intentionality, which depends entirely on the intentional states of observers or designers.

What I find puzzling, and what I hope you can help me clarify, is how these positions diverge when applied to a system that acquires representational content neither through explicit programming nor through biological evolution in the usual sense—say, a large-scale predictive-processing architecture trained via unsupervised learning on sensory input. On Dennett's design stance, such a system might seem to possess only derived intentionality, yet the evolutionary analogy could be stretched to cover its learning dynamics. On Searle's biological naturalism, however, the absence of intrinsic biological causation might seem to preclude original intentionality altogether, leaving the system with merely observer-relative content. Could you compare Dennett's and Searle's frameworks on this specific point, explaining whether Dennett's collapse of the original/derived boundary holds up against Searle's charge of biological reductionism, and whether the predictive-learning case genuinely tests the distinction or merely obscures it?
