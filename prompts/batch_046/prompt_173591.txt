I recently joined a philosophy of biology seminar after retiring from microbial ecology, and I witnessed a sharp exchange between two participants—Dr. Lena Park, a theoretical biologist, and Dr. Sam Okonkwo, a philosopher of science—over whether evolutionary selection can genuinely operate at levels above the gene. I am writing to ask for a rigorous, point-by-point adjudication of their arguments, as I found both positions partially compelling yet ultimately unsatisfying.

The disagreement unfolded over six moves.

(1) Park opened with a gene-centered position: the only entities that possess both high-fidelity replication and sufficient longevity across evolutionary time are nucleic acid sequences. Organisms are transient vehicles constructed by genes to propagate themselves; groups are even more ephemeral coalitions. Therefore, when we observe cooperative behavior in a microbial consortium, we should explain it either as kin selection among related gene copies or as direct fitness benefits to the individual vehicles carrying those copies.

(2) Okonkwo responded that this framing presupposes a clear boundary between replicator and vehicle that collapses in many real-world systems. In modular organisms and microbial biofilms, individual identity is physiologically porous. If the causal contribution of a gene to fitness is irreducibly mediated by the collective metabolic state of the group, then treating the group as a mere aggregate is an explanatory error, not just an ontological preference.

(3) Park countered that porous boundaries do not create new units of selection. Even when horizontal gene transfer decouples a gene from its original chromosomal lineage, the transferred sequence remains the replicator whose frequency changes in the metagenome. Group-level properties are simply the summed, context-dependent effects of gene-level strategies. To invoke group selection is therefore redundant: it adds an ontological layer without adding predictive power.

(4) Okonkwo then introduced what he called reticulate functional coherence. In engineered and natural consortia selected for collective degradation pathways, metabolic complementarity—not genetic relatedness—stabilizes membership. Genes move laterally among unrelated lineages, yet the consortium's overall degradation rate is heritable across ecological succession because daughter consortia inherit the same functional architecture via environmental templates and seed-bank bottlenecks. Consequently, the unit that exhibits heritable variation in fitness is the network of functional interactions, not any single gene pool or clonal individual.

(5) Park objected that this conflates ecological persistence with evolutionary reproduction. A consortium does not reproduce; it is replaced. Without a clear parent-offspring relation—without the transmission of discrete, bounded entities—there is no mechanism for cumulative selection. Heritability through environmental templates is too diffuse and noisy to support adaptation; it is maintenance, not evolution.

(6) Okonkwo concluded that Park's criterion of discrete reproduction is an unjustified holdover from Mendelian genetics applied to multicellular organisms. Reproduction in evolutionary theory should be understood as the transmission of any trait configuration that systematically influences fitness across generations. If the functional architecture of a consortium is transmitted with sufficient fidelity to generate differential survival and proliferation of consortia, then the consortium satisfies the conditions for being a unit of selection, regardless of whether its parts share a genome.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then state who is right about which specific points, what—if anything—both parties are missing, and which view is more defensible overall. In particular, address whether Okonkwo's notion of reticulate functional coherence genuinely establishes a novel unit of selection, or whether Park is correct that it can be fully decomposed into gene-level dynamics with appropriate environmental modeling.
