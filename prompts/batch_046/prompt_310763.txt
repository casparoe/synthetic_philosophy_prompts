I was roped into leading a 90-minute workshop for my friends grad reading group — mostly poli-sci students who've done the standard welfare-state canon but keep running together 'conditionality' with plain old means-testing. I want to teach them the specific conceptual move where workfare doesn't just add rules to benefits, but actually redefines 'desert' from an ex-ante status of need into an ex-post performance of bureaucratic labor. I'm calling this shift 'procedural undeservingness' for the session, but I need the teaching plan, not the lecture itself.

The crowd is smart but moralizes quickly — they either jump to 'well people should work for it' or dismiss every sanction as neoliberal cruelty. So I need the arc mapped out: what idea has to land first (probably the gap between screening for fraud and constructing a moral category?), then the carrying example. Would a real case like UK Universal Credit's tiered sanctions work, or should I invent a tighter hypothetical benefit with an explicit ritual? The example needs to feel concrete but shouldn't drag them into Poor Law historiography. Then the misconceptions: I expect they'll conflate procedural desert with moral character, and think I'm just describing fraud detection with extra steps. Exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off?

Also, what should I openly say I'm leaving out — the racialized US welfare history, the macro-efficiency debates? And what's a quick end-of-session check, maybe a classification exercise with a hypothetical case, that proves they've grasped the ontology shift rather than just repeating that workfare is harsh?
