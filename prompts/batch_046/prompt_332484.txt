I hold a hybrid position on rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori. I accept that natural kind terms are rigid designators in the full Kripkean sense, but I deny that all proper names are: a name is only rigid when its reference is not descriptively anchored, and in anchored cases—think of Neptune introduced via orbital-perturbation descriptions—the necessary a posteriori survives not because the name tracks the same individual across worlds, but because our epistemic access to the reference-fixing conditions is always mediated by contingent empirical discovery. This lets me preserve the modal result for water being H2O while allowing that descriptively anchored proper names lack cross-world rigidity. Before I commit, I want an honest map of what genuinely counts against this view. I am not asking only for the standard anti-descriptivist counterexamples; I want the specific cases, formal results, and modal scenarios that expose the hybrid: ambiguous baptism where descriptions diverge, theoretical term revision that shifts reference without renaming, contexts in which descriptive content seems to determine reference across worlds, and whether my anchored/unanchored distinction collapses into an unprincipled taxonomy. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a defender of this hybrid should concede it as damaging or can dismiss it, and note whether pure Kripkeans or descriptivists would treat it as vindicating their own view. Finally, assess whether the position is genuinely exposed or has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and if the latter, whether that is a defect for a metaphysical claim of this kind. I am writing for clarity, not for a bibliography.
