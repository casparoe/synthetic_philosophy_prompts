I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind working on comparative moral psychology, and I have been trying to refine a framework for assessing moral patienthood that disentangles neural scale from functional complexity. In a recent seminar discussion with colleagues in cognitive ethology, I defended the view that absolute brain size is not intrinsically morally relevant, but I want to push this further with a specific conceptual constraint that several people found suspicious. Call it the scaling independence thesis: the claim that if a cognitive architecture achieves a given level of representational integration through structurally different scaling laws — for instance, dense local connectivity in a small brain versus diffuse long-range connectivity in a very large one — then gross brain mass carries no independent moral weight whatsoever, not even as a defeasible heuristic or proxy for potential suffering. This seems to imply that we ought to assess moral status solely through functional and behavioral markers of representational capacity, regardless of whether the underlying neural substrate weighs fifty grams or five kilograms. But I worry this may be too quick, and that something important about the robustness or richness of conscious experience might be lost if we excise size entirely from our calculus.

What are the most important implications of adopting this thesis for our practical moral obligations to non-human animals? Specifically, does it force us to abandon brain size completely within a pluralist account of moral status, or can it be rehabilitated as one factor among many that tracks something genuinely important — perhaps the stability, granularity, or temporal depth of experience — without collapsing back into a crude encephalocentric hierarchy? Furthermore, how should this framework adjudicate boundary cases in which large-brained species exhibit relatively simple behavioral repertoires, while small-brained species display remarkably complex social cognition, without either reinstating size as a hidden default or sliding into an implausibly disembodied functionalism that ignores the material conditions of consciousness altogether? I am particularly interested in whether accepting scaling independence commits us to a radical revision of our obligations toward cetaceans and cephalopods, or whether it ultimately reinforces the liberal intuition that what matters is not the hardware's dimensions but the architecture of experience it sustains.
