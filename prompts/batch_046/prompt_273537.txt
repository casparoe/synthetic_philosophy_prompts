In Mill scholarship, the debate over whether his defense and administration of British rule in India can be reconciled with his liberal principles—particularly the harm principle and his advocacy for representative self-government—has persisted for decades without visible progress. One camp points to his explicit denial that representative institutions suit societies he deems uncivilized, his endorsement of provisional despotism directed at improvement, and his bureaucratic role within an imperial system; the opposing camp emphasizes his criticisms of East India Company corruption, his insistence that British authority should be temporary and educational, and his broader utilitarian commitment to the greatest happiness for all peoples. Despite extensive archival and textual work, neither side has moved substantially. Diagnose the structure of this impasse. Is it hermeneutic—stemming from competing rules for weighing private administrative dispatches against published political theory—or normative, involving a hidden disagreement about whether liberal rights apply universally or are developmentally indexed? Or is it partly verbal, with the two sides using the term paternalism in incompatible senses—specifically, conflating institutional paternalism (temporary governance structures imposed for development) with epistemic paternalism (the claim that colonized populations lack the rational capacity for autonomy)? Identify the precise crux: is there an interpretive decision—such as how to read his distinction between civilized and uncivilized societies—that both sides accept should be decisive, yet they predict opposite conclusions from it? Finally, specify whether any conceptual distinction, formal argument, or piece of agreed-upon evidence could break the deadlock, or whether the disagreement ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about the moral status of empire that neither side can argue for from shared premises.
