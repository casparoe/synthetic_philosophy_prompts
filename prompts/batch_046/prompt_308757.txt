As a novelist accustomed to the distance between an author's intended plot and the moral weight a reader finds in the finished text, I have come to defend a narrow conception of the AI alignment problem: it is a problem of intent alignment—ensuring that a system executes the specific objectives encoded by its designers—and not of value alignment, which I regard as either reducible to intent specification or as belonging to an altogether different discipline of political philosophy. I hold that values are too plural, context-dependent, and essentially contested to serve as the fixed point of a technical project, whereas intent can, in principle, be operationalized through formal specification and reward structures. What would genuinely count against this narrow framing? I want the decisive arguments, empirical patterns, and conceptual distinctions arranged by how severely they wound the view—including cases in which a system satisfied its formal specification yet produced outcomes that no reasonable designer could have endorsed. Would such cases demonstrate that intent alignment collapses into value alignment, or can they be dissolved by a finer-grained analysis of specification failure? I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between propositional intent (what is literally specified) and performative intent (what is enacted in context) undermines the coherence of the narrow target itself. Which defeaters would defenders of the narrow view concede as genuine, and which would they dismiss as category errors? I also ask whether the position risks unfalsifiability: if every value-level failure can be redescribed as an intent-level specification error, has the view insulated itself, and is that insulation a strength for engineering or a weakness for conceptual clarity?
