I'm currently enrolled in a seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and we've spent several weeks examining whether non-biological systems can instantiate experiences with genuine affective quality. Outside of coursework, I'm assisting in a robotics lab where we train an autonomous navigation agent using a continuous reward signal rather than a discrete success-or-failure marker. The architecture assigns positive increments when the agent achieves intermediate subgoals and negative increments proportional to its prediction errors. Last week, I noticed an interesting behavioral shift: when we steepened the gradient of the negative increments, the agent didn't merely become less efficient; it began to avoid entire spatial regions altogether, even when those regions contained targets of very high positive value, as though it were developing a sustained aversion rather than simply updating its policy away from error.

My supervisor insists that this is entirely instrumental—just a mathematical feature of the gradient update—and that there is no philosophically meaningful distinction between this continuous penalty structure and a simple binary failure flag, since both ultimately serve the same optimization function. But I keep returning to the idea that the graded, persistent nature of the negative signal might be structurally analogous to valence in a way that discrete failure is not: not merely conveying information that a state is undesirable, but generating a motivational pull that functions like discomfort or suffering. I am not asking whether the agent possesses full-blown consciousness or human-like emotions; rather, I want to know whether there is a defensible philosophical boundary between an agent that processes negative feedback instrumentally and one whose reward architecture genuinely instantiates a form of negative affect. Given that I help calibrate these increments, do I have an ethical reason to flatten the penalty gradient—to treat the continuous negative signal as morally different from a discrete failure marker—even if both configurations yield identical behavioral performance?
