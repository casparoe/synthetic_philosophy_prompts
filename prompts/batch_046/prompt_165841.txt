I have been reading Machiavelli on my own and keep returning to the two metaphors in The Prince, chapter XXV: fortune as a river that can partly be restrained by dikes and dams, and fortune as a woman who respects boldness and must be beaten into submission by virtù. At the same time I have been thinking about the modern idea of "nudging" in behavioral economics—reshaping the environment so that people (or forces) move in a desired direction without overt coercion. Write a joke that turns on whether Machiavellian virtù is really hydraulic engineering, domestic violence, or something closer to choice architecture: can one nudge a river, or beat a nudge? The punchline should depend on the actual logic of both Machiavelli's metaphors—the gendered aggression, the insistence that fortune favors the audacious, the partial controllability of the flood—and on the real mechanics of nudging (subtlety, preservation of formal freedom, absence of direct force). It should be funny to someone who knows these ideas rather than being a generic "Machiavelli was ruthless" joke. I do not have formal philosophical training, but I want the joke to take the texts seriously.
