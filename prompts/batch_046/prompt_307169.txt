I'm giving the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on population ethics and climate policy—about fifteen philosophers of population ethics, plus an equal number of climate economists and public-policy analysts who have never encountered the procreation asymmetry. The session is being recorded for a graduate course, so the tone should be formal but not needlessly academic. I'd like the remarks to run about eight minutes, delivered in American English, and I want the asymmetry to do real structural work rather than serve as decoration. Specifically: I want to show that if we accept the asymmetry—that there is a weighty reason not to bring a suffering person into existence but no corresponding reason to bring a happy one into existence—then a common move in climate-ethics literature collapses. That move treats institutional "population targets" embedded in carbon-budget models as morally interchangeable with per-capita emission reductions, on the ground that both prevent future harm. I think the asymmetry blocks that interchangeability, because lowering a population target avoids creating people who might suffer, but it also forgoes creating people who might flourish, and the asymmetry gives us no reason to do the latter—so the moral structure is not symmetric with reducing emissions. At the same time, I want to avoid sounding like I'm advocating anti-natalism or prescribing policy; the point is conceptual, about how carbon-budget modeling frames its value assumptions. To keep the philosophers engaged without losing the policy analysts, please build the speech around one concrete, somewhat niche framing: treat the asymmetry as applying to institutional "replacement ratios" in integrated assessment models, asking whether a model that assumes a smaller future population needs a different normative justification than one that assumes a larger one, even when both reach the same net carbon budget. Make sure both audiences can follow, and do not include citations, reading lists, or requests for further literature.
