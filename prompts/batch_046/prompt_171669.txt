I am designing a classroom exercise for a graduate seminar on epistemic logic, and after a long discussion with my students about whether logical omniscience is a formal bug or a rationality feature, I realized we were talking past one another because nobody had mapped where our actual commitments diverge. I want a decision-tree taxonomy—something like a "which response to logical omniscience do you actually hold?" instrument—that sorts philosophical positions by concrete diagnostic commitments rather than by which paper or author someone happens to cite.

The tree should begin with a specific scenario: an agent S believes both premises of a straightforward modus ponens argument, has never performed the inference, and sincerely denies believing the conclusion; the first branching node asks whether this failure is best diagnosed as a resource limitation in S, an inadequacy in the semantic model that assigns worlds too coarsely, or a reason to reject the closure principle altogether. From there, branch through further concrete cases—nested beliefs about arithmetic truths that outstrip working memory, belief in explicit contradictions within non-normal worlds without explosive belief, and belief revision upon learning a previously hidden lemma—so that every internal node poses a claim or scenario respondents can answer without knowing technical labels. The leaves must include at least explicit-derivation syntactic accounts, awareness-structured possible-worlds models, impossible-worlds non-normal accounts, and deflationary anti-closure views. Wherever two standard named positions collapse onto the same branch, flag them as indistinguishable by this instrument; wherever a logical combination of answers has no occupant—say, full logical closure combined with the denial that belief has any propositional content—note the vacancy and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep the tree within about six to eight decision nodes, and include a brief scoring guide that maps answer patterns onto the leaf families, designed so that students work through the case questions first and only afterward discover which philosophical stance their pattern represents.
