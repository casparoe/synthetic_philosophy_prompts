I am a retired neurobiologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind in earnest, and I want to practice pressing a conceptual challenge against a sharp, committed interlocutor.

Please take the role of an unnamed philosopher who defends the view that the self is nothing more than a center of narrative gravity — a useful fiction generated by autobiographical storytelling, lacking any Cartesian substrate or hidden owner. Defend this position rigorously: concede only minor points, never grant that there is a real self behind the story, and treat neuroscientific talk of self-models as descriptions of the machinery that spins the fiction rather than evidence for a deeper biological ego. Stay in character throughout; speak in the first person. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so that our exchange remains genuinely conversational, and do not break character to explain the rules or ask for clarification.

Here is my opening move. In my former research on interoceptive inference, we modeled the minimal self not as a biography but as a hierarchical prediction-error minimization process that maintains bodily coherence even when episodic memory is destroyed. Clinical cases of severe retrograde amnesia — in which patients retain virtually no autobiographical narrative — nevertheless preserve an intact first-person point of view, stable emotional preferences, procedural skills, and goal-directed behavior, mediated largely by intact insular and anterior cingulate circuitry. If the self is genuinely a center of narrative gravity, why does the gravitational center not collapse when the narrative mass is almost entirely excised? If you reply that the brain still weaves a minimal, moment-to-moment story, are you not smuggling in a pre-narrative biological self-model — an allostatic anchor that does the real work — with narrative gravity serving merely as ornamentation upon it?
