I'm taking a metaphysics seminar and got into a debate about Black's spheres and Leibniz's principle of the identity of indescernibles, and now I think I've accidentally argued that motion is impossible. Here's the chain.

(1) The identity of indescernibles says that if x and y share every property, they're literally the same thing. (2) Black's spheres are two spheres in an otherwise empty universe with the same mass, charge, radius, etc., seperated only by being in different places. (3) So if PII is true, spatial separation can't be a real relation — otherwise the spheres wouldn't be genuinely indiscernible. (4) But if separation isn't relational, then being-at-a-location has to be an intrinsic property, like "being exactly at point P". (5) That means sphere A has the intrinsic property being-at-A and sphere B has being-at-B, so they're discernible after all, and PII survives. (6) But apply this to anything: if two objects aren't in the exact same spot, they have different intrinsic location-properties, so nothing is ever indiscernible. (7) That makes PII vacuously true but useless for telling duplicates apart. (8) Worse, if location is intrinsic, then when something moves it sheds one essential property and picks up another — so it stops being itself and becomes a different object. (9) So either PII is false, or nothing can ever move.

I feel like the slip is somewhere between (3) and (5), but I can't name it. Which step should I reject, and what's the cost? I'd really like to keep (1) and also the idea that spatial relations are genuinely real, but I'm afraid those conflict.
