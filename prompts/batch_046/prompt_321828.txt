I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the attribution of biological functions, judging by my gut reactions to concrete cases rather than by theory. Here are my verdicts.

The human heart has the function of pumping blood. A heart with a severe congenital defect that never achieves effective circulation is malfunctioning, but I still think pumping blood is its purpose—it is failing at what it is meant to do, not lacking a purpose altogether. A malignant tumor divides rapidly, but I do not think rapid cell division is its function; that division is pathological, not the purpose of the tissue. The eyes of a fully blind cave-dwelling fish have no visual function; they are present, but not meant for seeing. The human appendix has lost its original digestive role, yet I think it currently has immune functions—it is not just a useless remnant. By contrast, a particular bony ridge on a mammal's skull that exists only because the gene that builds it is pleiotropically coupled to an adaptive jaw-development gene, and that ridge itself anchors no muscles and serves no mechanical purpose, has no function at all; it is a pure spandrel.

Now some harder ones. A bacterial gene that confers antibiotic resistance is horizontally transferred into a plant genome. In the plant, it produces a compound that aids drought tolerance, and natural selection in that environment now maintains it for that effect. I find myself wanting to say that its function is still antibiotic resistance—the one it was originally selected for—even though in this organism it never does that and is kept around only for drought tolerance. At the same time, consider a synthetic, de novo engineered vesicle inserted into a yeast cell that synthesizes ATP with the same biochemical mechanism as a mitochondrion. The vesicle has no evolutionary origin in that lineage; it was designed in a lab. The cell now depends on it for survival, and in culture cells lacking it are outcompeted. Here I want to say that it does have the function of ATP synthesis—it is genuinely meant for energy production in this system, despite having no evolutionary history.

These two judgments pull in opposite directions: in one case I privilege evolutionary origin over current contribution, in the other I privilege current contribution and design over origin. I also keep returning to this case: a DNA-based computing circuit engineered into bacteria that processes environmental signals but performs no metabolic or reproductive role for the cell. The bacteria grow fine without it, and it was never part of their evolutionary history, yet it reliably executes its designed logic. I go back and forth on whether it has a biological function. And consider a trait that arises by pure genetic drift, remains neutral for millennia, and then becomes essential after a sudden environmental shift; once selection begins maintaining it, does it retroactively acquire the function it now serves?

So: is there a coherent account of function attribution—etiological, causal-role, design-based, or some hybrid—that captures all these verdicts without ad hoc patches? If not, which principle comes closest, what cases motivate each of its clauses, and what does it predict for the drift-then-essential trait and the engineered signal-processing circuit? Crucially, which of my verdicts—especially the horizontally transferred gene and the synthetic vesicle—must be dropped for the sake of simplicity, and is there any more complex principle that saves both at a cost worth paying?
