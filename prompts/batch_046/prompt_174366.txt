I am preparing a short essay tentatively titled “Ontological Multiplicity Without Relativism: Anekāntavāda and the Structure of Standpoints,” and I would appreciate your critical comments on the line of thought before I revise it for submission. The notes below are not polished prose but should convey the architecture of the argument.

1. The received interpretation of Jain anekāntavāda treats it as an epistemic doctrine of humility: because human cognition is finite and perspectival, we grasp only partial truths about objects that are in themselves complex. On this reading, syādvāda—“perhaps it is so”—expresses suspension of judgment rather than a positive metaphysical claim.

2. I want to resist that reading. I take anekāntavāda to be metaphysical: the world itself is anekānta, in that any object genuinely possesses contradictory properties in different respects (deśa-kāla). The seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī) is therefore not a taxonomy of ignorance but a logical articulation of how reality supports affirmation, negation, and inexpressibility in qualified combinations.

3. From this I draw a comparison with modern standpoint epistemology. Standpoint theory holds that knowledge is perspectival because social location structures what a knower can see. The Jain doctrine of naya also employs perspectives, but these are not socially indexed; they are ontologically indexed. Each naya isolates a real aspect of the object—substance (dravya) versus mode (paryāya), or persistence versus change—without reducing one to the other.

4. If this is right, anekāntavāda offers a form of value pluralism that is not relativistic. Moral disagreements about, say, the permissibility of harm for greater good may reflect not merely cultural construction but the fact that ethical reality, like physical reality, genuinely supports incompatible descriptions relative to different but equally valid standpoints.

5. The obvious objection is that this violates the law of non-contradiction. The standard Jain reply distinguishes absolute contradiction from qualified (syāt) predication: “X is P in respect R” and “X is not-P in respect R′” are jointly true provided R and R′ are not identical. I think this reply works, but I am unsure whether I am glossing over technical distinctions in classical Jain logic—particularly the precise conditions under which a seventh, “indescribable” (avaktavya) predication is invoked rather than a simple conjunction of affirmations and negations.

6. I want to conclude that anekāntavāda can ground pluralistic democratic deliberation without collapsing into “anything goes.” If conflicting values are rooted in the ontological complexity of the situations being evaluated, then tolerance is not merely strategic but cognitively appropriate: it acknowledges that no single naya exhausts the moral object.

My questions for you are these. First, is my distinction between the epistemic and the metaphysical readings of syādvāda defensible, or does it depend on an anachronistic separation that classical Jain texts do not support? Second, does comparing naya to standpoint epistemology illuminate the doctrine, or does it risk importing social-constructionist assumptions that are foreign to Jain ontology? Third, am I treating saptabhaṅgī with sufficient logical care for a philosophical audience, and if not, which constraint am I neglecting? Fourth, does the move from ontological multiplicity to non-relativist pluralism actually succeed, or have I merely renamed the problem of contradiction without solving it? I am especially interested in whether steps 3 and 4 contain a hidden slide from descriptive metaphysics to normative ethics, and if so, where exactly the slide occurs. Please comment on accuracy, coherence, and the parts you find most or least convincing.
