As a novelist constructing a redemption arc in which the protagonist gradually realizes that every consequential choice she made was anticipated and shaped by her mentor—an arrangement meant to echo, at the level of form, the author's own determination of plot—I am trying to decide whether compatibilism or incompatibilism provides the more coherent philosophical framework for rendering her moral agency believable. Please write an essay that weighs the two positions, but introduce and examine the specific problem of what might be called narrative determinism: the fact that fictional characters are literally determined by an author's prior outline. Does this make compatibilism trivially true within fiction, since the character's will is always aligned with the author's design? Or does it instead show that all fictional freedom is a kind of hierarchical illusion in the Frankfurt sense, thereby undermining the emotional reality of redemption? Reach a definite verdict—either that compatibilism is indispensable to sustaining believable character transformation, or that incompatibilism is required to preserve the tragic weight of genuinely unchosen consequences—and justify it by appealing to the internal logic of narrative agency rather than to scholarly authority. Do not conclude with an unresolved balance.
