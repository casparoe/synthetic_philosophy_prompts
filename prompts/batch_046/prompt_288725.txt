As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been working through the classic "white horse is not horse" reasoning linked to the School of Names, and I have reconstructed it in a way that seems to extend well past its original case. I cannot find the fault in the extension, and I want to know where it breaks. Here is the chain as I understand it:

(1) The term "horse" picks out entities by a specific form or shape, whereas "white horse" picks out entities by that same form plus the property of being white.
(2) If two terms apply to entities by different sets of conditions, then the class named by the first is not identical to the class named by the second.
(3) Therefore "horse" and "white horse" name different classes, so a white horse is not a member of the class "horse."
(4) The same reasoning applies to any kind and any further property: "fast horse" is not horse, "tall tree" is not tree, and in general a K-with-P is never a K.
(5) But every actual instance of any kind possesses further distinguishing properties—location, speed, shade, age—so every particular instance fails to satisfy the conditions for membership in its own universal kind.
(6) Hence taxonomy collapses entirely: no particular ever falls under any universal kind.
(7) The School of Names clearly did not intend to abolish classification altogether; they used these distinctions to refine naming practices, not to dissolve them.

This proves far too much. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially reluctant to give up (1) and (4), since they seem faithful to how adding conditions changes what a name applies to. If I must abandon one of them, I want to understand precisely what is lost. Is the mistake an equivocation between intension and extension in (2), a hidden modal slide between (3) and (5), or something else entirely? And if the argument is actually valid, which of the plausible premises is the least damaging to surrender?
