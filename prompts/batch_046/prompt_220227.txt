Below is a summary that appeared in a philosophy-blog post comparing the two positions. Read it carefully.

David Lewis's counterpart theory denies that any object exists in more than one world; instead, other worlds contain only counterparts—objects that resemble the original in important respects but are numerically distinct. So when we say I could have been taller, we mean there is a world where my counterpart is taller, not that I myself am taller there. By contrast, transworld-identity theorists like Alvin Plantinga maintain that one and the same individual literally exists across worlds, so modal claims are about that very person's properties in other situations. The summary claims that Lewis developed counterpart theory mainly to avoid the problem of an object having contradictory properties—like being both five feet and six feet tall—in different worlds. It also claims that Plantinga's view requires treating being possibly taller as a genuine property of me, which forces him to accept that I actually have that modal property in this world, making possibility into a kind of actual hidden feature. Finally, it concludes that counterpart theory is essentially a linguistic convention about how to translate modal sentences into talk about other worlds, while transworld identity captures the literal truth of our modal talk.
