I am currently taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, and we have been working through the early intuitionist writings of L.E.J. Brouwer. I have come across a reconstructed summary of an argument from his 1907 doctoral dissertation, which I will paraphrase here. Brouwer appears to claim that mathematics does not originate in logical axioms or in linguistic symbols, but rather in a primitive mental intuition of multiplicity—the direct experience of one thing followed by another. Mathematical objects are therefore constructed through free mental acts rather than discovered or defined by formal rules. Logical principles, including the law of excluded middle, are said to apply only to constructions that have already been completed, not to infinite or open-ended processes. Furthermore, language is described as an imperfect secondary instrument for communicating these mental constructions to others; it plays no constitutive role in mathematics itself.

I think I grasp the general anti-formalist point, but I am trying to understand how this specific 1907 position relates to a tension I see in later constructivist developments. In particular, Martin-Löf's intuitionistic type theory treats every proof as a syntactic object—a term constructed according to explicit introduction and elimination rules—so that mathematical meaning is inextricably bound to a formal language. Some interpreters read Brouwer's later notion of the creating subject, associated with choice sequences introduced after 1918, as an idealized mental agent, but the 1907 text seems focused on finite, immediately given constructions. My question is whether Brouwer's 1907 denial of language's constitutive role necessarily conflicts with proof-relevant formalization, or whether one can consistently maintain that formal notation is merely a heuristic description of underlying mental constructions without being constitutive of them. Is there a principled distinction, internal to Brouwer's view, between using formal symbols as communicative aids and allowing them to define the objects of mathematics?

I would appreciate help clarifying whether this tension is genuinely present in the 1907 argument or whether it arises only when we anachronistically project later formal frameworks onto it, and specifically how one should understand the relationship between the pre-linguistic mental construction described in 1907 and the syntactic, proof-object identity that characterizes modern intuitionistic type theory.
