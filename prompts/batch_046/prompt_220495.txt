I'm preparing my position for a graduate seminar on moral conflict and want an audit before I present. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I think genuine moral dilemmas are possible at the all-things-considered level: there are situations in which an agent is subject to two or more obligations that are equally weighty, non-derogable, and jointly unsatisfiable.

(2) I accept the principle that ought implies can, but I understand can in terms of the agent's total individual action-space rather than pairwise satisfiability—so it is possible to be obligated to do A and obligated to do B even when one cannot do both together.

(3) I hold that practical rationality is essentially unitary: there is always exactly one correct course of action, determined by the overall balance of reasons, and apparent conflicts indicate incomplete reasoning rather than irreducible pluralism.

(4) I believe appropriate moral residue—lingering guilt or regret after acting—is rational only if the agent has violated the uniquely correct obligation; if the conflict was genuinely symmetric, such residue amounts to irrational self-punishment.

(5) I think ethics should proceed by reflective equilibrium, and I see no principled barrier to reaching a unified equilibrium that assigns precise weights to all values, thereby dissolving apparent dilemmas into hidden priorities.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, specify its severity, which commitment has to give, and what the least costly revision would be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. Also flag any pair that dissolves on closer inspection. In your analysis, please separate what is settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view as if it were the consensus.
