Having retired from experimental physics after three decades in the laboratory and turned only recently to philosophy of history and the philosophy of social science, I am preparing a take-home essay examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar on conceptual problems in comparative explanation. I want one essay prompt, together with a model answer of roughly eight hundred words and a detailed marking scheme, focused on the conceptual status of the Great Divergence question as it is usually framed—specifically, why Britain industrialized rather than China, often rendered as the Needham problem. I do not want students to answer by reciting familiar narratives about coal deposits, colonial markets, or Confucian institutions. Instead, I want them to work with a specific conceptual distinction I have been developing in my own reading: the difference between an asymmetrical counterfactual baseline, in which one historical trajectory is implicitly treated as the default or expected path while the other is cast as a deviation that demands special causal accounting, and a symmetrical comparative baseline, in which both outcomes are treated as equally contingent and therefore equally in need of explanation. The exam question should ask students to apply this distinction to the divergence, to assess whether treating Britain as the baseline commits what might be called a baseline fallacy, to consider whether pre-modern China's ecological and institutional conditions genuinely made industrialization less probable in a manner that could justify the asymmetry, and to reflect on what this means for the legitimacy of large-scale counterfactual reasoning in macrohistory. The difficulty should come from the conceptual architecture, not from obscure archival knowledge: a student who has only memorized standard labels about divergence should fail, while one who understands the logical structure of comparative explanation should succeed. In the marking scheme, please distinguish explicitly the moves that earn top marks—such as recognizing the hidden normativity in selecting a baseline—from the mistakes that should reduce a grade, such as conflating causal priority with counterfactual asymmetry or treating the Needham framing as purely empirical rather than conceptually loaded. Because I intend to circulate this to colleagues who dislike formatted documents, please present the exam question, the model answer, and the marking scheme entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, and no tables—just flowing paragraphs arranged like a formal letter. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations, and avoid verbatim quotations from specific texts; paraphrase any ideas you reference. Write in polished American English, keep the tone formal, and ensure the prompt is answerable by a model without access to online sources or specialized recent monographs.
