I was sitting in on a graduate seminar discussion about the ontology of affect that spilled over into coffee afterward, and two of the participants—I'll call them Sam and Jordan—got into a sustained disagreement about whether emotions are essentially representational. Sam defended what she called a "value-perception" account: emotions are perceptual representations of evaluative properties (danger, offense, loss) that carry correctness conditions in the way visual experiences do, so that being angry at an innocent person is literally a misrepresentation analogous to a hallucination. Jordan defended a "somatic-attunement" account: an emotion is not a representation directed at an object but a global bodily state—patterns of autonomic arousal, postural readiness, and action-tendency—that structures which features of the environment become salient in the first place; on this view, anger does not represent an offense but puts the body in "opposition readiness," which then makes offenses stand out, and emotional "error" is a practical misfit (like wearing winter clothes in summer) rather than a false claim.

(1) Sam opened by arguing that without representational content we cannot make sense of emotional correction: therapy works precisely by revising the appraisal ("he didn't actually insult you"), and if anger contained no representational claim, rational persuasion should leave it untouched—yet in practice showing someone they have no reason for offense often dissolves the anger, which only makes sense if the emotion embodies a claim that can be undermined. (2) Jordan replied that this confuses therapeutic mechanism with emotional essence: change often succeeds through breathing and posture shifts that restructure interoceptive predictions, without any explicit belief revision, and purely cognitive "corrections" frequently fail precisely because the bodily attunement persists independently; persuasion works not by falsifying a mental representation but by altering the predicted state of readiness so that incoming signals no longer sustain it. (3) Sam countered that Jordan's view cannot explain object-directed emotional reasoning—if fear is just a bodily pattern of "flight readiness," then recognizing that the shadow is harmless should have no special bearing on the fear, yet recognizing harmlessness typically extinguishes fear specifically because fear represents the object as dangerous. (4) Jordan answered that recognizing harmlessness changes fear not by contradicting a propositional content but by updating the predictive model that generates the bodily state: once the brain predicts that flight-readiness is unnecessary, the attunement collapses, and this also explains objectless moods like free-floating anxiety, which lack any representational target but are clearly emotional modifications of overall affective tone—something Sam's perception model struggles to accommodate.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating; then say who is right about which specific point, what—if anything—both parties are missing, and whether moods without clear objects pose a decisive problem for the representational view. In particular, address whether emotional error is genuinely representational, whether therapeutic change requires belief revision, and how the predictive-model mechanism Jordan invokes relates to the correctness conditions Sam insists upon. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give your reasoning with no preamble and no restating of the question.
