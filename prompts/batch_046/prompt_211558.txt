Please explain the conceptual tension in Plato’s treatment of drunkenness between its diagnostic function and its distorting effect, framed specifically through his tripartite psychology and the charioteer image from the Phaedrus. In the Laws, controlled symposia are proposed as a kind of character assay: by lowering the soul’s guard, wine reveals whether the appetitive and spirited parts remain subordinate when rational vigilance is relaxed. In the Symposium, by contrast, Alcibiades delivers an intoxicated speech about Socrates that is emotionally raw and structurally disordered—simultaneously revelatory and unreliable. Your task is to determine, conceptually, whether Plato treats alcohol as a pharmacological suppressor of the rational charioteer that allows the black horse to speak truths otherwise concealed by sobriety’s self-censorship, or whether it merely disables rational governance so that false, appetitively driven opinions surface without epistemic authority. Address in particular whether this implies that a philosopher-ruler ought ever be subjected to such a test, given that the rational part—the very faculty that defines philosophical rule—is precisely what is being chemically subdued. Resolve whether Plato ultimately regards wine as a window onto the soul’s hierarchical order or as a solvent that dissolves it, and close your response with a one-sentence summary of your conclusion on the final line.
