Consider the following exam question from a course on moral psychology and political ethics:

In his work on the ethics of memory, Avishai Margalit distinguishes "thick" moral relations—bound by shared history, loyalty, and care—from "thin" relations governed by universal moral obligations. He argues that the duty to remember historical wrongs is primarily anchored in thick relations, though it can extend in modified form. Philip Rieff, in his critique of therapeutic culture, contends that modern societies have replaced traditional moral authority with psychological expertise, transforming remembrance into a managed, expert-driven process of "working through" trauma. Analyze the tension between Margalit's ethics of remembrance and Rieff's skepticism about therapeutic memory. Specifically, address: (1) whether Margalit's framework permits a collective duty to remember within "thin" political institutions (e.g., a state's official memorialization), or whether remembrance is strictly confined to thick communal bonds; (2) how Rieff's critique might apply to state-sponsored memorial practices that employ therapeutic language—such as "national healing" or "trauma-informed commemoration"; and (3) whether there is a coherent position that accepts Margalit's claim that remembrance is a moral obligation in thick relations while accepting Rieff's warning that institutionalized remembrance risks becoming social control, and if so, what conditions would distinguish legitimate institutional remembrance from its therapeutic distortion.

A student submitted the following answer:

Margalit clearly separates thick relations, like families and close-knit communities, from thin relations, such as those between citizens and strangers. In thick relations, remembering past wrongs is part of caring for the shared life of the group; in thin relations, we only have a minimal duty not to harm, not a duty to actively remember. Therefore, Margalit believes that state-level memorialization is morally illegitimate because states operate through thin relations.

Rieff, in his critique of therapeutic culture, argues that modern culture has made memory into a psychological task managed by experts. He thinks that when societies mandate remembrance—through museums, national days, or trauma counseling—they are exercising a kind of authoritarian control over individuals' inner lives. From this, Rieff concludes that the only ethical response is active forgetting: societies should suppress public memory of trauma to protect individual autonomy.

Putting them together, I think there is a middle ground. We can accept Margalit's point that thick communities must remember, but apply Rieff's critique only to state-sponsored therapeutic memorials. So a family remembering a lost member is legitimate, but a government running a "healing center" is not. This works because Margalit restricts remembrance to thick relations, and Rieff shows that thin institutions corrupt it. Thus the condition for legitimate remembrance is that it must occur within thick relations without expert mediation.
