I spent thirty years as a field ecologist before retiring, and lately I have been working through John Dewey’s writings on democracy and scientific inquiry. I am drafting an essay that tests whether Dewey’s idea of democracy as a form of collective experimental inquiry holds up when measured against practices I know well—specifically, adaptive ecosystem management in conservation biology. In Dewey’s view, democratic society should function like a scientific community: problems emerge from shared experience, hypotheses about remedies are proposed and tested through action, and conclusions are held only as warranted assertibilities subject to revision. I want to argue that adaptive management—where conservation policies are deliberately treated as experiments, monitored for ecological and social outcomes, and revised iteratively—offers a surprisingly precise institutional analogue to Dewey’s model. Yet I also want to press a tension that I think Dewey underplays. In actual adaptive management programs, the power to frame the problem, design the monitoring protocol, and define success often remains concentrated among scientific managers and funding bodies, even when local stakeholders are included as data sources or consultative voices. Dewey seems to assume that broadening participation in inquiry automatically dissolves hierarchical control over knowledge production; the ecology of adaptive management suggests otherwise, since participatory structures can still embed what we might call epistemic gatekeeping—where the terms of the experiment are set before democratic deliberation begins. I am not claiming Dewey was wrong about democracy, but rather that his experimentalism needs a supplementary account of how institutional power shapes the prior framing of what counts as a problem worth investigating.
