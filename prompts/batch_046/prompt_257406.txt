Construct a thought experiment, set in an ordinary non-experimental situation and confined to one paragraph, in which a subject’s background belief or conceptual framework causally influences what they report seeing, such that a theorist committed to the cognitive penetrability of perception is compelled to say the subject literally perceives a specific high-level property (for example, seeing a face as angry, seeing a tree as diseased, or seeing a gesture as threatening), whereas a theorist committed to the cognitive impenetrability of early vision—holding that modular perceptual processes deliver representations that are subsequently interpreted—is compelled to deny that the high-level property belongs to the perceptual content itself and must instead locate it in post-perceptual judgment, inference, or attentional selection, with both theorists agreeing on the behavioral report, the environmental stimulus, and the neural pathway activated. Constraints: the scenario must not involve ambiguous figure-ground illusions (such as the duck-rabbit), must not depend on expert-trained perceptual discrimination, must not invoke demons, perfect predictors, or exotic physics, and must be realistic enough that a normal person could encounter it. After describing the scenario, demonstrate that each theory delivers the specified verdict, identifying the precise structural feature—whether the top-down influence modifies the representational content of an early visual state or merely selects among pre-formed modular outputs—that forces the divergence. Then explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into a familiar disagreement about interpretation rather than about perceptual content? Finally, state how the impenetrability theorist would attack the case, specifically whether they can redescribe the influence as attention-mediated selection or expectation-driven hypothesis testing that leaves the underlying perceptual representation untouched.
