As a journalist covering philosophy and conceptual debates in the behavioral sciences, I am developing a feature for an intellectually serious general audience on the boundary between moral obligation and moral excellence. I need an essay that addresses the following tension with philosophical rigor but without unnecessary technical jargon. The standard view in ethics holds that supererogatory acts—such as enduring extreme risk to save strangers or making profound personal sacrifices for distant beneficiaries—are genuinely optional: they merit admiration, yet omission is not blameworthy. But consider a hypothetical "moral surplus" framework, proposed here for analysis rather than drawn from any named school, in which agents maintain a running internal record of obligations discharged versus discretionary capacity accumulated over a lifetime. If one takes this accounting metaphor literally, an agent with substantial accumulated surplus possesses unrealized moral resources that only she can deploy.

The essay should examine whether this model collapses supererogation into a form of long-range, defeasible obligation—whether rational consistency demands that significant surplus eventually be "spent" through heroic or saintly action—or whether the genuine optionality of such conduct survives even under rigorous moral bookkeeping. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, particularly whether preserving optionality makes moral merit conceptually arbitrary and whether imposing a spending requirement undermines the very idea of going above and beyond. Please reach a definite conclusion and justify it, rather than ending in equivocation. Write in formal, polished prose appropriate to a long-form magazine essay. At the close, state how confident you are in your verdict and single out the specific point in your argument about which you are least certain.
