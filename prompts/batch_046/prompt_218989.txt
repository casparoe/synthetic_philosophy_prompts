I'm trying to determine what I actually think about judging and publicly honoring historical figures who achieved things widely considered great while also committing grave moral wrongs, especially where the defence that they were men of their time might apply. Here are my gut verdicts, which I want to reconcile if possible.

Thomas Jefferson: He authored foundational democratic texts and was a principal architect of American political institutions, yet he enslaved hundreds of people and wrote that Black people were inferior. I think his slaveholding was not a private moral blind spot but structurally integrated into his economic and social life, and he had access to abolitionist arguments that he actively suppressed. Verdict: We should continue to study and teach his political philosophy, but public monuments that honor him as a hero should be removed or radically recontextualized; honoring him without that framing symbolically endorses slavery.

Winston Churchill: His leadership in 1940-45 was decisive in resisting fascism, which I regard as a genuine and enormous moral achievement. At the same time, he was a committed imperialist, advocated chemical weapons in Mesopotamia, and his policies contributed to the 1943 Bengal famine. I think the wartime achievement is not morally canceled by the imperial harms, but neither does it cancel them. For his general imperial attitudes, I am somewhat sympathetic to the defence that he was a man of his time—those views were widespread among British elites around 1900. For the Bengal famine, however, I think that defence fails: he made specific policy choices in 1943 with direct knowledge of the conditions. Verdict: He should be recognized specifically for wartime leadership, but not celebrated as an unqualified national hero; the imperial and famine records should be taught as integral to his legacy, not footnotes.

Christopher Columbus: His voyages initiated sustained transatlantic contact, with enormous historical consequences, but they also initiated centuries of genocide, enslavement, and ecological devastation in the Americas. I think he was exceptionally brutal even by the standards of 1492—there is evidence he knew indigenous people were human and capable of Christian conversion, yet he still authorized enslavement and mutilation. At the same time, I feel the defence that he was a man of his time has more force for him than for Jefferson, because the moral framework recognizing full indigenous rights was genuinely less developed in late-fifteenth-century Europe than abolitionism was in late-eighteenth-century Virginia. Verdict: He should not be honored with public holidays or monuments; the harm was too direct and too intended, even if partly shaped by his era.

The tension: I seem to apply epistemic culpability selectively. Jefferson gets no excuse despite abolitionist voices being present; Columbus gets partial excuse despite arguments like those of Las Casas being available to him; Churchill gets partial excuse for general imperialism but none for the famine. I have been trying to organize this around three dimensions—structural entanglement (was the wrong a necessary means to the good?), epistemic culpability (did they have accessible moral knowledge they ignored?), and symbolic endorsement (does honoring them now imply approval of the wrong?). But my verdicts do not map cleanly onto them. For instance, Jefferson's wrong was not structurally necessary for the Declaration, yet I judge him harshly; Churchill's imperialism was structurally linked to Britain's global power, yet I partly excuse it; Columbus's contact was not structurally necessary for genocide, yet I partly excuse him on epistemic grounds.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a weighted combination of these dimensions, or a different one altogether—that captures all three verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? Or must I drop one?
