As a novelist drafting a narrative that moves between Parisian banlieues and British cities, I have come to hold a position that runs against the usual cultural framing: I take republican laïcité—not despite but precisely because of its refusal to encode group identity into public law—to be more genuinely universalist and protective of minorities than Anglo-American multiculturalism, which I suspect operates with a reified ontology of culture that risks ghettoizing the very communities it claims to empower. I want an honest map of where this view is exposed. What specific empirical patterns—educational attainment trajectories, labor-market integration, intermarriage dynamics, or patterns of political representation—would decisively undermine the claim that procedural neutrality produces better minority outcomes than group-differentiated recognition? Which philosophical objections are fatal rather than merely awkward: for instance, that laïcité masks a substantive secularism that privileges non-religious majorities; that formal equality denies the recognition necessary for democratic self-respect; or that cultural identity is not a private preference but a structural feature of agency that neutrality systematically erases? I also want to know what defenders of laïcité themselves would concede counts against their position—cases where formal neutrality clearly produces substantive exclusion, or where multicultural recognition demonstrably prevents social fragmentation—rather than only what critics say. Is there a live vulnerability that risks collapsing the argument into a mere defense of majority culture, or is the position well-insulated in ways that should themselves be judged a philosophical weakness? I am writing to understand the conceptual architecture, not to collect citations.
