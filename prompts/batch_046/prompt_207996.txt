I was recently appointed to a hospital ethics committee charged with revising our triage protocols, and in that context I have come to accept a modified version of Taurek’s view that the sheer number of potential victims does not generate a stronger moral reason to save the larger group—provided the rescuer acts in a purely personal capacity. I hold, however, that this non-aggregation principle is suspended when the decision-maker occupies an institutional role carrying an explicit universal fiduciary duty, such as a public-health authority distributing scarce vaccines across a population. I want an honest assessment of what would genuinely count against this hybrid position. Which kinds of cases, formal results in moral mathematics, or intuitions about role morality would have to fail for the view to collapse? Would empirical evidence showing that ordinary moral psychology draws no stable boundary between personal rescue and institutional allocation be fatal, or merely awkward? How should the view respond to counterexamples in which agents within institutional roles appear permitted to exercise partiality—say, a physician prioritizing her own child during a pandemic triage—without that undermining the institutional exception itself? And if aggregationist critics can demonstrate that the very concept of a universal fiduciary duty covertly presupposes the equal counting of all lives affected, is that an internal contradiction or only a definitional dispute? I would like the defeaters ranked from decisive to absorbable, with clear indication of which ones defenders of this position should concede as genuinely damaging and which they could reasonably dismiss as question-begging.
