I have noticed a recurring pattern in how value is assigned to artworks and historical artifacts that seems to resist simple explanation, and I am trying to determine whether philosophy of art or aesthetics has already named and mapped it with precision.

Consider three situations. First, there is the classic undetectable forgery: a painting is produced with the same materials, pigments, and brushwork techniques as a canonical original, expert analysis confirms physical identity in every observable respect, and viewers consistently report the same aesthetic experience; yet once the work's true causal origin—production by a different hand with no connection to the original artist—is revealed, its institutional and market value collapses entirely, while the original remains unaffected despite no change in anyone's perceptual access. Second, there is the authorized but posthumous extension: a bronze is cast from the artist's original mold after his death, using identical alloy and finishing processes, producing a form that is not a copy of an existing sculpture but a legitimate continuation of his intended design; nevertheless, such a cast is typically valued far below one that was supervised and signed during the artist's lifetime, even when the physical difference is imperceptible. Third, there is a less discussed reversal: an authentic work is miscatalogued for decades as a studio copy or forgery, displayed in minor collections and studied primarily as an example of imitation technique; when scholarship eventually restores its correct authorship, its price and prestige rise sharply, but part of that elevation seems to come not simply from the corrected label. Rather, the period of misattribution itself becomes incorporated into the object's biography—collectors and institutions speak of its passage through obscurity as if the narrative of having been underestimated adds a distinct layer of significance that a never-doubted original lacks.

What interests me is that the value mapping here is discontinuous and non-monotonic with respect to causal history. It is not reducible to the binary property of being touched by the artist's hand, since some genuinely authorized works (late replicas, posthumous casts) are valued differently from canonical originals. Nor is it reducible to any observable feature of the object itself, since the forgery and the authentic work share all of them. At the same time, the reversal case demonstrates that being believed false can, upon correction, become a positive feature rather than a neutral erased error.

Is there an established concept—perhaps in discussions of authenticity, provenance, or what some have called the aura of an original—that captures this specific interaction between causal origin, institutional belief, and narrative biography? More specifically, can the different mechanisms be separated: the problem of perceptual identity between original and copy; the problem of authority conferred by direct artistic supervision; and the problem of value accretion through a history of false attribution? Or do these collapse into a single notion of authenticity? If no existing term covers the cluster precisely, how might one define the boundaries between these mechanisms and coin a term for the non-linear valuation pattern they jointly produce, especially the reversal phenomenon where misattribution history becomes part of an object's valued identity?
