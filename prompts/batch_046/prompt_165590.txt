hey so i'm drafting an essay on Friedman's F-twist and i think i've hit on a distinction that might be useful but i'm probably overreaching. Friedman basically says unrealistic assumptions are fine if the predictions hold, and people usually take that to mean all simplifications are okay. But I want to split unrealistic assumptions into two kinds: shortcut-idealizations, where you drop a factor that doesn't change the causal structure (like ignoring air resistance for a falling rock), and what i'm calling mechanism-cancelling idealizations, where the unrealistic part actually removes the very process you're trying to study. I made that term up, by the way.

The example i'm using is agent-based macro models that assume perfect rationality but then claim to explain bubbles caused by irrational herding. If rationality is built in, the herding mechansim isn't just simplified — it's structurally absent, and any matching prediction is basically an accident of calibratoin. I think Friedman misses this because he only cares about output accuracy, not whether the model's internal causal story is contradicted by its own premises. I also mention climate models parameterizing cloud microphysics unrealistically but still getting global temperature trends right; maybe that's a shortcut, not mechanism-cancelling, since the macro trend doesn't depend on individual cloud dynamics? Or maybe it is mechanism-cancelling for regional rainfall predictions?
