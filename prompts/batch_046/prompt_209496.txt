Berkeley’s central point in The Querist is that gold and silver have no intrinsic economic worth; they function only as a ticket standing for labor and commodities already produced. This is perfectly consistent with his immaterialism, since material substance does not exist and gold is therefore nothing more than an idea sustained in perception. From this it follows that paper currency is literally an idea shared among minds, and that if every physical token were destroyed, the underlying value—being purely mental—would survive untouched. His position is essentially Locke’s labor-mixing account translated into exchange: value is created when individuals mix labor with nature, and money merely records that prior mixing. Berkeley avoids reducing value to pure subjectivity only by appealing to divine ordinance, which means God directly implants the correct idea of price into each human spirit, eliminating any need for social convention or public agreement about currency. The chartalist reading is wrong because Berkeley denies any social or state role in money altogether; value is fixed individually by mental states.
