I was discussing Aquinas's account of tyrannicide with two friends in my study group for a course on medieval political philosophy, and we reached an impasse that seems to turn on a real ambiguity in his view rather than on mere confusion about the text. One of them, Marcus, insisted that Aquinas permits the killing of a tyrant only when two strict conditions are met: the ruler must have seized power without any legitimate title, and the act must be carried out by a recognized public authority rather than by a private individual acting on his own judgment. From this, Marcus concluded that if a ruler was legitimately appointed—whether by election, hereditary right, or some other recognized procedure—but later governs in a tyrannical manner, private citizens have no right to resist by violence and must instead endure or seek lawful redress, since in such a case there is no superior magistrate with the jurisdiction to authorize deposition.

The other friend, Elena, agreed that Aquinas warns sharply against private individuals taking matters into their own hands, but she argued that Marcus was misreading the basis of that restriction. She claimed that for Aquinas the decisive issue is not merely how the ruler originally acquired office, but whether the authority he now exercises still serves the common good in a way that preserves the social order ordained by natural law. Elena pointed out that if a legitimate ruler systematically destroys the constitutional structures through which authority is mediated—dissolving representative councils, corrupting judges, and ruling solely through fear—then the obligation to obey is dissolved not only for private persons but for the corporate body of citizens, and any remaining magistrates who retain a genuine jurisdictional role may act to depose him. Marcus responded that Elena was importing later resistance theories into Aquinas, noting that Aquinas explicitly fears the anarchy that follows when individuals judge for themselves whether authority has been lost; unless there is a clearly superior power—such as a higher court or the Church—there is no "public authority" left to authorize resistance, and Elena's talk of a "corporate body" recovering authority sounds like a modern democratic idea alien to the medieval framework.

To press the disagreement, Elena proposed a specific hypothetical case that we had not encountered in any standard commentary: imagine a city-state governed by a dual council—half composed of nobles and half of elected guild representatives—that jointly appoints a magistrate for life. The magistrate, after legitimate appointment, dissolves the guild council but retains the loyalty of the noble half, then rules oppressively through that remaining faction. Elena asked whether, on Aquinas's principles, the remaining nobles count as the "public authority" entitled to remove him, or whether the destruction of half the constitutional machinery means that authority has reverted to the people as a whole, including those now unrepresented, such that even collective private resistance might be justified. Marcus maintained that without an intact constitutional order there is simply no legitimate public authority left, which means no one—neither nobles nor commoners—can licitly kill the tyrant, leaving only endurance, flight, or prayer. I am not asking for a bibliography, a list of readings, or citations; I want a point-by-point adjudication of who is right about what in this exchange, what both are missing about Aquinas's actual argument, and how his framework applies to Elena's dual-council hypothetical. Please present your answer entirely as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if you were writing a single letter in response.
