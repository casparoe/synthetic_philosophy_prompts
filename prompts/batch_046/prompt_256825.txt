Analyze the 2006 International Astronomical Union vote that reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet, treating the episode as a case study in conceptual slippage within a scientific microdomain—specifically, the interaction between copycat analogical extension and fluid boundary conditions. Rather than treating "planet" as a natural-kind term that was simply corrected, examine how the concept was originally extended to Pluto in 1930 through copycat projection: mapping properties of the eight classical planets—roundness, orbital dominance, gravitational centrality—onto a body that only partially satisfied them. Trace how this analogical stretch accumulated contradictory predicates over seventy years: Pluto's small mass, highly elliptical orbit, resonant relationship with Neptune, and eventual contextualization within the Kuiper Belt. Then turn to the 2006 resolution: did the IAU's three-part definitional criterion (orbiting the Sun, hydrostatic equilibrium, clearing the neighborhood) eliminate the fluidity, or did it relocate slippage to new boundary zones—such as the ambiguous status of Ceres, Eris, and bodies in other solar systems? In particular, evaluate whether the "dwarf planet" category functions as a stable intermediate kind or as a conceptual holding pen that exposes the underlying fluidity of "planet" itself. What does the Pluto episode demonstrate about whether explicit definitional criteria can discipline copycat reasoning, or whether fluid concepts necessarily generate residual indeterminacy at the margins? And running the analysis in reverse: does this case support a general claim that all scientific taxonomies involve unavoidable conceptual slippage, or does it instead show that some domains can achieve durable definitional closure once analogical overextension is corrected?
