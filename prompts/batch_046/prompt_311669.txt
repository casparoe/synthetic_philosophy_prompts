Consider the following paraphrase of Newton's reasoning about the rotating pail of water, drawn from the Scholium to the Definitions in the Principia (1687): initially the vessel turns about its axis while the water remains flat and at rest; gradually, as the motion is communicated, the water also revolves and climbs the sides, becoming concave with a higher circumference. Now this concavity is present whether the pail is turned relative to the distant stars or the stars are turned relative to the pail, since in either case the local relation between water and vessel is identical; yet the effect—the ascent—occurs in one situation and not in the other. Therefore, Newton concludes, the motion producing the effect is not merely relative but true and absolute, and there must exist an absolute space, independent of bodies, with respect to which true motion alone can be defined.

I want to understand whether this inference succeeds only on a suppressed premise that dynamical effects must be locally mediated between immediately adjacent bodies. If a relationist denies that premise and holds instead that true rotation is defined globally, with respect to the total distribution of matter in the universe—so that turning the pail relative to the distant masses is not dynamically equivalent to turning those masses relative to the pail—can she concede that the concavity reveals a real, non-relative acceleration without thereby accepting an absolute spatial container? In particular, could she account for the asymmetry by appealing to a global relational structure that fixes inertial frames through the collective influence of distant matter, rather than through a substantival space? Explain where Newton's argument genuinely forces the substantivalist conclusion and where it might only refute a locally restricted form of relationism.
