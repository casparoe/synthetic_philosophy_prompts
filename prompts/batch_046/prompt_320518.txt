As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing a long-form feature on how care ethics handles labor that is structurally invisible—work that demands emotional availability while denying the worker full relational standing. For this, I keep returning to Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day, in both novel and Merchant-Ivory film form. The narrative centers on Stevens, a butler who has organized his entire moral life around an ideal of professional service, suppressing grief for his father and affection for Miss Kenton in order to maintain what he calls dignity in service. Rather than treating Stevens as a simple case of work-life imbalance, I want to read him as a practitioner of institutional care: someone whose responsiveness to Lord Darlington’s needs is genuine, disciplined, and almost parental, yet systematically extracted from the reciprocal relationships that care ethics typically takes as its normative core.

The philosophical tension I want to explore sits at the intersection of dependency, class, and emotional labor. Stevens cares about the household with an intensity that borders on devotion, yet he treats Miss Kenton’s emotional labor and his father’s decline as disruptions to be managed rather than relationships to be sustained. Where does care ethics illuminate the moral cost of this arrangement, and where does it struggle—perhaps because it celebrates responsiveness and particularity without fully theorizing the damage done to a caregiver when responsiveness is compelled by wage dependency and professional ideology? I am especially interested in whether the distinction between caring for and caring about collapses in Stevens’s case, or whether it reveals a blind spot: that care ethics may not have adequate resources to distinguish healthy dependency from the colonization of personal attachment by institutional duty.

I am discussing this with a reading group that includes sociologists of labor, and I want a reading that takes Ishiguro’s form seriously—his unreliable narration, the layered temporality of the road trip, the way empty English landscapes mirror Stevens’s emotional architecture—without reducing the novel to an illustration of either virtue or vice. Does the work suggest that institutional care inevitably displaces relational care, or does it show something argument alone misses: that Stevens’s tragedy is not a failure to choose love over duty, but a learned inability to perceive love as anything other than unprofessional care? I would value a critique that moves in both directions—faithful to the philosophical idea and to the work’s resistance to being read as philosophy in costume—and that ends by saying what the novel contributes to the debate that pure conceptual analysis does not.
