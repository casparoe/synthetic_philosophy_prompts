I have recently found myself reflecting on rapid institutional change in my own political environment, and this has drawn me back to a long-standing historiographical problem. For decades, interpretations of the French Revolution have divided into three broad camps: those that treat the Revolution as driven by underlying class struggle and the rise of bourgeois social relations; those that emphasize ideology, political discourse, and the autonomous power of revolutionary language; and those that stress contingency, local particularity, and the unpredictable escalation of violence, treating macro-narratives as post-hoc constructions. Despite extensive engagement, the disagreement appears stalled. Class-struggle historians accuse revisionists of ignoring material interests; revisionists reply that class categories are anachronistic projections onto a society that did not experience itself in those terms; contingency-focused scholars argue that both sides impose false coherence on a chaotic sequence of events. The related question of whether revolutions necessarily turn against their own architects—whether the Terror was structurally inevitable or a contingent derailment—remains equally unresolved, with each camp treating the outcome as confirmation of its framework.

My working hypothesis is that this is not mainly a factual dispute about events between 1789 and 1794, but a deeper disagreement about what counts as a legitimate explanatory target: macro-social transformation, discursive formation, or event-level causation. Before I accept that diagnosis, I want to examine the actual arguments each side makes. Is the impasse substantive, verbal, or a case of different disciplines—social history, intellectual history, microhistory—answering different questions under the single heading of interpreting the Revolution? Do the sides differ about evidence, about standards of causal explanation, about which cases count as data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding structural determination versus human agency? And given your diagnosis, what would specifically count as progress: an empirical discovery, a conceptual distinction that clarifies the explanatory targets, a formal model of revolutionary escalation, or a reconceptualization of the question itself? Or is the disagreement of a kind that should not be expected to resolve into a single interpretation?
