Consider the judgments my colleague, Dr. Amara Okonkwo, makes in her graduate seminar on virtue and situation. She has ruled on whether various individuals possess specific virtues based solely on the behavioral patterns described below.

First, a trauma surgeon who remains composed and decisive in the operating room during catastrophic hemorrhages, but who avoids all physical risk outside work and admits she feels no particular emotional concern for her patients—only for the technical problem-solving. Okonkwo says she possesses the virtue of surgical courage but not compassion, and that these are genuinely separate achievements.

Second, a primary-school teacher who spends unpaid hours with struggling students and is unfailingly patient in the classroom, yet is chronically dismissive and irritable with her own family. Okonkwo says she lacks the virtue of patience altogether; true patience is not compartmentalized by social role.

Third, a community organizer who anonymously donates a substantial portion of her income for two decades, but explicitly states she does so solely to maintain her social reputation and avoid guilt, with no concern for recipients. Okonkwo says she possesses the virtue of generosity; the internal motivation is appropriate within her social framework and need not involve emotional identification.

Fourth, a senior diplomat who refuses to lie in official negotiations even when deception would advance her nation's interests, but who routinely deceives her spouse about trivial domestic matters. Okonkwo says she possesses the virtue of public honesty, though this reveals an incomplete integration of character.

Now consider a fifth case: a lead researcher in AI safety who repeatedly publishes accurate risk assessments, refuses lucrative industry positions that would require her to soften her conclusions, and warns regulators about potential harms—behavior that costs her professionally—yet lives in extreme luxury funded by family wealth, never donates to relevant causes, admits she feels no emotional concern for either human or synthetic welfare, and says she is motivated entirely by intellectual curiosity and the desire to be remembered as a prescient voice. Would Okonkwo say she possesses the virtue of intellectual integrity, or civic courage, or neither?

I am less interested in a simple yes or no than in the rule she seems to be applying, at least two other rules that fit her four earlier verdicts equally well, and what each predicts about the researcher. Give your best-guess verdict with the reasoning that distinguishes the rules, and then specify one further case—ideally one that involves moral behavior in a virtual or algorithmically mediated context—that would best discriminate between them.
