In discussions of the Hart–Fuller debate, I observe a recurring stall: once interlocutors move past the canonical post-war informer statute, the disagreement rarely turns on new examples or formal results, but seems to collapse into a dispute about the logical form of legal validity itself. Fuller’s camp often treats minimal moral decency as a scalar condition—something a legal system can satisfy to greater or lesser degrees—whereas Hart’s defenders treat validity as a binary status fixed by social acceptance rules, with morality entering only contingently. This raises the possibility that part of the impasse comes from conflating a thesis about necessary conceptual connections with a hidden disagreement about whether legal concepts are threshold or gradable predicates, analogous to debates about whether terms like “bald” admit of degrees. Is this diagnosis correct? Is the disagreement really about law and morality, about the burden of proof for necessary connections, or about the scalar structure of validity? And if the stall stems from this conflation, what distinction, reframing, or kind of example could actually move the debate forward—if any can?
