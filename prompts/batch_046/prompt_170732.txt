So I retired from a condensed-matter lab about three years ago—mostly fixing instruments and arguing about calibration errors—and lately I've been reading philosophy to avoid fixing the same leaky faucet every weekend. I got stuck on anthropic reasoning, specifically self-sampling versus self-indication, and I built this chain that feels like it should collapse somewhere but I keep walking through it without finding the door.

(1) The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) says I should treat my observer-moment as randomly drawn from a referance class of actual observers. (2) The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) says my existence is itself evidence for worlds with more observers, since I'm more likely to exist in them—this leads to the presumptuous philosopher problem, where you seem able to conclude the universe is enormous just because you're here. (3) To avoid that presumption, I stick with pure SSA. (4) But then I have to define my reference class without circularity: I can't just restrict it to "all humans in 2024" because I'm using the very fact I'm human and alive now to limit the sample. (5) So I broaden it to something like "all conscious biological entities on Earth with comparable cognitive complexity." (6) The trouble is, if bacteria or even nematodes have minimal sentience, they outnumber humans by something like 10^20 to 1, so finding myself human is like drawing one named grain from a beach—astronomically unlikely unless my theory already predicts human dominance, which is circular. (7) Okay, maybe I restrict by "capable of asking this philosophical question," but that just describes me again. (8) Now apply the same logic across time: if humanity survives and colonizes the galaxy, there could be 10^30 potentional future observer-moments. (9) So finding myself in 2024 should feel like being born in 3000 BCE and wondering why I'm not living in 2500 CE—SSA should make me near-certain I'm a distant future post-human. (10) Since I'm clearly not, SSA forces the Doomsday conclusion that extinction is near. (11) But here's the loop: if extinction is near, those 10^30 future observers never exist, which removes the statistical pressure that generated the Doomsday argument in step (9) to begin with. (12) Meanwhile SIA avoids that circularity but falls right back into the presumptuous philosopher trap—expecting I'm in the largest possible civilization, or even a Boltzmann brain in an eternal heat-death universe. (13) So both assumptions seem to generate self-refuting or wildly presumptuous conclusions when pushed consistently. Where does this actually break? Is the circularity charge in step (4) wrong—maybe restricting the reference class isn't circular but just conditioning on my evidence? Or is the mistake in (8), assuming future potentional observers belong in my present reference class? Or is there a hidden slide between "observer" as a physical system and "observer-moment" that lets the argument keep going? I'd like to keep (3) if I can, but I want to know what giving it up costs me.
