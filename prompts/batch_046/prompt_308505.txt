Construct a taxonomy—presented as a branching tree with labeled internal nodes—of philosophical positions on moral responsibility for harm caused by autonomous decision-making systems (e.g., adaptive algorithms, autonomous vehicles, or distributed robotic networks). Build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of named theorists: each internal node should pose a concrete diagnostic question that positions genuinely split on, and each leaf should be a coherent combination of answers.

Fix the root question: Is there any moral responsibility left for human agents once a system operates with genuine autonomy?

Then branch on the following four distinctions, ordered so that the tree remains readable and mostly binary:
1. Does responsibility require counterfactual control over the specific harmful act, or is design-stage foreseeability of the harm-type sufficient?
2. Must there be an ongoing, assignable duty to monitor, update, or intervene—failure of which grounds responsibility?
3. Should we recognize a non-human “system-level” bearer of accountability—not full moral agency, but a distinct locus of answerability?
4. If human responsibility is diluted across many actors (designers, deployers, users, regulators), does that dilution eliminate responsibility altogether or merely distribute it?

Each leaf should carry either a recognized label (e.g., the responsibility gap, distributed accountability, monitoring-duty liability) or the tag unnamed, followed by one sentence describing its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. Identify any two leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate—positions that share all four answers but differ only in emphasis or rhetorical framing. Also flag any logically possible combination of answers that remains unoccupied: briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or mere neglect in the literature.

Fix the audience as philosophy graduate students with basic normative ethics but no specialized background in AI governance, and limit the tree to six to eight leaves. The purpose is pedagogical: to help students locate their own intuitions about concrete cases—such as an adaptive medical-dosing algorithm that harms a patient, or an autonomous logistics network that causes a fatal collision—without asking them to recognize author names in advance. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
