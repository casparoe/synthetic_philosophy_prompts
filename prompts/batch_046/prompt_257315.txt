The student's answer reads as follows:

Nāgārjuna's catuskoti sets out four exhaustive positions regarding any phenomenon: that it exists, that it does not exist, that it both exists and does not exist, and that it neither exists nor does not exist. In his central treatise, he applies this schema to svabhāva, arguing that inherent existence is incompatible with dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda): if something existed by its own nature, it could not arise from conditions. Since all phenomena are dependently arisen, none possess svabhāva, and Nāgārjuna therefore rejects all four corners with respect to inherent nature.

Turning to the causal power: conventionally (saṃvṛti-satya), we may treat it as functioning in causal relations, which provisionally validates the first corner. Ultimately (paramārtha-satya), however, it lacks svabhāva and is empty. The correct Madhyamaka view is therefore that the power both exists conventionally and is empty ultimately, which corresponds to occupying the first and third corners within a two-truths framework. Moreover, since emptiness is itself empty, Nāgārjuna must reject the assertion that all things lack inherent existence as ultimately true; this shows that he actually affirms the fourth corner—neither existence nor non-existence—for emptiness itself. Consequently, the irreducible causal power is neither ultimately present nor absent, though it operates conventionally.
