Before I file my overview of responses to Benacerraf’s dilemma, I want the specification sheet—not a survey of answers, but the criteria any adequate resolution must satisfy. Write the full list of desiderata that a theory dissolving the tension between a causal condition on knowledge and the apparent acausality of mathematical objects must meet. For each requirement, state the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it: how the theory must treat ordinary arithmetical knowledge versus knowledge of physical objects; whether it must preserve a distinction between genuinely knowing that a theorem holds and merely being reliably disposed to assert it; and, specifically, how it should account for mathematical error and correction—say, the discovery of a hidden circularity in an apparently valid proof—when there is no sensory feedback loop that could mediate the revision. Identify which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences, for instance those that insist on a uniform causal epistemology across all domains versus those that permit domain-specific epistemologies. Flag any pair of requirements that look jointly unsatisfiable given standard constraints on causal theories or on ontological parsimony. Then tell me which single desideratum does the most work in eliminating the largest family of existing proposals, and briefly—without bibliography, citation, or named texts—how a platonist, a nominalist, and a structuralist each fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their interrelations rather than on scoring philosophers; the aim is to see whether the list itself forces a particular shape of answer, especially regarding whether mathematical knowledge requires representational contact with objects or can be grounded in structural tracking alone. No reading lists, please—I am writing for an audience that will not have the literature at hand.
