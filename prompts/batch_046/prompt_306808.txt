Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi—about the Nyāya theory of knowledge (pramāṇa-śāstra). Use exactly these six end-words, rotated through the lines in the standard sestina pattern: perception, inference, testimony, analogy, error, certitude. The first four stanzas should each center on one of the four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (analogy), and śabda (testimony)—while the fifth and sixth stanzas should treat the corresponding pramāṇa-ābhāsa, the fallacious appearances specific to each source. Structure the rotation so that the formal movement enacts logical dependency: inference should rely on perception for its premises, analogy should rely on both prior perception and testimony, and testimony should stand irreducible rather than reducible to inference. The envoi should pair error with certitude in its final lines to capture the Nyāya claim that recognizing a fallacious appearance is not an external failure but internal to the refinement and achievement of valid cognition. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with Nyāya epistemology recognizes the distinctions in the form itself, yet readable as verse without specialist knowledge. Do not include bibliographic references, scholar names, or citations; do not resolve the tension into either a naive realist defense or a skeptical dissolution—leave the interdependence and vulnerability of the sources structurally present in the rhyme and rotation.
