As a retired quantitative biologist now engaged with philosophy of science, I have developed a set of intuitions about the interpretation of heritability coefficients that I fear may not be consistent. I present them below, together with the cases that motivate them, and ask for the underlying principle—if any—that unifies them, together with an honest assessment of which verdict must be sacrificed.

First, when a cohort study reports a narrow-sense heritability of educational attainment near 0.40 and a policy analyst concludes that educational interventions are therefore largely futile, I judge this a clear category error. The coefficient partitions variance in a specific population; it does not set a ceiling on the efficacy of environmental change.

Second, if the same coefficient falls to near 0.20 in a society with radically more uniform schooling, I do not interpret this as showing that genes have become causally weaker. Rather, I take it to reflect the arithmetic fact that environmental variance has shrunk, altering the denominator of the ratio. This strikes me as a correct conceptual point, not a misuse.

Third, imagine a pharmacological agent that suppresses a metabolic pathway uniformly across all genotypes, reducing phenotypic variance without changing genetic variance. The heritability coefficient consequently rises. I accept the mathematics, yet I regard any inference that genes now explain a larger share of individual outcomes as philosophically misleading.

Fourth, in a twin study estimating aggression heritability near 0.50, a family member asks whether this implies that half of their own aggressive tendencies are genetically caused. I consider this interpretation categorically mistaken, though I find it difficult to articulate the error without merely restating that heritability is a population-level statistic.

Fifth, I am skeptical of a philosopher who argues that heritability estimates are therefore causally useless, precisely because they depend on the range of environments sampled. I believe they encode genuine information about the causal architecture of a trait in a given context—just not the information lay readers typically extract.

Sixth, and most puzzling, consider substantial gene-environment covariance: parents with high genetic predispositions for cognition also construct enriched home environments. The statistical heritability absorbs this covariance into the genetic term. A methodologist proposes reporting a counterfactual heritability that removes this covariance, representing the variance partition that would obtain under random environmental assignment. I am genuinely unsure whether this purified figure is philosophically more fundamental, or whether it excises a real feature of how genetic differences actually operate within human societies.

These verdicts leave me in tension. I resist interpreting coefficient shifts as shifts in causal importance in some contexts but invoke conceptual misinterpretation in others. I reject individual-level attribution, yet I defend the causal relevance of the population-level figure. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps concerning variance partitioning versus counterfactual causal attribution, or population-level description versus individual-level explanation, or the distinction between statistical heritability and reaction-norm stability across environments—that captures all six? If not, which of my judgments should be overturned?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate the specific clause that handles each case above, identify the case that most clearly threatens coherence, and apply the principle to an unconsidered scenario: a trait whose heritability is near zero in one culture and near 0.80 in another, where the difference arises not from any change in genetic variance but solely from cultural norms that suppress or allow phenotypic expression.
