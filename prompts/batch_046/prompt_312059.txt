I'm drafting the final take-home exam for my upper-level seminar in applied moral philosophy—mostly students with philosophy and quantitative social-science backgrounds, the kind of audience you'd find in rationalist forums debating animal-welfare metrics with Fermi-estimate precision. We've covered wild-animal suffering, feed-conversion ratios, and indirect harm, but I want a question that can't be answered by reciting the standard "chicken is worse because of headcount" summary that's circulated in popular write-ups. I need the exam to force students to build a comparison from scratch, using only round-number premises they should be able to hold in working memory (e.g., broiler lifespan ~6 weeks, beef-cattle feedlot life ~18 months, feed-conversion ratios roughly 6:1 for chicken and 25:1 for beef, and the fact that corn and soy cultivation kills rodents, insects, and birds via planting, harvesting, and pesticides). The question should require them to make explicit quantitative assumptions, then confront a specific philosophical crux: whether suffering that is instrumentally caused by growing feed should be weighted differently from suffering that is constitutive of the production process (the feedlot conditions themselves), and whether duration-weighted intensity of suffering matters more than raw headcount.

Write one essay prompt suitable for 90 minutes and approximately 1,000 words, worth 20 marks total. The prompt should ask students to compare beef and chicken on these grounds and to take a committed position—defending either beef or chicken as ethically worse—rather than laying out both sides neutrally and leaving the choice open. Please provide the exam question itself, then a model answer of roughly 700 words that takes a definite side, constructs the quantitative comparison from explicit premises, addresses the instrumental-versus-constitutive asymmetry head-on, and explains why the opposing weighting fails. Finally, provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks allocated (total 20) and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss each point. The scheme should clearly separate an A answer—which makes the specific conceptual move about weighting and integrates it with the numbers—from a B answer that gets the arithmetic right but treats the philosophical crux as an afterthought. Keep the tone formal and polished; do not include any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; and do not depend on obscure statistics or named theories that a small LLM wouldn't have properly internalized. If you reference any well-known argument, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
