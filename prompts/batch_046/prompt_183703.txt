I'm a software engineer working on distributed state machines, and I've been trying to map my intuition about system identity onto the philosophy of persistence without much formal background. Here is a rough essay sketch I'd like feedback on. I argue that standard version-control metaphors actually pull in two incompatible directions. On one hand, treating a Git repository as an enduring object—same repo, different file trees—seems natural when we say "I pushed to main." On the other hand, each commit is an immutable snapshot with fixed properties, and the repository's "history" looks like a four-dimensional worm of discrete temporal parts, which sounds closer to perdurantism. The tension becomes sharper with operations like interactive rebase, which retroactively replace commits in the middle of the sequence: if temporal parts are fixed and unalterable, then rewriting history seems to annihilate and replace parts rather than change properties of an enduring whole. I extend this to Raft consensus logs, where a crashed node can be restored from a snapshot plus replayed log entries—suggesting that what persists is not a single enduring node but a reconstructed chain of state-stages. I tentatively conclude that perdurantism handles immutable snapshots better, but that endurantism captures our practical language of system maintenance, and I wonder whether stage theory might resolve the mismatch by treating each deployed version as a counterpart rather than a part.

Could you comment specifically on whether this analogy misrepresents endurantism or perdurantism—particularly whether perdurantists really commit to the immutability of temporal parts in a way that makes "rebasing" ontologically destructive rather than just epistemic? I'd also appreciate thoughts on whether the Raft replay example genuinely supports one view over the other, or whether I'm smuggling in assumptions about identity conditions from distributed systems that don't translate. Finally, is there a coherent third position—perhaps closer to stage theory or to some form of relational essentialism—that would better account for software updates without forcing me to choose between an enduring repository and a worm of commits? I am not looking for citations or reading lists, just conceptual correction and suggestions for sharpening the argument.
