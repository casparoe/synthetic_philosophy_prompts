Please describe the most important implications of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem for institutional design and moral education, with particular attention to a distinction that standard treatments often elide: the difference between being moved by a wrong reason at the first order and being moved by a reason that one knowingly accepts as wrong at the second order. Begin by clarifying how Scanlon’s formulation distinguishes motivational reasons from normative reasons, and explain why supplying external rewards for virtuous conduct is thought to corrupt the agent’s relation to the value at stake rather than merely adding an auxiliary motive. Then address three specific issues. First, can an institution structure incentives so that agents come to treat external rewards as purely instrumental conditions for pursuing genuinely valued ends, and does this instrumental framing avoid the corruption problem or merely relocate it to the level of reflective endorsement? Second, apply the framework to self-regulation: when an agent deploys commitment devices or self-imposed costs to maintain healthy behavior in the absence of intrinsic motivation, is she giving herself the wrong kind of reason, and does the analysis change if she explicitly regards the device as a temporary scaffold rather than as a justification? Third, consider whether the problem generalizes beyond individual virtue to collective decision-making—specifically, whether a democratic body that endorses a just policy primarily because it serves group interests rather than because justice demands it can be said to have acted for the wrong kind of reasons, even when the outcome is identical. Conclude by identifying one domain where the wrong-kind-of-reasons framework offers clear guidance, one where it generates an apparent paradox, and one where it remains genuinely silent.
