I'm constructing a research agenda on the ontology of demographic cohorts—whether categories like Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z, and Generation Alpha denote real kinds, institutional kinds, or purely statistical artifacts. The debate touches sociology (age-period-cohort identification problems), philosophy of social science, and applied ethics (intergenerational justice, marketing targeting, labor-market policy), but much of the existing discussion remains conceptually muddled and repetitive.

Please identify the three to five main open conceptual questions or lines of implication in this area, and defend a strict prioritization of which two or three deserve the most sustained philosophical attention over the next several years. Your defense should be explicit about the criteria you are using: for each question, explain its tractability for conceptual rather than large-scale empirical work, how many downstream theoretical or practical debates depend on its resolution, and whether settling it would actually change policy, scientific practice, or normative evaluation rather than merely refining taxonomy.

To avoid well-worn survey answers, frame at least one of your prioritized questions through a less-discussed distinction—specifically, the difference between diachronic essentialism (the view that a cohort must share stable, causally integrated properties across its entire lifespan to count as a genuine kind) and synchronic clustering (the view that temporary, age-bound correlations in behavior or preference are sufficient for kindhood, even if the properties dissolve as members age). Also address whether resolving the ontology of generations carries genuine normative weight: for instance, does it affect how we should design intergenerational transfer programs, evaluate cohort-based blame for economic or environmental outcomes, or regulate targeted advertising?

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. I want the reasoning behind the agenda and the ordering, not references. Ground the discussion in concepts accessible without specialist sociological literature, but keep the framing precise, non-obvious, and focused on what deserves effort rather than on describing every position in the field.
