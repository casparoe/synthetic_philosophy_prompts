As a doctoral student in moral philosophy with a focus on reproductive ethics, I have repeatedly encountered an argumentative configuration in discussions of procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics for which I lack a precise label. The configuration proceeds as follows. One begins with the familiar principle of procreative beneficence—the claim that, among possible children, prospective parents have a reason to select the one with the best expected quality of life. A critic then presses the principle beyond the immediate reproductive act, asking whether parents ought also to select for traits that predispose the child to exercise the same principle beneficently when they later reproduce. This generates a recursive or intergenerational cascade: the selection criterion applies not only to the welfare of the child-to-be, but to the welfare of the child's potential descendants, with no obvious generational boundary at which the obligation terminates. Defenders of local, first-generation evaluation resist this extension, yet the critic notes that if future welfare genuinely counts, treating generations in isolation appears ad hoc. I am aware of the non-identity problem, but that concerns the comparison of different possible persons across policy choices; my puzzle concerns the recursive application of a single selection principle within one lineage. Is there an established concept—perhaps within debates on dynastic reasoning, longtermist reproductive ethics, or liberal eugenics—that captures this specific intergenerational extension? If not, how ought one to distinguish it from ordinary multi-generational welfare aggregation, and what conceptual boundary prevents the cascade from extending indefinitely?
