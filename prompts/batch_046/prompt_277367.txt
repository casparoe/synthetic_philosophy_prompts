Construct a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies philosophical responses to the St. Petersburg paradox, intended for a philosophy-of-decision-theory handout for advanced undergraduates. The tree must begin from a single root commitment—“I accept that the game offers an unbounded payout with an infinite expected monetary value”—and branch through concrete, label-independent questions that determine which resolution a respondent actually holds, without requiring prior knowledge of position names.

Design the branches around four genuinely separable distinctions: (1) whether the paradox is dissolved by a mathematical correction to the expectation calculation itself (e.g. truncation by a finite bankroll, failure of integrability conditions, or misapplication of the law of large numbers); (2) whether it is dissolved by altering the valuation of outcomes while retaining expected-value maximisation (e.g. bounded or logarithmic utility, risk-averse curvature); (3) whether it is dissolved by replacing the decision rule with one that does not rely on ensemble averages over infinite time (e.g. time-averaged growth-rate maximisation, ecological-rationality satisficing, or bounded-rationality heuristics—refer to these conceptually rather than attributively); and (4) whether it is taken to show only local or domain-restricted validity for expected-utility theory rather than a global failure.

Every leaf must be named if it corresponds to a recognised position (for example: “Bernoulli-style diminishing-utility resolution”; “finite-bankroll truncation”; “time-averaging / non-ergodic resolution”; “expected-utility localism”; “spurious-paradox view”) or marked “unnamed” if the answer-combination is coherent but apparently undefended. For each leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward commitment—the counter-intuitive claim it must endorse.

Also include:
- A “gaps” section listing any combination of answers permitted by your tree that reaches no leaf, with a brief diagnosis of why the cell is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).
- A “flags” section naming any pair of positions your tree genuinely cannot separate, identifying the shared branch and explaining what further diagnostic question would be needed to split them.

Present the result as a JSON object with exactly these fields: “root_commitment” (string), “branches” (array of objects each with “question”, “yes_path”, “no_path”), “leaves” (array of objects each with “leaf_id”, “path_description”, “position_name”, “awkward_commitment”), “gaps” (array of strings), and “flags” (array of objects with “position_pair”, “shared_path”, “explanation”). Write the entire response in British English, using British spellings throughout (e.g. behaviour, centre, programme, defence, maximisation). Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the tree to at most six leaves and no more than four branch levels.
