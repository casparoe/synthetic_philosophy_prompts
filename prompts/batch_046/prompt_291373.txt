I was recently moderating a discussion in my graduate seminar on the philosophy of social institutions, and a student introduced an elaborate hypothetical scenario that has stayed with me. She asked us to imagine a "neuro-architect"—a future engineer of human motivation—who redesigns our reward pathways so that satisfaction arises exclusively from collective agricultural labor. In this redesigned world, personal ambition withers, yet universal sustenance is guaranteed. My student then argued that because this state is conceivable and arguably preferable by utilitarian lights, any political philosophy that permits individual luxury goods must be incoherent. The class seemed immediately swayed; several nodded as though the scenario had delivered a decisive refutation of liberal distributive principles. Yet I felt strongly that the example was doing rhetorical work that outpaced its logical force, though I could not pinpoint whether the defect lay in its smuggled premises about human flourishing, in its suppression of the distinction between designed and emergent social orders, or in something else entirely.

As someone who has long taught the methodology of philosophical argumentation, I am reluctant to dismiss student enthusiasm, but I also worry that seductive imaginative constructions can short-circuit critical evaluation rather than deepen it. I am not seeking generic classroom-management tips; rather, I would like you to help me diagnose the precise philosophical structure that makes this kind of scenario persuasive despite its potential misdirection, and to advise me on how I might guide my students to distinguish genuinely illuminating hypothetical cases from those that merely manipulate our intuitive responses. How should I frame the analysis so that they recognize the difference between an argument that uses imagination to reveal hidden commitments and one that uses it to conceal contested assumptions?
