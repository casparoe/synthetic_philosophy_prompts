In my graduate seminar on self-knowledge, a colleague dismissed every third-person correction of affective reports with the line: “The mind is transparent to itself—if you report anger, then anger is present, and outsiders are always in the dark.” She was using it not merely to defend first-person authority over occurrent feelings, but to extend that authority to dispositional self-characterizations (for instance, being “chronically anxious”) and to treat transparency as licensing semantic authority over psychological descriptions applied to oneself.

Take that slogan and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. Which are near-platitudes about privileged access? Which are substantive and defensible claims about incorrigibility or direct acquaintance? Which are plainly false in light of confabulation, misattribution, and implicit bias? And which specific reading does she actually need—covering both occurrent states and dispositional traits—to block external correction? Present the options as a menu with verdicts (platitude, defensible but contentious, or false), and indicate which ones genuinely support the use she put the slogan to.
