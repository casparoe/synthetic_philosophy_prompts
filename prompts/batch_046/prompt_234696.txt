I'm grading a stack of short answers for an intro ehtics course. The question was: "Explain the differnce between Hume's is-ought gap and Moore's naturalistic fallacy, and whether one entails the other."

Here's one response:

Hume's is-ought gap, found in his Treatise, is the observation that people often slide from statements about what is the case to statements about what ought to be done without justifying the move. This means normative conclusions can't be pulled out of purely descriptive premises. Moore's naturalistic fallacy, in Principia Ethica, is the error of defining 'good' in terms of some natural property like pleasure or evolutionary fitness. The two are basically the same underlying point: Hume showed that values can't come from facts in reasoning, and Moore showed they can't come from facts in definition. In particular, Moore's open question argument applies Hume's insight to language — if good equals pleasure, then asking 'is pleasure good?' still makes sense, which proves the normative bit wasn't captured. So Hume's gap does entail Moore's fallacy, since both show that the normative can't be reduced to the descriptive. The only real difference is that Hume was writing about moral psychology and Moore about semantics.
