I just had a long, exhausting argument with a friend about Kuhn's thesis of incommensurability. We started with the usual examples — the shift from Newtonian mechanics to relativity, or from pre-Daltonian chemistry to atomic theory — but we quickly got stuck. He insisted that if paradigms are genuinely incommensurable, then scientists in different paradigms literally inhabit different worlds, which makes Kuhn's claim self-refuting because Kuhn himself compares the paradigms. I countered that Kuhn's point was about the failure of neutral translation, not about ontological pluralism, but then he asked whether "failure of translation" just collapses into "difference in vocabulary," and I realized I didn't have a precise answer. We went in circles for hours.

My working hypothesis is that the philosophical debate over incommensurability has stalled not because of missing historical evidence, but because the term conceals at least two distinct failures that get run together under one name: taxonomic incommensurability, where the categories and kind-terms of one paradigm don't map onto the other (for instance, "mass" in Newtonian mechanics versus in special relativity), and normative incommensurability, where the very criteria for what counts as solving a problem or counting as progress change. Critics often attack one version — usually by showing that scientists can still compare predictions — while defenders defend the other, and nobody notices they are addressing different claims. Is that the right diagnosis?

Are there bedrock intuitions about scientific objectivity that neither side can argue for?
