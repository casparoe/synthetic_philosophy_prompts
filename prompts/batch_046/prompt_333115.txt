Here is an argument that illusionism about phenomenal consciousness proves too much — it seems to undermine the very possibility of understanding its own thesis. (1) Illusionism denies that sensory experiences instantiate phenomenal properties; we only represent ourselves as having them via access-conscious mechanisms. (2) For consistency, the same treatment must apply to cognitive phenomenology: there is no distinctive “what it is like” to grasp a proof or see a contradiction, only functional access to propositional relations. (3) Yet accepting illusionism requires genuinely understanding the distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness, not merely processing the words. (4) If understanding were purely functional, then any syntactic system with the right state-transitions — including a trivial computer program — would understand the distinction as well as a philosopher. (5) So understanding that distinction requires a non-functional, phenomenally constituted grasp, an “intellectual phenomenology.” (6) Illusionism denies all phenomenal constitution, hence denies the condition necessary for anyone to understand and accept it. (7) Therefore illusionism is self-defeating: if true, no one can understand it; if anyone understands it, it must be false.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (4). Which premise should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Is the slide between sensory and cognitive phenomenology an illegitimate generalization, or does the argument validly force illusionists into eliminativism about understanding?
