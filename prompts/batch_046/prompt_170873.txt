A sovereignty-exception framework I have been tracking rules on five past cases as follows: in Kosovo (1999) it approved intervention despite the absence of UN Security Council authorization, citing an imminent mass-atrocity threshold and a cohesive regional multilateral coalition; in Iraq (2003) it rejected intervention because the harm threshold was not met, intelligence was fabricated, and multilateral consensus was absent; in Libya (2011) it approved a narrowly defined civilian-protection mandate but judged that the subsequent shift to explicit regime change violated mandate integrity and voided the moral basis of the operation; in Rwanda (1994) it held that non-intervention was catastrophic, yet maintained that rapid military occupation without embedded transitional institutions would have collapsed into a security vacuum rather than protected the population; and in Sierra Leone (2000) it approved a limited stabilization mission precisely because the mandate was narrow, genuinely multilateral, and included explicit exit conditions tied to local governance restoration.

Consider a new case: a UN-authorized coalition deploys with a strict safe-corridor and disarmament mandate in a region experiencing localized ethnic cleansing that is severe but has not reached mass-atrocity scale; the mandate is observed precisely, but there is no transitional administration plan and local institutions are expected to self-restore within weeks. Predict or recommend what the framework should conclude to remain consistent with itself, identify the closest analog among the five, state the competing rules—such as harm-threshold primacy, mandate-integrity primacy, and institutional-scaffolding necessity—and what each predicts here, give your best-guess verdict with reasoning, and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify what principle the framework would be adopting with each choice. Finally, propose a single further case that would most clearly distinguish the rules you identified.
