As a doctoral researcher in jurisprudence and philosophical logic, I am examining a structural feature of constitutional design that is ubiquitous yet conceptually under-examined: the coexistence of self-amendment provisions and entrenchment clauses within the same normative order. Most modern constitutions authorize a specific body or procedure to revise the constitutional text—think of Article V mechanisms or their analogues—while simultaneously declaring that certain provisions, occasionally including the amendment rule itself, lie beyond the reach of ordinary revision. The institutional result is a rule of change that governs its own potential alteration, but only under conditions that it itself stipulates. This is not merely a procedural curiosity; it embeds a form of normative self-reference at the foundation of legal authority.

To sharpen the conceptual tension, consider a clause formulated as follows: the amendment authority may replace itself, but only with a rule that does not explicitly deny the original clause's power to authorize replacements. Structurally, this mirrors the liar paradox translated into the domain of institutional authority. Actual constitutional orders typically avoid explicit contradiction by distributing interpretive authority across courts and legislatures, or by treating entrenchment as politically rather than logically absolute. Nevertheless, they institutionalize self-reference while depending on extra-legal conventions to prevent paradoxical collapse.

What philosophical theory of legal authority does this institutional practice presuppose? Must one adopt a hierarchical model in which every rule of change derives validity from a non-amendable foundational norm, or can a conventionalist framework—one that treats the rule of recognition as a social practice open to gradual revision—absorb the loop without theoretical collapse? Moreover, is the practice consistent with the same legal system's standard insistence, in statutory interpretation, that a present legislature cannot bind its successors, given that the constitutional amendment rule is precisely an attempt by the founding authority to constrain how future authorities may alter the rules of alteration?

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory of legal self-reference and normative authority that renders this design coherent. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate whether abandoning the underlying theory—for instance, adopting a fully flat, non-hierarchical conception of legal validity—would compel us to reject self-amending constitutional provisions altogether, or whether they could survive under a radically different philosophical interpretation.
