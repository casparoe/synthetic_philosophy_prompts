I'm sketching an essay that extends Dewey's experimentalist account of scientific inquiry into his political philosophy in a direction that, to my knowledge, hasn't been systematically pursued. The outline is as follows. First, Dewey treats inquiry as the transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinately unified one through the controlled interaction of observation and conceptual framing; the resistance offered by the environment is not an obstacle but a constitutive condition of warranted assertibility. Second, I apply this model to democratic deliberation: democratic disagreement is not a failure of preference aggregation but a form of social inquiry in which conflicting perspectives function analogously to experimental resistance, testing and refining collective judgments rather than blocking them. Third, I introduce what I call "epistemic friction" — the claim that productive democratic disagreement must possess a specific qualitative structure, neither too weak (mere divergence of preference) nor too strong (irreconcilable conflict rooted in identity rather than problem-solving), in order to serve a genuinely experimental function. Fourth, I argue that this framing exposes a tension Dewey does not fully resolve: if democratic legitimacy is partly grounded in the epistemic productivity of deliberation, then citizens whose social positions grant them richer, more direct access to the "problematic situation" appear to possess greater deliberative authority, which threatens the normative equality that democracy also demands. The essay concludes by asking whether Dewey's framework can be modified to preserve both experimental efficacy and egalitarian legitimacy, or whether the two aims are ultimately in conflict.

Please comment on these notes and the line of argument they propose. Specifically: (1) Is the analogy between environmental resistance in scientific inquiry and structured disagreement in democratic deliberation textually and philosophically defensible within Dewey's own writings, or does it import an external framework he would reject? (2) Does the concept of "epistemic friction" cohere with Dewey's account of inquiry, or does it smuggle in assumptions — for instance, about the intrinsic value of conflict — that are foreign to his pragmatism? (3) Is the tension between epistemic productivity and democratic equality genuinely present in Dewey's view, or is it dissolved by his conception of "the public" and shared interest in controlling social consequences? (4) Which parts of the outline seem interesting and which seem like unnecessary complications? I am especially interested in whether the position is vulnerable to an internal Deweyan critique: could one reply that democratic equality is itself an indispensable experimental condition, so that suppressing it in favor of epistemic hierarchy undermines the very social inquiry it is meant to serve? Please also suggest how the argument might be sharpened or where it risks collapsing into a more familiar position.
