I've been arguing with my roomate about how committees should make decisions when the parts depend on eachother. We were using this made-up faculty vote on a new gen-ed requirement: people vote separately on (P1) critical reasoning improves job outcomes, (P2) our curriculum doesn't teach it well, and (P3) employers value it over other skills, then they vote on the conclusion C that we should mandate a logic course. Most people say yes to P1 and P2 but no to P3, and they also vote yes on C. The chair wants to aggregate premise-by-premise and derive C, but that forces "add the course" even though P3 failed, which feels wrong. But if we just vote on C directly we ignore that people endorsed P1 and P2. So my study partner wrote up this chain that seems to show democratic voting is broken for complex issues. Here it is:

(1) Any rational collective procedure must preserve consistency between premises and conclusions.
(2) Premise-based aggregation preserves consistency but can yield a conclusion that the majority rejects.
(3) Conclusion-based aggregation respects the majority on the conclusion but can yield conclusions that contradict premises the majority accepted.
(4) So both standard democratic procedures violate some rationality condition.
(5) If every democratic procedure violates rationality, then democratic bodies should never vote on interdependent policy questions and should deferr entirely to expert panels.
(6) But that means giving up democratic control over curriculum design and similar issues.
(7) Therefore democratic aggregation is self-defeating for complex policy.

I think the slip is somewhere between (4) and (5) — maybe it's assuming there's no middle ground? — but I also worry (1) is too strong if reasoning isn't strictly deductive. I really want to keep (2) and (3) since they just describe the discursive dilemma, and I don't want to give up democratic control so I'm suspicious of (5). Which specific step should I reject, why did it look okay at first, and what do I have to pay if I drop it? Also, if there's a hidden equivocation or scope shift I'm missing, point it out. I don't need a bibliography, just the diagnosis.
