I have recently found myself drawn into a sharp philosophical disagreement about the epistemic status of scientific idealisations, particularly in the wake of a seminar discussion concerning what is often termed Friedman’s F-twist—the claim that models may legitimately rest on assumptions known to be false of their targets, provided they deliver accurate predictions. Rather than adjudicating between the instrumentalist defenders of this view and their critics, I wish to see whether a good-faith adversarial collaboration could produce a document both sides might sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than obscured by rhetoric.

The first camp maintains that unrealistic assumptions—such as perfect rationality in economic agents or frictionless surfaces in mechanics—function as isolating devices that abstract away from complicating factors without misrepresenting core causal structures, and that predictive accuracy is a sufficient, if not necessary, warrant for retaining them. The second camp contends that many so-called isolating assumptions actually distort the underlying mechanisms in ways that render predictive success epistemically hollow: a model may predict well for the wrong reasons, and without representational fidelity it cannot support counterfactual inference or genuine explanation. I ask you to treat these not as straw positions but as live research programmes, and to inject a specific analytical distinction—between predictive insulation (the capacity of a model to resist falsification by anomalous data) and explanatory insulation (the capacity of a false assumption to be shielded by auxiliary hypotheses)—as one of the cruxes both sides must address.

Please draft the joint statement in British English. It should contain: (i) a numbered section of agreed claims, covering the formal structure of idealisation, the difference between abstraction and misrepresentation, the respective roles of predictive and explanatory success, and the conditions under which a model ought to be revised; (ii) a disagreements table in which each camp’s precise thesis is formulated in language its own adherents would accept, especially regarding whether explanatory adequacy is reducible to predictive robustness across domains; (iii) the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party—including whether there exists a principled boundary between isolating and distorting assumptions, and whether the distinction between predictive and explanatory insulation is substantive or merely verbal; and (iv) for each crux, the argument, empirical case, or conceptual test both sides agree is relevant, together with each side’s explicit prediction of its outcome. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—perhaps over the meaning of ‘explanation’ or ‘realism’—identify which part and explain why. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
