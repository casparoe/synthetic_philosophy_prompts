I'm preparing notes for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I've become convinced that accuracy-first frameworks—those that evaluate doxastic states primarily by expected epistemic utility or predictive accuracy rather than by evidentialist coherence or justification—carry implications for scientific institutional design that go well beyond individual belief-formation. In particular, I keep returning to a structural tension: if each researcher rationally allocates cognitive effort toward domains where prior belief accuracy is already high and belief-revision costs are low, the community may systematically underinvest in high-variance, high-payoff research programs that are individually risky yet collectively indispensable. This looks like a collective-action failure built into the very rationality conditions that accuracy-first theories prescribe. What are the most important implications of this tension? Should we read accuracy-first epistemology as undermining standard peer-evaluation metrics that reward individual predictive success, or does it instead demand structural correctives—such as mandatory portfolio diversification in funding mechanisms—to prevent what might be termed epistemic free-riding? And where exactly should we draw the line between a rational individual's avoidance of controversial domains and a collective epistemic failure that institutional design ought to correct?

I'm especially interested in how this plays out at the boundary where individual and collective rationality seem to diverge sharply. Suppose a research community uses accuracy-based reputation tracking: scientists who maintain high personal calibration scores gain influence, while those who pursue speculative frameworks suffer temporary accuracy penalties. Over time, the metric that was meant to track truth-seeking behavior might reshape the distribution of scientific labor so thoroughly that high-risk inquiry becomes structurally irrational, even when everyone agrees it is collectively necessary. Does accuracy-first epistemology have the internal resources to diagnose this as a pathology, or does its focus on individual doxastic states render it blind to emergent failures of collective knowledge production? And if we do accept the need for institutional intervention, what form should it take without violating the core commitment that epistemic evaluation should remain anchored in accuracy rather than in social or procedural values? I want to understand whether the theory forces us toward a kind of epistemic socialism—deliberately insulating risky inquiry from market-like accuracy pressures—or whether there is a more minimal, mechanism-based solution that preserves both individual rationality and collective discovery.
