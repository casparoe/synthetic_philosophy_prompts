As a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently begun working seriously in philosophy of normativity, I want to specify what an adequate theory of free-floating rationales must actually deliver before I commit to any particular account. By “free-floating rationales” I mean reasons—whether explanatory or normative—that guide, constrain, or justify outcomes without being possessed, entertained, or endorsed by any reasoning subject. I am not asking for a bibliography or a historical survey; I want the full desiderata specification, with particular attention to cases that sit outside standard agent-centered frameworks.

Please write the requirements list that any theory in this area must satisfy. Organize your answer around the following demands:

1. Core phenomena and motivating cases. Identify the specific scenarios the theory must explain, with the reason each is non-optional. Include at least: (a) evolutionary adaptations that exhibit functional coherence and selective “design” in the absence of propositional attitudes or beliefs; (b) institutional norms or rules that continue to direct behavior and justify decisions long after the original architects and their personal rationales have disappeared; (c) optimization targets in artificial learning systems that shape outputs without being represented as reasons by the system itself. For each, state exactly what the theory must say about the ontological status of the rationale involved.

2. Non-collapsible distinctions. List the conceptual distinctions the theory must preserve—such as that between a rationale being genuinely normative versus merely causally effective; between reasons that were originally anchored in an agent’s mental states but have since become detached, versus those that were never agent-anchored at all; and between explanatory reasons (why something happened) and justifying reasons (why it ought to have happened). Give the case behind each distinction and flag any pair you think may be jointly unsatisfiable, explaining whether the tension is real or merely an artifact of one camp’s prejudices.

3. Consistency constraints from adjacent sciences. Specify the results from evolutionary biology, cognitive ethology, and machine-learning theory that the theory must respect—particularly those that forbid attributing propositional content, intentionality, or reflective endorsement to selection processes, non-cognitive organisms, or algorithmic objective functions.

4. Negotiability and exclusionary power. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or can be relaxed if the theory gains explanatory power elsewhere. Then identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the dominant existing approaches—broadly, agent-centered constructivism, causal-naturalist reduction, and pragmatic anti-realism—and explain why.

5. Brief scoring of contenders. Without developing full historical exegeses, evaluate in a paragraph each how agent-centered constructivism, causal-naturalist reduction, and pragmatic anti-realism fare against your list. Note where each fails outright, where it collapses a required distinction, and where it must pay an unacceptable ontological cost.

6. Presentation. Deliver this as a structured specification rather than a narrative essay; a table is acceptable where it clarifies the mapping of desiderata to cases. Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and the tensions among them, rather than on the historical views.

Please inject into the specification one further niche constraint of your own design—something that forces the theory to address rationales operating in distributed, non-individual systems—so that the list is not satisfied by merely recycling standard debates about moral reasons or practical rationality.
