In philosophy of religion, Wittgenstein's view is often introduced through the analogy of spectacles: religious belief is like a pair of glasses through which one sees the world, not an object within the world that one examines. Proponents use this to license the claim that religious belief is not a falsifiable hypothesis but a form of life that frames what counts as evidence. More recently, some writers have combined this with the notion of epistemic scaffolding — the idea that religious frameworks act as temporary cognitive supports that mature secular reasoning can eventually discard without loss — arguing that if belief is merely spectacles, then taking them off should leave vision intact. How far does the spectacle analogy hold? Lay out the correspondence (spectacles, the eye, the visible world, the act of putting them on, the optometrist) and then the disanalogies that bear on the scaffolding claim — in particular, whether the analogy illegitimately treats belief as an optional accessory rather than as a condition of seeing at all, and whether Wittgenstein's own notion of a form of life is compatible with removal. Does the conclusion that religious belief is disposable scaffolding survive? Be explicit about where your reasoning is most vulnerable and what consideration would reverse your verdict.
