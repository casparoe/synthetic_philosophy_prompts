For my dissertation chapter on practical rationality, please set out the desiderata that any adequate normative theory of sunk-cost reasoning must meet. The theory should explain standard fallacious escalation—continuing a failing course of action solely because of prior, irrecoverable investment—yet also accommodate cases where honouring sunk costs appears rational, specifically when those costs serve as credible commitment devices within long-term collective enterprises or as expressions of diachronic personal integrity, without reducing the latter to mere psychological bias. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind it, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or specific to a particular camp (e.g., consequentialist, deontological, virtue-theoretic, or institutional), and note whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable in light of results from decision theory or social-choice dynamics. I am particularly keen to see whether a strict forward-looking optimisation requirement can be reconciled with a requirement that agents preserve narrative continuity across irreversible choices, and which of these two does the heavier exclusionary work.
