I am a retired evolutionary ecologist who, since leaving active research, has been working seriously through philosophy of science. Tomorrow I am to deliver the opening remarks—about eight minutes—at the workshop “Constraints and Design: Evolutionary Thinking Across Disciplines,” hosted jointly by our biology and philosophy departments. The audience will be roughly thirty people: evolutionary biologists, philosophers of science, graduate students from both sides, and two administrators who oversee the joint funding that makes the event possible.

I want the remarks structured around the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique of adaptationism, but applied reflexively to the workshop itself. I do not wish to tell the conventional story that this interdisciplinary space was intelligently designed to solve a pre-existing problem. Rather, I want to suggest—without insulting the organizers—that the most fruitful interactions here are likely spandrels: architectural byproducts of the load-bearing separation between the departments, later co-opted into collaboration. This should do real structural work: it must constrain me from using functionalist language that treats every feature of our program as an optimal adaptation to a defined purpose, while still allowing me to welcome the room and set a serious intellectual tone. The biologists should hear a precise, non-caricatured version of the spandrels argument; the philosophers should grasp the methodological point that asking “what is this for?” can mislead when structure precedes function; and the non-specialists, especially the administrators, should understand the institutional claim without being lost in jargon.

Please write the speech. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or verbatim quotations from any published text; paraphrase the conceptual distinction in plain language. Keep the tone polished, respectful, and appropriate for a mixed audience of experts and newcomers.
