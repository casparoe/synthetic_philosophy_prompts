I'm working on a long-form feature about belief revision and the philosophy of probability, and in my research I came across an unnamed essay in a recent ideas magazine that attempts to popularize the Judy Benjamin problem for a general audience. The author describes the classic setup—Judy knows she belongs to one of three companies, Red, Blue, or Green, with different base rates for commandos versus rear-guard personnel—but then introduces a twist that I have not encountered in the standard literature. According to this summary, after Judy conditions away Red and computes her weighted average credence in being a commando, she receives further testimony not about which company she is in, but about the rigor of her rear-guard training. The author claims that because this testimony is probabilistically dependent on her original assignment in a way that is not captured by any event in her initial sample space, standard Bayesian conditionalization breaks down. Instead, the author proposes what he calls a dynamic conditional rule, which yields a precise revised credence of roughly twelve percent rather than the fifteen percent a naive averaging would produce. He concludes that this demonstrates a general failure of the Principal Principle for self-locating beliefs and that philosophers such as Lewis and van Fraassen were mistaken to treat conditional credence as derivable from unconditional probabilities through the ratio formula.

Something in this summary strikes me as technically slippery, and I want to make sure I am not misrepresenting the philosophical stakes in my article. The initial move from learning that one is not in Red to computing a weighted average across Blue and Green seems defensible as a first approximation, but I am uncertain whether Judy's priors should be uniform across the three companies or whether the problem is specifically engineered to test updating on a conditional belief rather than on an observed event. More importantly, the author's invocation of a dynamic conditional rule and the specific numerical result appear to conflate the classic Judy Benjamin puzzle with later debates about Sleeping Beauty-style self-location or about updating on evidence that is itself indexical. The claim that this refutes the Principal Principle also feels like an overreach; I understand Lewis's principle as addressing the alignment of subjective probabilities with known physical chances, not as a universal algorithm for every case of conditional belief revision. Could you audit this summary with care? Please separate what is an accurate rendering of the Judy Benjamin scenario, what is a common but defensible simplification, what subtly misrepresents the relationship between conditional credence and unconditional probability, and whether the conclusion that Bayesian conditionalization fails here actually follows from the premises laid out.
