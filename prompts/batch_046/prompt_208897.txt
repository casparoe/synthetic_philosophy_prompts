I'm delivering the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop—half evolutionary biologists, half moral philosophers—convened to mark the retirement of a senior colleague whose career has turned on a specific, rarely acknowledged tension in Darwin's work. The workshop is titled "Darwin's Unresolved Dual Stance," and its premise is this: in The Descent of Man Darwin offers what looks like a continuous evolutionary account of morality, tracing social instincts from lower animals up through human sympathy and the moral sense. Yet in his private M and N notebooks, and in correspondence that he never published, he repeatedly worries that the human capacity for reflective self-judgment—our ability to condemn our own past actions on grounds that have no obvious adaptive payoff—introduces a break in that evolutionary continuity, one he never resolves. At the same time, Darwin's religious position is not the simple atheism often attributed to him: in his autobiography he describes himself as an agnostic who, even at his most skeptical, found it impossible to conceive the universe as the result of unguided chance, an admission that sits in tension with the naturalistic framework of his science. Write a fifteen-minute address that takes both tensions seriously, uses them to frame the day's discussions rather than to resolve them, and is accessible to biologists with no philosophical training and to philosophers with no close knowledge of Darwin's texts. The speech should avoid hagiography or demonization, should make clear why these unresolved questions matter for contemporary evolutionary ethics rather than treating them as historical curiosities, and should close with a concrete invitation to the workshop's task: to determine whether modern evolutionary ethics has inherited Darwin's silence or genuinely answered it. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the framing is conceptual, not a literature review.
