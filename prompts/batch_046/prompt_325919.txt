I sit on the institutional ethics board of a research university that recently integrated an autonomous resource-allocation platform—internally designated LabGate—into its grant-management pipeline. The platform operates through a federated architecture: three separately certified sub-modules evaluate budget compliance, safety verification, and strategic priority, each producing an independent score; a denial is issued not by any individual module, but only when an emergent interaction among their outputs crosses a composite threshold. Two months ago, this interaction produced an automatic denial of a junior researcher’s request for critical imaging equipment. No single module registered an explicit objection—the budget score was marginal, the safety flag referred to an outdated protocol, and the priority weighting reflected a generic departmental ranking—yet their combination triggered a veto that caused the researcher to miss a grant milestone and suffer substantial professional harm. The vendor maintains that responsibility lies with the university for configuring the interaction weights; the administration insists that each module was independently certified and therefore safe; and the department chair, who merely activated the system according to procurement guidelines, argues that she could not have anticipated an emergent outcome that none of the component audits predicted. The injured party is seeking institutional redress, but every actor can point to a different node in the architecture as the proper locus of fault, leaving our board uncertain whether blame should be distributed, concentrated, or reconceptualized altogether.

I am concerned that our usual frameworks for assigning moral responsibility—those that trace harm back to a discrete intentional agent—may be inadequate here, not because anyone acted maliciously, but because the decision emerged from a structure specifically designed to prevent any single human or subsystem from exercising determinative control. At the same time, I am reluctant to conclude that no one is responsible, since that would leave the injured researcher without recourse and the institution without an obligation to reform. I want to understand whether there is a coherent philosophical position that allows us to hold a collective or structural entity accountable without inventing a fictional agent, or whether we must instead identify a particular human role—perhaps the chair who implemented the system, or the administrators who selected the architecture—as bearing a distinctive form of ownership over outcomes that the system produces. I am especially interested in whether the distinction between causal contribution and moral ownership can be made to do real work in this context, rather than serving as a mere semantic distinction.
