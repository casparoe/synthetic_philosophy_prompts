hey, i'm trying to work out this argument about presentism in historiography for a seminar paper but it feels like it proves way too much and i can't spot the leak. here's the chain i built:

(1) every historical description sorts past actions into categories — corruption, gift-exchange, revolution, genocide, whatever.
(2) the categories we actually have are products of our present culture and theory; we don't have direct access to past conceptual shemes.
(3) so describing the past always means applying present concepts to it.
(4) if applying present concepts is presentism, then all history is presentist.
(5) but if all history is presentist, then the anti-presentist rule "don't apply present standards" is itself an application of a present standard (our current preference for neutrality), so its self-undermining.
(6) therefore either presentism is unavoidable and not a vice, or the concept of presentism collapses.
(7) yet we clearly want to say some descriptions are anachronistic (calling Roman patronage "bribery" in a modern legal sense) and others are okay (calling it "clientelism" with careful translation). so (4) can't be right.
(8) but any line we draw between okay and not-okay present concepts will itself be drawn with present tools, so we're back in the loop.

where does this actually break? is it an equivocation in "applying present concepts" — like between translating and judging? or is there a modal slide somewhere? if i have to choose, i'd rather keep (3) and (7) than (5), but i want to know what i'm paying if i reject the self-undermining step. also please say what would change your mind here — like if someone showed historians could genuinely inhabit past conceptual schemes without present mediation, or whether the whole thing collapses once we separate descriptive translation from normative evaluation?
