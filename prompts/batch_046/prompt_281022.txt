Browning's study of Battalion 101 shows that ordinary, middle-aged German policemen killed thousands of Jews in Poland mainly because of peer pressure, deference to authority, and the gradual escalation of tasks—what he describes as the incremental nature of genocide. Only a small minority refused, and they did so without severe punishment, which shows that individual moral choice was still present but overwhelmed by social dynamics. Goldhagen, in Hitler's Willing Executioners, rejects this as insufficient. He argues that the killers were motivated by a deep, specifically German eliminationist antisemitism—a cultural belief that Jews were a deadly threat that needed to be removed—that made killing Jews not just acceptable but desirable. The mutual exclusivity argument holds that if Browning's situational factors are doing all the explanatory work, then Goldhagen's cultural factor is unnecessary; conversely, if the culture is necessary, then situational differences should not matter.

This argument fails because the two explanations operate at different analytical levels. Browning explains the proximate mechanism—how ordinary people were led to pull the trigger—while Goldhagen explains the distal cause—why they were willing to do so when other groups in similar situations might not have been. They are complementary: situational pressures explain the variance in refusal rates within Battalion 101, while cultural ideology explains why Germans as a group participated at high rates compared to, say, Italian or Romanian auxiliaries who were in similar structural positions but killed less eagerly. Goldhagen can even accommodate Browning's refusal data by saying that the refusers were the minority who had not fully internalized the eliminationist worldview, whereas Browning can explain why even committed antisemites might hesitate if peer pressure were absent.

There is, however, a hidden tension. Browning implies that the dynamics he observed—conformity, authority, and incremental commitment—are universal human tendencies, supported by experiments like Milgram's obedience study and Zimbardo's prison study. If these mechanisms are universal, then any population placed in the same structural conditions should produce the same killing rates, which undermines Goldhagen's claim that a specifically German culture was required. Goldhagen responds that without the cultural hatred, the situational pressures would not have been directed at Jews specifically; but this only shows that culture selects the target, not that it is necessary for the killing itself. Once we recognize that the mechanisms are universal and the target selection can be arbitrary, Goldhagen's specifically German eliminationism becomes a contingent historical feature rather than an explanatory necessity. Therefore, the accounts are not strictly exclusive, but Browning's framework absorbs the explanatory weight, leaving Goldhagen's cultural claim as descriptive background.
