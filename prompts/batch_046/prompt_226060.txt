I am serving on the exam committee for our upper-level graduate seminar in philosophy of medicine, and I am dissatisfied with the usual essay prompts on health and disease, which tend to invite little more than a summary of the biostatistical theory associated with Boorse and the harmful dysfunction analysis associated with Wakefield. For this year's final, I want a 90-minute essay question that forces students to apply these frameworks to a genuinely puzzling constructed case rather than to rehearse textbook distinctions.

Specifically, the question should center on a hypothetical physiological state I will call persistent allostatic accommodation: imagine a subpopulation that has lived for many generations in a chronically resource-scarce environment, such that their stress-response and metabolic systems have gradually recalibrated to a new, stable statistical baseline that is now typical for that group, even though individuals in this state exhibit reduced lifespan and reproductive success relative to the ancestral population. The exam question should ask students to adjudicate whether persistent allostatic accommodation counts as a disease, requiring them to articulate exactly where the naturalist's dependence on deviation from a reference-class baseline succeeds or fails once that baseline itself shifts, and where the normativist's appeal to harmful dysfunction succeeds or collapses—especially if the evaluation of harm risks smuggling in the very statistical norms it claims to transcend. Please draft the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately 650 words, and a marking scheme organized around five essential analytical moves (20 marks total), including for each a brief note on the specific conceptual error that typically causes students to lose points there.
