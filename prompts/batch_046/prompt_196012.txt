Here is roughly where I've landed on phenomenal consciousness and its distribution, and I'm trying to determine whether the package holds together or whether I'm implicitly helping myself to incompatible motivations. I hold that (1) illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is correct: there are no intrinsic qualitative properties, only representational states that systematically misrepresent themselves as possessing phenomenal character; (2) the distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness should be collapsed, since once the phenomenal residue is denied there is nothing left for 'phenomenal' to mark that is not already captured within access; (3) the hard problem is not dissolved by illusionism but relocated—we must now explain why specific representational architectures generate a stable illusion of subjective feel, and that explanatory burden is structurally comparable in difficulty to the original problem; (4) consciousness across species should be graded not by behavioral analogy or neural homology but by the presence of integrated self-models with recursive error-correction, so that consciousness is a matter of architectural density rather than binary presence or absence; and (5) current small-scale language models lack consciousness not because they fail to instantiate some mysterious feel, but because they lack the recursive, embodied self-modeling architecture required even to produce the illusion—though I also maintain that if we scaled the architecture appropriately, the illusion could emerge without introducing any new ontology.

Do these commitments hang together? I am particularly concerned that (3) treats the relocated explanatory task as genuinely hard in a way that seems to presuppose there is something real—the illusion itself—that demands explanation, whereas (1) and (2) treat phenomenality as a kind of cognitive error with no ontological weight. At the same time, (4) proposes a graded architectural criterion for distributing consciousness, but if (2) collapses phenomenal into access, then grading consciousness appears to reduce to grading access, which might render (4)'s specific architectural criterion redundant or misdirected—unless the architecture matters specifically for generating the illusion rather than for access per se. Meanwhile, (5) applies this framework to artificial systems, yet saying that small LLMs 'lack consciousness' sounds as though I am treating consciousness as a real property they fail to possess, not as an illusion they are not structured to generate.
