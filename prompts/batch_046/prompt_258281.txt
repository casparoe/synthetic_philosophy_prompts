Write an essay on the following controversy. In his paper Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?, David Lewis argues that ordinary moral intuition accepts penal substitution when the penalty takes the form of a fine but rejects it for imprisonment or death, and he uses this asymmetry to cast doubt on whether the theological doctrine of penal substitution—where one agent bears the punishment deserved by others—is genuinely coherent in analogical form. Some philosophers have proposed that Lewis's own modal-realist metaphysics, with its ontology of concrete possible worlds and counterpart relations, offers an unexpected resource for the defender of the doctrine: if punitive suffering can be distributed across worlds via counterpart relations, then one individual's suffering in the actual world might stand in the precise substitutive relation to another's deserved punishment elsewhere. Others reply that importing Lewisian world-indexing collapses penal substitution into mere vicarious suffering, abandoning the strict identity conditions required for genuine penal transfer and thus undermining the doctrine from within.

Weigh these lines of argument with care. Assess whether the legal-philosophical distinction Lewis draws between transferable and non-transferable penalties survives translation into his modal ontology, or whether the non-transferability he identifies applies with equal force regardless of counterpart relations across worlds. Reach and defend a definite conclusion: does Lewis's broader philosophical system ultimately rehabilitate penal substitution, expose a hidden incoherence in it, or leave the theological debate entirely untouched? Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or formal citations; proceed entirely through conceptual analysis.
