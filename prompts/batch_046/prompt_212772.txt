I'm trying to work out what I actually take "free will worth wanting" to require, testing my intuitions against concrete agent profiles rather than against abstract conditions like alternative possibilities or ultimate origination. Here are my gut verdicts: a person who spends months weighing a career change, feels genuine tension among competing values, and eventually endorses the choice as genuinely hers clearly possesses it; a person in the grip of severe addiction who takes the drug, recognizes the act as alien to her deeper values, and reports feeling unable to have done otherwise in that moment lacks it in that specific act, though she retains it generally; a non-human animal—say, a crow inspecting and selecting a specific tool for a novel problem with flexible adjustment—has a limited, non-reflective form of it, but not the full variety; an AI system initialized with fixed core reward weights by its designers, able to revise sub-goals but never its fundamental utility function, and that selects actions through deterministic expected-outcome computation, does not have it, even when its behavior is unpredictable; a human with a predictive neural interface that flags likely regret and suggests alternatives before action, but who can and sometimes does override the system, still has it, because the override capacity is structurally and experientially hers; and a "forecast-locked deliberator"—an agent that must make a binding preliminary commitment to an evaluative framework before running a full deterministic consequence computation, so that there is real procedural openness in the commitment stage but deterministic closure afterward—I think possesses the free will worth wanting, though I struggle to reconcile this with my denial in the AI case, since both involve deterministic evaluation downstream of an initial open stage. Is there a principle—perhaps centered on reflective endorsement, value-formation under the agent's own critical restructuring, or structural openness at the right level of description—that captures all or most of these judgments? If the best principle requires overturning one of my verdicts, which should go? In particular, what does it imply about an agent whose values are partly shaped by an external architecture she did not originally choose, but who can gradually restructure that architecture through sustained critical reflection? Please also say clearly what argument or finding would change your mind about which verdict to abandon, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail—especially in distinguishing the forecast-locked deliberator from the fixed-weight AI.
