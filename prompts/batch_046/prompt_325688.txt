I'm taking a course on nineteenth-century philosophy of religion and we've been working through Kierkegaard's pseudonymous writings, particularly the problem of faith and ethics in Fear and Trembling. I keep returning to the section where Johannes de Silentio describes Abraham's situation not merely as a conflict between duty and divine command, but as a structural suspension of the universal ethical order for the sake of a singular, absolute relation to the absolute. The text presents Abraham as unable to speak, because any explanation he offers would have to be translated back into the universal language of ethics, thereby annulling the very particularity that defines his faith.

I'm struggling with a specific tension in the passage: if the ethical is defined as the universal, and Abraham's act is justified precisely by being outside or above this universal, then in what sense can Abraham be said to believe rather than simply to have abandoned rational and moral coherence altogether? The passage seems to suggest that the knight of faith makes the movement of infinite resignation—giving up Isaac in the ethical sphere—only to receive him back by virtue of the absurd, through a second movement that has no mediation in the universal. But I can't tell whether this return is meant to be understood as an actual restoration of the ethical relation (Isaac lives, the father-son bond is preserved) or as a purely inward, subjective transformation that leaves the external ethical order permanently ruptured. Could you help me parse this? Specifically, I want to understand: (1) whether the teleological suspension of the ethical requires that the universal actually be violated in the external world, or whether it can remain intact externally while being suspended only in the knight's inwardness; (2) how the distinction between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith operates in this specific case, particularly regarding whether Abraham's silence is a failure of communication or a positive expression of his singular relation; and (3) whether Kierkegaard's framework allows for any intersubjective recognition of Abraham's act, or whether faith is necessarily opaque to every ethical observer—including Sarah, Isaac, and the community—such that recognition itself would betray the suspension. Please don't just summarize the standard interpretation; I want to see the conceptual machinery working on these three points, especially where the text seems to pull in opposite directions.
