She noted that imperial collapse typically involves territorial overextension and military exhaustion, whereas software ecosystems decay through technical debt, licensing shifts, or community fragmentation; because the causal mechanisms differ, importing imperial base rates is not just imprecise but conceptually confused. Leo countered that the outside view does not require identical mechanisms, only relevant structural similarities. Large coordinating systems, he said, share dynamics of dependency, bureaucratic inertia, and resource concentration, so grouping empires with major platforms under large-scale institutions is defensible. He added that insiders consistently overestimate longevity because they focus on unique internal strengths — the inside view — whereas the outside view forces an explicit base rate that corrects for optimism bias. Maya responded that Leo's grouping is post-hoc feature selection: he highlights dependency and inertia because they fit his analogy, but he could equally highlight the reproducibility of source code to exclude empires, which shows that the reference class is being shaped to justify the forecast rather than discovered independently. Leo replied that all reference-class choices involve judgement, but rejecting cross-domain analogies entirely eliminates forecasting; the value of the outside view is not that the class is perfect, but that it makes assumptions transparent and anchors predictions to observed frequencies rather than narrative.
