I'm a philosophy undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar on machine ethics and the design of artificial moral agents. Over the past few weeks we've examined several frameworks for implementing ethical reasoning in autonomous systems, but one institutional practice I've encountered in recent regulatory filings and engineering white papers seems to sit awkwardly with everything we've read. Several autonomous-vehicle manufacturers deploy internal collision-avoidance algorithms that appear to operate on expected-harm minimization—aggregating probable injury across passengers, pedestrians, and other road users—while simultaneously programming their explainability modules and public-facing documentation to generate deontological-style justifications, appealing to primary duties of care, non-derogable safety thresholds, and occupant rights that are explicitly said to override pure welfare aggregation. The two layers are not treated as rival architectures but as a single, coherent system.

This pattern raises a question I haven't been able to resolve with the standard literature on machine ethics. What philosophical commitments about moral reasoning does this dual-layer practice actually presuppose? Must it rely on a form of moral pluralism—perhaps a division between computational reasons and justificatory reasons—or can a unitary normative theory accommodate both layers without contradiction? Is there an implicit equivocation here between what determines the decision and what legitimates it, and if so, does that undermine the coherence of the institution's ethical design? Finally, if the presupposed view were replaced by a fully transparent consequentialist framework in which both computation and explanation appeal to the same harm-minimization metric, what concrete changes in liability law, regulatory approval, and public trust would necessarily follow?
