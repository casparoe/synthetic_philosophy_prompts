A philosophy podcast I listened to recently offered the following summary of Davidson's radical interpretation: the principle of charity is so demanding that an interpreter must attribute to a speaker beliefs matching the interpreter's own about observable reality; if a speaker systematically denies obvious truths—say, that snow is cold—then Davidson's framework classifies them not as holding false beliefs but as failing to speak a language at all, producing noise rather than meaningful utterances. From this the host inferred that Davidson is committed to a single correct conceptual scheme shared by all rational beings, making incommensurable worldviews literally unthinkable because anyone with a radically different organization of thought could never be interpreted as having beliefs. The host then applied this to artificial intelligence, arguing that an AI which assigns probabilities or categories differently from human observers would automatically fail Davidson's charity condition and therefore be treated as non-rational noise rather than as a different kind of mind.

I suspect this account is too tidy and may conflate Davidson's methodological charity with a substantive psychological claim, but I am not sure where exactly the distortions lie. Could you audit the summary? Please distinguish what Davidson actually held—particularly regarding the scope of charity, whether belief identity is required, and how he handled persistent disagreement—from the overstatements and misreadings. Does his rejection of conceptual relativism genuinely depend on the claim that there is only one conceptual scheme? And is the host correct that Davidson's framework cannot accommodate minds that organize information in ways substantially different from ours, or does that conclusion rest on the errors in the summary?
