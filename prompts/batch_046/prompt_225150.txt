You have been asked to deliver the dedication address for a new civic monument being unveiled in the town square: a single wall inscribed with names drawn from both the local deportation registry and the administrative staff lists of the same era, with no labels distinguishing victims from functionaries. The audience is deliberately mixed—survivors' descendants, local officials, high-school students on a required field trip, and historians of twentieth-century political philosophy who will recognize any misstep with Karl Jaspers’s framework of collective guilt. Your own family history complicates the occasion: one branch sheltered refugees; another branch processed the paperwork. You are a philosopher, not a lawyer, and you have been asked to speak about whether a nation—or a town—can bear guilt across generations, using Jaspers’s distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt as the structural spine of the address. The speech must do real work: it should honor those who suffered without offering false metaphysical consolation—phrases like “they watch over us” or “all is forgiven” are explicitly unavailable to you, and the survivors’ families have made clear they do not want them—while addressing the teenagers and officials without turning the monument into either a courtroom indictment or a feel-good ritual of closure.

To avoid both cheap absolution and indiscriminate accusation, you have been developing a local concept with an archivist—call it the “silence tax”—the idea that unacknowledged collective wrong does not disappear but compounds as a kind of civic fatigue, visible in eroded institutional trust rather than in direct punishment. You want this idea to shape the speech’s middle section, but you are unsure how far to push it: for the historians, it risks sounding like a sociological fiction unless it is tightly linked to Jaspers’s claim that political guilt is borne by citizens as members of a state, not distributed like criminal blame; for the families, it risks sounding like an abstraction that replaces real grief with a metaphor about bureaucracy; for the teenagers, it might explain why the monument lists unnamed names rather than heroes and villains. The speech should therefore navigate exactly what can and cannot be said under Jaspers’s distinctions—why we can speak of shared political responsibility without saying every present citizen is a perpetrator, why moral guilt may attach to bystanders who did not resist, and why metaphysical guilt, if mentioned at all, must not be used to blur these lines into a vague universal culpability. You need to land on a specific stance: that the monument’s refusal to label is not moral relativism but an acknowledgment of layered guilt, and that the town’s task is not to “move on” but to stop paying the silence tax through deliberate, structured remembrance.

Write the speech—approximately six to eight minutes—so that the philosophical framework genuinely constrains its language: specify which phrases are permissible and which are foreclosed by Jaspers’s distinctions, show how the silence-tax concept helps the mixed audience understand the monument’s design, and conclude with an invitation to the teenagers and officials that avoids both self-flagellation and complacency. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; do not quote Jaspers verbatim; and do not invent specific historical names or legal verdicts not implied by the prompt. The speech should feel like it could only be given at this particular wall, on this particular day, to these particular people.
