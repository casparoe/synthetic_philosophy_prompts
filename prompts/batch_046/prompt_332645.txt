In a seminar on well-being, an interlocutor keeps falling back on the line: "Only the absence of craving and suffering matters; every other supposed good is just a more elaborate form of disturbance." They deploy it to dismiss questions about whether knowledge, artistic creation, or loving relationships could be intrinsically valuable even for a being with no cravings. I need to bring a precise disambiguation to our next meeting. What are the distinct theses this line could be expressing — for instance, a claim about the ontology of value, a psychological claim about human motivation, a normative claim about rational preference, a metaphysical claim about the nature of suffering, or a methodological claim about how to measure well-being? Lay them out as a numbered menu. For each, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive, defensible, or false; whether it actually supports dismissing knowledge or love as "mere ornament"; and which version a defender of tranquilism would actually need to hold to make the dismissal stick. Do not appeal to specific authors or texts; work from the conceptual distinctions themselves.
