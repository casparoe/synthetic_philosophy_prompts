I run an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar with about twelve students, meeting for a single ninety-minute session. Design a structured in-class simulation — complete with assigned roles, staged decision rounds, physical or token-based materials, and a small-stakes payoff scheme — that makes participants feel, through what actually happens among them, that gradual mind uploading and substrate replacement do not dissolve the personal-identity problem but convert it into a live coordination problem between competing claimants to one autobiographical life. To keep the prompt from being generic, inject the following protocol feature: call it “asynchronous module migration with parallel operation,” a staged replacement in which old and new substrates both run concurrently but unsynchronized for an intermediate period, generating divergent first-person reports about the same recent experiences and forcing both to claim “narrative sovereignty” over a single stream of memory and commitment. Arrange the rules and incentives so that students must negotiate bodily control, journal authorship, and future promises between the “Host” and the “Successor” before anyone names the philosophical view, and structure the rounds so that they hit a “double-occupancy” conflict — both claimants present, both functionally continuous, neither clearly eliminable — that a simple fifty-one-percent cutoff cannot resolve. Include a precise timeline and role assignments, the payoff or scoring mechanism (no real money required), and instructions for the facilitator to stay silent during the conflict phases. Then provide a debrief that maps each simulation stage onto a specific feature of the gradual-replacement debate, predicts what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run (for example, students inventing an arbitrary sharp boundary just to end the discomfort), explains exactly how to handle that failure without delivering a lecture, and states frankly which feature of personal identity — actual phenomenological continuity experienced over long temporal durations — the activity necessarily cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally at the end. The idea should emerge from the rules and from the students’ own negotiations, not from an opening explanation; do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation request.
