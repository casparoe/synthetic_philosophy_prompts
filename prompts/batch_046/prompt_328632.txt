In a recent graduate seminar on the philosophy of history, I kept returning to the classical materialist dictum that social being determines consciousness, rather than the reverse—what is routinely compressed into the slogan that the economic base determines the superstructure. The immediate occasion was a disagreement over whether the emergence of open-access scholarly publishing norms in the late twentieth century should be explained chiefly by the collapsing marginal cost of digital reproduction (a shift in the economic base) or whether it represents an autonomous normative evolution within academic culture that retains independent causal efficacy (a superstructural development not reducible to base dynamics). Participants on one side kept invoking the slogan to dismiss appeals to scholars' intrinsic normative commitments as mere ideology, yet the phrase seemed to do radically different work in different mouths. Could you disentangle the precise theses concealed within that one-liner? Lay them out as a menu of distinct readings—from the trivial to the radical—including at least: (i) a weak causal-influence claim that economic conditions shape but do not fix ideas; (ii) a functionalist claim that superstructural elements tend to stabilize or reproduce the base; (iii) a strong reductive determinism that renders normative reasoning epiphenomenal; and (iv) a methodological priority claim about explanatory ordering rather than ontological reduction. For each, indicate whether it amounts to a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or a falsehood. Then tell me which specific reading is actually being invoked when someone uses the slogan to reject an argument about open-access values as automatically reducible to the economics of digital copying, and whether that reading genuinely licenses the dismissal or collapses into one of the others.
