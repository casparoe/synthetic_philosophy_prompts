I'm putting together a handout for an advanced aesthetics seminar, and after arguing with some friends about whether a thoroughly restored old master painting still counts as "the original" in any meaningful sense, I realized the standard "aura versus market price" framing misses the actual conceptual splits. I'd like you to construct a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with concrete branching questions—of positions on the value of originals, expert forgeries, and restored works, designed so that a student can answer each node without already knowing which philosophical label it corresponds to.

The tree should be built around three successive splits rather than around named theorists. First, ask whether the value at stake is primarily epistemic (direct access to the artist's actual gesture or intention), relational-causal (standing in the correct historical-causal chain regardless of appearance), or institutional (embedded in practices of attribution and display that could in principle be transferred). Second, ask whether material restoration that replaces substantial parts of the object destroys the value in question even when the visual and tactile result is indistinguishable from the pre-damaged state. Third, ask whether a perfect, undetectable forgery can ever instantiate any of the values the original carries, or whether its causal origin necessarily deprives it of them regardless of its properties. At each leaf, provide either the name of an established position (for instance, "pure causalism" or "institutional constructivism") or label it "unnamed," followed by one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—such as being forced to claim that two visually identical canvases hanging in the same gallery differ radically in value for no perceptible reason. Where the tree cannot cleanly separate two positions, flag that pair explicitly rather than papering over the overlap. Finally, identify any combination of answers that seems logically coherent but that nobody actually defends, and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Keep every branch phrased as a claim or case a respondent could agree or disagree with on substantive grounds, not as a definitional test of terminology.
