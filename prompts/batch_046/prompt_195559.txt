Consider the persistent impasse between moral foundations theorists and normative ethicists regarding whether the well-documented psychological divergence between left and right—liberals weighting care and fairness far more heavily, conservatives distributing weight across loyalty, authority, and sanctity—tells us anything about whose moral framework is closer to correct. For more than a decade, the psychologists have refined the descriptive asymmetry with cross-cultural data, while philosophers have repeatedly blocked the inference from “this is how cognition is distributed” to “this is how it ought to be distributed,” typically invoking some version of the naturalistic fallacy. Yet neither camp has substantially retreated, and the debate now circulates through the same moves: evolutionary-functional explanations on one side, demands for independent normative justification on the other. Diagnose this stall. Is it a verbal disagreement about whether “moral foundations” denotes a psychological mechanism or a normative pillar? Is it a substantive disagreement masked by shared vocabulary—perhaps about whether moral cognition is calibrated to distinct social ecologies and whether such calibration carries justificatory force? Or are the sides answering different questions entirely, with one investigating the architecture of judgment and the other asking which judgments survive reflective equilibrium? In your diagnosis, specify what each side is actually committed to: does the psychologist’s appeal to adaptive coherence implicitly treat psychological stability as normative authority, and does the philosopher’s resistance presuppose a unitary moral ontology that MFT itself denies? Finally, say what, if anything, could move the debate—an empirical finding about cross-cultural moral convergence, a conceptual distinction between moral epistemology and moral ontology, or an argument that the disagreement is bedrock and unresolvable. Do not survey the literature; analyze the structure of the disagreement itself.
