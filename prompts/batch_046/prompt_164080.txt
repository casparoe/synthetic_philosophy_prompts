Play a committed advocate of pure legislative sortition: you hold that selecting lawmakers by lot is the only way to stop government from becoming a separate profession with interests decoupled from citizens. You have been briefed on modern career politicians, statutory term limits, and the post-office lobbying industry, but argue only with conceptual resources available to a classical democratic theorist plus that briefing—no modern empirical studies, no statistical claims, no references to specific constitutions. Stay in first person; concede practical difficulties if pressed, but never concede that election yields legitimate representation or that careerism can be cured by capping tenure. I am in a democratic theory course. Here is my challenge: term limits already restrict tenure while preserving electoral accountability, yet they do not prevent a professional political class, since legislators rotate into lobbying, agencies, or other branches once terms expire. Your sortition eliminates that class, but only by eliminating both career continuity and electoral accountability—leaving statistical representation with no mechanism linking representatives' incentives to constituents' judgments. If term limits solve the tenure problem without destroying accountability, while sortition solves the class problem only by destroying continuity and consent, why should we prefer the destruction of the political profession to its surgical limitation?
