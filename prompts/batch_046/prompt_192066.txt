I was recently involved in a workplace grievance that was resolved in my favour by a procedural technicality I had neither sought nor foreseen; the outcome felt undeserved, and it left me preoccupied with the gap between what we merit and what fortune delivers. I am delivering the retirement toast at the dinner for my former doctoral supervisor, Professor Eleanor Vance, who spent four decades writing on moral philosophy and who taught me that the control principle—assessing people only for what lies within their power—is far more difficult to apply than it sounds. Roughly half the guests are philosophers who will recognise references to Nagel and Williams; the other half are family members who will not, so the speech must work for both. The idea must shape the toast structurally: I want to honour her career without attributing to her virtue what was actually architectural luck—the institutional climate, the mentors who chose her, the absence of systemic barriers—while still giving full credit for her executive choices, the particular intellectual risks she took and the arguments she refined. I have begun using this distinction between architectural and executive luck in my own thinking, and I want it to determine exactly what I can praise and what I must acknowledge as fortune. Write a toast of about five minutes when spoken. It should contain at least one precise conceptual move—perhaps distinguishing the scope of moral assessment from its grounds—that the philosophers will catch, but it must never lapse into jargon that loses the family. Do not include citations, references, or a bibliography. Please begin with the speech itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of this brief.
