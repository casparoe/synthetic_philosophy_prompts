I take the view that American folk morality—which treats individual conscience and situational exception-making as morally prior to procedural order—is ultimately more defensible than the German folk morality that subordinates personal judgment to rule-following, collective predictability, and what might be called administrative virtue. I want an honest map of this position’s exposure, not a defense. What observations, structured cases, or patterns in social psychology would genuinely count against it? In particular, are there conditions—perhaps involving normative overhang, where formally correct rules produce locally visible harms that participants recognize but are institutionally barred from remedying—where the German orientation protects moral goods that a conscience-centered culture systematically erodes through second-order instability? Which of these defeaters would committed defenders of the American view concede as serious, and which would they dismiss as misdescribing the phenomenon? Rank the threats from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate whether the position risks collapsing into generic liberal individualism or retains distinctive content that makes it vulnerable in its own right. I am especially interested in whether privileging conscience over procedure hides a cost in preserving long-run trust, and what it would take for that cost to outweigh the value of retained moral agency.
