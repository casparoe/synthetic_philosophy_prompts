I retired from a geology lab a few years back and have been reading philosophy for fun. Lately I've been stuck on conscription and these armed-neutrality countries -- Switzerland, Finland, Sweden -- and I cooked up an argument that proves way to much, but I cant find the exit.

(1) If democratic citizens are the ones who authorize war, then the people who actually fight should be drawn from that same citizen body; otherwise it's just a board ordering laborers into danger they won't share.

(2) A professional army breaks that link: the soldiers become a specialized class, and politicians can vote for conflicts without personal exposure.

(3) So democratic legitmacy requires universal conscription, not a volunteer force, whenever a state keeps armed forces.

(4) Armed neutrality doesn't change this, because Switzerland still trains every citizen, and Sweden and Finland kept conscription partly to preserve that citizen-soldier link.

(5) So even a country that swears never to fight offensively needs conscription if it wants democratic legitmacy in defense.

(6) But if that's right, then countries with all-volunteer forces -- the US, UK, Canada -- are systematically illegitimate in every military decision.

(7) That cant be right, so one step is broken.

(8) I want to keep (1) and (4) because they capture something real about moral hazard in outsourced defense, but I'm willing to pay for dropping (3) if that's the cleaner fix. I feel like there's a slide somewhere between authorization and obligation -- maybe an equivocation, maybe a scope shift -- but I cant name it. Which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
