You are a professional philosopher specializing in epistemology and the philosophy of mind, with particular familiarity with Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and its Limits. Before evaluating, briefly outline for yourself Williamson's actual definitions of luminosity and the margin-for-error principle, his stance on the KK principle, and the structure of his anti-luminosity sorites argument. Then proceed.

A philosophy blog post I read offers this summary: Williamson introduces luminosity as the claim that whenever a condition—such as feeling cold or knowing that p—obtains, the subject is automatically in a position to know that it obtains. The post says Williamson thinks knowledge is luminous, which is exactly why he accepts the KK principle, that knowing p entails knowing that you know p. To defend this pairing, he runs a sorites-style case: imagine a room that dims imperceptibly, one lumen at a time. At stage 0 you know it is bright; because knowledge is luminous, at stage 1—barely darker—you still know it is bright. Iterating this eventually forces the conclusion that you know the room is pitch black, which is absurd. So, the post concludes, Williamson rejects luminosity and therefore also abandons the KK principle: he holds that one can know p without knowing that one knows p. The post adds a further moral: this breakdown of transparency shows that introspection is generally unreliable for knowledge-attributions, and that the real philosophical lesson concerns vagueness in mental-state concepts rather than anything distinctive about epistemic iteration.

Audit this summary claim by claim. Separate what is accurate, what is a standard popular simplification, and what is subtly or clearly wrong—paying special attention to whether the post reverses Williamson's actual defense of the KK principle, whether it conflates luminosity with transparency or introspective access, and whether the sorites case is being used to defend or to undermine the view attributed to him. Finally, assess whether the post's concluding moral about introspection and vagueness survives correction, or whether it depends on the misrepresentations you have identified.
