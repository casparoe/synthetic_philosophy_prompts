I have recently been appointed conceptual advisor for an interdisciplinary museum exhibition on technological and institutional futures, and in developing the framing narrative I have become convinced that public understanding of historical inevitability is both oversimplified and consequential for policy. Write an essay addressing whether history is genuinely “locked in” through path dependence, or whether the same functional outcomes would have arrived regardless of early contingent events. Weigh the principal arguments for strong path dependence—including increasing returns, network externalities, irreversible institutional commitments, and the “frozen accident” logic familiar from evolutionary biology—against convergence arguments that invoke functional selection pressures, competitive optimization, and evolutionary analogies suggesting that surface forms may vary while deep functional equivalents eventually emerge. In your analysis, apply the distinction I have been using between “hard path dependence” (where an early contingent choice structurally forecloses genuinely different alternatives) and “soft convergence” (where institutional or technological surface forms differ across counterfactual histories, but functionally equivalent outcomes eventually dominate). Consider at least two domains—such as energy infrastructure and the methodology of empirical science—rather than treating history as monolithic. Reach and justify a specific verdict: does one framework dominate across these domains, or must we adopt a domain-specific hybrid? Your conclusion should not end on a non-committal note; defend it with explicit reasoning about where the lock-in is real, where it is illusory, and what this implies for how we should treat present-day institutional choices that appear irreversible.
