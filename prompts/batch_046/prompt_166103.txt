I've been reflecting on the old Damascus story lately—partly because a close friend recently relocated despite a family warning about danger in a specific city, and partly because I'm drafting a seminar paper on logical fatalism and future contingents. In the classic version, a man learns he is fated to die in Damascus, flees to Aleppo, and is killed there when a collapsing scaffold bears an inscription naming Damascus. I have come to accept a particular resolution: the fatalist argument from bivalence plus the principle of the fixity of the past fails not because future-tensed statements lack truth-values, but because it conflates the temporal indexical "will die in Damascus" with a de re modal property that binds every prior node in the causal chain. More precisely, I think the argument only succeeds if we treat the inevitability of the event as equivalent to the inevitability of the path taken—if we deny that the agent's flight can itself be part of the causal sequence that makes the prophecy true without being causally determined by it in advance. If we distinguish path-inevitability from event-inevitability, then the Damascus scenario undermines not free will but only our tendency to read backward narrative coherence as backward causation. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which premises about the logic of future contingents, which alternative readings of the Damascus narrative, and which results in the free will literature—say, about branching-time models or the transfer of necessity—would actually damage it? Which objections would defenders of this view concede as serious rather than merely awkward, and which can be dismissed as conflating logical with causal necessity? I'd especially like to know whether the position risks unfalsifiability—whether every counterexample can be absorbed by redrawing the line between path and event—and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice for a view meant to preserve both bivalence and practical agency.
