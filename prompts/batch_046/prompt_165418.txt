I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of mind where we're examining how intuition pumps shape judgments about consciousness, and I found myself caught in a disagreement between two fellow students, Marcus and Elena, over a modified zombie scenario Marcus proposed for a presentation. He describes what he calls the Gradual Replacement City: imagine a metropolis of ten million people in which, over the course of several generations, each resident is quietly replaced by a physically and behaviorally identical counterpart that entirely lacks phenomenal consciousness. Because the replacements occur one individual at a time and social institutions adapt imperceptibly, the city never collapses; governance, commerce, and cultural life continue exactly as before, even after every original conscious inhabitant has vanished. Marcus claims this pumps a powerful intuition that phenomenal consciousness is causally inert—since its total absence produces no observable disruption, its presence explains nothing in the physical dynamics of the world—and he insists the scenario is genuinely conceivable, not contradictory, because we are merely stipulating phenomenal absence in functional duplicates rather than deriving it. Elena objects that the pump succeeds only by concealing a hidden premise: that behavioral and functional equivalence entails phenomenal absence, which is precisely the physicalist thesis that needs defending rather than assuming. Marcus replies that he is not arguing from equivalence to absence but inviting us to imagine absence as a bare stipulation and then to notice its functional invisibility; Elena counters that stipulating phenomenal absence in a being that retains every behavioral and cognitive role tied to consciousness is conceptually unstable, comparable to stipulating a square circle, because our concept of a conscious agent is internally connected to those roles, so the scenario does not describe a real metaphysical possibility but a contradictory fiction made palatable by sorites-style framing. Marcus then concedes that intuition pumps are not logical proofs but heuristic devices, arguing that if the scenario genuinely unsettles our naive conviction that consciousness must make a causal difference, then it has exposed a gap in our pre-theoretical commitments regardless of whether it establishes physicalism. Elena responds that a heuristic which achieves its effect by masking a contradiction through incremental replacement is not illuminating but deceptive, and that both of them have ignored how the pump's persuasiveness might come not from conceivability but from an affective shift—our discomfort at imagining empty shells running a society—which is a psychological artifact rather than philosophical insight.
