I observed a sharp exchange between two colleagues—Dr. Lena Okonkwo and Dr. Samuel Reed—during a recent workshop on distributive ethics. I stayed out of the argument, and neither convinced the other. Here is the exchange, reproduced as faithfully as I can.

Lena opened by arguing that prioritarianism escapes the leveling-down objection entirely. The objection, she said, applies only to egalitarian views that treat equality as intrinsically valuable. Prioritarianism assigns moral weights to individuals solely as a function of their absolute well-being levels. In pure leveling-down—where the better-off are made worse off without anyone being made better off—the weighted sum necessarily falls. Therefore prioritarianism never recommends it. To charge prioritarianism with vulnerability here, she concluded, is a category mistake: it confuses a distributive weighting scheme with an egalitarian axiology.

Samuel replied that this defense works only if well-being is entirely non-relational and the weighting function is strictly separable across individuals. He proposed a modified framework—relational-sensitive prioritarianism—where an individual's well-being includes a comparative component, such as relative social status or access to positional goods. On this view, reducing the well-being of the best-off can indirectly raise the well-being of the worst-off by lowering the baseline of comparison, even without material transfer. In such cases, the weighted sum might increase after a leveling-down move. So prioritarianism, if it takes well-being seriously, cannot dismiss the objection so quickly; it must either deny that well-being is comparative—which he called empirically dubious—or admit that leveling-down can be justified under its own logic.

Lena countered that Samuel had smuggled egalitarianism back in through the back door. Once well-being depends on relative position, she argued, one is no longer within prioritarianism but within a hybrid relational-egalitarian framework. The core prioritarian commitment is that moral urgency attaches to absolute deprivation, not comparative disadvantage. Even if comparative disadvantage affects absolute well-being, the direct recommendation is still to improve absolute conditions, not to level down. The leveling-down objection targets direct recommendations to reduce the top for equality's sake, and prioritarianism makes none.

Samuel pushed further. Even granting Lena's point about direct recommendations, he said, standard prioritarian weighting functions—typically concave transformations of absolute well-being—do not specify how to aggregate when interaction effects exist. If the marginal value of benefiting B depends on A's level—for example, because the social meaning of B's deprivation is intensified by A's extravagance—then the aggregation is non-separable. Lowering A could then be part of an optimal package for maximizing the weighted sum, not because equality is good in itself, but because A's high well-being suppresses the weight assigned to B. This, he insisted, is not smuggling egalitarianism; it is recognizing that the weight function itself may carry cross-individual parameters.
