Write a dialogue between two decision theorists, Mara and Jonas, both fully versed in game-theoretic rationalizability, debating whether full iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies—allowing domination by arbitrary mixtures—can be coherently applied in institutional settings with continuous strategy spaces. Frame their disagreement around a concrete but non-standard scenario: a three-member arbitration board in which each member simultaneously submits a binding settlement figure from a continuous interval, the board’s decision rule selects the median proposal, and each member’s utility depends on that median plus a private bias term (unknown to the others) that shifts their ideal point. Mara should defend the convex-rationalist position: that common knowledge of rationality requires each member to eliminate any proposal lying outside the convex hull of the rationalizable proposals for the other two members, and that this should be iterated until a fixed set remains, even if the dominating mixture is non-degenerate. Jonas should defend the epistemic-minimalist position: that in continuous spaces with private bias terms, identifying the precise mixture weights needed to establish dominance requires knowing the very distribution of surviving strategies one is trying to compute, so the procedure either presupposes the conclusion or demands probabilistic beliefs about opponents that go beyond what common knowledge of rationality alone can supply. Require that they respond to each other’s actual arguments rather than talking past them—Mara must address Jonas’s circularity charge by distinguishing belief in rationality from belief in specific probabilities, and Jonas must address that distinction by showing why constructing the dominating mixture still requires fixing weights contingent on the rationalizable set itself. Have them reach no easy resolution, but require each to concede one valid point made by the other. Finally, instruct the response to separate what is technically settled about iterated dominance with mixtures—specifically, that allowing mixtures can strictly shrink the rationalizable set compared to pure-strategy elimination alone, and that the procedure is formally well-defined for finite games—from what remains genuinely contested, including whether the epistemic conditions for applying mixture-dominance are coherent with the common-knowledge premises that motivate the iteration, and whether institutional settings with continuous proposals and private information can satisfy those conditions. Ask that no contested interpretation be presented as the consensus view among specialists.
