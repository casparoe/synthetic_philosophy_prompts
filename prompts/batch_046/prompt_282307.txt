I spent most of last evening arguing with a close friend—she works in virtue ethics—about whether any positive mental state carries intrinsic well-being value, or whether well-being is strictly the absence of craving and suffering. She kept pressing the asymmetry objection: if suffering is intrinsically bad, why treat pleasure as merely the temporary suspension of an absence rather than as intrinsically good in its own right? I think she misses the phenomenological structure, but the exchange made me realize that my position needs sharper articulation before I defend it publicly. Next Thursday I present at a workshop on theories of well-being; the audience is about fifteen people, mostly analytic ethicists—several committed virtue ethicists, a couple of preference-satisfaction theorists, and one Buddhist scholar who specializes in duhkha. I defend what I am calling tranquilism: well-being is exhaustively constituted by the absence of craving and suffering, while apparently positive goods—achievement, affection, aesthetic rapture—have at most instrumental value as mechanisms that suppress or distract from craving. I support this with a phenomenal-contrast argument: we register positive states only against a background of prior deficit or tension, so their apparent intrinsic value is actually the value of relief. I extend this to reject flourishing accounts not merely normatively but metaphysically, treating flourishing as a reification of temporary equilibrium states that masks an underlying absence.

Prepare me for the Q&A: give me the objections this specific audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they will come, each phrased the way a virtue ethicist or a preference-satisfaction theorist would put it. For each, I need a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is weakest and where I should concede rather than overreach. In particular, I need to know how to handle the charge that my contrast argument proves too much—collapsing all experience into negative relief—and whether I should concede up front that tranquilism struggles to explain why agents pursue goods before they suffer. I also need the objection I am hoping nobody asks, likely something about whether my view collapses into negative utilitarianism or whether it makes well-being indistinguishable from non-existence. Do not give me generic presentation advice or bibliographic suggestions; I need a precise prep sheet mapping each likely attack onto the part of the argument it targets, so I can see exactly where the defense breaks if I fail to hold the line.
