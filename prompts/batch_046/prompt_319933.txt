Examination Question:
Thomson's lamp begins in the on position and is toggled at intervals that halve in duration—one minute, thirty seconds, fifteen seconds, and so forth—converging at the two-minute mark. At exactly two minutes, is the lamp on or off? First, present the argument that there is no determinate answer, attending closely to the symmetry of the toggle sequence and the absence of a final operation. Then address the determinacy objection—that an actual lamp must occupy a definite physical state—without appealing to empirical physics, but rather by examining whether the notion of a completed infinity demands what might be termed narrative closure: a terminal event that resolves the sequence. Is the absence of such closure a defect in the physical description, or in our expectation that every process must conclude?

Student's Answer:
The lamp begins on. The toggles occur at one minute, thirty seconds, fifteen seconds, and so on, producing infinitely many state-changes that accumulate at the two-minute mark. Because the cardinality of the toggle set is infinite, there is no final operation, and therefore no basis for assigning even or odd parity to the sequence. The states are symmetric: without a terminal toggle, there is no reason to prefer on over off. Hence, at the limit, the lamp has no determinate state. This is a supertask, an infinite task completed in a finite span; its logical consistency is secured precisely because the limit point is not a member of the sequence, so no contradiction arises from an infinite number of changes.

Against the determinacy objection, one should recognize that the objection presupposes a discrete endpoint—a last instant at which the state is fixed. But time is continuous; the two-minute point is a limit, not an endpoint. Consequently, asking whether the lamp is on at exactly two minutes is like asking for the value of a function at an essential discontinuity: the predicate lacks application conditions, not because the world is contradictory, but because the process has no narrative closure. As Thomson herself notes, in the absence of a causal toggle at the boundary, the lamp retains its initial state—on—by default, though this is a logical rather than physical retention. Furthermore, just as Zeno's dichotomy is resolved once we see that an infinite series of spatial intervals can sum to a finite distance, so too the lamp's temporal sequence sums to exactly two minutes, which means the task is fully completed and its outcome should be determinable; the only obstacle is our lack of a parity rule for infinite sets, which is a notational limitation rather than an ontological gap. Thus, the absence of an ending does not imply a suspended or superposed state; it implies that the concept of state-application has reached its boundary.
