I'm trying to work out my actual commitments about whether prosperity and social order can legitimate authoritarian governance, testing my intuitions against concrete cases rather than abstract slogans. Singapore under Lee Kuan Yew delivered extraordinary material development, low corruption, and public safety through severely restricted political competition and press control; my gut says this arrangement was partially justified, though not fully, because the trade was explicit, the benefits were broadly shared, and some institutional feedback channels—however narrow—remained open. China's post-1978 trajectory, lifting hundreds of millions from poverty while maintaining one-party rule with extensive surveillance and the structural elimination of dissent, feels different to me—more defensible on pure welfare grounds, yet somehow less legitimate—because I think the absence of any credible mechanism for error-correction or leadership replacement matters independently of the welfare gains achieved. A hypothetical benevolent autocrat who delivers identical GDP growth and crime reduction but permits local councils to debate policy without party control feels more legitimate than the real-world analogues, which suggests my judgment tracks something closer to institutional reversibility than to output alone. The tension becomes sharpest with a paired comparison: Pinochet's Chile achieved macroeconomic stabilization and growth for parts of society alongside systematic torture and disappearance, and my verdict is that this is not legitimate under any framing, whereas a democratic regime that delivers the same average prosperity but through volatile, corrupt, clientelistic politics feels less legitimate in practice yet remains legitimate in principle—implying that my view allows democratic failure to diminish quality without destroying justification, while authoritarian success cannot build it from scratch if basic recognition is absent. There's also the case of a prosperous authoritarian enclave that delivers excellent outcomes for citizens but systematically excludes a resident minority from those benefits; my intuition is that the regime's legitimacy collapses entirely, not merely distributively, which points toward a clause about non-instrumental recognition of all subjects under the regime's power. I keep returning to whether my resistance to the Chinese case is merely a liberal bias I ought to discard, or whether it genuinely tracks an epistemic condition—some form of openness to correction—that prosperity and order cannot substitute for. Could you state the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that best captures this pattern, specifying which clause is motivated by which case, applying it to an unconsidered scenario—say, a technocratic authoritarian system that achieves climate stability and universal health outcomes but prohibits all electoral politics and independent courts—and being explicit about which of my verdicts each principle must overturn?
