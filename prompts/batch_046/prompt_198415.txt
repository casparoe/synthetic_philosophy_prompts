I'm developing material for a philosophy-writing workshop on applied ethics, and I need a sustained parody that works from inside the view rather than mocking it from the outside. Please write a mock agony-aunt column—approximately 600 words, in the deadpan, earnestly formal voice of a fictional magazine called Applied Deontics—responding to three brief reader letters about mundane social dilemmas: whether to warn a colleague that their presentation contains an error, whether to remind a roommate that rent is overdue, and whether to turn off a noisy air conditioner that is keeping a neighbor awake. The columnist must be an unwavering defender of the doing/allowing distinction, treating it as morally decisive and never sliding into consequentialist reasoning; every answer should turn on whether the reader's proposed intervention counts as "doing harm" or "merely allowing harm to occur."

To keep the satire conceptually sharp rather than vague, invent and consistently apply a three-tier taxonomy of omissions—call them Passive Non-Intervention, Active Withholding, and Constructed Absence—so that the columnist can classify each reader's situation with exaggerated rigor. The humor should come from extending this taxonomy into ordinary life: for instance, deciding whether reminding someone of rent is Active Withholding of financial distress or Constructed Absence of relief, and treating the distinction as carrying clear moral weight. The piece should be legible to a reader who knows the philosophical debate about baselines, continuous cases, and the asymmetry between killing and letting die, but it should not depend on recognizing any specific author or text.

Keep the tone deadpan and internal—no winking, no direct statement that the view is absurd—so that the satire operates through the logic of the column itself. Do not include footnotes, citations, bibliographic references, or named philosophers; let the conceptual machinery stand on its own. The goal is a piece that feels fair to the distinction being parodied because it genuinely understands what makes it philosophically difficult, even as it applies that difficulty to questions about air conditioners and rent reminders.
