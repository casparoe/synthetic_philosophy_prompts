I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether Jeremy Bentham’s defense of consensual non-procreative sexual practices can be reconciled with his broader commitment to a transparent, calculable social order. To organize my thoughts, I have sketched the following notes for a short essay, and I would value your critical comments on their coherence, historical plausibility, and philosophical rigor.

The thesis I want to defend is that Bentham’s argument in his essay on offences against oneself—specifically the part in which he contends that acts such as paederasty cause no direct harm and therefore ought not to be criminalized—does not merely extend his utilitarian calculus into the private sphere, but actually introduces a structural tension with the architectural logic of the Panopticon. In the Panopticon writings, Bentham treats visibility and the anticipation of observation as prerequisites for rational utility-maximization; the subject modifies conduct because he might be watched. By contrast, in his sexual ethics Bentham celebrates practices that are inherently concealed from public view, and he dismisses the popular antipathy toward them as an irrational prejudice unfit to ground legislation. If significant pleasure can be maximized in spaces entirely opaque to any inspector, then the panoptic model of social discipline loses its purchase over a substantial domain of human behavior. I propose that Bentham resolves this tension not by extending surveillance into private life, but by redefining the relevant public: the community whose interests count is not the observing crowd, but the aggregate of affected individuals, including those whose sympathies are not aroused. On my reading, this marks a subtle but real shift in his theory of value—from one in which social transparency secures utility, to one in which utility is secured precisely by shielding certain pleasures from social transparency.

My friend objected that I am importing too much architectural metaphor into Bentham’s ethics, noting that the Panopticon concerns institutional design rather than a general epistemology of pleasure. She also observed that in unpublished manuscript drafts on sexuality, Bentham occasionally voices concern about demoralization spreading through example, which sounds like a worry about visibility after all. I do not want to overstate the importance of those unpublished fragments, but I am unsure whether they undermine my reading or can be interpreted consistently with it. Could you comment on whether the tension I describe is genuinely present in Bentham’s published work, whether my proposed resolution—privileging aggregate welfare over visibility—does justice to his utilitarianism, and whether there are parts of my argument that strike you as historically anachronistic or philosophically muddled? I am especially interested in sharpening the distinction between Bentham’s descriptive psychology of antipathy and his normative claim that antipathy can never legitimate coercive law. Any suggestions for strengthening the thesis, identifying hidden premises, or pointing out weaknesses I have overlooked would be very welcome.
