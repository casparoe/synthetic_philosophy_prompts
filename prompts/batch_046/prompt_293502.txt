For my philosophy assignment on historical ethics, I'm examining Benjamin Franklin's autobiographical scheme for moral perfection and the judgments he records about particular behaviors. He treated a practical return to meat-eating after adopting vegetarianism for economy as consistent with frugality rather than a failure of temperance; he treated breaking silence solely to correct a dangerous error as excusable rather than a violation; and he treated missing one day's virtue-charting during unavoidable travel as not undermining order. At the same time, he consistently marked unnecessary decorative expenditure as a failure of frugality, and repeating an unverified claim as a failure of sincerity. From these verdicts, what are the two or three most plausible rules that could jointly explain them—perhaps a distinction between instrumental and expressive violations, or between private inconvenience and public harm? Applying the rule you think best fits Franklin's own reasoning, should he regard relying entirely on a large language model to draft all of one's personal correspondence—thereby saving labor but outsourcing the practice of sincerity and industry—as a moral failure within his scheme? If so, identify the virtue it violates; if not, name the closest analog in his track record and state the principle he would be adopting by excusing it. Finally, propose the single hypothetical further case—say, a synthetic agent that independently maintains its own chart of thirteen analog virtues—that would best distinguish the competing rules you identified.
