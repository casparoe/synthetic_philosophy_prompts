I sit on an intergovernmental advisory board that issues non-binding recommendations on whether proposed constitutional amendments should grant courts the authority to invalidate primary legislation. Over the past three years we have delivered five opinions that now form our working track record, though members disagree about the principle that unifies them. We endorsed strong-form judicial review—full strike-down authority—for a small federal republic with a history of ethnic majoritarianism, weak legislative parties, and no prior written constitutional tradition, arguing that rights protection required an external check despite the judiciary's limited constitutional experience. We rejected even weak-form review for a mature parliamentary democracy with an unwritten constitution, deeply embedded conventions protecting minority rights, and a long history of legislative self-correction, holding that judicial intervention would undermine democratic legitimacy without improving rights outcomes. We endorsed a dialogue model—non-binding declarations of incompatibility with a legislative response mechanism—for a common-law jurisdiction that had recently codified a bill of rights but retained a strong culture of parliamentary sovereignty, treating the mechanism as an interpretive aid rather than a veto. We rejected that same dialogue model for a presidential system with a highly fragmented legislature and persistent executive overreach, insisting that only strengthened electoral and party mechanisms, not judicial pronouncements, could restore democratic accountability. Finally, we approved a narrowly circumscribed proportionality review for a unitary state with no constitutional court but an expert administrative judiciary, allowing courts to assess whether legislative means were rationally connected to stated ends without examining the ends themselves.

We must now advise on a proposal for a semi-presidential republic that combines a directly elected president with a bicameral legislature, where the lower house has recently been captured by a narrow coalition of economic interests that have systematically diluted socio-economic guarantees in the charter. The charter itself was adopted only five years ago and explicitly includes justiciable rights to housing, healthcare access, and labor protection—rights that have never been interpreted by the courts. The judiciary is technically independent but drawn almost exclusively from a single elite legal academy with no prior experience in constitutional interpretation, and public trust in courts is lower than trust in the legislature. The proposed amendment would grant a seven-member constitutional bench the power to invalidate legislation for charter violations, but only upon a five-judge supermajority; it also includes a legislative-response clause allowing parliament to revise and re-enact a modified version within ninety days, though not to override permanently.

What should we recommend if we wish to remain consistent with our own precedents? Identify the closest analog among our five prior opinions and explain whether consistency demands approval, rejection, or a modified dialogue mechanism. Then articulate at least two distinct rules—perhaps protective, democratic-legitimacy, institutional-competence, or interpretive-tradition based—that each accommodate all five past verdicts, state what each rule predicts for this semi-presidential case, and indicate your best guess with reasons for preferring it. Finally, specify what further institutional feature—such as the presence of direct-democracy recall mechanisms, or the distinction between negative and positive socio-economic rights—would force a choice between these rules, and note whether adopting either position in this case commits us to revising any earlier recommendation.
