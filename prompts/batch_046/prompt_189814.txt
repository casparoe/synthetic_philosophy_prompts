Please act as an expert moral philosopher specializing in the structure of obligation and supererogation, but reason from first principles rather than citing texts. Before delivering your verdict, spend a few sentences brainstorming the conceptual landscape: distinguish individual entitlement from collective coordination, the role of personally borne cost in generating praise asymmetries, and whether aggregation can dissolve or preserve a beyond-duty category.

In a seminar on ethical theory, two colleagues—Maya and Jonah—got stuck in a back-and-forth about whether supererogation is a real deontic category or just a framing effect. Maya opened by arguing that supererogation collapses under scrutiny: if an act produces sufficient moral reason and no stronger competing reason blocks it, then it is required. The feeling that something is “above and beyond” is, on her view, just psychological awe at the magnitude of the good or the cost, not evidence of a distinct status between “required” and “merely permitted.” Jonah replied that this misses the crucial asymmetry in blame and praise. We do not blame someone for failing to donate a kidney, yet we praise the donor differently than we praise someone who refrains from assault. For Jonah, supererogation tracks the boundary of legitimate moral demand: others are not authorized to compel an agent to bear a certain kind of personal cost, so omission is fully permissible despite being suboptimal, and voluntary acceptance of that cost generates a special evaluative response.

Maya countered that Jonah has only restated a rights-based permission, not established a positive category. “Permissible but good” already explains why omission is not blameworthy; adding “supererogatory” is redundant rhetoric unless it does independent work. Jonah then introduced a more specific framework he called a distributed-threshold model. On this view, an act is supererogatory not when its aggregate good exceeds some global bar, but when an agent voluntarily crosses a personal cost-threshold that others lack standing to impose. He then extended the model collectively: a group can form an unrequired coordination mechanism—say, a mutual-aid rescue network—where each individual contribution is small and within ordinary duty, but the collective organization itself crosses a group-level threshold. No single member was required to initiate the network, yet the coordinated result is heroic. So, Jonah claimed, collective supererogation is coherent even when every constituent act is within duty.

Maya pushed back that Jonah commits a category error. If no individual was required to organize and each contribution is permissible, then the collective result is merely the sum of permitted acts, not a collective instance of going beyond duty. Applying “threshold” to the group, she said, is like attributing guilt to a crowd when every member is innocent: it assumes methodological individualism is false without argument. The special gratitude we feel toward organizers, she added, is explained by counterfactual comparison—things would have been worse without them—not by a mysterious collective deontic status.

Jonah answered that methodological individualism is precisely what is at issue in institutional ethics. In practical contexts, coordination mechanisms can generate obligations that do not exist for isolated agents, and gratitude directed specifically at organizers reflects recognition that they bore a distinctive initiating cost—social risk, administrative burden, opportunity cost—that was not distributively required of anyone. To reduce this to summed permissions, he argued, eliminates the phenomenon that needs explaining: why we distinguish sharply between a crowd that happens to help and a group that deliberately builds an unrequired system of aid.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. Who is right about whether supererogation reduces to “permissible but good”? Is Jonah’s distributed-threshold model coherent, and does it genuinely preserve supererogation rather than rename aggregation? Can collective supererogation exist if every individual contribution is within duty, or is Maya’s category-error charge decisive? What are both parties missing—perhaps about the relationship between praise, demand, and institutional design? Finally, state who is more right overall and what conceptual distinction or thought experiment would most help resolve what remains unsettled.
