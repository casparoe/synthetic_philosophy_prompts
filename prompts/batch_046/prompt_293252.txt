Student answer: Frege noticed that "Hesperus is Hesperus" is trivial and knowable a priori, while "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is a major empirical discovery, despite both names referring to Venus. If meaning were only reference, the statements should have identical cognitive value. Frege solved this with the Sinn/Bedeutung distinction: the names share the same Bedeutung (Venus) but have different Sinne—one presenting Venus as the morning star, the other as the evening star—so the thoughts expressed differ.

This resolution does commit Frege to psychologism, because Sinn is essentially a private mental idea. Different speakers might associate different mental images with "Hesperus," so the sense is subjective and not publicly shared.

As for direct reference, Frege actually anticipated causal-chain theories of naming. He thought that once a name's reference is initially fixed by a description, it can be transmitted causally with the Bedeutung intact but without retaining any Sinn. Therefore, in cases like "Hesperus is Phosphorus," the informativeness comes not from different senses but from learning that two causally linked names converge on the same object. This shows that direct reference can explain the discovery without Fregean modes of presentation.

Because the senses differ, substituting one name for the other in belief reports can change the truth value, but in every other context—including mathematical equations—substitution is always valid and carries no change in meaning whatsoever.
