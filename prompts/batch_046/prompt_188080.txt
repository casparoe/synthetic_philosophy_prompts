I'm working on a local history project about how our town remembers the closure of the mill in 1987. The archival record is unambiguous: the mill declined over eight years, with partial layoffs, two ownership changes, and a final bankruptcy that lasted eighteen months. But in every oral interview I have conducted, residents describe it as "the day they locked the gates"—a single morning, a single betrayal by one manager, a single community wound. When I show people the timeline, they do not dispute the dates. Instead they say things like, "That may be what the papers say, but that is not what happened." They treat the historian's complex account not as wrong in detail, but as belonging to an entirely different kind of thing—like describing a funeral by listing biochemical processes. I have seen parallel patterns elsewhere: a neighborhood remembers a riot as "when they came for us," though police logs show scattered incidents across weeks; a family remembers a bankruptcy as "the day everything was taken," though court filings show a gradual liquidation over months. In each case the community is not inventing facts; it is defending a specific shape—sudden, bounded, morally unified—that the record dissolves into continuity.

Does this resistance have an established name? I am familiar with the general distinction between collective memory and historical record, but I am asking about something more precise: the refusal not of evidence but of narrative morphology, where the dispute is over whether an event can remain "the same event" once its temporal boundaries, causal structure, and moral coherence are redrawn. If there is a concept for this specific kind of conflict—where a community accepts the facts yet rejects the historian's reconfiguration of what counts as part of the event—I would like to know it and how it relates to ordinary myth-making, to selective remembrance, and to simple factual error. If no established term captures it exactly, I would welcome a precise coinage together with its boundary conditions: what distinguishes it from cases where people genuinely deny documented events, and from cases where memory is merely abbreviated rather than structurally defended? What would the concept predict about a situation in which historians deliberately present a complex past in the community's own bounded, singular form—would that be seen as faithful representation, or as a different kind of distortion altogether?
