I was having an argument with my friend last week about why corruption indexes always rank Nordic countries at the top and places with heavy clientelist cultures near the bottom. The thing is, these surveys treat giving your kid's teacher a small gift at the end of term, or bringing a contractor lunch while they inspect your building permit, as signs of corruption. But they don't treat a senator taking a fundraiser's call and then inserting a line-item in a bill as corruption — they call that "constituency service" or "access". So the practice of global anti-corruption auditing seems to assume that corruption is specifically the violation of an impersonal, rule-bound bureaucracy, not just influence-trading in generall. Is that the only way to read it?

What philosophical commitments does that auditing practice actually presuppose? Does it need a theory that says formal-legal neutrality is the only legitimate basis for goverment action, and that any personal obligation or gift-reciprocity is a moral defect? Or could the same practice be justified by a pure efficiency argument — that clientelism just produces worse outcomes — without assuming Western norms are universal? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and tell me whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a communitarian who thinks gift-obligations are part of social fabric still accept these rankings, or does the practice quietly rule that out?

Also, if we tried to build a corruption metric that wasn't just exporting one culture's idea of the state, what would it have to measure instead? Would it have to distinguish "transactional" influence from "relational" obligation, and if so, what concept of corruption survives that seperate distinction?
