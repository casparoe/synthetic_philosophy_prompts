I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has turned to philosophy of history and ethics. Next month I will moderate a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher—let me refer to her as Dr. Vasquez—who defends a view she calls mnemonic non-interference. She argues that dramatizations of real, deceased persons do not wrong the dead themselves, since the dead lack interests and moral standing, but that they can inflict a distinct damage on living audiences through what she terms counterfactual biography: a representation that introduces no checkable falsehood—names, dates, and documented utterances remain intact—yet systematically selects, compresses, and re-sequences events to generate an emotional or thematic arc that the actual life never supported. Over time, she claims, such portrayals achieve mnemonic saturation, displacing the more complex archival figure in public memory without leaving any explicit record of distortion. Her normative claim is therefore not traditional fidelity to biography, but the preservation of a register separation between narrative art and historical record, enforced not by censorship but by transparent framing devices—explicit anachronism, fictionalized dialogue markers, or structural disclaimers—that prevent audiences from treating the dramatization as an epistemic substitute. Critics reply that her distinction between archival harm, which falsifies evidence, and mnemonic harm, which merely displaces memory, collapses in practice, because most biopics achieve their effects precisely through the compression she describes, and that privileging register separation ultimately privileges documentary austerity over artistic expression. I want a sequence of interview questions that makes her standard deflections—particularly her claim that artistic license and mnemonic harm operate in separate registers—progressively harder to maintain. The sequence should anticipate her likely reply to each pressure point and include the immediate follow-up, and it should end with one precise question about what empirical finding about audience memory, or what conceptual revision to her framework, would actually count as evidence against her view. Please avoid generic biographical openings and do not ask for bibliographies or reading lists.
