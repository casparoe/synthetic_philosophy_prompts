I have been working through Nietzsche's idea of eternal recurrence, especially in the form where it functions as an ethical criterion rather than a cosmological claim—something like asking whether one could will the eternal repetition of one's present life without recoiling in horror—and I keep noticing the same argumentative pattern in both classroom discussions and secondary accounts that I do not have a precise name for. The pattern runs like this. A speaker first presents recurrence as a practical test: because every moment returns infinitely, the quality of one's present choice carries infinite weight, and one ought to live only in ways that one could affirm being repeated forever. Then an objector points out that if the sequence is already fixed, then present willing changes nothing; the loop will unfold identically regardless of one's attitude, so the criterion cannot actually guide action or ground responsibility. At this point the speaker shifts ground, arguing that recurrence is not meant to change outcomes but to describe an attitude—an unconditional affirmation of fate, a kind of embrace of whatever occurs—so that the ethical demand is not about altering the loop but about transforming one's relation to it. But immediately afterward, in order to show that this attitude is not ethically empty, the speaker appeals to vivid examples of cruelty, pettiness, or suffering that one would clearly not want to see repeated without end, thereby treating the content of the repetition as the very thing that supplies normative force. I have seen this happen repeatedly, always with the same rhythm: deny that recurrence has consequential relevance in order to escape fatalism, then quietly reintroduce consequential horror to preserve the criterion's bite.

Is there an established concept for this specific oscillation—this simultaneous denial and reliance on the normative significance of what the loop contains? It does not seem to be plain equivocation, since the speaker is not merely sliding between two unrelated meanings of a single term; it is not quite the motte-and-bailey structure, because neither register is an obviously indefensible extension hidden behind a defensible core, and the two positions are presented with roughly equal emphasis rather than being deployed strategically for defense and offense. Nor is it a straightforward performative contradiction, because the speaker can hold both views in different contexts without explicit logical conflict. If philosophers of Nietzsche or argumentation theorists have named and mapped this exact configuration, I want to know the term and, more importantly, how its boundaries are drawn—what counts as an instance and what distinguishes it from nearby failures such as strategic ambiguity, register-switching in metaphysical arguments, or dialectical self-undermining. If there is no established name, then please coin one, provide a precise definition, and indicate the boundary cases that separate it from those neighboring phenomena.
