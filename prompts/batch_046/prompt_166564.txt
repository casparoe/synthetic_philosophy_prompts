I am helping to design assessment materials for an undergraduate metaphysics seminar, and I have grown dissatisfied with the standard Ship of Theseus quizzes that reduce the debate to a binary choice between physical continuity and functional persistence. For this module I want a more diagnostically sharp instrument: a sorting quiz of roughly eight concrete cases that classifies respondents according to their implicit commitments about diachronic identity for artefacts, but one that pushes well beyond the textbook stalemate by introducing distinctions that rarely appear in introductory surveys. The cases should include not only the classic gradual plank replacement, but also edge scenarios such as instantaneous teleportation of every original plank to a remote site where they are reassembled into the original configuration; gradual replacement in which every new plank is made of a different material with different structural properties; the creation of a digital twin that receives continuous real-time updates from sensors embedded in the physical vessel; and a situation in which the original ship is destroyed but its identity is formally transferred to a successor vessel by an act of institutional registration, regardless of physical overlap. Each case must present two or three answer options written in plain language without philosophical labels, so that a student answers on intuition rather than by selecting a named theory they have read about.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least six clearly characterised positions: strict mereological essentialism (no replacement tolerated); functional-continuity with an anti-branching clause; narrative-social constructivism (identity sustained by collective recognition and institutional record); process-ontology (the ship understood as an unfolding spatiotemporal event rather than an enduring object); informational-pattern essentialism (identity tied to the preservation of a design specification regardless of material instantiation); and a deflationary view that denies there is any substantive fact of the matter. You should construct the logical space of answer combinations first, then locate each named position within it, and explicitly flag any combinations that remain unoccupied—briefly diagnosing whether the gap arises from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Do not include questions that ask students to identify a theory by name; the instrument must sort purely by judgments about cases. Please take a definite position in your design: state which distinctions you treat as primary—for example, whether material continuity or informational preservation forms the more fundamental axis—and which you treat as derivative, giving your reasons. Write the instrument and its explanatory framing in British English, and include a brief note indicating where your taxonomy relies on contested interpretations of the original paradox or takes liberties with standard presentations.
