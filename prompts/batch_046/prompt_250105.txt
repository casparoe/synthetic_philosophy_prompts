Write a dialogue between Dr. Elena Vasquez, who defends a revised form of aggregate welfare maximization, and Dr. Marcus Chen, who presses the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, set in a seminar room after a lecture on distributive justice. The discussion should center on whether the familiar Rawlsian charge—that utilitarianism improperly extends the principle of rational choice for one person to social arrangements, treating distinct individuals as parts of a single life—can survive Derek Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity. Vasquez should argue that if the self is merely a series of psychologically connected stages rather than a metaphysically substantial ego, then the boundary between separate persons becomes as conventional as the boundary between temporal parts of one biography; she should claim that the separateness objection therefore presupposes a Cartesian substantial self that reductionism undermines, and that once that presupposition falls away there is no principled barrier to offsetting burdens borne by one social role or temporal stage with benefits distributed to others, just as present sacrifices are routinely justified by future goods within one life. Chen should reply that separateness is fundamentally a normative thesis about the structure of practical reason, not a metaphysical thesis about substance: even on a reductionist view, the fact that present suffering belongs to this experiential center rather than that one generates an independent, non-transferable reason, and the absence of any super-agent—whether an enduring ego or a collective social bearer—actually strengthens the objection by showing there is no legitimate entity that could justifiably absorb one stage's severe loss for another's moderate gain. The dialogue must feature real back-and-forth, with each speaker directly addressing the other's specific argument rather than shifting ground, and should apply their disagreement to a concrete policy in which a small group is subjected to substantial, non-compensated harm in order to produce widely distributed, moderate benefits for a large population. Vasquez should contend that without metaphysically thick personal boundaries there is no owner of the harm whose rights are violated, while Chen should insist that the wrong consists precisely in treating each experiential center as a fungible unit within a larger calculus, regardless of whether those centers are linked by psychological continuity into one narrative life or distributed across many. Both characters should be equally philosophically competent, and neither should rely on appeals to authority or citation.
