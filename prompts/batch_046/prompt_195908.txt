My roommate got me stuck on this argument about nuclear brinkmanship and I can't see where it breaks. (1) A deterrent only works if the adversary thinks you might actually push the button. (2) The best way to make them think that isn't speeches but structural commitment — dead-hand mechanisms, delegated authroity, launch-on-warning protocols that take the decision away from one person. (3) So the rational strategist should build a posture where retaliation is automatc or at least outside thier direct control in the moment. (4) But if retaliation is truly outside your control, then in the crisis you're not "threatening" anyone; you're just a spectator to your own machinery. (5) The adversary then isn't weighing your resolve, they're weighing whether they can disable your sensors or command links — which is a technical problem, not a psychological one. (6) So perfect credibility destroys the strategic logic of deterrence: it turns a battle of wills into a race to disable equipment. (7) To keep it a battle of wills, you have to keep human choice in the loop. (8) But keeping human choice in the loop means you might blink, which weakens credibility. (9) Therefore every rational move to strengthen deterrence either cancels its own logic or reintroduces the weakness it was meant to fix. (10) My roommate says this proves rational deterrence theory is circular — it demands a posture that must simultaneously have and lack human agency. I feel like the slip is somewhere between (4) and (5), maybe treating "outside my control right now" as "outside my will altogether," but I'm not sure. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
