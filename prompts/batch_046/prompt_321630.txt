I'm preparing a philosophical profile on climate justice and economic transition for a reading group, and I would like a consistency audit before I present it. My commitments are as follows. (1) I favour a stringent carbon tax over direct regulation, on the grounds that pricing externalities respects procedural autonomy and avoids placing excessive epistemic burdens on central regulators. (2) I regard democratically planned degrowth — a deliberate contraction of material throughput in wealthy economies — as thermodynamically unavoidable rather than merely optional, because the empirical record undermines claims of absolute decoupling at the requisite speed. (3) At the same time, I maintain that workers and communities embedded in carbon-intensive sectors possess a desert-based claim to transitional compensation, scaled to the disruption they endure. (4) I understand desert as backward-looking and choice-sensitive: compensation is owed for voluntary commitments and foreseeable harms arising from them, but not for structural positions into which people were born or socialised. (5) I also hold that when a market failure carries existential risk, the state acquires a positive duty to regulate directly — including prohibiting certain activities — rather than relying solely on price mechanisms.

Please evaluate whether this package hangs together. Identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. For each genuine tension, specify what the least costly revision would be and what it costs me normatively. Be precise about whether the difficulty lies primarily between (1) and (5), between (3) and (4), or in the interaction of (2) with (3) given a contracting economic base. Please reply in British English.
