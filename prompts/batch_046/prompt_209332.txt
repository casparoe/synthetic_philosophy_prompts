I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am preparing an interactive explainer—aimed at educated non-specialists such as lawyers, policy analysts, and historians without formal economics training—on Amartya Sen's thesis that major famines are best understood as political and entitlement failures rather than as natural food shortages. I want the piece structured as a guided conceptual exercise rather than a narrative history, so I am asking for a teaching plan: the precise dependency structure through which the idea should be introduced, the one concrete scenario that should carry the explanation, the misconceptions that will arise in sequence and where to intercept them, a check that distinguishes genuine comprehension from mere discomfort, and an explicit boundary around what should be left out at this level.

The audience will arrive with the intuitive model that famine equals drought plus harvest failure—what Sen distinguishes from entitlement collapse—so the explanatory arc must begin by destabilizing that assumption before any case study is presented. I want to avoid the standard chronological ordering (Bengal 1943, then Ireland in the 1840s, then the Great Leap Forward) and instead open with the Great Leap Forward's statistical invisibility: how catastrophic mortality occurred without a detectable collapse in aggregate national food availability per capita, precisely because the entitlement structure—wages, procurement quotas, and local distribution—was distorted rather than the harvest destroyed. Only once that case has done its work should we move to Bengal, where food was physically present in markets but labor-market collapse and wartime price mechanisms destroyed specific groups' command over it, and finally to Ireland, where grain exports continued while local direct-access entitlements collapsed. Plan the sequence: what conceptual piece—perhaps the distinction between direct entitlement and trade-based entitlement—must be firmly in place before the Great Leap case can function? What single localized entitlement chain—maybe a rural laborer paid in grain, with grain priced in a distant urban market and transportation breaking the link—should serve as the running example, and what must it include (wage formation, price transmission, direct access) and deliberately exclude (aggregate GDP, meteorological data) so that it illuminates rather than distracts?

Map the misconceptions in the order they will arise. First, that labeling a famine "political" implies intentional genocide rather than structural failure—this should be headed off immediately after introducing entitlement collapse but before presenting Bengal. Second, that functioning markets naturally correct entitlement failures—this should be intercepted during the Bengal case, when readers will see prices rising and assume supply should respond. Third, that the Irish export flow proves food was available and therefore the famine was not a failure of entitlement—this should be defused precisely when Ireland is introduced, by showing that availability at the national level is compatible with catastrophic local entitlement collapse. Specify exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be addressed and what brief counter-example or distinction should do the work.

Finally, design one check for understanding—perhaps a single counterfactual question about a hypothetical entitlement-chain break—that would reveal whether a reader has grasped that famine can occur without aggregate food decline, rather than having merely memorized that "famine is not about food shortage." And state clearly what I should openly refuse to explain: I intend to set aside formal welfare-economics derivations, the demographic quantification debates surrounding specific mortality figures, and Sen's later capabilities framework, acknowledging these limits explicitly so the explanation remains anchored in conceptual structure rather than technical apparatus.
