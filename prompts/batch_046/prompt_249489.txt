You serve as senior ethics compliance officer for a cabinet ministry. Ministers routinely face proposals that require authorizing acts violating ordinary moral norms—deception, coercion, or collateral harm—to avert greater catastrophe. Your office needs a reusable decision procedure, executable by internal staff in under thirty minutes without external consultation, to classify whether a proposed action is a genuine dirty-hands case (morally necessary yet personally or institutionally tainting) rather than (i) an ordinary permissible compromise, (ii) a prohibited moral failure, or (iii) a case of distributed institutional corruption in which no single agent bears dirty hands.

Ground the procedure in standard philosophical accounts of dirty hands, but structure it around three conditions that are often conflated: (A) the action is genuinely unavoidable given the agent's specific institutional role; (B) the wrong-making feature is intrinsic to the act itself, not reducible to a foreseen side-effect; and (C) the agent retains moral residue—that is, the wrong is not fully absorbed or neutralized by institutional authorization. Deliver the procedure as an ordered checklist of no more than seven decision nodes (binary or triage), followed by a red-flag list of cases it cannot settle without direct ministerial judgment. Specify at each stage who must sign off and what documentation is required, and identify the exact point at which the procedure mandates a halt rather than a recommendation.

Apply the checklist to three scenarios: (1) authorizing targeted deception of an allied government to prevent an imminent terrorist attack; (2) granting a regulatory exemption that knowingly causes localized, irreversible ecological damage to secure national energy stability; and (3) a situation in which the procedure classifies the act as dirty hands but the minister refuses to accept personal or institutional taint—explain what the procedure says about this refusal and precisely where its authority ends.

Finally, flag the procedure's hard limits: collective authorization boards that diffuse individual agency, retrospective reclassification once outcomes are known, and actions in which the wrong-making feature itself is contested (for example, whether deception is always wrong or only wrong when it undermines democratic accountability). Include one worked case in which the procedure yields an answer the minister will find unwelcome, and state clearly what the procedure is not designed to do.
