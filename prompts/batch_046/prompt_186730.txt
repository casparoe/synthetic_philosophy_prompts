I am preparing a seminar session on the ethics of moral assessment and have been thinking about how Sophocles' Oedipus Rex anticipates and complicates the modern philosophy of moral luck. In particular, I am interested in whether the play distinguishes between circumstantial moral luck—being placed in situations not of one's choosing, such as being exposed as an infant or raised in Corinth rather than Thebes—and resultant moral luck, where the outcomes of one's actions turn out to be catastrophic despite reasonable intentions and ignorance, as when Oedipus kills the stranger at the crossroads and marries Jocasta. The modern debate, especially in Nagel's taxonomy and Bernard Williams's discussion of agent-regret, tends to treat these as separate categories with different moral implications: agent-regret may be appropriate for outcomes one causes but does not blame oneself for, whereas moral taint arising from resultant luck is more controversial.

Could you read Oedipus Rex through this lens? I would like you to examine specific moments—the messenger's report of Laius's death, Oedipus's confrontation with Jocasta about the prophecy, and the self-blinding scene—to see whether Sophocles treats Oedipus's suffering and self-punishment as a response to circumstantial bad luck, to resultant bad luck, or to something that does not fit either category. Where does the philosophical framework illuminate the play's treatment of guilt and ritual pollution? Where does the play resist being read as an illustration of moral luck theory—for instance, does the chorus's final judgment that Oedipus has been reduced to nothingness suggest a collapse of moral identity that is stronger or weaker than what Williams's concept of agent-regret allows? And finally, what does the tragedy add to the philosophical debate about moral luck that purely conceptual argument misses—perhaps something about the public, ritual dimension of acknowledging harm that abstract discussion of individual responsibility tends to neglect?
