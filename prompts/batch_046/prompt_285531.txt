As a retired physicist who spent three decades working on statistical mechanics before turning to philosophy in retirement, I have been studying P.F. Strawson’s essay on freedom and resentment with a small discussion group, and I have reconstructed an argument that seems to me to annihilate precisely the distinction Strawson wishes to preserve. I present it with numbered steps so that the failure point is visible, and I ask which link to break, because the premises feel as natural to me as a Hamiltonian reduction.

(1) Strawson holds that moral responsibility is constituted not by a metaphysical property of libertarian freedom, but by the appropriateness of reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation—through which we treat one another as members of a moral community.

(2) To regard someone as a member of this community is implicitly to regard them as capable of reciprocal regard: they must be open to moral address, responsive to reasons, and genuinely the source of their actions in a way that distinguishes them from objects we merely manage or cure.

(3) In my former scientific practice we treated causal explanation as scale-invariant: if the dynamics of a system are fully fixed at the fundamental level, then descriptions at higher organizational levels—whether of a gas reaching equilibrium or of a brain deciding—carry no independent causal weight; they are predictive heuristics rather than genuine origins.

(4) Applying this principle, if every human action is fully determined by neural and environmental conditions governed by natural law, then the personal-level description of an agent “choosing” is no more causally real than the description of a molecule “wanting” to diffuse.

(5) Consequently, no human agent is genuinely the source of their actions in the sense required by (2); every action traces back to conditions outside the agent’s rational control.

(6) Therefore, reactive attitudes are never appropriate, and we ought to adopt Strawson’s “objective attitude”—the stance of management, therapy, and prediction—toward all persons.

(7) There is a reflexive twist: holding someone responsible requires being in the participant stance, yet viewing oneself as an object of causal explanation (as (6) demands) precisely suspends that stance. So the argument does not merely restrict responsibility; it makes the practice of holding anyone responsible self-undermining.

(8) Strawson’s explicit reply is that determinism is irrelevant because reactive attitudes are part of our natural human form of life and are not suspended by theoretical beliefs about causation. But if (3) and (4) are correct, this reply looks like an unjustified exemption—like insisting that thermodynamic descriptions remain causally potent even after we have shown them reducible.

The conclusion—that we must abandon moral responsibility entirely and view all human interaction through the objective attitude—strikes me as absurd, and I would prefer to preserve Strawson’s participant stance if I can. I am particularly reluctant to surrender (3), since it reflects the reductionist habits of my scientific training, but I do not wish to pay the price of abandoning naturalism altogether. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, address whether the flaw lies in the scale-invariance claim in (3), in the requirement of genuine origination in (2), or in the reflexive consistency condition in (7), and explain why the rejected step looked plausible before it failed. Price out the alternatives: what does it cost to deny that causation is scale-invariant, to deny that reactive attitudes require causal origination, or to deny that the objective attitude must be applied reflexively?

Verdict: the step that should be rejected is (3), because effective theories in physics already show that higher-level descriptions carry autonomous explanatory and causal weight, and surrendering it allows Strawson’s participant stance to remain compatible with naturalism without requiring libertarian freedom.
