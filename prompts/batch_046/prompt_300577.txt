Two people in my philoseminar got stuck arguing about Kant and animals. (1) Alex said it's pretty cut and dry: animals are Sachen, not persons, so there's no direct duty to them; cruelty is wrong only cause it makes us meaner to other people. That's the indirect-duty line everyone learns. (2) Blake said thats the high-school version and misses how in the Anthropology Kant describes animal suffering as an analogon rationis — it structurally mirrors human pain and activates rational sympathy. So we're not just using animals to practice being nice; we're recognizing something genuinely analogous in them. (3) Alex came back that even if sympathy gets triggered analogically, it's still our own rational nature we're responding to. The animal is basically a mirror — you don't have duties to mirrors. (4) Blake said okay but if the reason cruelty is bad is that it damages our capacity to feel that analogical sympathy, and that capacity is what lets us respond to human suffering too, then harming animals isn't just "breaking the mirror" — it's actively degrading the mechanism we need for direct duties to people. So there's a stronger, almost derivative directness here. (5) Alex said thats indirect duty with extra rhetorical steps; Blake still hasn't shown animals are ends-in-themselves, just that they're especially important instruments for human moral health. (6) Blake admitted direct moral agency is probably out, but argued there's a middle ground — moral considerability without agency — that Kant's framework actually supports once you take the analogon seriously, even if Kant never named it.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say who's right about what, and what both of them are missing — especially about whether there's really a stable middle ground between indirect duty and full direct status.
