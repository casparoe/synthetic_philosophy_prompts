I'm working through some readings in metaethics for an independent study, and I have kept noticing a particular argumentative maneuver that I cannot quite map onto the standard taxonomy of errors. The pattern runs like this: a speaker first defends a normative claim—for instance, that people have a stronger moral obligation to kin than to strangers—by appealing to its evolutionary prevalence or statistical commonness, treating widespread occurrence as a kind of credential for the norm. When an interlocutor objects that this derives an ought from an is, the speaker retreats to a purely descriptive stance, insisting that they are only offering a causal or evolutionary explanation and are not making any prescriptive claim at all. Once that objection has been acknowledged and withdrawn, however, the speaker returns to normative language, arguing that social policies should respect or even reinforce the trait precisely because it is rooted in human nature, thereby using the same descriptive fact as a constraint on what ought to be done. I have seen versions of this in debates about tribal loyalty, about gendered divisions of labor, and about the limits of altruism, and in each case the speaker treats the descriptive retreat not as an abandonment of the normative claim but as having cleared the path for it.

My question is whether this specific three-step configuration—normative credentialing via prevalence, retreat to pure description under the is-ought objection, and then normative re-engagement through the very same descriptive fact—has an established name in the philosophical or argumentation literature. I know it sits near Moore's naturalistic fallacy and near Hume's is-ought gap, but it does not seem to be exactly either: the fallacy usually involves defining good in natural terms, whereas here good is never defined at all; and Hume's gap is a structural point about entailment, whereas this is a rhetorical sequence that exploits the gap defensively rather than crossing it in a single inference. There may also be overlap with the appeal to nature, but that is typically a direct inference from naturalness to goodness, not this two-step oscillation protected by a descriptive shield. Is there a recognized concept—perhaps in the philosophy of biology or in the study of informal fallacies—that captures this exact movement, and if there is none, how should it be carved? I want to understand its edges: does the descriptive claim need to be genuinely explanatory, or can purely statistical claims do the same work? And is the key feature strategic equivocation, or is it specifically the use of the is-ought gap as a defensive retreat that enables the return?

Please answer in American English, and please do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I am trying to work through the framing on its own terms without relying on named authorities.
