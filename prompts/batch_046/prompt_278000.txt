I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of history of science and environmental historiography, and I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern for which I lack a precise label. In recent scholarship, entities such as pathogenic microbes, long-term climatic shifts, and volcanic aerosol events are frequently introduced as historical actors in order to correct overly anthropocentric narratives. The pattern typically unfolds as follows: the non-human factor is initially described with explicit agential language—for instance, that a particular bacterium reshaped urban sanitation policy, or that a volcanic winter compelled geopolitical realignment—but this attribution is immediately followed by an extensive series of qualifications insisting that the agency was never autonomous, always mediated by social structures, institutional responses, or human interpretation. The result is not a genuine pluralization of causation but a kind of ontological double-entry: the non-human entity is granted symbolic membership in the explanatory narrative, yet every causal pathway that might have operated independently of human structures is systematically redirected back through them. The bacterium does not cause the policy change; human reactions to the bacterium do, and the bacterium's agency is reduced to being the occasion for those reactions. I observe this in discussions of the demographic effects of the Black Death, in accounts of agricultural impacts during the Little Ice Age, and in analyses of the 1815 Tambora eruption's influence on state formation—each time, the microbial, climatic, or volcanic actor is named prominently, then methodologically dissolved into its human mediations. Is there an established concept—perhaps in science and technology studies, actor-network theory, or the philosophy of history—that captures this specific move, where the rhetorical elevation of non-human agency is paired with its simultaneous causal evacuation? How does it differ from a standard interactionist or co-constructivist account, in which human and non-human elements genuinely share explanatory weight? If the literature lacks a specific term, what would be the appropriate way to characterize this pattern—something along the lines of performative agency attribution or causal hollowing—and what boundary conditions would distinguish it from legitimate, non-reductionist multi-causal explanation? Please provide your response in American English.
