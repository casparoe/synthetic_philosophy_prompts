I am grading an undergraduate exam in the sociology of religion and historical social thought. The exam asks students to work through the problem of explaining macro-level change with cultural values, using Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis as the central case. Below is the exam question and one student's response. Exam question:
Weber's thesis about the Protestant ethic and the rise of rational capitalism is frequently criticized for granting causal power to values without making them epiphenomenal reflections of material conditions. Address the following:
(a) State the specific explanatory dilemma Weber confronts: how can values be causally efficacious without being reducible to economic interests, and without floating free of material constraints?
(b) Explain Weber's concept of elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft) and clarify why he treats the relationship between the Protestant ethic and the capitalist spirit as contingent rather than necessary. In doing so, distinguish his position from both Marxist base-superstructure determinism and from Hegelian world-historical idealism.
(c) Weber emphasizes worldly ascetic conduct of life (Lebensführung) rather than theological doctrine per se. Explain why this distinction is meant to block the reduction of macroeconomic transformation to an aggregation of private religious beliefs.
(d) One critic charges that Weber's framework is circular: material conditions select which values become dominant, yet those values are then said to reshape the very conditions that selected them. Briefly evaluate whether Weber can escape this circularity without surrendering the causal relevance of culture.

Student answer:
Weber faces the problem that if cultural values are merely superstructural reflections of material conditions, they cannot exercise independent causal force; yet if they are entirely autonomous, they become historically inexplicable. He resolves this tension through the concept of elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft), which describes a contingent, non-necessary alignment between the Protestant ethic and the emerging practices of rational capitalism. Unlike Marx, for whom the economic base determines the cultural superstructure, and unlike Hegel, for whom world-historical ideas unfold dialectically regardless of material constraints, Weber treats the relationship as a historically specific convergence in which neither pole is reducible to the other.

Weber's method of ideal types allows him to isolate worldly ascetic conduct of life (Lebensführung) as the operative mechanism, rather than theological doctrine alone. The doctrine of predestination generated psychological anxiety about salvation, and individuals resolved this by seeking signs of election in disciplined economic activity. Because Weber focuses on conduct rather than belief per se, he avoids reducing macroeconomic transformation to an aggregation of private religious opinions. The capitalist spirit thus emerged because the ethic directly caused individuals to reinvest profits rather than consume them.

Regarding the charge of circularity—namely, that material conditions select viable values, which then reshape those same conditions—Weber's framework escapes it by treating the interaction as a feedback loop rather than a linear causal chain. Material interests constrain which values can take root, but once institutionalized, those values alter interest-formation itself. Furthermore, Weber's ideal-type analysis demonstrates that the Protestant ethic functioned as the necessary precondition for rational capitalism in the West, confirming that values possess genuine causal efficacy rather than being epiphenomenal.

Overall, Weber succeeds in granting culture causal weight without abandoning the material conditions that make particular cultural configurations possible.
