I am designing a single fifty-minute seminar for first-year philosophy undergraduates who have just completed an introduction to the tripartite analysis of knowledge—justified true belief—and who have read, in translation, the portion of Plato's Theaetetus treating false belief, but who have not yet encountered any post-1963 epistemology. The students are mathematically inclined and tend to dismiss social or perceptual anecdotes as contrived, so I want to avoid both the standard Smith/ten-coins job-interview case and the fake-barn scenario; instead, the session should turn on a structural feature of reasoning (for instance, a false lemma in a deductive or probabilistic chain). Plan the explanatory arc: first, specify the conceptual dependency—what must be firmly secured about justification and truth before a counterexample can register as a structural failure of the analysis rather than as a quirky exception or a lucky guess; second, propose one carrying example that instantiates the Gettier structure—justified true belief formed via a false intermediate premise—detail exactly what properties it must possess and what it must deliberately lack (e.g., no direct perceptual error, no statistical fluke, no social contingency) so that students see the breakdown is in the tripartite conditions themselves; third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise—such as the belief that Gettier cases are merely accidental, that justification requires infallibility, or that patching the analysis with a "no false lemmas" clause solves the problem without further cost—and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off and how; fourth, provide one diagnostic question or brief task for the end that distinguishes students who understand why the tripartite analysis fails structurally from those who have only memorized the counterexample; and fifth, state explicitly which subsequent developments—such as modal safety conditions, virtue-theoretic accounts, or causal theories of knowing—to leave out, and frame that omission honestly without misleading the students about the current state of the field. The plan should be self-contained, should not request readings or citations, and should assume no external resources are available during the session.
