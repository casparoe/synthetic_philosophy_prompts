As I work on a historical novel set in the court of François I, I have become preoccupied with a conceptual problem that my protagonist—a poet-translator—would face when attempting to carry the manner of Clément Marot into another language. I have constructed an argument about poetic translation that strikes me as suspicious because it concludes either that all interlingual rendering of verse is impossible or that translating poetry is no different from translating a manual, yet I know that translators have in fact rendered Marot. The argument proceeds as follows. (1) If a rendering is genuinely a translation of a poem rather than its replacement, it must preserve the features that constitute the original as a poem—above all its formal architecture: syllabic meter, rhyme scheme, and phonetic patterning, including the way Marot's phrase ton beau generates semantic ambiguity through its musical placement, allowing it to be heard as both your beautiful and the beautiful tone. (2) Preservation of form requires preserving the same formal properties in the target language, since altering meter or rhyme position changes the poem's structural identity. (3) Because languages differ in phonotactics, stress patterns, and syllabic inventory, cross-linguistic identity of formal properties is impossible; one cannot reproduce a French decasyllable with its specific rhyme dynamics in English without either violating English prosody or substituting an altogether different structure. (4) Therefore any interlingual rendering necessarily abandons the original form. (5) Once form is abandoned, only semantic content remains. (6) But semantic content alone is exactly what a prose paraphrase delivers, so a form-abandoned rendering is not a translated poem but a poem replaced by prose. (7) Hence either poetic translation never occurs, or formal preservation is inessential to it; yet if formal preservation is inessential, there is no principled distinction between translating Marot and translating an agricultural manual, which seems to erase the art of poetic translation entirely. I would prefer to keep the intuition that translating Marot demands something more than paraphrase—perhaps a structural homology rather than strict identity—but I cannot locate the faulty step. Which premise or inference should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost? Please distinguish between what is settled in the philosophy of translation—such as the consensus that literal word-for-word identity is neither necessary nor sufficient—from what remains genuinely contested, for instance whether formal analogy can carry poetic meaning across languages, and do not present any disputed position as if it were the established view.
