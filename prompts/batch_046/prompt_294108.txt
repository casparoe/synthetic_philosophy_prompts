I have been working through puzzles about inductive confirmation with a philosophy study group, and our judgments about particular observations have diverged in a pattern that standard presentations of Hempel’s paradox do not seem to cover. Consider four scenarios. First, we are testing the universal claim that every emerald is green. We examine a sheet of white paper: it is not green and it is not an emerald. Our unanimous intuition is that this observation provides essentially no inductive support for the emerald hypothesis, and we treat it as epistemically inert.

Second, we test the claim that all ravens are black. We observe a white shoe: it is not black and it is not a raven. Here the group splits sharply. About half hold that, by logical equivalence with the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens, the observation technically confirms the raven hypothesis, even if only by an infinitesimal amount; the other half deny that it confirms in any meaningful sense, insisting that the logical transformation masks a genuine asymmetry in projectibility and that we should not count observations outside the kind’s natural domain.

Third, we test the claim that all copper wires in this specific circuit are conductive. We place a wooden stick in the circuit and observe that it does not conduct electricity and is not made of copper. Unlike the emerald case, we do not merely feel that confirmation is negligible; we feel it is categorically inappropriate, as if the observation falls outside the domain over which the hypothesis was intended to range, making logical equivalence irrelevant.

Fourth, we test the claim that every planet in this solar system has mass below 10^25 kg. We then learn that a distant star—clearly not a planet—has mass well above that threshold. Rather than leaving our confidence unchanged or slightly confirming the claim (since the star is a high-mass non-planet, matching one logical form of the hypothesis), several of us feel that this observation actually weakens our belief, because it shows that very large masses are cosmically nearby, undermining the impression that our solar system’s uniformity is law-governed rather than accidental.

Our verdicts therefore form an uneasy pattern: negligible confirmation for the paper, split judgment for the shoe, categorical irrelevance for the stick, and possible disconfirmation for the star despite logical equivalence. Is there a single principle—perhaps involving explicit domain restriction, causal or mechanistic relevance, or the distinction between accidental generalizations and law-like uniformities—that delivers all four judgments? If not, which verdict should be abandoned, and what does the resulting principle predict for a case we have not discussed: observing that a synthetic polymer, engineered specifically to be an insulator and placed entirely outside the circuit, fails to conduct electricity?
