Write a short story (approximately 700–900 words) about an institutional “authenticity assessor” in a near-future bureaucracy that applies Sartrean existentialism as administrative doctrine. The protagonist, Mira, evaluates citizens for structural bad faith—systematic self-deception about radical freedom—using a framework built on Sartre’s distinctions between facticity and transcendence, and on the phenomenon of the look (le regard). During an audit of a retired architect who has voluntarily confined himself to a single repetitive task, Mira realizes that her own evaluative authority depends on denying her freedom to choose the criteria she applies. Treat radical freedom not as heroic autonomy but as an unavoidable burden that generates institutional pathologies when bureaucratized.

After the story, include a section titled Commentary on philosophical liberties (approximately 200–250 words). In it, separate what is broadly settled in Sartre’s philosophy—such as the distinction between being-in-itself and being-for-itself, and the claim that bad faith involves lying to oneself about one’s freedom—from issues that remain genuinely contested, including whether radical freedom is compatible with deep social determinism, the precise scope and mechanism of the look, and whether any institutional framework can avoid falling into bad faith. Identify specific points where the narrative invents or stretches Sartre’s arguments, especially the bureaucratization of existential assessment, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.
