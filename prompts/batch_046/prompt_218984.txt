I recently joined a deliberative research cooperative that makes publication and funding decisions by consensus, and I have been trying to map my own judgments about when a member may act alone without group approval. My verdicts are these. If a member unilaterally submits a grant proposal using the collective's name and shared preliminary data—believing speed is essential, though the group had planned to wait for stronger evidence—I think that is clearly wrong, and worse than if they had quietly donated part of their personal salary to hire an assistant for the same project without asking anyone, which strikes me as acceptable, even admirable. If a member, convinced a particular statistical method is correct, rewrites the shared analysis code overnight and pushes it to the repository before the meeting where it was to be discussed, I think that is wrong, though somewhat less bad than the grant submission. If another member, seeing an emerging public crisis related to our field, issues a statement in the collective's name warning policymakers—even though we had agreed to remain neutral until further review—I think that is wrong in a different and possibly graver way than the code rewrite. Finally, if a member silently corrects a computational error in a draft about to be circulated externally, without announcing the change, I keep oscillating: sometimes it seems like harmless maintenance, sometimes like an impermissible substitution of collective authorship. I am drawn to the idea that the wrongness tracks not mere unilateral action but something like unilateral substitution of collective judgment—where the member replaces a decision that belongs to the group—yet I cannot make that distinction consistently explain why the private donation is fine while the silent correction feels dubious, or why preemptive public representation is worse than internal code revision.

Is there a precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that captures the pattern in these verdicts, stating exactly which clause is motivated by which case? I want the principle to predict my judgment on a case I have not listed: suppose a member unilaterally negotiates a data-sharing agreement with an external institution that binds only her own future work, but uses the collective's reputation as leverage in the negotiation, without claiming to speak for the group. I also want an honest accounting of which of my five verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without contortion, and whether saving every verdict requires a complexity that makes the principle implausible. Please take a definite position: defend one principle as the best reconstruction, even if it forces me to overturn one or two of my intuitions, rather than presenting a menu of options and leaving the choice to me. I am particularly interested in whether there is an asymmetry between actions that add resources to the collective and actions that commit the collective representationally or epistemically, and whether that asymmetry can be stated without collapsing into a vague appeal to "respect for process." Give your reasoning directly, and state clearly where you think my oscillating case—the silent correction—should land once the principle is fixed.
