For my upper-level seminar on moral aggregation and rescue ethics, I am preparing an adversarial-collaboration exercise between two classmates who have reached a sustained impasse. One holds a Pairwise-Comparison View, inspired by Taurek’s argument that rescue obligations do not scale with numbers: she maintains that if you can save either one stranger or five strangers—equal proximity, equal need, no prior obligations, equal cost to the rescuer—you face a symmetric moral choice rather than a stronger duty to save the five, because moral comparison operates only between individual pairs and generates no aggregate obligation. The other holds a Proportional-Rescue View: he maintains that, absent special relationships or qualitative differences, the number of lives imperiled is directly morally relevant, so saving five is strictly obligatory over saving one, and treating the choice as symmetric is a category error about how rescue duties distribute.

Please draft the joint statement these two should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims both sides can sign. This should include agreement on the formal structure of the baseline rescue scenario; agreement on what needs explaining—specifically, why intuition pulls in both directions and what role equality between persons plays; agreement on which arguments are genuinely on the table, including whether aggregation violates fairness among victims and whether proportionality collapses into implausible trade-offs with trivial goods; and agreement on the admissibility of hypothetical variations (e.g., graded-cost rescue ladders, structural diversion cases) as relevant evidence.

2. A disagreements table with two columns, each phrased in wording that its own side would accept. One column should state the Pairwise-Comparison View’s core thesis about token-indexed moral comparison and distributive neutrality—namely, that the moral weight of a rescue obligation is fixed pairwise and does not accumulate across distinct individuals. The other column should state the Proportional-Rescue View’s core thesis about type-indexed moral weight and scaling obligations—namely, that the class of imperiled lives generates a collective rescue duty that strengthens with cardinality. Do not render either position as a strawman.

3. The cruxes—the specific sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I take the central cruxes to be: (a) whether moral equality between persons entails that no quantitative difference in the number of imperiled lives can generate a stronger rescue duty; (b) whether a “split-stream” case—in which saving one person requires actively maintaining a direct path to her while resources that would otherwise save five are diverted, but the five are not in your direct path—reveals that pairwise comparison fails to capture structural features of rescue; and (c) whether accepting proportionality forces an implausible commitment to saving a marginally larger number even when the rescuer’s costs scale sharply.

4. For each crux, identify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case. For crux (b), both should accept the split-stream scenario as admissible evidence; the Pairwise-Comparison side should predict that the direct-path victim retains a symmetric claim not overridden merely by the downstream five, while the Proportional-Rescue side should predict that the structural diversion makes the duty clearly proportional. For crux (c), both should accept a graded-cost rescue ladder—saving five at moderate risk versus six at catastrophic risk to the rescuer—as relevant; record each side’s prediction about where the obligation breaks and whether proportionality permits or forbids the trade-off. For crux (a), both should accept an argument from moral ontology about whether obligations are constituted pairwise or collectively; record whether each side predicts that such an ontology resolves the dispute in its favor.
