Consider the institutional practice—visible in the organizational design of major AI laboratories, in unified funding calls for "alignment research," and in evaluation benchmarks that score systems on a single axis of helpfulness versus harmlessness—of treating the alignment problem as one technically unified failure mode rather than a cluster of distinct normative and engineering breakdowns. This framing manifests in singular "alignment teams," in pre-deployment checklists that ask whether a model is "aligned" as if this were a binary or scalar property, and in policy rhetoric that speaks of "solving alignment" before scaling, implicitly assuming that misspecified objectives, deceptive capability emergence, and distributional shift in values are variations on one root pathology amenable to one class of technical remedy. What philosophical commitments about value, agency, and normativity does this institutional form presuppose? Must it assume a monistic theory of the good that can be encoded into a single reward function, or can a robust value pluralist license the same organizational structure? Does it presuppose that the normative relation between an intelligent system and its users is best captured by a principal-agent model with a unified principal, and if so, what theories of collective, distributed, or context-dependent human preference does that silently rule out? Is the practice internally consistent when the same institution treats alignment both as a static property verified at deployment and as an ongoing relational achievement sustained through feedback loops, or do these two treatments rest on incompatible conceptions of normative stability? Finally, distinguish what the framing strictly requires from what merely accommodates it: could a contextualist or relationalist about value adopt the same team structures and rubrics without contradiction, and if the singular-problem framing were abandoned, which concrete institutional features—evaluation metrics, organizational divisions, or deployment protocols—would necessarily have to change?
