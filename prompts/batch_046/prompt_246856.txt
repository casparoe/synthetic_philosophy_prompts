I was discussing with friends over dinner some months ago whether crushing a spider involves any genuine harm, and since then I have been thinking independently about the moral implications of insect sentience. I have developed a view I call the "gradient-uncertainty principle": rather than treating our moral obligation to avoid harming insects as proportional to our confidence that they are conscious, I argue that it should scale with the product of our epistemic ignorance and the sheer number of individuals affected — so that the less certain we are, combined with the greater the population, the stronger our precautionary reason to refrain from harm. Next Thursday I present this to the ethics and sustainability committee of a large agricultural cooperative. The audience consists of four agronomists and supply-chain directors who treat pest management as an unavoidable economic necessity, a veterinary ethicist who accepts welfare arguments for vertebrates but remains doubtful about invertebrates, and a conservation biologist who regards interference with invertebrate populations as ecologically reckless. I need a Q&A preparation sheet — not a philosophical defence in the abstract, but the objections these particular people will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they are likely to be, phrased in the practical and scientific vocabulary they use rather than in academic jargon. For each objection, please provide a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, together with an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or relies on an unsupported assumption. I should also like guidance on what to concede at the outset: I accept that total elimination of harm is impossible and that I am not proposing an outright ban on pesticides, but rather a calibrated precautionary threshold within integrated pest management programmes. Finally, please identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks — I fear it will be whether my argument, if applied consistently, requires us to avoid harming bacteria or soil microbiota, thereby rendering it practically absurd — and suggest how I might deflect it without seeming to abandon my principle. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works; I have no access to online resources during the talk, and I need reasoning that stands on its own.
