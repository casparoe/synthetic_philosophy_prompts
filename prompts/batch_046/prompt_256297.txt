I am planning a single fifty-minute session for a reading group composed mostly of longtime rationalist-forum readers who have encountered Camus in passing and have heard of contemporary analytic accounts of meaning—Wolf’s fitting-fulfillment view, Metz’s objectivism—but have never worked through the tension between them systematically. The concept I want them to leave with is not “absurdism” as a literary posture but a specific structural claim: that meaningfulness in life requires not only objective value and subjective attraction, but a third condition I will call reflexive endurance—the sustained practice of pursuing a project while maintaining explicit awareness that cosmic significance is unavailable, where the endurance itself becomes part of what makes the pursuit meaningful rather than merely tolerable. Do not explain this synthesis to me; design the pedagogical arc.

What conceptual dependency must be secured before this third condition can even be introduced, and why opening with Sisyphus as the emblem of happy absurdity would actually derail the session? Which single carrying example—drawn from ordinary life rather than heroic achievement—should anchor the discussion, and what properties must it have and lack? Map the sequence of misconceptions they will generate—I anticipate at least “this collapses into stoicism,” “reflexive endurance is just chronic dissatisfaction,” and “if cosmic significance does not exist, the third condition is a consoling fiction”—with the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted and neutralized. Provide the diagnostic question at the end that distinguishes understanding the structure from merely adopting an aesthetic stance, specify which elements from standard Wolf or Metz frameworks should be explicitly bracketed at this introductory level and why, and note what the audience should be able to do with the idea that they could not do if they had only memorized Camus’s conclusion. The group is intelligent but philosophically unschooled; they will try to resolve the tension too quickly.
