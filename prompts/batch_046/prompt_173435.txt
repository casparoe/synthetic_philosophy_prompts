Following a lengthy and rather heated discussion with a friend over dinner this evening, I find myself uncertain about a summary she offered of Max Black's well-known spheres argument against the identity of indiscernibles. She presented it more or less as follows. In a hypothetical universe containing nothing but two spheres of identical size, colour, and internal composition, separated by a fixed distance, the two objects share every intrinsic property. If one adopts a relational theory of space, then even their spatial properties are identical: each stands in precisely the same distance-relations to the other, so there is no relational difference either. Consequently, the spheres are genuinely indiscernible yet numerically distinct, which directly falsifies Leibniz's principle that identical properties entail identity. She went on to claim that this thought experiment simultaneously undermines the principle of sufficient reason, since there can be no explanation for why one sphere occupies the left-hand position and the other the right-hand position. She further argued that the case shows qualitative identity cannot ground numerical identity, and therefore that structural realism—the view that objects are nothing over and above the structure of relations they instantiate—is false; instead, she said, one must accept primitive individual essences, or haecceities, to distinguish them. Finally, she concluded that Black's argument therefore establishes the necessity of absolute space, for only by appealing to non-relational, absolute locations can one restore distinguishability without contradiction.

I am inclined to think she has conflated a couple of distinct principles, overstated the metaphysical payoff, and perhaps misread the dialectical purpose of the example, but I should like a careful audit. Would a philosopher of metaphysics accept each of these sentences? In particular, I want to know whether she is right to present the argument as a direct refutation of the principle of sufficient reason rather than merely of the identity of indiscernibles; whether she is fair to the relationalist in saying that no spatial distinction remains; whether her claim that Black proves absolute space is a legitimate inference from the counterexample or a misreading of its purpose; whether there is any subtle confusion between the identity of indiscernibles and its converse, the indiscernibility of identicals; and whether her leap to haecceities and the rejection of structural realism genuinely follows from Black's premises or introduces an unrelated commitment. If parts of her summary are accurate but misleadingly framed, I should like to know which corrections preserve her core insight and which dissolve it entirely. I have no interest in bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I simply want to understand whether I can rely on her account in our continuing argument.
