I teach a seminar on ethics and religion for about 12 grad students, we meet for 90 minutes and people are pretty sharp but they always reduce Buddhist ethics to "its all about intention, right?" I want an in-class game that makes them feel the actual pull of the consequentialist challenge inside Buddhist thought, specifically around skillful means (upaya) and the Abhidharma mental-state audit. The niche thing I'm trying to get at is this: in some Mahayana narratives a bodhisattva does something that immediately causes mental suffering to others—like deception or coercion—to eventually bring about liberation, and the tradition praises the actor's intention (cetana) and the long-term result simultaneously. But Abhidharma analysis says karma tracks only the mental state of the agent at the moment of action, not the downstream effects on other minds.

So I need an activity where students split into "bodhisattva decision-makers" and "karmic auditors." The decision-makers get a scenario card describing a crisis—say, a group is about to commit mass violence, and the only intervention involves lying to one person in a way that will cause them acute mental distress now but prevent harm later. They have to pick an action and write down the mental state (intention/volition) they claim to hold. The auditors get only the mental-state descriptions of the actors and the immediate mental-state descriptions of the affected people, with zero info about outcomes; they score the karma on a simple card-based scale. Then in round two, the long-term outcomes are revealed—some strategies worked, some backfired, some had hidden suffering—and the auditors can revise scores, after which the whole group debates whether revision was justified.

The rules should force people to feel the conflict, not just talk about it. Maybe a point system where auditors lose points if they revise purely based on outcome, but decision-makers gain points if liberation eventually occurs? Or maybe not—I'll leave the payoff scheme to you, but keep it simple, no real money, just paper and cards. Afterward, debrief by mapping round one to Abhidharma evaluation (cetana only), round two to the Mahayana upaya defense (outcome-sensitive praise), and the revision tension to the conventional/ultimate truth distinction. Be honest about what this can't reproduce—like the metaphysical continuity of karma across rebirths, or the non-self view that dissolves the agent/patient boundary. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong, like everyone immediately siding with outcomes in round two and ignoring mental states, and how to handle that without shutting the game down. Don't include a bibliography or reading list—just the design, timeline, roles, and materials.
