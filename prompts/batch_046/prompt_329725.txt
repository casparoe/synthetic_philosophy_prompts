I've been working with a resolution of the backward induction paradox in the centipede game that doesn't rely on bounded rationality, altruism, or risk preferences. The idea is that common knowledge of rationality is epistemically unstable across sequential nodes: when an agent reaches node n, they can recognize that the reasoning context at n+1 will be locally structured in a way that doesn't transparently inherit the global strategic calculus. So continuing is compatible with rationality if future reasoning is treated as context-sensitive rather than globally consistent across the tree. This dissolves the paradox without requiring agents to be irrational or to care about others' payoffs. What would genuinely count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: formal results that show local reasoning must collapse back into global consistency; conceptual distinctions (say, between strategic and procedural rationality) that the view misapplies; specific variants of the game where the resolution still forces termination too early; and whether defenders of this position would concede any of these as damaging rather than dismissing them as missing the point. I'm especially interested in whether the view risks being insulated from falsification by redefining rationality at each node, and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice for this kind of claim.
