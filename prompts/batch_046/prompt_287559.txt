Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in communicative ethics: the Directness camp and the Indirectness camp.

The Directness camp holds that explicit, unvarnished correction is generally morally preferable to face-saving indirection; that white lies—small falsehoods told to spare feelings—are wrong because they violate the listener's rational autonomy and treat them as unable to bear truth; and that norms approximating Dutch directness express genuine respect rather than rudeness.

The Indirectness camp holds that high-context, face-preserving communication—exemplified by Japanese norms of indirect refusal and implicit correction—is often morally required; that relational harmony and the avoidance of unnecessary humiliation are genuine goods that can justify strategic ambiguity or small omissions; and that universal demands for explicitness risk imposing one culture's interactional norms as moral absolutes.

Produce a document with the following parts:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—about the descriptive patterns of Dutch and Japanese communicative norms, about the definition of a white lie, about the distinction between descriptive cultural variation and normative evaluation, and about any formal or empirical points both sides accept—phrased so that neither camp is forced to concede its normative stance.

2. A disagreements table. For each camp, state its precise core thesis in wording that camp itself would accept. Include the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one side. Key cruxes should include whether face-preservation is an independent moral good or merely a preference for emotional comfort; whether directness respects autonomy or instead transfers emotional labor to the listener; whether white lies violate a duty of sincerity even when they produce better outcomes; and whether cultural variation in communicative norms undermines the universality of any single norm.

3. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant, with each camp's predicted assessment recorded in advance. For example, both might agree on the relevance of a constructed cross-cultural research collaboration in which a senior researcher gives indirect feedback ("Perhaps we could think about the method") that the junior interprets literally, delaying correction of a fatal methodological error—each side predicting whether the harm stems from the indirectness itself or from a failure of shared context.

Separate what is settled (e.g., that Dutch and Japanese communicative norms differ descriptively, that white lies occur across cultures) from what is genuinely contested among people who understand the topic. Do not present any contested normative claim—such as that directness is always respectful, or that indirectness is inherently manipulative—as settled consensus.
