I am preparing the closing exercise for my graduate seminar in philosophy of language and artificial cognition, where we have spent the semester examining whether statistical processing can amount to semantic understanding. For our final session, I want to present an original poem—roughly eighteen to twenty-two lines, in blank verse—that structurally enacts Douglas Hofstadter's critique of statistical machine translation: specifically, the claim that a system can produce grammatically flawless, fluent output without any internal representation of meaning, reference, or intentionality. The poem should unfold in three movements: an opening section that articulates a precise philosophical claim about intentionality in disciplined English; a middle section that preserves the grammatical skeleton and rhythmic cadence of that claim but replaces every content-bearing word with phonetic echoes and syntactic placeholders, creating a phantom language that sounds like comprehension but carries none; and a closing section that attempts to translate the claim back into coherent English, yet with subtle lexical substitutions and deliberate metrical fractures that allow fluency to return while understanding remains displaced. The structural turn—the point at which grammatical coherence becomes most seductive and semantic grounding is most absent—should coincide with the poem's volta, so that the form itself performs the very shallowness it describes.

Please avoid resolving the tension with either technological triumphalism or humanist sentimentality. I want the audience to leave with the same unease our discussions have produced: the recognition that surface coherence can perfectly mimic depth without ever achieving it. To reinforce this, use internal assonance and half-rhyme that hold firmly in the first section, dissolve entirely in the phantom-language interlude, and re-emerge in the final section in distorted, almost-parodic form, so the ear experiences a false recovery that mirrors the false comprehension of statistical translation. The idea should be rendered with enough accuracy that a specialist in philosophy of mind or cognitive science recognizes the argument, yet the poem must stand on its own for listeners unfamiliar with the debate—no citations, no scholarly apparatus, no explanatory footnotes, just image, rhythm, and the gradual unmooring of meaning from form.
