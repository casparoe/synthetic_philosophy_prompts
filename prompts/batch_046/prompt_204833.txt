I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, but I've been thinking about dispositions, finks, and masks through distributed system design. Consider two microservices that both carry a disposition to fail under concurrent load. In Service A, an external retry-loop with exponential backoff masks the failure: the crash tendency remains intact, but the trigger condition never actually reaches it. In Service B, an embedded health-check detects the exact load spike and dynamically rewrites the service's own state-transition rules precisely at the moment of trigger, removing the failure tendency exactly when it would manifest—functionally a fink. I want to defend the view that for artifactual systems, the metaphysical distinction between masking and finking collapses. Both cases, I claim, are just nodes in an "intervention topology" defined by whether the interfering mechanism is endogenous or exogenous to the system's state-transition graph, and by whether it operates continuously or only at the trigger boundary. Argue that the traditional binary taxonomy should be replaced by this graded, graph-relative account of state-intervention relations for artifacts, and that this shows the mask/fink distinction tracks engineering architecture rather than deep metaphysical structure.
