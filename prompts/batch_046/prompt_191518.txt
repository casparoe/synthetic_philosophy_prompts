I have been thinking on my own about sphexishness — the wasp Sphex ichneumoneus that drags a paralyzed cricket to a burrow, checks the burrow, pulls the cricket inside, and will keep repeating the check even if you move the cricket away during the check — and I keep wondering whether that kind of mechanical rigidity is really the opposite of free will, or whether our praise of flexibility is just prejudice. I am not a philosopher by training; I work in logistics, and watching colleagues either follow an unchanging protocol or constantly adapt made me want to see the idea tested in a concrete case rather than in abstract argument. Could you design a thought experiment that meets these specifications?

Construct a realistic, one-paragraph scenario set in an ordinary workplace — perhaps a hospital ward or a factory floor, with no brain implants, demons, or perfect predictors — that compares a completely sphexish agent (running an invariant behavioral script regardless of contextual changes) with a maximally flexible agent (revising goals and methods in real time in response to every input). Engineer the case so that, surprisingly, the rigid agent performs an action that one defensible philosophical account counts as genuinely free or autonomous, while the flexible agent performs an analogous action that the same account counts as merely determined or unfree, despite possessing far greater cognitive complexity. After the scenario, please provide the following sections in this order, each roughly the same length: first, "The Scenario"; second, "The Flexibility Requirement Verdict," stating what a defender of the view that free will requires real-time revision and adaptability would say about each agent and why; third, "The Rigid Commitment Verdict," stating what a defender of the view that invariant, historically endorsed scripts can constitute autonomy would say; fourth, "The Isolating Feature," identifying whether the work is being done by the historical origin of the script, the absence of present-moment deliberation, or modal stability across nearby worlds, and explaining exactly why; fifth, "The Removal Test," describing what happens to the verdicts if we strip away that feature — for example, if the rigid agent never originally adopted the script and is only malfunctioning; and sixth, "The Critical Attack," explaining how an opponent of the rigidity-friendly reading could undermine the case without simply rejecting the intuition outright. Please avoid bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading; I want the reasoning to stand on its own.
