I'm a musician and musicologist with working knowledge of notation theory and performance practice, but little formal philosophy of biology. Restate the principal debates about defining life — specifically the metabolic or self-maintaining account, the gene-centred replicative account, and the functional or thermodynamic account — entirely in the vocabulary of musical works, scores, performances, instruments, and acoustic realisation. Use the distinctions I actually work with: the score as a set of instructions, the performance as the realised sound-event, and the instrument as the material substrate that produces it. For the purpose of this translation, also employ the idea of a "ghost performance": a notated work that is never sounded, a sounding event that lacks corresponding notation, or a performance executed on an instrument other than the one specified in the score. For each biological account of life, map its claims onto these categories as precisely as you can; say which parts translate cleanly, which must be distorted (for instance, treating metabolic maintenance as "tuning" or replication as "copying the score"), and which have no counterpart in this vocabulary at all. Pay particular attention to the borderline cases of viruses and minimal synthetic cells: does a virus correspond to a score without an instrument, to a ghost performance, or to something else? Does a synthetic cell built from non-living components map onto electronic synthesis, onto an instrument playing a score it did not compose, or onto a different category altogether? Finally, provide the residue report: does what fails to translate reveal a genuine limitation of the musical framework, or does it suggest that the biological debate depends on smuggling in analogies that are not explicitly acknowledged? Please answer in British English.
