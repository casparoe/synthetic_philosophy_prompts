I am presenting a thesis at an interdisciplinary seminar on rationality, self-control, and intertemporal choice. The audience is precisely divided: behavioral economists who treat hyperbolic discounting and the sophisticated-naive distinction as settled descriptive machinery; philosophers of action working in a broadly Davidsonian framework who understand weakness of will as a failure of all-things-considered practical judgment rather than a preference reversal; and cognitively oriented researchers from rationalist-adjacent circles who expect explicit, mechanistic, and ideally computationally tractable models. Here is the argument they will be responding to.

Thesis: Akrasia is not best explained by Davidson’s model, in which an agent acts against their best judgment because a competing desire is causally stronger, nor by the standard behavioral-economic reduction to hyperbolic preference reversal. Instead, I propose that weakness of will arises specifically when an agent’s intertemporal bargaining structure produces what I call an epistemic veto point: a moment at which an earlier temporal stage does not merely bind the later stage through an external commitment device, but actively restructures the option space accessible to the later stage, so that the later stage’s hyperbolic discount curve is evaluated over a truncated set. This explains why agents often report that they “would have chosen differently had the option been visible,” without requiring any change in the underlying discount rate. The normative implication is that weakness of will can be locally rational for the later self—given the truncated menu—while globally defective in a way that standard preference-revealed-choice analysis cannot detect, because the truncation itself is invisible to revealed preference.

Please prepare me as a speaker, not as a defender: give me a prep sheet ordered by how likely and damaging each objection is, phrased in the vocabulary that subgroup would actually use. For each objection, indicate its real force, what I should concede up front, the reply I must have ready with its weakest point, and whether it is fatal, serious, or merely awkward. I need particular readiness for three lines of attack: the behavioral economist who will argue the epistemic-veto mechanism is redundant given existing models of present bias plus sophisticated hyperbolic discounters; the Davidsonian who will charge that I have smuggled in a second agent without solving the unity-of-person problem; and the mechanistically minded critic who will ask why, if option-space truncation is real, neuroeconomic studies do not show explicit computational signatures of it. What is the single reply that must land for the proposal to survive? And what is the objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks—perhaps that the view collapses into a hidden-variable dualism about the self, or that by allowing post-hoc reconstruction of veto points it renders akrasia unfalsifiable? Include in your map the one point where I am most exposed if pressed for time.
