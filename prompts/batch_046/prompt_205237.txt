I've been trying to sort out my view on free-floating rationales for a metaethics seminar, and I want to check if my commitments actually cohere before I present anything. Here's where I stand: I think normative reasons can exist without any reasoner around—like, there's a reason not to smash a beautiful crystal formation in an empty universe, even though nobody will ever see it or care. At the same time, I hold that 'ought implies can' is basically correct, but I interpret 'can' as 'could come to be motivated by this consideration through rational reflection.' I also think evolutionary debunking arguments don't touch free-floating reasons, since natural selection never shaped human access to them in the first place. And I still want to say that agent-relative reasons—like my special obligation to my kid—are fully real and can sometimes outweigh those free-floating ones. Do these hang together? I'm especialy worried that if reasons float free of reasoners, then either 'ought implies can' collapses into nonsense, or it forces me to deny that free-floating reasons really obligate anyone in particular. There's also maybe a hidden tension between saying debunking fails because selection never touched these reasons, and still claiming I can come to know them through reflection. Plus the partiality thing feels like it might clash with the idea that free-floating reasons are always authoritative. Can you go through the pairs, tell me which tensions are genuine versus just apparent, rank them by severity, and say what the cheapest revision would be for each? I really don't want to give up the crystal-in-the-void intuition if I can avoid it.
