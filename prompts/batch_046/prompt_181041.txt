Give an example—drawn from the philosophy of historiography—of a conceptual issue in which Leopold von Ranke's evidentiary ideal of reconstructing events precisely as they unfolded conflicts with both E.H. Carr's thesis that historical facts are constituted by present interpretive selection and Hayden White's claim that narrative form imposes meaning prior to empirical verification, where the conflict does not arise from direct contradiction but from a shared hidden premise concerning the ontology of missing evidence. Frame the case around a scenario of systematic archival erasure—say, the deliberate destruction of administrative records in a colonial bureaucracy—such that the absence of documentation does not function as a mere gap to be filled but as an active, causally productive structure that shapes which remaining traces can be assembled into a coherent narrative. Show how this forces both the Rankean reconstructionist and the Whitean constructivist to treat silence as a neutral background condition rather than as a historiographical agent in its own right, and indicate whether resolving the tension requires revising one of the three positions or whether it exposes an irreducible conceptual conflict in the very notion of historical objectivity.
