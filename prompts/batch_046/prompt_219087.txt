I'm taking a course on rational belief and we got to the Sleeping Beauty problem, and I think the whole debate is being overblown because people are mixing up indexicals with actual evidence. So Beauty knows the protocol: coin flip Sunday night, heads means she gets woken Monday and Tuesday with amnesia in between, tails means just Monday. When she wakes up and thinks "I'm awake now," she hasn't learned anything about the coin—she'd be awake in both outcomes, just maybe on Tuesday too. So her credence in heads should stay at 1/2, right? The 1/3 answer seems to come from counting "awakenings" like they're data points and treating them as if they favor tails, but that's like saying "I'm experiencing this moment" is statistical evidence for tails because there are more moments under tails. That feels like a category mistake—it's just self-locating belief, knowing you're at a particular time, which shouldn't update your belief about some external coin flip any more than knowing I'm in Chicago updates my belief about weather in Tokyo.

But my professor said the "self-locating" part is exactly why it's philosophically hard, and mentioned centered worlds and some principle of indifference applied to them. I don't see why this needs special machinery—can't we just model her ignorance about whether it's Monday or Tuesday as a regular hidden variable in a Bayes net? If I don't know which room I'm in, I don't change my beliefs about the building's architecture. So why do philosophers treat this as a deep problem about "centered worlds" rather than a straightforward case of indexical ignorance that leaves the coin probabilty untouched? Is the thier position just committing a base-rate fallacy with experiences, or am I missing something about why self-locating belief is structurally different from not knowing your GPS coordinates?
