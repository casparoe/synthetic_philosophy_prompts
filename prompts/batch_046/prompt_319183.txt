Having spent my career in condensed-matter physics, I have lately been trying to understand how energy policy encodes philosophical commitments. I am struck by a specific institutional contrast. In Germany, the nuclear exit was accompanied by a regulatory culture that treats any residual catastrophic risk—however statistically quantified—as sufficient reason for prohibition, and that frames high-level waste disposal as an open-ended intergenerational moral burden requiring perpetual institutional guardianship rather than a closed engineering problem. In France, by contrast, the state maintains and expands a standardized fleet, treats waste through reprocessing and geological storage as a contained technical cycle, and integrates nuclear generation into carbon-accounting as a baseline low-carbon source. Both policies claim to serve climate mitigation and intergenerational justice, yet they appear to presuppose incompatible pictures of technological risk, of the moral status of imposing future harm, and of what distinguishes a solved problem from an inherited one.

What philosophical commitments does this divergence actually presuppose? Does the German framework require a deontological veto on activities that might catastrophically harm future generations regardless of comparative climate benefit, or can it be reconstructed within a risk-averse consequentialism? Does the French framework presuppose that engineered redundancy transforms moral risk into mere technical failure, and does that commit it to a particular philosophy of human mastery over nature? Reconstruct the implicit theories of risk, responsibility, and intergenerational value in each practice; distinguish what each policy strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; and say whether the two practices are philosophically incompatible or whether a single coherent view—perhaps one about epistemic humility or about the ontology of collective harm—could license both. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforward probabilistic cost-benefit framework applied uniformly across energy sources, what specific institutional practices in either country would have to change?
