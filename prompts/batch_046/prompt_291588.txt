I've been wrestling with a tension in the historiography of science that feels like it should have an obvious resolution, but I keep going in circles. There's the familiar constructivist argument that 'the Middle Ages,' 'the Renaissance,' and 'the Scientific Revolution' are retrospective periodizations—narrative devices that impose false discontinuities onto otherwise continuous cultural change. More recently, I've encountered claims that change-point detection algorithms applied to citation-network density, manuscript-transmission rates, and urban literacy curves across Europe from 1200 to 1700 show smooth logistic growth with no statistically significant breakpoints around 1500 or 1600. This seems to strengthen the case that all three labels are historiographical fictions. But here's the crux that confuses me: if the Scientific Revolution is merely an invented boundary on par with the Renaissance, then what explains the tightly coupled cluster of institutional innovations—state-sponsored scientific academies, standardized peer-review protocols, and the mathematization of experimental practice—that consolidate rapidly after 1650? Either there was a discrete shift in epistemic regime, which would make the Revolution ontologically real and thereby undermine the constructivist critique of the other two periods, or we must account for modern science's distinctive predictive efficacy as a purely gradual, threshold-crossing accumulation that never actually constituted a 'revolution.' I suspect I'm conflating the ontology of historical labels with the causal structure of institutional change, but I can't see exactly where the frame slips.
