I've been working through Al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa for a graduate seminar on medieval Islamic philosophy, and I'm drafting an essay that connects his occasionalist critique of necessary causation to a somewhat niche problem in contemporary philosophy of science: the conditions required for a valid "intervention" in causal modeling when background mechanisms are unstable or opaque. Rather than treating Ghazālī as merely making theological room for miracles, I want to argue that his denial that fire necessarily burns cotton anticipates—without being reducible to—the modern worry that causal inference requires not just observed regularity but stable background conditions that license counterfactual manipulation, and that when those conditions are metaphysically unguaranteed (as they are on occasionalism, since God recreates every connection ex nihilo), the concept of intervention becomes structurally ambiguous in ways that parallel debates about external validity in epidemiology.

Here are the core notes I intend to develop into the essay. First, Ghazālī's claim that God could create burning without fire, or fire without burning, without logical contradiction is usually read as straightforward occasionalism: only God is a true cause, and created things are mere "occasions." Second, I want to resist the reading that this renders phenomenal causation illusory. Instead, I propose that Ghazālī preserves a real but metaphysically thin regularity—what we might call "phenomenal causation"—that is fully available to human knowledge and practical reasoning, even though it carries no ontological power of its own. This, I suggest, is not merely Humean constant conjunction with a theological overlay, but a principled separation between epistemic habit and metaphysical necessity. Third, the niche move: I compare this separation to the debate among causal inference theorists about whether knowing that an intervention produces an effect requires knowing the mechanism linking them, and whether such knowledge is undermined when the causal structure is unstable across contexts. On my reading, Ghazālī's universe is one in which stability conditions are never metaphysically secured—only habitually observed through the sunan Allāh—so his occasionalism functions as a radical limit-case for mechanistic explanation rather than a denial of observed order. Fourth, I ask whether this makes Ghazālī closer to a skeptical position about mechanism, or closer to the view that mechanisms are always provisional descriptions of divine habits. I lean toward the latter, but I worry this over-systematizes a text that is primarily polemical.
