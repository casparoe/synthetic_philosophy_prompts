You chair the ethics review panel for a regional bioconservation-technology authority that licenses field installations combining living ecosystems with autonomous environmental-control systems. Your reviewers have biology training but no formal philosophy background; each proposal receives thirty minutes of review, must yield a signed recommendation (grant, deny, or escalate), and the director who signs off is not a specialist. You need a reusable decision procedure to determine whether a given entity in such an installation—biological or artificial—falls within the scope of moral protection, defined here as protection from unnecessary interference or harm rather than full rights-bearing status. The procedure must be grounded in the view that sentience and moral status are matters of degree rather than binary properties, running from clear cases through intermediate biological cases (insects, cephalopods) to borderline artificial systems (adaptive climate controllers, predictive sensor networks). Design the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests or checkpoints: state each question in plain language, specify what evidence counts as a sufficient answer, indicate where the procedure mandates a “no protection” stop, where it mandates an “escalate to human judgment” stop because the issue is one of values rather than empirical assessment, and flag which steps are irreducibly judgment calls despite being formatted as procedural steps. If you invoke philosophical distinctions—such as between phenomenal experience and access consciousness, or between valenced suffering and mere homeostatic regulation—state them in your own terms without citation or bibliography. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) relocating a colony of bumblebees for habitat restoration; (2) installing an adaptive HVAC-learning network that optimizes temperature, humidity, and airflow across the installation using predictive occupancy and biological stress models; and (3) deploying simple robotic pollinators with minimal sensory feedback loops—treat this as the case where the procedure yields an answer you, as the practitioner, will find unwelcome or counterintuitive, identify exactly where in the sequence that result is produced, and say whether and on what grounds it should be overridden. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: identify its likely false positives, its likely false negatives, and the categories of moral or metaphysical question that it deliberately leaves outside its scope.
