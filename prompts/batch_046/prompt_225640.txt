I was arguing with a friend about whether we can ever really identify what's going on in domains where the information is just structurally hidden from us - like the intrinsic nature of physical stuff, or maybe what's happening inside an AI's weights if interpretability hits a hard wall. My friend laid out this argument that seems to show either we have to be eliminativists about half of science, or we have to let in these weird 'epistemic ghost' entities that only exist to plug gaps. Here are the steps he gave me:

1. Ontological identification should only happen when we have direct or indirect access to the identifying properties.
2. For lots of scientific entities - electrons, black hole interiors, other minds - we lack access to intrinsic properties and only have structural/relational ones.
3. So if (1) is right, we shouldn't actually identify those entities as real; we should be eliminativists or at least agnostic about there ontology.
4. But scientists clearly do identify them, which means (1) must be wrong, and identification can happen through structural roles alone.
5. If structural-role identification is enough, then anything that plays the right explanatory role should be ontologically admitted - including hypothetical entities whose only function is to explain why our access is blocked.
6. These 'epistemic ghosts' are structurally indistinguishable from legitimate entities, so either we admit them too, or we reject structural identification altogether and go back to eliminativism.
7. So we're stuck between global skepticism about hidden-domain ontology and letting in a bunch of spooky filler entities.

This feels like it proves too much either way. Which premise is the weak link? Is it that identification requires access to intrinsic properties, or is there a way to block the slide from structural identification to ghost-admission without just begging the question? I want to keep the idea that we can talk about electrons, but I also don't want to have to defend ghost entities. Where does the argument actually break?
