Below is a draft of a short philosophical essay on the right to strike. I would welcome your comments on three specific points: first, whether the legal distinction I rely on is historically and doctrinally accurate; second, whether my conceptual framing is philosophically coherent; and third, whether I have missed an obvious objection that turns my argument on its head.

The essay argues that American labor law encodes a hidden hierarchy between two kinds of strike action. Under the framework established by the National Labor Relations Act, workers who strike in response to an unfair labor practice—such as the termination of union organizers—are protected against permanent replacement and generally entitled to reinstatement. Workers engaged in purely economic strikes over wages, hours, or working conditions enjoy no comparable protection; employers may hire permanent replacements. Rather than treating this asymmetry as a legislative accident, the essay proposes that it reveals a deeper normative structure: the law protects strikes not when they seek to redistribute economic surplus, but when they defend the institutional preconditions for collective voice itself.

From this observation I develop a conceptual distinction. I call strikes that protect the organizational conditions of bargaining "structural-voice strikes," and strikes that deploy existing bargaining power to alter distributive outcomes "distributive strikes." The central claim is that the moral justification for the right to organize may rest less on workers' entitlement to a fair share of output—though that matters—than on a prior right to preserve the democratic infrastructure of economic life. On this view, a structural-voice strike is not merely instrumental to future distributive gains; it is constitutive of the workers' status as agents capable of collective self-determination. A distributive strike, by contrast, exercises a capacity that already exists, and its protection is therefore contingent and replaceable.

Please comment specifically on whether this framing distorts the historical purpose of strike protections, whether the distinction between structural-voice and distributive strikes can bear the philosophical weight I place on it, and whether there is a stronger counterargument—perhaps rooted in the idea that distributive demands are themselves expressions of collective autonomy—that renders the framing backwards. I am not looking for citations or a reading list; I want to know whether the argument holds together on its own terms.
