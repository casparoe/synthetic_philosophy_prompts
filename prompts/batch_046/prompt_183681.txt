I have been trying to determine my own considered judgments about distributive justice, specifically about the boundary between what people deserve because of their choices and what they suffer or enjoy due to circumstances beyond their control. Rather than defend a theory from the start, I want to see whether my intuitions on specific cases can be captured by a single principle, and where they force that principle to become more complex.

Consider the following verdicts.

First, an individual is born with a severe genetic disorder that significantly limits lifespan and quality of life. I judge that society owes substantial compensation; this is not her fault.

Second, an adult with full information and reasonable alternatives chooses to smoke heavily for decades and develops lung cancer. I judge that she should bear the primary costs; this is option luck.

Third, a worker accepts a dangerous construction job because it is the only employment available in her depressed region that pays enough to support her family, and then suffers a disabling injury. I do not treat this as pure option luck — the choice was structurally coerced — yet it is not pure brute luck either, since she actively assumed the risk. I think compensation is warranted, though perhaps not at the same level as in the first case.

Fourth, a low-lying island community is rendered uninhabitable by sea-level rise driven primarily by emissions from distant generations and nations. The residents contributed negligibly to the cause. This feels like brute luck, but it is distributed across a collective rather than an individual.

Fifth, a qualified job applicant is rejected by a hiring algorithm that predicts high turnover based on demographic proxies — commute distance, neighborhood data — rather than on any individual choice or trait of hers. She never made a decision that warranted the penalty, yet the system treats her as if she had. I think this is brute luck that has been algorithmically disguised as option luck: structural conditions are converted into apparent individual failure.

Sixth, a child grows up in severe poverty in a society that guarantees equal formal schooling, because her parents — themselves facing severely constrained options — could not provide the cultural capital and navigational knowledge required to benefit from credential markets. The child made no choices, yet the deprivation partly traces to parental decisions made under duress. I think she deserves compensation, but any adequate principle must explain how intergenerational transmission modifies the luck-choice boundary.

Is there a coherent principle — perhaps a refined or multi-dimensional version of the brute-luck versus option-luck distinction — that delivers all six verdicts? Specifically, does the standard binary distinction survive the pressure from Case 3 and Case 5, or must it be replaced by a framework that distinguishes direct choice, structurally constrained choice, and structurally disguised brute luck? Furthermore, how should the principle treat intergenerational chains, as in Case 6?

Apply your best principle precisely: state its conditions, map each clause onto the cases above, and identify which case forces the most significant revision to the standard view. Then address the borderline case I keep returning to: an adult declines free, widely available financial-literacy education, then loses her savings in a speculative investment she did not understand. Is her loss option luck? Or does the social obligation to make education genuinely accessible convert her refusal into something closer to brute luck? Finally, predict the verdict your principle gives for a case I have not listed — for instance, a person born into a society where beneficial genetic enhancement is available only to those who pay, who declines it not out of principle but out of ignorance of its advantages, and then suffers avoidable harms from remaining unenhanced.
