I'm working through the formal structure of prioritarian social welfare functions, specifically those that apply a continuous, strictly decreasing weight to welfare gains as a person's baseline well-being increases—something analogous to constant-elasticity weighting. The canonical response to the leveling-down objection is that prioritarianism values absolute weighted welfare rather than equality per se, so harming the well-off without benefiting anyone reduces the total weighted sum and is therefore impermissible. But if the priority weight is continuous and approaches zero asymptotically, then any finite harm to the very well-off can be multiplied by a weight arbitrarily close to zero, making the moral cost of that harm arbitrarily small in magnitude. This seems to imply that there should be some implicit threshold—a point where the weighted loss is so negligible that it can be traded off against even a purely structural improvement in equality, or against no other welfare change at all. Otherwise, the theory appears committed to forbidding every instance of pure leveling, regardless of how trivial the harm. Where exactly do prioritarian theorists place this cutoff, and doesn't introducing it require smuggling equality in as an independent value rather than deriving the prohibition solely from weighted welfare aggregation?
