As a novelist constructing a cosmology for a long-form work, I have devised a fictional universe that is perfectly symmetric: every event, object, and consciousness in the eastern hemisphere has an exact qualitative duplicate in the western hemisphere, with no intrinsic feature distinguishing them except their spatiotemporal relation to one another. Thinking through Black's two spheres and Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, I have arrived at an argument that seems to collapse the very possibility of distinct characters. Here are the steps:

(1) In this universe, Protagonist A in the east and Protagonist B in the west share every qualitative property—every action, every mental state, every relational feature except being spatially situated relative to eastern versus western landmarks.

(2) By the identity of indiscernibles, if two entities share all properties, they are numerically identical.

(3) Therefore, A and B are not two characters but one character existing in two locations.

(4) If a single character can occupy two spatiotemporal positions without contradiction, then location is not an essential property of personal or narrative identity.

(5) If location is inessential, then any entity—whether in another possible narrative world, in a reader's mental reconstruction, or in a derivative adaptation—that duplicates all qualitative properties of this protagonist must also be numerically identical to him.

(6) Consequently, there is only one possible protagonist across all fictional worlds and all minds that conceive of him, which renders authorship, adaptation, and narrative distinction illusory.

(7) I reject this conclusion, yet I cannot see where the chain breaks.

Which premise or inferential step should I abandon? I am particularly attached to preserving (3) as a literary conceit—the idea of a single consciousness bifurcated across a symmetric world—while retaining (2) as a governing law within the fiction. Does saving (3) require me to deny (1) by treating spatiotemporal relations as genuine properties rather than mere external descriptions, and if so, does that not destroy the symmetry my plot requires? Alternatively, is the error a hidden modal slide between (4) and (5), or an equivocation on what counts as a property across worlds? Please diagnose the flaw precisely, explain why the problematic step looked defensible, and indicate what each standard philosophical response—denying purely qualitative sameness, accepting weak discernibility, or restricting the principle's domain—would cost me as a world-builder.
