I was arguing with a friend about evolutionary psych the other day and we kept circling around this one move I don't have a word for. You know how adaptationists will say some human trait — like our love of storytelling, or the fact that we blush — was directly selected for because it aided survival? Then critics come in with the spandrel idea: maybe it's just a side-effect of selection for brain size or social cognition. The adaptationist usually concedes "sure, it's a spandrel" but then immediately uses that concession to defend the broader framework, saying "see, evolutionary thinking still explains it, just indirecty."

What strikes me is that the spandrel concept was originally meant to show that not everything is an adaptation, but in practice it gets turned into an escape hatch. Any time an adaptive story fails, you call the trait a spandrel of some other adaptation, and suddenly the failure isn't a problem for adaptationism anymore — it's just a different kind of evolutionary explanation. I know Gould and Lewontin introduced spandrels partly to push back against pan-adaptationism, but I'm wondering if there's a specific name for this reversal, where a limiting concept gets co-opted into a defensive one. Is it just an auxillary hypothesis? Or is there a term for this kind of "protective belt" move in evolutionary reasoning? And if there's no established term, how should we distinguish it from genuinely admitting that selection doesn't explain the trait in question?
