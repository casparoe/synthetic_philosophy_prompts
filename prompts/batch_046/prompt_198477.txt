I have recently become the primary caregiver for a relative whose continued well-being requires resource inputs that scale non-linearly—far beyond what equal distribution could justify—forcing me to confront whether extreme need can legitimately override ordinary fairness norms. For my philosophy workshop of eighteen graduate students in a single 75-minute session, design an in-class allocation game that makes participants feel the pull of a utility monster modified by a veil of ignorance: each player holds a hidden utility-conversion card, and only one card generates exponentially greater welfare per unit received, but no one knows whose card it is until after the vote. Present your design as exactly five numbered points, each one to two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the physical rules, card distribution, and non-monetary payoff scheme; (2) the decision round that forces a choice between strict equality and funneling resources to a suspected high-converter; (3) the debrief mapping in-game behavior onto the modified utility-monster argument; (4) the predictable failure mode in the first run and the facilitator's remedy; and (5) the precise feature of the idea—specifically the comparison of interpersonal utility magnitudes—that the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally.
