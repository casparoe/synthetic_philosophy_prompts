The debate over whether the doctrine of double effect explains the divergence between switch and footbridge judgements in trolley-style cases has been running since Foot and Thomson introduced the framework, yet it has reached a peculiar stalemate: defenders and critics now broadly agree on the intuitive verdicts, agree that intention is the pivot, and have spent decades refining the means-versus-side-effect distinction without either side conceding ground. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it substantive — about whether intentions carry a distinctive moral weight that outcomes do not — or partly verbal, turning on whether "intending harm" is being read in the philosophy-of-action sense (the plan-structure of the agent) or in the normative-ethics sense (the object of rational choice)? Or are the two camps answering different questions altogether, one asking how agents psychologically structure their choices and the other asking what makes an action permissible? My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is sustained by an unnoticed ambiguity in "intention" that makes each side's argument sound decisive to itself and circular to the other. Assess that hypothesis, using what each side actually says about means, side-effects, and directness, and tell me whether anything — a conceptual distinction that disentangles the two senses, a formal model of agency that renders the means/side-effect boundary determinate, or a reframing of the cases that removes the ambiguity — could genuinely move the debate, or whether the impasse is irreducible because it tracks bedrock differences about the moral significance of what we do versus what we allow to happen.
