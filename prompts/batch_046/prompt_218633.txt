hey, I've been trying to figure out if there's actually a principel behind my gut feelings about right and wrong, or if I'm just being inconsistent. I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, so bear with me if this sounds weird.

Here are my verdicts:
- If I find a colleague has copied proprietary code into our open-source repo without a licence, I think that's wrong and should be flagged, even if it makes the feature ship faster.
- But if I tell my team lead I'm "nearly done" with a task I'm actually stuck on, just to avoid an awkward conversation, I feel that's okay, even though it's technically a lie.
- Telling my partner their awful haircut looks fine: totally okay.
- Telling a client their data was "successfully migrated" when I actually lost part of it due to a script error: not okay, and it feels worse than the team lead lie, even though both involve deception.
- When I'm completely burnt out, saying no to helping a random person on a forum with a basic query feels acceptable.
- But saying no to helping that same person when their live production database is corrupting and I could fix it in thirty seconds feels wrong, even though I'm equally burnt out in both cases.
- I also think refusing to maintain legacy code I inherited but don't technically own is morally okay, whereas refusing to fix a critical bug in that same codebase just because I'm annoyed with management is not okay, despite the situation being identical.

There's one case I keep going back and forth on: telling a junior dev their truely broken pull request is "interesting" rather than "broken" because I don't want to crush them. Sometimes that feels kind; other times it feels patronising and wrong, like I'm treating them with kid gloves.

I've been wondering if there's some hidden principle like "moral latency" — the idea that wrongness depends on how long the harm takes to surface, like eventual consistency failures in distributed systems. That kind of fits the data-loss lie being worse than the progress lie, but it doesn't explain why the burnt-out cases split, or why the haircut lie is fine. So is there a coherent account that captures all of these? Which verdicts would I have to drop to make it work? And what does it predict for the "interesting" case? Also, what about a case I haven't listed — say, deleting negative but honest code reviews to protect a teammate's mental health?
