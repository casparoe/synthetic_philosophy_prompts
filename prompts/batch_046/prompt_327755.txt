I'm auditing a philosophy of mind seminar and, in trying to apply functionalism's commitment to multiple realizability consistently, I think I've stumbled onto a tension that standard treatments seem to overlook. The familiar thought experiments—silicon brains, Martians, inverted spectra—all ask whether a non-biological substrate can realize an individual mental state like pain or belief, on the assumption that if the relevant causal role is duplicated, the mental state follows automatically. But this framing treats mental states as atomistic modules that can be realized in isolation from the system's overall organizational pattern—what I'd call its "phenomenal integration architecture," the specific way discrete states are bound into a unified experiential field. Consider a scenario in which substrate A realizes pain, belief, and desire through a hierarchical predictive-coding architecture, while substrate B realizes the exact same discrete causal roles through a distributed associative architecture with different cross-modal coupling and temporal dynamics. By hypothesis, every individual functional profile is preserved, yet the global structure integrating those states differs.

So my question is this: doesn't functionalism face a dilemma? Either it must smuggle in an unstated requirement of architectural uniformity—undermining multiple realizability at the system level—or it must concede that there is a "system-level phenomenal residue" that varies with substrate even when every discrete mental state is held constant?
