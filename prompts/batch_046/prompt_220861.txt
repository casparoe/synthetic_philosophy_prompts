In debates about the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem people keep reaching for the musician analogy: you're playing for a paycheque rather than for the music, the notes are right but the reason is wrong, and that somehow makes the performance inauthentic. It's used to argue that external rewards always corrupt the value of an act. I want you to audit it properly: lay out the correspondance table -- what's the music, what's the paycheque, what's the audience, what's the 'genuine' performance, and what plays the role of the musician's internal state? Then show me where the mapping breaks: does it smuggle in a picture of authenticity as uncaused? And here's a twist I haven't seen pushed -- apply the same structure to scientific curiosity, where a researcher is funded to discover something she genuinely finds beautiful. Does the paycheque still corrupt the curiosity, or does the analogy collapse there? Tell me whether the conclusion that incentives ruin authenticity survives, or wether the analogy was carrying it the whole time.
