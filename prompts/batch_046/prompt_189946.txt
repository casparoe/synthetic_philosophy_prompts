Next month I present to a philosophy of science colloquium my argument that Jain anekāntavāda—particularly the saptabhaṅgī schema of sevenfold predication—supplies a formal framework for accommodating persistent contradictions in ensemble climate modeling that Western pluralism treats only as provisional disunity. I claim that by indexing each contradictory prediction to a distinct epistemic standpoint (drṣṭi) rather than to an absolute ontology, the seven modes allow high-sensitivity and low-sensitivity equilibrium climate sensitivity estimates to be affirmed simultaneously without collapsing into relativism or requiring an eventual unification.

The audience is precisely this: three scientific pluralists (one Cartwright-style disunity advocate, one pragmatic pluralist, one reductionist skeptic), two analytic epistemologists suspicious of applying non-Western logical frameworks to empirical science, and one historian of Indian philosophy who knows Jain texts well. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually receive, ordered by how likely and damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary and framing of the camp that will raise it, with a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede up front, and what I should hold. Identify the one objection I am hoping nobody asks. Separate settled scholarship on anekāntavāda and saptabhaṅgī from what is genuinely contested—especially my extension to climate-model pluralism—and do not present my interpretive application as the consensus view among Jain scholars or philosophers of science.
