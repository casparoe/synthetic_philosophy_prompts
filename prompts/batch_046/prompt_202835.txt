Draft the joint statement that a Hard Anatta Reductionist and a Narrative Constructivist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Buddhist no-self (anatta) and personal identity. The Reductionist holds that the five aggregates (skandhas) exhaust what exists in personal life, that diachronic identity is a conventional fiction sustained by social and linguistic practice, and that moral agency is best reconstructed as causally linked but ontologically discrete mental events with no underlying owner. The Constructivist holds that anatta denies only a permanent, independent metaphysical substrate (atman), but that a dependently originated, first-personal narrative self—structured by embodied memory, moral commitment, and experiential continuity—is both ontologically real in a conventional or dependently-arisen sense and indispensable for explaining moral responsibility and the coherence of practice.

Your deliverable should be structured as follows.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Include agreement on: the historical formulation of anatta as a rejection of an eternal, unitary soul; the formal structure of the skandha analysis and what it rules out; the conditions under which a person can change or persist; and the shared rejection of a metaphysically substantial self that stands apart from mental and physical processes.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state a precise divergence in wording that the respective camp would accept as an accurate formulation of its own view. Cover at least: (a) whether narrative continuity has any ontological status beyond conventional designation; (b) whether moral responsibility requires a persisting agent or can be fully distributed across discrete mental events; (c) how to interpret severe psychological discontinuity.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. For each crux, provide a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. Invent one case that does not appear in standard literature: a hypothetical subject whose autobiographical narrative is systematically disrupted—preserving emotional responsiveness, moral reasoning, and behavioral patterns, but severing the first-personal link between present and past mental states—while asking whether this subject retains moral agency, whether they are the same person in any meaningful sense, and whether philosophical interpretations of karmic continuity apply. Each camp should predict in advance what it expects on each of these three points.

4. For each crux, state what both camps agree would count as genuinely relevant evidence or argument, and note where the available reasoning is inconclusive for both.

5. A brief section identifying whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive. If so, specify exactly which formulations are equivalent and explain why disagreement persists despite that equivalence.

Write in a formal, polished style appropriate to an academic working document. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not invoke obscure textual sources that depend on precise philological details; work from the general conceptual framework both sides share.

Finally, state how confident you are in your overall analysis, and single out the specific claim, formulation, or prediction you are least sure about, with a brief explanation of why.
