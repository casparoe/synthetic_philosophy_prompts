As a novelist drafting an essay for a quarterly that publishes philosophical reflections on narrative craft, I am working on a piece provisionally titled The Ghostwriter's Belief. The essay examines whether fictional characters can be said to bear derived intentionality in the technical sense employed by philosophers of mind. I frame the question through a speculative scenario: a writer collaborates with an automated system that generates a character's psychological profile not through conscious authorial design but through functional pattern-matching—assigning beliefs and desires according to narrative coherence rather than lived experience. I argue that this arrangement parallels the minimal predictive system Dennett describes, often referred to as a two-bitser, which adopts the intentional stance toward other agents without itself possessing original intentionality. From this parallel, I draw the conclusion that the mental states attributed to fictional characters need not trace back to the original intentionality of the author or to any biological consciousness; rather, they may be purely functionally derived, arising from the interpretive practice of readers much as Dennett's minimal agent derives predictive utility from attributing beliefs. I present this as a direct challenge to Searle's biological naturalism, which maintains that all derived intentionality ultimately depends on original intentionality in conscious beings.

I am concerned that I may have conflated Dennett's intentional stance—an interpretive or predictive strategy—with the metaphysical dependence of derived mental content upon original mental states that Searle insists upon. I also worry that I have mischaracterized the two-bitser as itself lacking all original intentionality, when perhaps Dennett's point concerns only the economy of explanation rather than the ontological source of mental content. Furthermore, I am unsure whether applying this framework to literary characters introduces a category error: characters are not agents that can be interpreted, but representations that depend for their meaning on an interpreting audience. Could you assess these specific points? I ask for a precise evaluation of the philosophical accuracy of each move, of the coherence of the analogy between automated narrative generation and Dennett's minimal system, and of whether my use of Searle's distinction holds up under scrutiny. I do not need a bibliography, a reading list, or general encouragement—only a rigorous, point-by-point commentary on where the argument succeeds and where it subtly fails.
