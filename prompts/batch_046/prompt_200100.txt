You work as a risk-communication officer at a regional emergency-management office, issuing public hazard forecasts—hurricane tracks, seismic aftershock sequences, epidemic curves—without a specialist mathematician on call and typically with fifteen minutes to sign off on an alert level. Write a reusable decision procedure that translates the conceptual distinctions among determinism, deterministic chaos, and stochastic openness into an ordered checklist you can actually run. For each step, specify the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether resolving it requires a factual judgment or an irreducible value call. The procedure should classify any forecast into one of three regimes—deterministic, chaotic-but-bounded, or genuinely open—and should state precisely where it stops with a mandatory classification and where it must hand the decision upward to a senior officer because the remaining issue is risk tolerance rather than dynamics. Include explicit rules for compound events in which one subsystem behaves chaotically and another deterministically, specifying how to merge their classifications into a single alert recommendation. Apply the procedure to three cases: a seventy-two-hour hurricane-track update supported by high-resolution initial-condition data; a post-mainshock aftershock sequence with poor near-field sensor coverage and highly nonlinear stress redistribution; and an infectious-disease projection in a region with unknown vaccination coverage and multiple circulating variant dynamics, where the procedure should yield a recommendation that you personally regard as overly cautious. Conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot determine—specifically, whether metaphysical determinism has any practical bearing on the choice of alert level, and which steps remain vulnerable to misfire when initial-condition data are incomplete but not obviously inadequate.
