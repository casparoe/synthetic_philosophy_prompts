I'm trying to isolate what I actually take religious belief to be, testing my intuitions against concrete cases rather than accepting a ready-made theory. Here are my verdicts, with the kind of reasoning behind each, and I want to know whether they hang together under one principle or force me to split the concept.

Case 1: A person asserts on Easter that "Christ is risen," treats the claim as a metaphor for personal renewal, lives with no reference to it in practical reasoning, and feels no obligation arising from it. Verdict: this is cultural or aesthetic participation, not religious belief in any substantial sense.

Case 2: A patient refuses chemotherapy because she believes God will heal her; the belief is explicit, emotionally central, directs action, and she cannot articulate reasons that would persuade a non-believer, yet she holds it with complete confidence. Verdict: clearly religious belief.

Case 3: A climate scientist organizes her career and identity around the claim that human activity is destabilizing the climate, treats dissent as not merely wrong but morally intolerable, and describes this commitment as "sacred," though she denies any deity. Verdict: structurally religious—functioning as a framework that regulates practice and is held with a distinctive certitude—though not theistically so.

Case 4: A mathematician insists that mathematical objects exist mind-independently; she treats this as a fundamental feature of reality that explains the applicability of mathematics, but it plays no role in her emotional or ethical life. Verdict: metaphysical belief, not religious.

Case 5: A Buddhist monk follows precepts, meditates, and participates in rituals; when asked whether he "believes" in rebirth, he says the practice matters, not the proposition, and he cannot translate his commitment into a set of assent-statements. Verdict: religious belief is present, but it is not primarily propositional assent.

Case 6: A secular humanist holds that all humans possess equal dignity; she treats this as non-negotiable, derives absolute ethical obligations from it, and experiences its violation as a kind of sacrilege, though she grounds it in no transcendent source. Verdict: religious in form, even if secular in content.

My tentative thought is that religious belief is a framework commitment that regulates practice and is held with a certitude that is not answerable to ordinary evidential challenge—what one might call a "grammar" rather than a hypothesis. But I am unsure whether this saves Case 3 and Case 6 only by expanding "religious" so widely that it loses its point, or whether it wrongly excludes Case 4.
