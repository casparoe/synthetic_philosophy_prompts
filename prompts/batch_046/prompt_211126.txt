I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether Whig history is a correctable cognitive bias or an unavoidable feature of narrative coherence—she insisted that selecting past events for present relevance is just rational explanation, while I kept trying to isolate a specific distortion in treating the present as the moral telos of history, but we kept talking past each other because neither of us could point to the felt experience of that pressure. I need a classroom simulation for a 75-minute seminar of about sixteen upper-level students that makes participants actually feel the pull to write the past as inevitable progress toward their present endpoint, not merely understand the concept intellectually. Design an endpoint-variant narrative construction exercise: divide students into small groups and give each group identical archival fragments about a real or plausible nineteenth-century reform movement—some successes, some dead ends, some ambiguous failures—while assigning each group a different institutional outcome as its present (for instance, a modern welfare-state framework, a minimal-state regulatory regime, and an invented counterfactual regime that never actually existed). Each group must construct a coherent historical narrative leading to its assigned endpoint, classify fragments as necessary precursors or regrettable detours, and defend the result to peers who hold other endpoints. Include explicit rules and a non-monetary reputation mechanism, a minute-by-minute timeline for the session, a debrief that maps the groups’ selections and omissions onto the distinction between causal explanation and teleological moral congratulation, an honest admission that the emotional satisfaction of inevitability cannot be reproduced without direct facilitator framing and must be supplied there, and a frank prediction of what will misfire the first time this is run and how to correct for it.
