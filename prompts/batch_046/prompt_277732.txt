I have just come away from a long, rather heated argument with a close friend about whether international anti-corruption metrics apply universal standards or quietly export Western institutional norms, and I want to understand where our disagreement is genuinely conceptual rather than empirical. The practice that puzzles me is this: development institutions score countries downward for personalized patronage networks and ceremonial gift-exchange in public hiring—practices common in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and East Asia—while treating structurally analogous exchanges in Western administrative life, such as post-regulatory employment in the industries one formerly oversaw or contract-based lobbying through formal legal channels, as categorically distinct and not as corruption indicators. My friend insists this is consistent universalism: corruption is nothing more than a departure from formal procedural impartiality, regardless of cultural context. I suspect the metrics instead presuppose a specifically liberal-contractualist conception of the state, in which only deviations from codified rules and transparent procedures count as corruption, while exchanges that remain within formal legality are definitionally excluded. What philosophical theory of corruption does that scoring practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory with precision, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates comfortably, and say whether it is compatible with rival conceptions—say, one that defines corruption as any systematic subordination of public purpose to private gain irrespective of formal legality. Identify where the metrics are philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories might license the same classifications. Finally, if we replaced the implicit theory with that broader, purpose-based view, what concrete changes would be required in how these institutions measure, classify, and sanction corruption?
