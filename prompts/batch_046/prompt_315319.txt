I've been trying to steelman a resolution to the paradox of deontology that I haven't seen fully articulated in the literature—though it borrows from agent-relative moral theories and institutional design discussions common on rationalist forums. The view is this: the deontological prohibition against direct harm is not absolute but lexically prior within a bounded domain, and it weakens only when the agent's causal contribution is structurally mediated—say, through budgetary rules, voting mechanisms, or organizational hierarchies—rather than enacted through direct bodily intervention. Call this 'mediated-agency triage.' On this account, refusing to kill one to harvest organs for five remains impermissible because the surgeon's causal role is unmediated, whereas a legislator who votes against a tax that would have funded life-saving treatments does not violate the same prohibition, since the harm flows through distributed institutional architecture rather than her individual bodily action. The framework aims to preserve the core deontological intuition without generating the standard paradox that one must allow greater harm to avoid doing a lesser harm.

But I want to stress-test it: what would actually count against mediated-agency triage?
