I have been thinking about actions that seem admirable yet clearly not required—giving away most of one’s income, or risking one’s life for a stranger. The philosophical literature calls this supererogation, but I find the debate curiously static: Kantians tend to absorb such acts into imperfect duties, while others insist they lie genuinely beyond obligation. I suspect the impasse hides a deeper disagreement about the structure of moral reasons itself—specifically, whether reasons operate like a budget with a fixed ceiling of obligation, leaving only discretionary “leftover” space, or like an overflow system where reasons can accumulate past that ceiling and generate genuinely optional excellence. Is the deadlock therefore substantive—about the ontology of moral reasons—or is it largely verbal, turning on whether we reserve the word “duty” for everything morally weighty? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the discussion: a refined logic of reasons, a phenomenological account of praise and blame, or a different kind of distinction altogether?
