I am currently taking an upper-level seminar in philosophy of psychiatry, and I need to design a 75-minute classroom activity for about 16 undergraduate students who have only a passing familiarity with Ian Hacking. The goal is to make the group feel the force of looping effects—specifically, how a psychiatric classification can alter the very behavior it describes, which then forces the category itself to shift—without anyone lecturing about it first. To keep this from being a rehearsed discussion of well-known cases, I want the activity centered on a hypothetical, narrowly defined proposed diagnosis: “context-collapse dysregulation,” understood as an inability to maintain stable attentional frames during rapid switching between digital work platforms.

Please design a structured simulation with the following elements. First, specify the room layout, the division of roles (labeled subjects versus evaluators/clinicians), and the materials needed—nothing that requires real money, but some form of feedback mechanism that gives participants a reason to change their behavior. Second, provide a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes. Third, lay out the rules precisely: how the initial assessment is conducted, what tasks the labeled group must perform, and how the evaluators are required to revise their criteria in response to observed behavioral changes, so that the participants themselves discover that the category is unstable before anyone names it. Fourth, include a debrief that maps each phase of the activity onto Hacking’s distinction between indifferent and interactive kinds, explicitly noting where the loop closes and where it breaks down. Fifth, be frank about which feature of real-world looping—such as the historical sedimentation of institutional practices and record-keeping—this single session cannot reproduce, and tell me exactly how I, as facilitator, should supply that missing element verbally. Sixth, warn me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how to handle it. The participants should come away seeing that classification is not merely descriptive but performative, and they should reach that insight through the rules and stakes of the game rather than through my explanation. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the activity must work for students who have not read Hacking’s texts in detail.
