Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently turned to philosophy of mind, I am trying to grasp the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis in a form precise enough to survive scientific scrutiny. Rather than rehearse familiar anecdotes about color lexicons, consider this constructed case: a language in which every finite verb carries an obligatory evidential morpheme—direct perceptual, inferential, or reportative—with no neutral default. If native speakers of this language consistently assign different Bayesian prior weights to testimonial versus observational updates, is that best interpreted as strong linguistic relativity altering the architecture of reasoning, or as weak relativity merely reshaping the salience and retrieval costs of hypotheses? Please explain the conceptual issue at stake, particularly how one might distinguish a genuine restructuring of inferential machinery from a contingent bias in attention or memory access.
