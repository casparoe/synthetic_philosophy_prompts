I am taking an advanced seminar in infinite ethics, and our instructor has advanced a tentative framework—let us call it scale-sensitive prioritarianism—for comparing worlds that contain infinite populations. Over several sessions she has rendered a consistent set of judgments that seem to cohere, but I am unsure whether they depend on a single underlying principle or on a family of principles that happen to coincide on the cases she has discussed so far. Specifically, she maintains that a world with infinitely many individuals each experiencing well-being distributed with asymptotic density approaching zero but never reaching it is superior to a world with the same aggregate profile arranged in discrete, periodic epochs of suffering and flourishing, despite both worlds containing infinite amounts of each. She further holds that augmenting an already infinite baseline of barely positive lives with a finite cluster of excellent lives produces no net improvement, whereas introducing that same finite cluster into an otherwise empty world yields substantial value. Finally, she treats an infinite ascending chain—where each successive generation experiences twice the well-being of its predecessor—as incommensurable with any world of uniform well-being, refusing to rank them rather than declaring them equal.

Given this track record, I want to predict her verdict on a new scenario: a world populated solely by intermittent existents, entities that exist for a single temporal unit, then cease for ten, repeating infinitely, with high well-being confined strictly to the moments of existence. The temporal density of well-being here matches the asymptotic baseline she endorsed, yet the distribution is discontinuous at the microscopic scale. I also want to know how she would assess a fractal world, in which well-being and suffering are arranged in self-similar clusters at every scale of magnification, so that no smoothing or averaging operation can eliminate either.

My request is not merely for the predictions, but for the analysis behind them. Please identify at least two distinct rules—perhaps one grounded in temporal averaging across intervals, another in pointwise evaluation weighted by continuity, and perhaps a third that appeals to integration across scales—that are each consistent with her three earlier judgments. For each rule, state what it predicts about both the intermittent-existents case and the fractal world. Then give your best guess as to which rule she actually employs, with the reason for preferring it given her prior commitments. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—distinct from both intermittency and fractal structure—that would definitively separate the two leading rules. I am not asking for bibliographic references or citations; I want the conceptual reconstruction drawn strictly from the judgments themselves.
