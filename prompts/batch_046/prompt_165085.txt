I have recently become the primary caregiver for an aging dog whose cognitive decline—disorientation, disrupted sleep-wake cycles, and an apparent failure to recognize familiar spaces—has made the philosophy of animal minds abruptly personal. This experience has led me back to Descartes’s bête-machine doctrine, not as a historical curiosity but as a live conceptual framework: if one takes seriously the claim that animal behavior can in principle be explained by mechanistic physiology without recourse to rational soul or conscious thought, then the burden shifts to identifying exactly which observed capacities, if any, genuinely resist that reduction.

Given this framing, I am trying to decide where limited intellectual energy should go over the next few years—whether in philosophy of mind, comparative cognition, or the ethics of artificial minds. Which open conceptual questions about animal minds are most worth settling first, in the sense that resolving them would restructure downstream work on moral status, comparative neuroscience, and the design criteria for sentient AI? I am particularly interested in three candidate problem-clusters. First, whether there exists a non-arbitrary threshold between reflexive mechanism and minimal phenomenal awareness, and how one might defend such a boundary without smuggling in human-like rationality as the implicit criterion. Second, whether Descartes’s own physiological writings—especially his account of the pineal gland and the flow of animal spirits—can be interpreted as leaving conceptual space for a non-dualist but still non-reductive account of animal affect, or whether that reading collapses into anachronism. Third, how spectrum-style arguments about gradations of sentience should inform practical resource allocation in animal welfare and in the evaluation of synthetic minds.

Please rank these clusters—or substitute others you regard as more upstream—and defend the ordering. For each, specify why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers working without direct laboratory access, what kind of conceptual or empirical evidence over the next few years would move it up or down your list, and which implications you regard as overrated. In particular, I want you to examine whether the inference from some form of animal awareness to specific policy conclusions about captivity or experimentation actually holds, or whether it depends on hidden premises that deserve scrutiny. Deliver a defended agenda rather than a survey; I want to know what deserves the years, not merely what remains unresolved.
