I'm drafting a novel in which a protagonist must choose between remaining at the bedside of a dying parent and accepting a once-in-a-generation artistic commission that demands total absence. While researching the ethical architecture of the scene, I overheard an argument between two philosophers at a writers' residency—I'll call them Elias and Soren—that has stayed with me, because neither could concede a point without collapsing something essential about the other.

Elias opened by saying that when two values are genuinely incommensurable—say, filial duty and creative vocation—reason reaches a hard boundary. There is no common currency in which to weigh them, so whichever path one takes necessarily violates a real claim the other value makes. He described this as an unavoidable moral remainder: not guilt exactly, but a rational residue that proves the choice was underdetermined by reason alone.

Soren replied that Elias was conflating incommensurability with mere incomparability. The absence of a shared metric, she said, does not entail the absence of rational ordering. She proposed that values can be compared through structural coherence—not by reducing them to a single scale, but by asking which choice integrates more fully with the agent's established commitments and long-range practical identity, without treating coherence itself as a hidden master-value that competes with the others.

Elias countered that structural coherence is precisely the hidden master-value in disguise. If coherence is what licenses the comparison, then filial duty and artistic vocation are being weighed against a third term, and incommensurability dissolves into monism about coherence. He insisted that for values to remain genuinely plural and incommensurable, no second-order principle can adjudicate between them; otherwise the conflict was never truly hard, only computationally complex.

Soren conceded that coherence functions as a second-order condition, but denied that it operates as a first-order metric. She argued that coherence does not supply a unit of measurement—like utility points or dignity weights—but rather a formal constraint: a rational agent cannot simultaneously affirm contradictory commitments. Thus, choosing the commission over the bedside is not trading filial duty for coherence; it is recognizing that one's practical identity already contains resources for prioritizing without reducing either value to the other.

Elias then introduced what he called the covering-value challenge: any rational comparison, even structural, requires a covering value under which both items fall. Without such a cover, preference replaces reason. He claimed Soren's coherence is just that cover, renamed.

Soren's final move was to distinguish covering values from enabling conditions. Coherence, she said, is not a value that competes with filial duty or art; it is a condition on having values at all. A choice can therefore be rational—supported by domain-specific reasons—while still leaving both values genuinely unsatisfied and incomparable in any metric sense.
