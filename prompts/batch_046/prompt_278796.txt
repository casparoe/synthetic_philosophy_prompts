I cover the philosophy of science and artificial intelligence for a general audience, and I have been struck by how the debate over attributing beliefs to large language models has settled into a repetitive pattern without advancing. The dispute typically runs as follows. Proponents of the intentional stance argue that predicting an LLM's responses—especially in multi-turn reasoning, deception, or role-play—requires treating it as holding beliefs about the world, intentions toward the user, and preferences over outcomes, and that this predictive success is precisely what justifies the attribution under Dennett's framework. Critics respond that the stance was formulated for entities with continuous sensory-motor engagement, evolutionary pressures, and integrated cognitive architectures, and that applying it to a stateless, next-token predictor trained on internet text severs the link between predictive success and genuine mental attribution; they add that mechanistic interpretability now offers alternative predictions without invoking mental states. I want to understand why this exchange has stalled. Is it a substantive disagreement about whether LLMs instantiate the functional organization that makes belief-attribution literally true? Is it a verbal dispute about whether belief requires biological embodiment or can apply to any system with the right input-output profile? Or are the two sides answering different questions—one about predictive methodology in engineering, one about ontological commitment in philosophy—under the single heading of the intentional stance? Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says, indicate whether a hidden empirical question—say, whether intentional framing improves out-of-distribution prediction compared to purely mechanistic models—is doing the work, and suggest what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, or a change of question. Also address this specific tension: both sides agree that a pure lookup table mimicking an LLM should not be attributed beliefs, yet they disagree about whether a trained transformer is closer to the lookup table or to a cognitive agent. Does this shared intuition reveal that the debate is really about visibility of internal architecture rather than about predictive practice? If so, what does that imply for whether progress should be expected?
