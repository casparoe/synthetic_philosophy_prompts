Write the opening address, approximately eight minutes when spoken aloud, for the first session of a three-day interdisciplinary workshop titled Scaling Minds: Metrics of Moral Weight. The audience of roughly sixty is split evenly between comparative neuroanatomists — fluent in encephalization quotients, neuronal packing densities, and the well-known but conceptually awkward divergence between absolute brain mass and cortical neuron counts in cetaceans versus great apes — and moral philosophers with no laboratory background. Your remarks must structurally turn on the problem of whether moral consideration scales with total brain volume, with total neuron number, or with some other architectural feature entirely, using that cetacean-primate discrepancy as the concrete pivot. To keep the speech from becoming either a generic welcome or a disguised literature review, invent and embed a specific hypothetical case study for the occasion: call it the Aequitas organism, a chimeric model with a brain mass falling between that of a domestic pig and a human adult, but with neuronal architecture that maps cleanly onto neither, so that volumetric, neuron-count, and connectivity-density metrics all pull in different directions and intuition collapses. The speech should do real structural work: it must frame the workshop's central disagreement, establish that neither half of the room can claim methodological neutrality, and leave both sides unsettled in a productive direction without resolving the tension. The tone should be formal and conversational, appropriate for colleagues who will work together immediately afterward. Do not cite specific studies, name particular authors, or include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further references. Do not use verbatim quotations from real philosophical or scientific texts; paraphrase any familiar principle or maxim without quotation marks. Avoid referring to highly obscure technical results that a well-read but non-specialist model could not be expected to know; rely instead on the conceptual tension itself and the invented Aequitas case to drive the argument.
