I'm working through Berkeley's economic writings and find a conceptual knot that I can't untangle without stepping outside the usual summaries. Berkeley treats money not as a commodity with intrinsic worth but as a kind of sign or counter—something whose function is purely representational, directing labor and goods rather than embodying value. At the same time, he advocates for a national bank issuing paper credit backed by land and public confidence, and he insists that such credit can store purchasing power across time, circulating even when individual holders are not actively attending to it. The tension I want explained is this: if money is genuinely just a token or idea in the representational sense he favors, and if, on his metaphysics, the being of sensible things consists in being perceived, then in what sense does a banknote continue to exist—and to bind obligations—during intervals when no mind is perceiving it? Is there a coherent Berkeleyan account of institutional persistence that separates social obligations from the esse est percipi of material coins, or does his economics implicitly rely on a kind of collective, enduring perception that his philosophy of individual minds doesn't obviously provide? I don't need the historical details of his banking proposals; I'm after the conceptual architecture—how his semiotics of value, his immaterialism about substance, and his notion of social agreement might fit together without contradiction.
