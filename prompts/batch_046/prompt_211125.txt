Nobody has a philosophy degree, we just like thinking about how people reason about each other in games and decision stuff. Lately I got obsessed with this idea that in strategic situations it's not enough for everyone to be rational - they also need common knowledge that everyone is rational, and then common knowledge of that, going up forever. I read there's something called epistemic game theory that studies exactly how this breaks when people have slightly wrong beliefs about what others believe.

I want to design a classroom game where we actually feel that break happen, not just hear about it. The setting is our usual room with tables pushed together. Small stakes: each pair that coordinates correctly splits a $5 coffee gift card. Here's my rough concept - split into four pairs. Each pair gets a private envelope with a number card (either 4 or 5) and a rule card saying "you may only press the green button together if you are 100 percent certain your partner knows the number is 5 and is rational about pressing." Then there's a message chain across pairs: each round they can send one written note, but I secretly "lose" about 20 percent of them without telling anyone which ones. After three rounds of potential messages, every pair decides whether to press the button together.

Can you design the exact payoff table and card distribution so that if common knowledge were perfect they'd always press and win, but with the lost messages and the infinite regress of "do they know I know?" they usually hesitate or fail?
