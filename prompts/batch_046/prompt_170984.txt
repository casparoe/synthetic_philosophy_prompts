I've been working through Berkeley's master argument in a reading group on early modern immaterialism, and I've reconstructed a version that seems to prove not only that material objects don't exist, but that other minds don't either—something Berkeley explicitly denies. Here is the chain: (1) The master argument holds that conceiving any object O as existing unperceived is impossible, because the very act of conception forms an idea of O, and that idea is itself perceived by the conceiver—so O is necessarily perceived in the idea. (2) This reasoning applies symmetrically to mental states: conceiving another person's thought S requires forming an idea of S, which is necessarily my own mental state and therefore perceived by me, not by them as an other. (3) So I can never genuinely represent another mind's states as genuinely other and unperceived by me; every putative representation of another spirit collapses into my own idea. (4) If representation of other minds is impossible in principle, then Berkeley's immaterialism collapses into solipsism: only my ideas and God remain. (5) But Berkeley insists on the reality of other finite spirits, so (4) must be rejected. (6) The standard escape invokes a use-mention distinction—representing O via an idea is not identical to perceiving the idea itself as O—but this looks like it undermines the original argument, which also requires representing an unperceived tree via an idea without reducing the tree to the idea. (7) The overextension might instead lie in (2)'s symmetry claim, treating mental representation as necessarily self-referential in a way that physical-object representation is not, though it's not obvious why the vehicle of representation should behave differently in the two cases.

I'd like to preserve both Berkeley's rejection of matter and his acceptance of other minds. Which step should I reject—(2)'s symmetry, (6)'s appeal to the use-mention distinction, or the slide from (3) to (4)—and what does that rejection cost the internal coherence of his immaterialism? Give me a committed answer: take a definite position on where the argument breaks, explain why that step looked plausible, and price the damage to Berkeley's system rather than laying out options and leaving me to choose. I'm especially interested in whether fixing the argument forces us to adopt something like a modern transparency-of-representation thesis that Berkeley himself wouldn't have recognized.
