Write a villanelle about Hempel's paradox of confirmation. Fix the two refrains as the logically equivalent formulations of the hypothesis—"All ravens are black" and "All non-black things are non-ravens"—and let them alternate through the poem's nineteen lines according to the form, meeting together in the final couplet. Use the repetition structurally: pair the first refrain with lines describing observed black ravens that feel like straightforward confirmations, and pair the second with lines describing observed non-ravens that happen not to be black—copper wires, snow, green leaves—so that the reader accumulates the same evidential tension Hempel describes. Place the turn in the penultimate quatrain, at the moment the equivalence condition is acknowledged, so that the closing couplet holds the two refrains in direct adjacency without resolving whether observing a white shoe confirms the claim. Make the poem accurate enough that a philosopher of science recognizes the Nicod criterion and the conflict with logical equivalence; do not explain the paradox in prose within the poem, and do not resolve it—let the villanelle's circular form embody the way the paradox keeps returning.
