I've been arguing with friends from an AI alignment reading group about whether Grice's account of speaker meaning can be operationalized in institutional design, and I think the idea might serve as the structural spine for the opening remarks I'm delivering at next month's workshop, "Language and Commitment: From Conversation to Contract." The audience will be split roughly evenly: philosophers of language and logicians who treat the Cooperative Principle and the maxims of conversation as familiar machinery, and legal theorists and machine-learning engineers who tend to think of meaning as semantic content plus noise to be filtered out. I want the remarks—about eight minutes, formal but not academic—to do real work: they should show, using Grice's distinction between what is said and what is conversationally implicated, that treating an utterance as exhausted by its literal content systematically erases the speaker's actual commitments.

Specifically, I want the speech to build around two Gricean properties—calculability and cancellability—to demonstrate that a speaker can mean more than she says without that surplus being part of the literal meaning, and that she can cancel an implicature without contradiction, which proves the implicature was never semantically entailed. The concrete case should be institutional: perhaps a committee chair declaring that "the proposal meets every formal requirement" in a context where the room understands her to be signaling deep reservation, or an AI system interpreting a human instruction that carries a scalar implicature about the scope of permitted actions. I want you to weave in a somewhat niche angle I've been developing: in multi-agent or human-machine settings, the failure to recover intended implicature is not merely a pragmatic error but a structural misalignment, because the receiving system treats the literal string as the complete input vector and therefore acts on a commitment the speaker never actually made. Don't ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; don't invoke obscure secondary literature; and don't explain Grice to the philosophers—explain the institutional stakes to everyone else. The speech should open by naming the split in the room, develop the single case carefully, and close with the claim that any institution encoding human language into rules—whether a court interpreting contracts or a model interpreting instructions—needs a theory of speaker meaning, not just semantics.
