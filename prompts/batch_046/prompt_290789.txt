I am revising the syllabus for my graduate metaphysics seminar and have become preoccupied with a tension that I think my students should grapple with directly. On one side stands the familiar distinction between cranes—explanatory structures that build complexity from simpler, well-understood bases—and skyhooks, which invoke unexplained explainers to account for higher-level phenomena. On the other side lies the problem of greedy reductionism, the tendency to explain away the organizational significance of a complex system by reducing it to its simplest constituent parts. Most classroom treatments apply these ideas to evolutionary biology or consciousness studies, but I want to push the framework into less familiar territory: the ontology of social institutions. Specifically, I am wondering whether a strictly crane-based, anti-skyhook ontology can account for the emergence of normative institutional reality—things like the deontic powers embedded in legal contracts, accreditation standards, or academic tenure—without either smuggling in an irreducible collective intentionality or collapsing into a greedy reductionism that dissolves institutions into mere aggregates of individual behavior.

Could you please describe the most important implications of maintaining such a framework—cranes only, no skyhooks, with greedy reductionism rigorously avoided—for how we should understand the metaphysics of institutional facts? I am particularly interested in whether this approach forces us to reconceive deontic powers as purely emergent relational properties sustained by distributed practices, or whether it reveals an unavoidable explanatory gap that only a skyhook could fill. Please write your response in American English, and please do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or suggested readings; I am seeking direct conceptual analysis that a student could engage with without consulting external sources.
