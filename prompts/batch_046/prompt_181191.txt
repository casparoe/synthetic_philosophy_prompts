Provide a rigorous conceptual analysis of the "quining qualia" strategy—the argument that phenomenal properties, or qualia, are not merely epistemically hidden but incoherent as theoretical posits. Focus specifically on the logical move from inaccessibility to ontological incoherence: explain how a proponent might argue that the notion of an intrinsic, non-relational qualitative character involves a category mistake, treating what is actually a functional or relational feature of cognitive access as if it were a bare, structureless substrate. Then examine the following tension. If one denies that any residual "what-it's-like" property remains beyond complete functional and physical description, one appears committed to the view that a perfect functional duplicate of a conscious subject would lack consciousness; yet if one grants that the duplicate is conscious, the denial of qualia seems to collapse into the claim that consciousness just is the functional organization, which renders the eliminativist thesis redundant rather than genuinely revisionary. Is there a coherent intermediate position—perhaps reconceiving qualia not as non-existent entities but as theoretical posits that fail to satisfy their own conditions of individuation—such that the distinction between eliminativism about phenomenal properties and an austere form of representationalism dissolves? In your answer, trace the logical structure directly rather than appealing to bibliographic authority, and address whether this intermediate view avoids both the charge of leaving an explanatory gap and the charge of changing the subject.
