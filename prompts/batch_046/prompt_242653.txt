I am preparing the question sequence for a colloquium Q&A with an unnamed philosopher who defends a stringent form of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. She holds that there are no qualitative properties of experience as they are naively conceived; rather, our apparent acquaintance with phenomenal character results from a systematic misapplication of phenomenal concepts driven by a cognitive transparency bias, whereby representational content about experience is mistaken for an intrinsic feature of experience itself. Recently she has extended this framework to temporal consciousness, arguing that the apparent flow of time is structurally analogous to stroboscopic apparent motion — not an illusion that possesses a property, but an illusion of a kind of property, constructed from discrete representational snapshots that the mind integrates into a continuous seeming.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions in British English that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. The sequence should turn on the actual content of her position: press where the literature presses, anticipate her standard reply, and include the follow-up keyed to that reply. In particular, include:

- a question that presses the distinction between thin phenomenal presence (the bare sense that something is occurring) and thick phenomenal presence (the rich qualitative character), asking how illusionism can account for both without collapsing them into a single misdescription;

- a question on her stroboscopic-motion analogy for temporal flow, specifically asking what empirical finding about temporal integration windows or neural binding mechanisms could count against rather than for her view, together with a follow-up anticipating her response that such data only concern representational mechanics and leave the conceptual error untouched;

- a question about the introspective objection: if illusionism is true, then in judging ourselves to be in pain or seeing red we are systematically mistaken about the very state we occupy, which appears self-undermining because the judgement seems to be about itself;

- a final question designed to make it progressively harder for her to deflect objections by insisting they are empirical rather than conceptual matters.

The questions should be suitable for a formal colloquium setting, should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should avoid generic biographical questions.
