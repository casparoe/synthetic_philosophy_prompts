I've been thinking about pain asymbolia—cases where patients report feeling pain but show no avoidance behavior or negative affect—and I built an argument that now feels like it dissolves too much. (1) Asymbolics genuinely feel pain, as shown by accurate verbal reports and preserved nociceptive pathways. (2) If a mental state's badness were intrinsic to its qualitative character, then anyone in that state would necessarily experience it as bad and be motivated to avoid it. (3) Since asymbolics lack both negative affect and avoidance, the badness of pain cannot be intrinsic to its sensory quality; it must be a separable evaluative layer. (4) Apply the separation principle symmetrically: imagine a subject who has intense distress, avoidance, and conceptual labeling of a bodily state as harmful, yet reports no qualitative character resembling pain—call this evaluative dissociation without sensation. (5) By (3), this subject is not in pain, only suffering from a non-painful state they mislabel. (6) This implies that pain is nothing more than suffering plus an inert qualitative tag, which makes the qualitative aspect morally and motivationally redundant. (7) Generalized, no sensory state is intrinsically aversive; all badness is contingent on separable evaluation. (8) But then arguments in animal ethics that appeal to the intrinsic badness of pain—independent of the animal's conceptual evaluation—lose their force, reducing to claims about contingent distress responses. (9) I find that conclusion hard to swallow, yet (1) and (2) seem well-supported. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I'd prefer not to abandon (1), and I'm suspicious of (4) as a manufactured edge case, but I want to know the standard ways philosophers of mind price the alternatives.
