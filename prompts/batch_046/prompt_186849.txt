I'm delivering the valedictory address at my high school graduation ceremony next Thursday, and I would be grateful if you could help me compose it. This year our senior class completed an unusual year-long exercise: every student maintained a Franklin-style moral-perfection journal modeled on the grid he describes in his autobiography — seven columns for the days of the week, thirteen rows for the virtues in his specific order, with black marks recording each lapse. We undertook this not in expectation of reaching perfection, since Franklin himself acknowledges that he never did and continued revising his little book well into old age, but precisely because the attempt was meant to reshape us. The audience will be mixed: about two hundred classmates who lived with these grids, parents and grandparents for whom Franklin may be little more than a founding father on currency, and several faculty members — particularly our history teachers — who will notice any misrepresentation of his reasoning, such as his decision to place Temperance first because he believed a cool and clear head was the necessary foundation for Silence, Order, and the virtues that depend on self-command.

Because Franklin's view is that moral perfection is asymptotic — approached but never reached, with failure built into the practice rather than disqualifying it — I want that idea to govern the structure of the speech rather than serve as decoration. This means the address should not congratulate the graduating class on having arrived at maturity or moral completeness; it should avoid any rhetoric that treats our parents' guidance as having produced flawless adults; and it should still express genuine gratitude without falsehood. Please write a formal, polished speech of no more than 650 words — approximately five minutes — appropriate for an eighteen-year-old speaker. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of any kind, and if you invoke Franklin's own language, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
