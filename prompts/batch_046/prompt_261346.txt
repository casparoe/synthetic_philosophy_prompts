I am a high school student working through an independent study in philosophy of knowledge, and I have become particularly interested in the Nyāya school's account of the pramāṇas — the four recognized instruments of valid cognition: direct perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), analogical comparison (upamāna), and authoritative verbal testimony (śabda). What strikes me is that Nyāya thinkers do not treat these as interchangeable; they assign precise conditions under which each yields yathārtha (genuine) rather than ayathārtha (illusory) cognition, and they devote considerable attention to conflicts between them — especially whether śabda can be set aside when pratyakṣa appears to contradict it, or whether upamāna can establish a novel category when direct resemblance is weak.

Rather than writing an essay, I would like to explore this through fiction. Could you write a short story, set perhaps in an isolated research outpost where a small team has discovered an anomalous artifact or transmission, in which the characters must collectively determine what the object actually is? The narrative should weave all four pramāṇas into the plot in a natural way: one character insists on what she directly perceives; another draws an inference from traces; a third relies on analogy to a known technology; and a fourth appeals to an archived manual or expert record. Crucially, I want the story to center on a specific conceptual tension from Nyāya epistemology — for instance, the problem of whether testimony retains its authority when it conflicts with present perception, or the conditions under which analogical knowledge collapses into mere resemblance. The characters should argue like real people, not like philosophical caricatures, and the resolution should leave the epistemic status genuinely ambiguous or at least contested, reflecting the difficulty of adjudicating between valid sources of knowledge. Please do not include citations, footnotes, or a bibliography; I am interested in the conceptual dynamics embedded in the narrative itself.
