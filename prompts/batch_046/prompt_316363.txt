I am preparing a long-form piece on the politics embedded in technological design, centered on Langdon Winner's claim that artifacts have politics—particularly his observation that nuclear power plants require centralized, hierarchical control structures to operate safely. I am struggling with what seems like an unacknowledged tension: if an artifact's physical properties genuinely mandate a specific form of social organization for safe operation, then Winner's thesis appears to collapse into hard technological determinism, where engineering constraints dictate governance regardless of democratic will. If that is the case, then treating democratic deliberation over energy infrastructure as a meaningful choice seems like a category error. Yet the scholars I have interviewed continue to invoke Winner precisely to argue for more democratic oversight of technology, not less. Could you help me see where the reasoning breaks down? Specifically, does Winner's framework contain a normative escape hatch—such as the option to reject the artifact entirely—or is his determinism actually "soft" in a way that preserves space for collective choice without contradicting the claim that the technology, once adopted, carries authoritarian politics with it?
