I've been working through an essay for a course on classical Chinese philosophy that tries to disentangle Zhuangzi's butterfly dream from the Cartesian skeptical tradition that usually frames it. Rather than treating the passage as asking how we know we are not now dreaming, I argue it stages an ontological dissolution of the subject-object boundary—what I am tentatively calling trans-subjective metamorphosis—in which the distinction between Zhuangzi and the butterfly is not an illusion to be corrected but a temporary coagulation of transformations (hua) with no underlying substrate. On this reading, the standard no-self interpretation is also misleading, because denying a stable self still presupposes the category of subjecthood that Zhuangzi is trying to dissolve entirely; the self is not absent but fluid to the point of being unassignable. I then push the point into a tension: if transformation is total and there is no persistent substrate, then the narrative voice that reports that Zhuangzi woke and was a man seems to presuppose exactly the continuity it denies, and moral or cognitive accountability across dream and waking states collapses because there is no enduring agent to bear it.

I want comments specifically on whether this reading forces Zhuangzi into a framework he would not recognize—particularly whether distinguishing epistemic skepticism from ontological dissolution is anachronistic given pre-Qin metaphysics—and on whether the internal tension I identify (the narrative voice requiring continuity that the ontology denies) is a genuine problem for the interpretation or merely a failure on my part to read the text as performative rather than propositional.
