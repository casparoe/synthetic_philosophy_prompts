I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years working through philosophy of archaeology and the philosophy of history, and I am drafting a position paper for a workshop on inference from material culture. Before presenting, I want a consistency audit of the following commitments, which I have adopted in different contexts and now need to hold together. (a) Archaeological interpretation is structurally an inverse problem: we observe material traces and must infer the generative social and mental processes, and it should therefore borrow formal methods from inverse-problem theory, including explicit regularization conditions that constrain underdetermination. (b) I am a methodological naturalist: I reject supernatural causation, but I accept that mental representations, collective intentions, and institutional rules are natural entities capable of figuring in genuine causal explanations of material patterns. (c) I hold that artifacts are not merely passive traces of pre-existing beliefs but are cognitive artifacts that partially constitute the mental and institutional processes they are taken to represent; consequently, the distinction between "symbolic" and "functional" objects collapses in practice, since every functional object in a ritual context carries symbolic load and every symbol has functional consequences. (d) Parsimony is a regulative ideal: when two reconstructions account equally well for the physical and distributional evidence, we should prefer the one that posits fewer novel social institutions or specific propositional beliefs. (e) Ethnographic analogy is indispensable for bridging the gap from objects to institutions; without comparing living societies to ancient remains, we have no non-arbitrary basis for selecting among underdetermined interpretations. (f) I accept interpretive charity—we should attribute to past agents beliefs that are rational relative to their environment—but I also think evolutionary-style debunking arguments apply to our own intuitive applications of charity: many judgments about what counts as "rational" in a given environment are culturally biased and unreliable. (g) There are objective facts about what past societies believed and intended, independent of our present interpretive frameworks. (h) Nevertheless, when the archaeological record genuinely underdetermines belief-state—when two mutually incompatible belief systems or institutional rules would generate materially identical signatures—there is no further fact of the matter about which one they actually held; the underdetermination is ontological, not merely epistemic. (i) Reflective equilibrium between general theoretical principles (parsimony, naturalism, charity) and particular case judgments—for instance, that a given burial pattern indicates hierarchical priesthood rather than kin-based elder council—is the correct method for stabilizing inference.

I am particularly unsure about the interaction between (c) and (d): if artifacts constitute the mental processes, then reducing institutional ontology might simultaneously reduce the mental reality being explained, which seems to undermine parsimony rather than serve it. I also worry that (e) and (f) pull in opposite directions—analogy imports rich structures, charity biases our selection among them—but I cannot tell whether this is a deep conflict or a superficial one that dissolves under closer inspection. Meanwhile, (b) and (h) might appear incompatible if one assumes naturalism requires every mental state to leave a unique material trace, yet I suspect that tension dissolves once underdetermination is properly understood as ontological rather than epistemic.

Please audit this set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and specify the least costly revision that restores consistency. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each state which commitment would have to be weakened or abandoned and at what theoretical cost. In addition, please indicate what specific argument or finding would change your assessment—for example, a demonstration that cognitive-artifact ontology necessarily violates parsimony, or a proof that ethnographic analogy can never be constrained by charity without circularity—and note explicitly where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
