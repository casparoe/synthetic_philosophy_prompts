In a reading group on practical reason, I've been defending a somewhat non-standard version of the consequentialist cluelessness problem. Rather than treating our ignorance of distant outcomes as a symmetric epistemic limitation that applies equally to all long-range options, I want to say that the difficulty is structural: any evaluation procedure that tries to maximize over world-histories must apply criteria that are themselves partly constituted by those world-histories, which makes the evaluation function reflexively unstable rather than merely uncertain. This framing seems to block the usual reply that we should just proceed with expected-value reasoning using wide confidence intervals, because the instability is in the metric, not just in the inputs. But I want to know how exposed this position actually is.

What would have to be true for it to fail—whether that's an empirical demonstration that institutional planners do navigate reflexive evaluation without arbitrariness, a formal result showing that reflexive criteria can be stabilized under certain conditions, or a philosophical argument that the instability collapses into ordinary uncertainty? I want you to distinguish what would be genuinely fatal from what would only be awkward, and to say what a defender of this structural view should themselves concede counts against them. In particular, does the view risk insulating consequentialism too thoroughly by turning every objection into a feature of the evaluation framework, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a sign that the position has been emptied of content? Also, which rival approach—perhaps a satisficing or localist revision—would be strengthened if this structural framing turned out to be wrong, and what specific case or result should I be watching for that would settle the matter?
