I've been working through a problem in moral psychology that keeps surfacing in discussions on rationalist forums, and I'd value a decomposed credence rather than a bare opinion. The specific claim is this: an action possesses full moral worth if and only if the agent's recognition of the right-making feature functions as the counterfactually dominant reason—meaning that in the nearest possible worlds where the agent's non-moral motives (say, social approval or emotional gratification) are absent but the moral feature remains present, she still performs the action—rather than merely being a co-present reason that happens not to be corrupted by those motives. My own credence in this counterfactual-dominance condition is about 0.60; a colleague I was discussing this with over coffee holds it closer to 0.35, preferring a 'co-presence with transparency' view where the right reason need only be genuinely operative and recognisably so to the agent, without needing to win a counterfactual contest.

Please give me your own number—your credence that counterfactual dominance is necessary for full moral worth—together with a decomposition. Break it into at least these sub-questions: (i) whether moral worth is best understood as binary or as a graded property that can be diluted by competing motives; (ii) whether requiring counterfactual dominance sets the bar implausibly high, such that ordinary acts of kindness performed with mixed motives would systematically lack worth; (iii) whether an agent's introspective access to her motivational structure is reliable enough to support a 'transparency' condition, or whether we should treat motivational topology as partly opaque; and (iv) whether the distinction between dominant and co-present reasons even tracks a morally significant difference, or whether it collapses into a merely psychological taxonomy. Assign a rough probability to each, explain briefly how they combine to yield your overall figure, and tell me which sub-question your verdict is most sensitive to.

Finally, please say explicitly what argument or evidence would change your mind—whether a convincing phenomenological report that agents rarely experience one reason as dominant, a formal model of practical reasoning that makes counterfactual dominance incoherent, or something else—and flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail, particularly regarding the move from psychological description to normative evaluation.
