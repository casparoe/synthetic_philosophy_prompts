I'm writing a study guide for my philosophy of artificial cognition seminar, and I want to organize where philosophers actually stand on attributing beliefs to AI systems—not by the labels they use, but by the concrete commitments that separate their positions. Please construct a decision tree (not an author-by-author list) that takes a student from an initial diagnostic question to one of several leaf-positions, with each internal node phrased as a claim or case a student could answer without already knowing which philosopher it points to.

Root node: You observe a large language model consistently producing accurate, context-sensitive outputs about a domain. You say, "The system believes that X." Is this statement best understood as a literal description of an internal mental state, or as a move within a predictive, interpretive, or normative practice?

Branch 1—Literal interpretation:
- Node 1a: For the attribution to be literally true, must the system possess phenomenal consciousness, or is functional organization (e.g., information integration, goal-directed processing, appropriate causal roles) sufficient?
  - If functional organization is sufficient (1a-i): Does full mechanistic transparency—knowing every parameter, activation pattern, and gradient—undermine the literal attribution, or can one literally believe something even when the underlying mechanism is completely exposed?
  - If consciousness is required (1a-ii): Do you allow that an artificial architecture could in principle satisfy this condition, or do you hold that biological substrate is necessary?

Branch 2—Non-literal interpretation (predictive, interpretive, or normative):
- Node 2a: Is the justification for belief-talk purely pragmatic—its predictive and explanatory utility—or does it require embedding the system in a social practice of accountability, reason-giving, or interpretive charity?
  - If purely pragmatic (2a-i): Is this stance available only when mechanistic explanation is unavailable or impractical (the classic intentional-stance condition), or is it also appropriate when we have full transparency into the model's weights and computations?
  - If social/normative embedding is required (2a-ii): Can an isolated, non-interactive system ever be a proper target of belief-attribution, or must it be situated within human institutions that treat it as a responsible agent?

The leaves should be clearly characterized: (i) strong functional literalism (functional architecture sufficient, transparency irrelevant); (ii) biological-consciousness literalism (consciousness required, artificial substrate excluded); (iii) transparency-blocked instrumentalism (predictive stance available only under opacity); (iv) transparency-permitting instrumentalism (predictive stance available regardless of mechanistic knowledge); (v) normative/pragmatic attribution (requires social embedding and accountability practices); (vi) pure predictive instrumentalism (always available, never literal, no social conditions); (vii) global eliminativism (belief-attribution to AI is never appropriate).

Please also identify any logical combinations of answers that are unoccupied—such as a view that requires consciousness but denies artificial substrate could ever satisfy it combined with a claim that transparency never blocks attribution—and briefly diagnose whether each gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and specify what further distinction would be needed to split them. Keep every node answerable without reference to any named philosopher.
