I want a specification, not a survey: the full list of requirements that an adequate normative theory of sunk-cost reasoning must satisfy if it is to answer whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, and if so, under exactly which conditions. The theory must cover at least three structurally different cases: first, a scientific research program that has consumed substantial funding without yielding results, where abandonment frees capital but dissolves a team whose collective expertise is not portable to other problems; second, a personal vocational commitment built over years of training, where continuing imposes further costs but leaving threatens the narrative coherence of the chooser's practical identity; third, an institutional infrastructure investment—say, a transportation or energy system—that has failed on its original technical metrics but is now woven into regulatory, social, and maintenance networks that depend on its continued operation. For each case, derive the specific desideratum it imposes: must the theory distinguish resource costs from identity-constituting commitments? Must it preserve a difference between honoring a prior expenditure and being rationally bound by it? Must it account for the rational weight of maintaining path-dependent institutional stability versus optimizing from scratch? Then examine conflicts among your desiderata: can a theory both treat all past expenditures as normatively inert and still explain why dissolving an embedded institutional system carries a different rational burden from dropping a failed experiment? Can it respect the distinction between having moral or practical status and the weight of that status without collapsing into a simple cost-benefit calculus? Identify any subset you think is jointly unsatisfiable given standard rational-choice constraints, and say why. Briefly score three broad approaches against the list—a pure forward-looking model that considers only future consequences, a commitment-based view that treats prior choices as generating continuing reasons, and a relational-institutional view that weighs costs against the social structures they sustain—but keep the weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on the scorecard. Finally, take a definite position: state which single desideratum you regard as non-negotiable for any adequate theory, and identify which of the three approaches fails it most clearly and for what reason.
