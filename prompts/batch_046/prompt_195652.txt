I'm working through a problem for my metaphysics seminar that links the special composition question to naturalistic theories of mental representation, and the conclusion seems to pull in both directions at once. Here is the chain. (1) Teleosemantic accounts fix mental content by causal-functional history: a representation is about whatever conditions in the environment reliably caused it in evolutionary or learning history. (2) If special composition is restricted, then some combinations of parts—say, my coffee mug and a specific crater on the far side of the moon—do not form any object. (3) Nevertheless, through associative learning or complex causal chaining, a cognitive system could enter a mental state jointly responsive to both the mug and the crater, with a unified functional role. (4) The teleosemanticist must assign this state some content. If she says it represents a single fused object, then she attributes content anchored to an object that does not exist, breaking the causal anchoring that makes the theory naturalistic. (5) If she says it represents the mug and the crater separately, she needs a non-circular criterion for distinguishing this state from one that genuinely represents a composite object—like a table—whose parts do compose a whole. (6) But any such criterion seems to presuppose an answer to the special composition question itself. (7) So to avoid circularity, the teleosemanticist must guarantee that every combination jointly available to cause a unified state actually composes an object; that is, she must accept unrestricted composition. (8) Yet unrestricted composition entails that scattered entities everywhere form real objects, which seems wildly implausible as ontology. (9) So the argument seems to prove that either restricted composition undermines naturalistic mental content, or unrestricted composition is true despite its absurdity.

I would prefer to keep premise (1) and also keep restricted composition, but I cannot see the exit. Where does this slip—perhaps in the move from (3) to (4), or in the claim in (6) that no functional distinction is available without prior ontology? Please identify the weakest step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives, especially whether rejecting (5) requires giving up on determinacy of mental content altogether. Also, please say what would change your mind: which distinction, counterexample, or formal result would lead you to reject a different step, and where your diagnosis is most likely to fail.
