I spent thirty years as an atmospheric chemist, retired recently, and have since begun studying philosophy in a structured way. I now find myself caught in a recurring practical dilemma that feels conceptually deeper than ordinary indecision. Former colleagues ask me to continue peer-reviewing manuscripts and serving on climate-policy advisory panels. When I agree, I tell myself that my specific expertise is irreplaceable and that the stakes are too high to withdraw; when I refuse, I tell myself that retirement is an objective boundary that automatically dissolves professional duty, as though obligation expired like a subscription. I recognize that both explanations serve the outcome I prefer, and that both treat conditions I actively maintain—my continued visibility, my willingness to respond, even the social convention that retirement is a hard line—as if they were unalterable constraints I simply discovered. I am not asking for career advice. Rather, I want to know whether this pattern constitutes a specific philosophical failure: treating self-cultivated institutional roles as if they were brute, unchosen circumstances in order to avoid owning the choice to continue or stop. If it is recognized, what is its precise structure, and how does it differ from ordinary rationalization or from weighing competing obligations? I am especially interested in whether there is an analysis of the case where the "given" one hides behind is not one's body or past, but one's constructed professional identity.
