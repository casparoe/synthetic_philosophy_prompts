In a seminar on political philosophy, a colleague dismissed my defence of an international tribunal by invoking Pascal’s line that ‘justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is tyrannical’. She read it as showing that, since the court has no army of its own, its verdicts are empty gestures, yet giving it coercive power would make it despotic; therefore, she argued, we ought to abandon the project altogether rather than reform it. I am sure the slogan is being made to carry more weight than it can bear, but I cannot pinpoint the sleight of hand.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that one-liner might actually be expressing? For each reading, specify: (i) what ‘justice’ denotes—retributive, distributive, or procedural; (ii) what ‘force’ denotes—a monopoly on violence, coercive sanction, or structural capacity; (iii) whether the claim is a tautology, a contingent empirical observation, or a normative principle; (iv) whether it is defensible; and (v) whether that particular reading actually supports her conclusion that an unenforced tribunal should be abandoned rather than supplemented with enforcement mechanisms. Present the readings as a menu rather than selecting one, and indicate which interpretation she is most likely smuggling in to make her case.
