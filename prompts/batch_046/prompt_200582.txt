I run a philosophy workshop with about 12 grad students, 90 minutes, and I want an activity about well-being theories that doesn't just rehash Nozick's experience machine in the usual way. Instead, use a "modular plug" scenario: participants are split into engineers, subjects, and auditors. The engineers build "life packages" for the subjects by mixing real vs simulated modules -- pleasure, achievement, relationships -- each with different budget costs and authenticity tags. The subjects rank what they care about, then in round two learn that one module they thought was real was actually simulated, but they can't tell which unless they request an "authenticity audit" that permanently lowers the subjective value of that module even if it was real. The auditors score each package using three rubrics (hedonic, desire-satisfaction, objective-list) but they only see partial data and have to fight over which score counts. Include clear rules, a timeline with rounds, and a debrief that maps round one to desire-satisfaction failure, round two to the hedonism/authenticity conflict, and the audit cost to objective-list concerns about reality and knowledge. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: since the simulation is perfect by design, participants won't actually feel the phenomenological gap between real and simulated achievement, so the facilitator has to supply that intuition in discussion. Predict that some group will try to game the budget by labeling everything simulated and hoarding resources -- block this with a mandatory 30% real-content floor and explain in the debrief that this artificial constraint mirrors the "cost" objection to pure hedonism. Write this casually, american english, maybe with a typo or two, and don't ask for citations or a reading list.
