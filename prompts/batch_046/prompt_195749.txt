Some archaeological heritage boards have this unwritten rule: if an artifact type — say, a particular clay seal or blade shape — shows up in stratified deposits across 400+ years with basically zero stlyistic drift, they classifiy it as functional/utilitarian and deny it protected ritual-status for repatriation claims. The working assumption seems to be that genuine religious or institutional belief always produces visible material innovation over time, whereas practical needs stay static. What philosophy of inference from objects to beliefs does that presuppose? Is it committed to the idea that symbolic content must be stylistically expressed, or just that continuity implies absence of meaning? Could the same practice survive if we thought beliefs often stabilize material forms precisely because they're sacred and shouldn't be altered? Reconstruct the implicit theory, check whether it's actually required by the classifcation system or just one interpretation among several, and sketch what a repatriation framework would look like if it started from the opposite premise — that long material continuity can be a sign of deeply held normative commitment rather than its absence.
