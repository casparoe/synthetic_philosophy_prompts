I'm taking a course on the philosophy of mathematics and I'm trying to clarify exactly where Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems cease to be informative and where they remain structurally illuminating when applied outside formal arithmetic. I have formed tentative verdicts on several proposed extensions, but I am not sure they hang together.

Case 1: The Lucas-Penrose argument that human mathematical intuition transcends any consistent formal system because a human can recognize the Gödel sentence of that system as true. My verdict: this overreaches. The argument assumes the mind operates as a single, consistent, recursively axiomatized system with a fixed interpretation, which we have no reason to grant.

Case 2: The claim that if physics were eventually formalized into a single consistent axiomatic theory, Gödel's first theorem would entail the existence of true physical statements unprovable within that theory. My verdict: this also overreaches, but for a different reason—physical theories are not formal systems in the syntactic sense required by Gödel, since they rely on empirical interpretation and open-ended revision rather than recursive derivability.

Case 3: The suggestion that legal reasoning exhibits Gödelian gaps: any consistent body of statutory law will contain cases that the law itself, applied consistently, cannot resolve without judicial interpretation. My verdict: this is a legitimate structural analogy. The gap arises not from syntactic incompleteness but from the interpretive openness of legal concepts.

Case 4: The direct application of Gödel's second theorem to arithmetic: we cannot prove the consistency of Peano arithmetic using only methods formalizable within Peano arithmetic itself. My verdict: this is correct and should not be analogized carelessly.

Case 5: The argument that ethics, if formalized consistently, must contain true moral propositions that the system cannot derive—similar in form to Case 3 but applied to normative reasoning. My verdict: I am inclined to reject this, even though it looks parallel to Case 3. Moral disagreement seems to stem from substantive conflict rather than formal undecidability within a shared axiomatic framework.

Case 6: The popular claim that Gödel's results prove artificial intelligence will always possess logical blind spots—that any consistent AI reasoning architecture will fail to recognize some true statements within its domain. My verdict: I think this is largely a misreading, closer to Case 1 than to Case 4, though I keep wavering because the language of formal system is used so loosely in computer science.

What principle, if any, distinguishes the legitimate analogies in Case 3 and Case 4 from the illegitimate ones in Cases 1, 2, 5, and 6? Is the key factor the presence of a genuinely recursive, interpretation-fixed axiom set, or is it something about whether the domain permits empirical or interpretive supplementation? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause of it is motivated by which case, and show what it predicts for a case I have not listed—say, the claim that a consistent and complete taxonomy of biological species is impossible because Gödel's theorem applies to classification systems. Finally, be honest about whether saving all six verdicts requires an ad hoc patch, and if so, which verdict—Case 3 or Case 5—should be overturned to preserve a simpler principle?
