Having spent a career measuring allometric scaling and comparative neuroanatomy, I have recently begun studying moral philosophy and find myself stuck on a debate that seems empirically settled yet philosophically frozen: whether brain size is morally relevant. The data are uncontroversial—encephalization quotients vary widely, cortical neuron density differs across phyla, and body-size corrections are standard practice in biology—yet the ethical discussion has barely moved. One camp treats absolute brain mass or neuron count as at least a probabilistic proxy for the complexity of interests that ground moral status; the other insists that only specific organizational architectures, never scale alone, could bear normative weight. Both sides accept the same comparative neuroscience, agree that suffering matters, and offer no empirical predictions that differ.

Why, then, has this stalled for so long? Is it a substantive disagreement about which natural properties ground considerability, a verbal dispute over whether "relevant" means causally explanatory, statistically correlated, or normatively sufficient, or a hidden normative clash about the burden of proof? I am particularly curious whether part of the impasse stems from an unexamined analogy: in biology we correct metabolic measures for body mass via allometric scaling, yet in ethics no one asks whether moral weight should be similarly corrected, leaving gross brain size to do misleading work. Given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, or a reframing—perhaps separating informational capacity from phenomenal richness—that would actually advance things? Or is the question malformed, asking neurobiology to settle a boundary that requires an independent ethical argument? What kind of disagreement is this, really, and what would count as genuine progress?
