Write a dialogue between two scholars—Dr. Elena Vasquez, a political theorist, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a historian of ideas—debating American exceptionalism not as a patriotic slogan but as a contested conceptual structure that intertwines description, norm, and ideology. Vasquez defends a refined descriptive thesis: American political development exhibits a counterfactual-robust institutional path dependence—specifically, the sustained interaction of federalism, judicial supremacy, and a non-confessional civic religion—that would have persisted across a range of nearby historical counterfactuals (for instance, an earlier abolition of slavery or the absence of the New Deal coalition). She insists that this robustness can be assessed without presupposing that the outcome was normatively destined. Chen rejects this framing not by denying institutional particularity but by arguing that "exceptionalism" operates as an ideological performative: it retroactively selects certain liberal-constitutional features as the salient baseline for comparison, normativizes them as the implicit telos of modern politics, and thereby obscures structurally analogous path dependencies in other settler-colonial republics. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues: Vasquez should explain why descriptive counterfactual robustness is not dissolved merely by the normative framing used to report it, and she should address whether her own selection of which institutions count as salient already smuggles in the exceptionalist baseline. Chen should explain why the performative critique does not collapse into a generic social-constructionism that denies all comparative institutional analysis, and he should respond specifically to Vasquez’s counterfactual test—perhaps by challenging which counterfactual worlds are admissible without already importing the normative framework she claims to bracket. Both interlocutors should be equally competent; neither should be a strawman. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or appeals to specific texts by name; the argument should stand entirely on its conceptual merits.
