Someone says parts make up a whole only when they have some kind of unified causal power that isn't just the sum of the parts — like an organism's metabolism or a machine's functioning. But then you point out that if you replace every cell in my body over ten years, the causal profile stays the same but intuitivly it's still me, whereas if you scatter my atoms and reassemble them elsewhere with the same causal links via remote signals, suddenly it's not me anymore, even though the integration is identical. So the defender adds a split: there's the kind of togetherness that makes something one thing right now, and then there's the kind that keeps it the same thing as parts change over time, and they apply one rule to organisms and another to artifacts or scattered copies, but they never explain why the split tracks ontology rather than just saving their intuitons. I can think of a couple other cases where this happens — like with quantum-entangled particles that share a joint state but aren't spatially connected, or with distributed computing networks where nodes are replaced constantly. Is there an established name for this exact move, where criteria about parts being bound together for composition get quietly split into a "right now" version and a "over time" version specifically to dodge counterexamples about replacement and scattering?
