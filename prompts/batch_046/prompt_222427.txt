Work within the ethics of memory and forgetting—specifically the tension between Margalit’s arguments for collective remembrance and Rieff’s case for strategic forgetting—but apply it to a case the standard literature largely ignores.

My half-formed idea is this: the familiar debate frames remembering and forgetting as obligations of individual or collective subjects—survivors, nations, families—balancing historical justice against psychological health and social renewal. I want to ask whether there is a structurally different problem for institutional archives, especially public ones governed by democratic mandates. Archives do not “heal,” yet they constantly make choices about retention, decay, de-accession, and algorithmic expiration that determine what future citizens can know. The precise question is whether such institutions have a positive duty to design forgetting—through scheduled data decay, selective non-preservation, or curatorial omission—and whether the moral justification for that duty must borrow entirely from Margalit’s ethics of remembrance and Rieff’s therapeutic argument for letting go, or whether it requires an independent framework grounded in democratic legitimacy, epistemic hygiene, or the conditions for fair deliberation. In other words, does the institutional case collapse back into personal ethics, or does it reveal a hidden dependency in the personal case?
