In a philosophy of mind course I am taking, a friend recently argued that Molyneux's problem is essentially settled once we accept that the brain contains amodal spatial representations—geometric structures that are neither specifically visual nor tactile but can be accessed by either modality. This struck me as too quick, but I am struggling to articulate exactly where the difficulty remains. The canonical formulation asks whether someone blind from birth, upon suddenly gaining sight, could immediately distinguish a cube from a sphere by vision alone if they had previously known them only through touch. Most textbooks frame this as an empirical question about sensory integration; I want to push past that framing and examine a deeper conceptual tension between structural isomorphism—the idea that vision and touch share the same geometric description—and phenomenal access, or the qualitative, modality-specific feel of spatial properties.

Could you explain, without appealing to neuropsychological case studies or bibliographic references, whether positing an amodal spatial schema actually dissolves Molyneux's problem or merely relocates it? Specifically, I would like to understand whether cross-modal identification requires shared phenomenal character, shared structural description, or some prior abstract representational format, and where precisely the philosophical gap lies if we grant the existence of such a schema. Please aim for an intuitive but conceptually rigorous account, and do not end by simply listing unresolved empirical questions—clarify where the conceptual pressure actually sits.
