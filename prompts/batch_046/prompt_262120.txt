Does the scale argument for the moral importance of insects—that astronomical population sizes multiplied by even a minute per-capita probability of sentience yield overwhelming aggregate weight—survive if we replace scalar point-estimate probabilities with a structurally uncertain model space?

State the question with enough precision that it admits a determinate answer. The standard formulation treats the probability that an insect is conscious as a single value p, however small, and computes an expected moral value across the population N (often estimated near 10^19). Critics either drive p toward zero or reject expected-value aggregation across ontological categories. Both responses, however, assume that our epistemic state can be captured by a distribution over parameters within one fixed model of sentience. The proposal should ask whether the argument collapses when we acknowledge structural uncertainty: we do not merely lack a precise value for p, but we do not know which architectural model is correct—whether something like integrated information, global workspace dynamics, predictive-processing accounts, or yet-unarticulated sub-cortical architectures are the right framework for attributing experience at all, and each framework implies radically different conditions for consciousness in organisms lacking a cortex.

Set out why existing answers leave this open. Much of the literature either treats p as a subjective credence calibrated to behavioral evidence without modeling competing architectures, or it dismisses the scale argument on normative grounds (e.g., that expected value fails across species boundaries) without examining whether the epistemic framing itself undermines the computation. Your task is to determine whether this gap is fatal.

Propose an approach that is conceptual rather than empirical—no new experiments on insect cognition, no commitment to resolving the neuroscience within the semester. Construct a minimal decision-theoretic scaffolding in which an agent assigns weights not to a single p but to a family of admissible models M_i, each carrying its own internal parameter estimates and its own mapping from neural/behavioral features to sentience. Examine whether the aggregate expected moral weight remains robust across reasonable weighting schemes (uniform over models, parsimony-weighted, or calibrated by analogical reasoning from vertebrates). Explain why this framing is preferable to the obvious alternatives: lowering p within one model begs the question against architectures that assign high probabilities, while purely normative rejections ignore the epistemic problem altogether.

Say exactly what a positive result looks like—perhaps that all admissible architectures assign non-negligible weights to high-N populations, so structural uncertainty does not dissolve the scale concern—and what failure looks like—perhaps that the admissible models diverge so radically in their internal p-estimates that no principled aggregation is possible, leaving the scale argument without a stable conclusion.
