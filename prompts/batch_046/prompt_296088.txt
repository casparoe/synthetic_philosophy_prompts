I'm prepping a module for my grad phil-of-mind seminar and keep circling back to this tension that I think students gloss over too quickly. Dennett's two-bitser — that minimal robot with just two bits of internal state that we read through the intentional stance — is usually taught as the classic case of purely derived intentionality. It doesn't really want anything; we just project beliefs and desires onto it because the predictive strategy works. Searle, of course, says that's precisely the point: the syntax is never sufficient for semantics, and whatever aboutness the two-bitser seems to have comes entirely from us, not from inside the system. But I want my students to push past the standard standoff.

Here's the twist I'm injecting: imagine we don't just have the bare two-bitser, but one wired with coupled error-correction — the occurence of internal feedback means its bit-flips aren't just triggered by sensors, but are regulated by an internal homeostatic mechanism that preserves the system's own operational coherence. Not biological survival, necessarily, but a functional integrity condition that causally feeds back into the representational updates. The question is whether this homeostatic coupling gives the system anything like original intentionality, or whether Dennett's stance-based derivation still swallows the story whole. Does Searle's biological naturalism do hidden work here by smuggling in normativity, or can Dennett's functionalism absorb the twist without collapsing into panpsychism?

So compare Dennett's two-bitser framework with Searle's original-versus-derived intentionality disctinction, specifically running both through this modified case. I want them to argue which view handles the coupled system better and where each one actually breaks down — not just list differences, but show me the fracture points. And please, no bibliographies or reading lists; this is for discussion, not a term paper. Just clear reasoning, maybe a little messy if that's where the argument takes you.
