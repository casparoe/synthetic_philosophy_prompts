I have recently become personally invested in this question after my brother, who is preparing for ordained ministry, asked me whether divine omniscience can genuinely coexist with human freedom. I tried to construct a Molinist defence for him, but the reasoning now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to prove far too much.

(1) If Molinism is true, God possesses middle knowledge: for every possible person P and every possible circumstance C, God knows whether P would freely choose X in C.
(2) The formation of belief is, for rational agents, an exercise of libertarian freedom; hence there are counterfactuals of doxastic freedom—let us call them CDFs—contained within God’s middle knowledge.
(3) Therefore God knows, prior to creation, precisely which philosophers in which seminar settings would freely accept or reject Molinism.
(4) God actualises exactly one feasible world; within it, the actual distribution of philosophical opinion is fixed by the CDFs that obtain in the circumstances God selected.
(5) In the actual world, Molinism is a minority position among analytic philosophers of religion.
(6) So, in every feasible world containing these same philosophers in these same circumstances, they freely reject Molinism.
(7) Yet God could have actualised a different feasible world—say, one in which the same thinkers encountered a different sequence of arguments earlier—where the relevant CDFs yield widespread acceptance.
(8) If God is maximally good and Molinism is true, then God has reason to actualise a world in which His true account of providence is widely recognised.
(9) Hence God ought to have actualised a world in which Molinism is widely accepted.
(10) God evidently did not do so.
(11) Therefore either God lacks middle knowledge, or Molinism is false, or God is not maximally good.

What troubles me is that one could run an identical argument for any true but philosophically obscure thesis—Kantian deontology, compatibilism, even the law of non-contradiction—thereby implying that God is either ignorant or malicious whenever correct views remain marginal. Where does this chain go wrong? If I must preserve one claim, I should like to keep (1) together with (8), but I want to know exactly which step is weakest, what standard Molinist resources—such as the feasible-world distinction, transworld depravity, or the denial that belief-formation is libertarian—would have to pay to reject it, and whether paying that price collapses the view into occasionalism or determinism.
