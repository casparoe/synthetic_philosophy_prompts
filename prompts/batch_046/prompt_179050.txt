At a philosophy of mind reading group last week, two colleagues—Maya and Jonah—got into a sustained disagreement about Daniel Dennett’s critique of the Cartesian theater and his alternative multiple drafts model of consciousness, and I found myself unable to referee. Maya opened by arguing that Dennett’s central achievement is dissolving the Cartesian theater—the idea that there is a single, central point in the brain or mind where conscious experience is presented to a homuncular observer. On her reading, the multiple drafts model replaces this picture with parallel, distributed processes that generate various representations or “drafts,” none of which is canonically the conscious one; consciousness is just the availability of these drafts for report and behavioral control, with no final “showing” to an inner self and no need for a single narrative thread to be completed.

Jonah countered that this relocates rather than destroys the theater. If genuinely competing drafts are being generated across different neural streams, he said, then some mechanism—whether global workspace broadcasting, attentional selection, precision weighting, or narrative integration—must determine which drafts influence behavior and verbal report. Functionally, that selection mechanism is doing exactly what the Cartesian audience was supposed to do: it picks which representations count as “the” experience. He added that Dennett’s own talk of “editorial processes” smuggles in a director, and that without some principle of selection the model collapses into an unmanageable cacophony where no draft has any privileged status.

Maya replied that Jonah is conflating distributed competition with centralized selection. In split-brain and blindsight cases, she noted, different hemispheres or modules can generate conflicting drafts without any central arbiter resolving them, which is precisely what the model predicts. The “selection” is evolutionary and local—drafts compete for control of speech and action—rather than theatrical. There is no stage, only a population of representations.

Jonah then pressed a more specific objection about phenomenal binding. He said that if one draft carries color information and another carries shape information, and they are processed in separate streams with no central presentation point, then the unified experience of, say, a red circle requires either a synthesis stage—which is just the Cartesian theater relocated—or we must treat phenomenal unity as a post-hoc narrative construction with no underlying coherence. He claimed that defenders of the drafts model implicitly rely on a “virtual audience”—a functional stand-in for the observer—to explain why subjects report unified experiences rather than fragmented ones. Without that stand-in, he argued, the model cannot distinguish between genuinely unified consciousness and a mere illusion of unity constructed after the fact.

Maya answered that Jonah’s challenge assumes reportability requires a witness, which is exactly the Cartesian assumption Dennett rejects. On her view, temporal synchronization, predictive coherence, and narrative framing can explain why reports come out unified without any virtual observer doing the framing. The “editorial” language is metaphorical, describing how later drafts revise earlier ones in light of context and query, not how a director approves a final script for presentation.

Please go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate who is right about which specific point. In particular: is Jonah correct that selection mechanisms in the drafts model are functionally equivalent to a Cartesian audience? Is Maya right that distributed competition avoids central arbitration? And on the binding issue, does Jonah’s “virtual audience” charge successfully show that the drafts model reintroduces the theater under another name, or does Maya’s appeal to temporal synchronization and narrative framing answer it without presupposing a central observer? In your response, please clearly separate what is relatively settled in contemporary philosophy of mind and cognitive science from what remains genuinely contested among researchers working on consciousness, and do not present any one contested interpretation—whether Dennett’s, Jonah’s, or Maya’s—as if it were the consensus view.
