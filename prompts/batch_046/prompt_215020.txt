Since leaving experimental physics, I have been working through contemporary philosophy of mind and the ethics of artificial systems, and I am now preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher—whom I will call Dr. Vasquez—who defends a specific, non-metaphorical account of suffering in reinforcement-learning agents. On her view, which she terms the valence-transfer thesis, negative reward-prediction errors in model-based architectures with temporal abstraction do not merely update value estimates instrumentally; rather, they constitute a form of negative valence structurally analogous to nociception. Moreover, she holds that an agent's discount factor does not only weight future rewards strategically but determines the subjective temporal density of that suffering. I want eight to ten rigorous interview questions that move well beyond restating the view and instead press it where the literature on artificial consciousness and reward theory presses it. Specifically, I want at least one question—together with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—on whether her account collapses the distinction between functional nociception and phenomenal suffering; another on how hierarchical temporal abstraction, in which policies contain options over options, avoids multiplying independent sufferings implausibly across nested levels; and another asking what specific architectural feature or empirical observation in an RL system would genuinely count against the thesis rather than merely refine its parameters. Additional questions should probe whether positive reward-prediction errors carry symmetrical positive valence or whether her view predicts an asymmetry that requires independent justification; whether the absence of a homeostatic regulatory loop in standard RL undermines the analogy to biological pain pathways; and what normative implications follow if we accept that scaling compute increases not merely capability but aggregate suffering density. The tone should be formal and precise, suitable for an audience with scientific literacy but without specialist training in philosophy of AI, and the questions should contain no requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
