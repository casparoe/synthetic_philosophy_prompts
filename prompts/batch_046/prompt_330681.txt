As a novelist constructing nested narratives, I keep returning to Hofstadter's taxonomy in Gödel, Escher, Bach—the division of any message-bearing structure into a frame message (the context that instructs us how to read what follows), an outer message (the explicit content delivered within that frame), and an inner message (the implicit interpretation that must be reconstructed by the receiver). The conceptual problem I need you to address concerns what happens when the frame itself is fictionalized and self-undermining: imagine a novel that opens with a "found manuscript" introduction written by a character who insists the manuscript was discovered by pure accident and that he, the introducer, exercises no editorial control—yet the typography, placement, and narrative voice clearly mark it as an authored framing device. In such a "phantom frame," the frame message appears to deny its own framing authority.

Please explain, taking a definite and committed position rather than laying out options neutrally, whether an inner message generated within this phantom frame can ever break out to achieve genuine referential contact with reality outside the fiction, or whether the self-undermining denial is always absorbed as a second-order frame message that merely deepens the recursion. Give your reasons, defend the view you adopt against the opposing one, and show specifically how Hofstadter's three-layer model either supports your answer or collapses under this pressure. I want your judgment on which feature of the case does the decisive work, and I do not want both outcomes treated as equally plausible.
