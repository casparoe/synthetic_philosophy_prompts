I am trying to specify the full set of desiderata that a science of consciousness must meet if it adopts heterophenomenological principles—treating first-person reports as data about a subject's heterophenomenological world rather than as direct access to intrinsic phenomenal properties—without collapsing into eliminativism or covert behaviorism. Give me the requirements list: the phenomena the framework must accommodate rather than dismiss (including reportability gaps, confabulatory narratives, split-brain dissociations, and the specific challenge of artificial systems that generate coherent first-person-style reports in the absence of biological neural substrates); the distinctions it must preserve (especially between being disposed to report a state and actually instantiating it, and between first-person authority over reports and third-person accessibility to the underlying mechanisms); the empirical results from cognitive neuroscience and psychology with which it must remain consistent (such as blindsight, global workspace dynamics, and the neural correlates of access versus phenomenal consciousness); and the philosophical commitments it must either explicitly embrace or rigorously avoid (for instance, the ontological status of the heterophenomenological world and the neutrality of the method with respect to realism about qualia). For each requirement, provide the motivating case or consideration. Then identify which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable for the method and which reflect only preferences of Dennett's particular camp. I particularly want an assessment of whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable—whether, for example, preserving first-person authority while denying direct introspective access forces an instability that makes the method collapse either into an implicit realism about phenomenal properties or into a form of eliminativism it officially rejects. Please separate what is settled in the philosophy of mind and consciousness science from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation of heterophenomenology as if it were the consensus view.
