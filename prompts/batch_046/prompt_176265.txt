As the doctoral student representative on the advisory board of our university’s Museum of Science and Public Understanding, I need to circulate a written opinion before our vote at month’s end. The board must decide on a proposal for a six-month temporary exhibition, “Demarcation in Practice,” that juxtaposes mainstream archaeological scholarship with three pseudohistorical frameworks: the Atlantis-as-Thera/Minoan-collapse hypothesis; the ancient-astronaut account of megalithic construction; and Fomenko’s New Chronology, which applies statistical analysis of astronomical records and textual parallels to argue that much documented “ancient” history is a compressed medieval fabrication. The curators’ distinctive proposal—why it reached us rather than being approved administratively—is an interactive station where visitors apply Fomenko’s own statistical methods to datasets of texts and astronomical observations, generating alternative chronologies in real time. Proponents argue that performing the reasoning teaches demarcation more effectively than passive display; opponents argue that giving institutional space, technology, and pedagogical legitimacy to a method that systematically erases documented history crosses from studying pseudohistory into platforming it. Please draft the memo I will circulate: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with concrete conditions and an explicit time frame, include reasoning the board can act on, and treat seriously the strongest consideration on the side you reject. The recommendation should be unambiguous enough that the board can vote directly on it.
