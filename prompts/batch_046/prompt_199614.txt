Write the desiderata that any adequate metaphysical theory of cross-world object persistence—whether through literal transworld identity or counterpart relations—must satisfy to count as complete. Present your answer as a specification list: for each requirement, state the concrete case or conceptual pressure that motivates it, flag whether it is non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference, and note which distinctions the theory must preserve rather than collapse. The list should cover at least: (a) how to treat objects whose parts differ across worlds without being forced into a four-dimensionalist ontology of temporal or modal parts; (b) the compatibility of modal intuitions about personal identity—e.g., that one could have had different parents—with an anti-essentialist stance on biological origins; (c) the identity conditions for artifacts and social kinds whose persistence depends on external relations and institutional facts rather than intrinsic structure; (d) the problem of one-to-many counterpart relations for entities that undergo fission, with particular attention to collective entities such as states or organizations that split in some worlds but remain unified in others; and (e) whether the theory can maintain a distinction between genuinely metaphysically possible worlds and merely epistemically accessible scenarios without importing a privileged accessibility relation. Then, focusing on the subset in (b), (c), and (d), argue whether all three are jointly satisfiable without either abandoning the essential/accidental property distinction or committing to an ontology of modal parts, and identify the structural tension if there is one. Finally, indicate which single desideratum does the most damage to both mainstream essentialist transworld-identity views and unrestricted counterpart theories, and explain exactly why it is fatal to each.
