I was sitting in on a long-running philosophy discussion circle—the kind that meets in university common rooms and spends too long on whether analytic frameworks can even map onto non-Western traditions—when a disagreement broke out between two regulars about whether Buddhist ethics is best reconstructed as a form of long-run consequentialism. The first speaker, Mara, argued that the Four Noble Truths frame dukkha as the fundamental problem and its cessation as the terminal good, which makes the tradition structurally consequentialist: the precepts against killing, stealing, and lying are just high-reliability heuristics for reducing aggregate suffering across rebirths, and the emphasis on cetana (volitional intention) is a practical proxy rather than an intrinsic moral criterion. A skillful intention correlates with welfare-promoting outcomes in a karmic system where mental states determine future conditions, but if you could reliably reduce suffering with an unwholesome mental state, the Buddhist framework should in principle endorse it.

Nikhil pushed back that this is a projection of Western normative ethics onto a soteriological practice. In Abhidharma and Theravada commentarial traditions, he said, the moral quality of an action is fixed at the moment of volition: a bodhisattva who acts with pure compassion but accidentally causes harm performs a kusala action, while someone who saves lives out of hatred or pride performs an akusala action. The goal is not aggregate welfare but the purification of the agent’s mind—greed, hatred, and delusion are to be uprooted because they bind the actor to samsara, not because they fail a utilitarian calculus. Nikhil then raised the Mahayana upaya cases—masters who lie, steal, or even kill out of compassion—which Mara took as proof of consequentialism: the rules are broken precisely when the welfare gain is overwhelming. Nikhil countered that in these narratives the master never weighs outcomes; they act spontaneously from sunyata and non-dual compassion, and the rightness of the act consists in expressing that purified mental state, not in producing the best state of affairs. Mara replied that without some outcome-check this collapses into unfalsifiability: any harmful act can be retroactively justified as pure intention, which makes Buddhist ethics useless as an external decision procedure. Nikhil responded that it was never meant to be an observer-facing decision procedure—it is a first-person technology of transformation, where the only outcome that ultimately matters is the cessation of the three poisons in the actor.

They turned to me and asked me to referee, but I was genuinely stuck. Can you walk through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific claim—particularly about whether cetana functions as a proxy for welfare or as an intrinsic criterion, whether upaya cases involve implicit weighing or spontaneous expression from emptiness, and whether the absence of an external decision procedure is a feature or a bug? Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing, especially regarding whether karma operates as a causal mechanism linking mental states to future conditions or as a kind of moral ledger that rewards and punishes, and how that distinction bears on the consequentialist reconstruction.
