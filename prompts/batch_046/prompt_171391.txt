At a workshop on scientific communication, Maya and Leo argued about whether a research team had properly asserted that compound X shows no neurotoxicity under long-term exposure. The claim was later confirmed true, but at the time the lead investigator relied only on statistical correlations, having not screened for biochemical confounders; unknown to her, an unmeasured variable happened to cancel the toxic pathway, so her belief was correct largely by epistemic luck.

Maya (1): The assertion was appropriate. The investigator sincerely believed the claim, it was in fact true, and science routinely communicates well-supported hypotheses without requiring certainty. Demanding knowledge—justified true belief free from luck—would paralyze public research and conflate epistemic norms with practical risk-management.

Leo (1): That misses the knowledge norm for assertion. Because she had not ruled out the confounder, she did not know the claim was true; she held only a lucky true belief. Asserting transfers epistemic authority, and allowing assertions on lucky belief erodes the normative boundary between knowing and guessing.

Maya (2): But your standard is impossible in frontier science, where mechanisms are always partly hidden. Replace it with a belief norm—assert only what you hold with justified high credence—and we preserve accountability without demanding omniscience. Her credence was well-supported by the data she possessed.

Leo (2): A credence norm cannot distinguish this case from one where the same statistics arise by pure coincidence. Only the knowledge norm explains why we should blame her: she presented herself as an authority on a matter she had not actually secured. That reality later cooperated does not retroactively justify the risk she imposed on her audience.

Maya (3): You treat knowledge as an all-or-nothing threshold that ignores degrees of justification. She was not guessing; she had strong inductive support. If we blame every assertion that depends on an unknown factor, we blame nearly every scientific claim ever made.

Leo (3): I do not demand omniscience—only that the speaker be in a position to know. Here she was not, because the confounder was precisely the kind of risk a responsible neurotoxicity assessment should have screened for. Her failure was not universal ignorance, but a specific gap that undermined her justification.
