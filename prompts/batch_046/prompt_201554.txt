As a novelist constructing a narrative about institutional and personal self-correction, I have noticed a recurring structural pattern that I cannot name precisely. Consider an archivist who drafts a protocol directing her future self to revise any judgment she now makes; she can only adopt the protocol if she already trusts her present reasoning, yet that trust is precisely what the protocol was meant to secure from the outside. Or consider a governing charter that empowers a future board to amend the charter itself: ratifying the charter requires confidence in the drafters' judgment, but the charter's authority was designed to ground that confidence. In each case, an agent attempts to bootstrap its own reliability by delegating authority inward—to a future version of itself—only to find that the delegation presupposes the very reliability it seeks to establish. The circularity feels more specific than ordinary bootstrapping, more structural than reflective equilibrium, and sharper than a simple infinite regress: it is a failure mode of self-trust in which the mechanism of correction and the authority being corrected are the same reasoning process observed at different times. Is there an established concept in philosophy—particularly in work on self-trusting agents, modal reflection, or formal epistemology—that captures this exact configuration?
