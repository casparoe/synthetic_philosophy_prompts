I was arguing with a friend who does medieval history about presentism, and I think I've landed on a position that's a bit weirder than the usual 'check your bias' advice. The claim is that presentism isn't mostly about moral judgement—it's structural. Even neutral-seeming historians organize the past with present-tense causal grammar and present-day taxonomies (calling something a disability or market failure in a 14th century context) that work like an invisible grid. So I think historians can escape this, but only through what I'm calling foreign-language historiography: you write about the past using only the conceptual vocabulary and grammatical tense available to the actors themselves, refusing to translate into modern terms even when it makes the account harder for contempory readers to parse. You don't flag bias; you structurally block present categories from entering the frame.

What would actually count against this? I don't want the standard objections about empathy being impossible. I want the specific cases or methodological results that show this approach either collapses back into presentism or produces history that's unintelligble and useless. For example, if cognitive studies show readers literally can't follow causal explanation without present-indexed language, is that fatal or just impractical? Or if archives reveal that past actors themselves used hybrid, anachronistic categories—so there's no pure past vocabulary to recover—would that kill the position, or could it absorb that by using the least anachronistic layer available? I also want to know what defenders of this structural-escape view would themselves concede is a real problem versus what they'd dismiss as misunderstanding the method. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether this position is genuinely exposed or just well-insulated behind qualifications.
