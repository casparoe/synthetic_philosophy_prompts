I have recently begun working with an archive of regional identity documents, and in discussions with colleagues the compressed claim that all traditions are invented keeps surfacing—usually deployed as a conversation-stopper when someone asserts that a particular ethnic practice is authentically ancient. The phrase is clearly a distillation of the Hobsbawm-Anderson literature on invented traditions and imagined communities, but in our meetings it performs wildly different labor: sometimes it serves as a banal reminder that cultural practices evolve; sometimes as a strong thesis that there is no meaningful distinction between genuine and fabricated heritage; and sometimes as an almost metaphysical assertion that collective identity is purely fictional and therefore illegitimate as a basis for political claims. Could you separate out the precise theses this one sentence could be expressing—indicating which are platitudinous, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which is actually being invoked when a speaker uses it to shut down a claim about historical continuity? In particular, I am trying to determine whether invented here means genealogically false (the practice lacks the claimed antiquity), ontologically constructed (all social realities are brought into being through collective belief and repetition), or something more specific about ritualized norm-inculcation; and whether imagined entails fictionality, mental representation, or merely the absence of face-to-face interaction among members. Please distinguish what is broadly settled in the relevant scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among historians and philosophers of social ontology, and do not present a disputed interpretation—say, that nations are therefore arbitrary, or that tradition carries no normative weight—as if it were the consensus view.
