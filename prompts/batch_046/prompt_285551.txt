You are a professional philosopher of law and an experienced seminar facilitator. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm how a classroom simulation could make the structural tension between retributive finality and institutional fallibilism viscerally apparent without relying on lecture. Then design a runnable 90-minute activity for 16 graduate students in moral philosophy.

The setting: a simulated capital-sentencing commission with rotating roles—three "sentencers" who must assign either execution or life imprisonment to three anonymized case files; one "review auditor" who can overturn prison sentences but not executions after hidden "truth cards" (revealing actual innocence or guilt) are shown post-decision; and the remaining students as "framework advocates" assigned either a retributive or deterrence rationale they must defend consistently. Each case file contains ambiguous evidence; the hidden cards establish that one executed defendant was actually innocent, one imprisoned defendant was guilty but wrongfully acquitted in the hidden truth, and one is correctly assigned.

The rules must be arranged so participants discover for themselves that the irreversibility of execution interacts differently with the two rationales: retributivists experience that a settled moral judgment about desert cannot be unsettled, while deterrence advocates discover that an instrument designed to prevent harm has produced an un-correctable harm. Use small stakes—public consistency ratings assigned by peers—to motivate genuine commitment to assigned frameworks rather than personal intuition.

In your response, provide: (1) the timeline and role instructions; (2) the debrief questions that map each phase onto the philosophical problem, specifically the asymmetry between error-correctable and error-irreversible punishment; (3) an honest prediction of what will go wrong, such as participants treating the revealed innocence as an emotional shock rather than a logical feature of the institution; and (4) a frank note on which aspect of real-world capital punishment the simulation cannot reproduce—likely the phenomenological weight of state-sanctioned killing and the temporal scale of appeals—and that the facilitator must supply this explicitly.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke specific statistical studies or named legal cases that require verification. Focus on the conceptual structure: retribution, deterrence, and the risk of executing the innocent.
