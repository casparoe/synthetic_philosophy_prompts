In John Taurek's essay Should the Numbers Count?, he contends that in a forced-choice rescue scenario—saving one stranded climber on one peak versus saving five stranded climbers on another—an impartial agent lacks any reason to prefer the larger group and ought instead to decide by a fair coin flip. First, reconstruct Taurek's argument by explaining why he regards harm as non-aggregable across distinct persons, and how this commitment leads him to deny that the five possess a collectively stronger claim than the one. Second, evaluate the objection that an ex-ante procedural rule—assigning each potential victim an equal lottery ticket, so that the group of five holds five collective entries and the lone climber holds one—already respects equal moral worth without requiring moment-by-moment indifference. Taurek's central thesis is that in rescue situations we should not allow the numbers to determine our choice. He maintains that harm is essentially personal and non-transferable: the death of each member of the group of five is a separate, complete tragedy, but these five tragedies cannot be summed into a single greater harm that competes with the death of the lone climber. Because there is no aggregate victim whose suffering outweighs the individual's, the five do not present a stronger collective claim. An impartial agent should therefore flip a fair coin between rescuing the one and rescuing the five, treating the options as morally equivalent.

This line of reasoning depends on a strict separation of persons that mirrors Rawls's rejection of interpersonal utility comparisons in the design of basic institutions. Just as Rawls denies that we may sacrifice one person's liberties for the greater social welfare, Taurek denies that we may sacrifice one climber for the greater number saved. The parallel is close: both thinkers treat aggregation across individuals as a category mistake.

A prominent objection, developed by Frances Kamm, proposes an ex-ante weighted lottery in which every individual receives one ticket, giving the five climbers five entries and the lone climber one. This respects equality of moral worth because each person's chance is determined by the same rule. However, Kamm argues that this procedure collapses into rule-utilitarianism: over repeated applications, the rule saves more lives on average, which is precisely the aggregative reasoning Taurek seeks to avoid. Consequently, the lottery fails to preserve his non-aggregative intuition, and the fair coin flip remains the only consistent position. One might reply that the ex-ante evaluation concerns fairness to each person in the particular case rather than long-run statistics, but even on that reading the five still possess five times the collective probability of rescue, which violates the equal standing Taurek demands.
