I'm a retired biologist who got into philosophy late, so bear with me. I've been chewing on the procreation asymmetry — strong reason not to make a suffering kid, no matching reason to make a happy one — and I think I've stretched it until it breaks.

(1) The asymmetry works because there's no victim in non-creation: a happy person who never exists isn't harmed by missing out.
(2) But this same logic applies via the non-identity problem: any climate or tech policy changes exactly which people exist centuries from now.
(3) So if I push for big carbon cuts, I determine one set of future people lives (some suffering, some happy); if I do nothing, a totally different set exists.
(4) The asymmetry then says: among those who suffer under 'do nothing,' those are real victims we should avoid creating. Among the happy people we miss by cutting carbon, there's no victim — they never exist to be deprived.
(5) So we should always pick the policy that minimizes future population, even near-extinction, since that wipes out all suffering-risk with zero victim-cost on the happiness side.
(6) That feels like a reductio, but I can't spot the crack. Is the mistake applying the asymmetry beyond direct reproduction (step 2)? Or treating climate choices as identity-determining (step 3)? Or is the asymmetry itself only defensible for direct procreative choices?
(7) Wherever the error is, tell me what giving it up costs. And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested — don't present one philosopher's view as the obvious consensus.
