Draft one 75-minute essay question for the final exam of an upper-level philosophy seminar on rational choice and decision theory. The question should address the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery, but it must be unanswerable by mere summary: present a modified variant in which Platonia agents share common knowledge of rationality yet differ in reasoning-depth transparency—each knows that others may halt recursive reasoning at different levels—while the corresponding Luring Lottery variant awards the prize only when exactly one agent deviates from the symmetric convergence point. Ask students to reconstruct the symmetry argument and identify precisely where outcome-independence is challenged; to explain how the divergence incentive in the lottery alters the rational calculus; to evaluate whether superrational coordination requires an identical reasoning algorithm or only shared structural rationality, including a response to the objection that algorithmic differences should sometimes produce coordination failure; and to separate clearly what is settled about the logical structure of these arguments from what remains genuinely contested among decision theorists, without presenting either the algorithm-dependence or the pure-structural view as consensus. Supply the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks: precise reconstruction of the symmetry argument (5 marks); correct analysis of the divergence incentive (5 marks); evaluation of the algorithm-versus-structure distinction (6 marks); separation of settled and contested claims (2 marks); and avoidance of the misconception that superrationality entails an empirical prediction of coordination failure (2 marks). Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and ensure that the grading criteria test conceptual understanding rather than recall of author names or standard labels.
