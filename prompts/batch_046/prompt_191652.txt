I have been trying to work out whether sentience is really a binary switch or more like a dimmer—so that insects might have a faint trace, crabs a stronger one, and humans the brightest setting, with something like a learning thermostat perhaps registering only the faintest flicker if it predicts discomfort at all. This made me think about the 2022 UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act, which recognized octopuses and crabs as sentient but left insects outside the boundary. I would like to analyze that legislation through the lens of a gradated, rather than all-or-nothing, account of moral status. Specifically: what nervous-system or behavioral criteria actually justify drawing the line where Parliament did; whether including decapods but excluding bees tracks a real difference in degree or merely reflects human intuition; and whether a predictive homeostat that learns to avoid causing thermal discomfort has any minimal claim on this scale. I also want to understand what the case reveals back about the theory: does legislating a sharp boundary undermine the idea of a smooth gradient, or does it show that gradation is descriptive while policy must be binary? Finally, where does the gradient view stay silent—does it prescribe extending protection downward to insects, or upward to artificial systems, or is it only a map without directions?
