I've been puzzled by the asymmetry in how indicative and counterfactual conditionals respond to learning that their antecedents are false. Once I see that the switch wasn't flipped, saying "If the switch is flipped, the light comes on" seems pragmatically inappropriate or withdrawn, yet "If the switch had been flipped, the light would have come on" remains fully evaluable. Some treat this as a mere pragmatic difference in assertion conditions, but I wonder if it reveals a deeper semantic split. If both conditionals are analyzed via selection functions—epistemic for the indicative, similarity-based for the counterfactual—shouldn't the indicative also stay assessable after I learn ¬A, just evaluated over worlds compatible with my updated knowledge where A holds? The fact that it doesn't suggests to me that indicative conditionals aren't world-selection devices at all, but direct expressions of my current credence distribution over actual-world trajectories, with no shared logical form with subjunctives. So is the distinction between them not just a difference in modal base, but a difference in logical kind—credence-expression versus counterfactual dependence?
