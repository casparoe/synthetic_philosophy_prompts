Consider the reference-class problem as it applies to a reasoner calibrating confidence in a formal inference they have just completed. The reasoner belongs to several overlapping reference classes with divergent base rates of error: professional mathematicians working in proof theory (low error rate), human agents relying on unaided working memory (moderate error rate), members of a specific research program that has recently shown elevated false-positive rates due to a shared heuristic bias (high error rate), and—introducing a synthetic, niche condition—“partitioned agents” who have good reason to believe that in some epistemically accessible worlds one or more of their core reasoning modules was replaced by a lower-fidelity functional analog without altering their first-person experience.

Rank the following five proposed resolution principles by how well they determine the correct reference class for this reasoner’s self-prediction. Assign each a tier: S (unambiguously best), A (sound but with notable costs), B (partially adequate), C (misleading), or D (actively worse than an arbitrary choice). For each tier, provide a single sentence of justification. Then state explicitly which single principle you would apply in this specific case, and defend the full ordering without hedging.

The principles are:
1. Maximum Specificity: select the narrowest class for which reliable statistics exist and membership is certain, ignoring broader classes entirely.
2. Causal Invariance: restrict the reference class to membership conditions that are causally stable across the relevant possibility space, excluding contingent local conditions.
3. Indexical Equilibrium: weight all worlds in which the reasoner exists with identical indexical properties equally, aggregating base rates across that equilibrium set.
4. Peer Deference: choose the class corresponding to the expert group you would rationally defer to upon discovering an error, regardless of your actual social or professional position.
5. Partition Integration: split the reference class by cognitive-partition type (intact module vs. analog-substituted module) across epistemic possibilities, apply base rates separately within each partition, and integrate over subjective uncertainty about which partition you actually inhabit.

Take a committed position: do not present a balanced menu of options. Identify whether any of these principles collapses into incoherence when translated into the vocabulary of Bayesian updating over self-locating beliefs, and if so, report that collapse as its critical failure mode. If one principle survives that translation cleanly while the others do not, say so explicitly and let that determine your S-tier placement.
