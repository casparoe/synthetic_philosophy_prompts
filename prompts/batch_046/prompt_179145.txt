Two camps have spent years arguing past each other on whether weakness of will (akrasia) and hyperbolic discounting describe the same underlying failure or categorically different phenomena. Camp One, the all-things-considered judgment theorists, holds that genuine akrasia requires an occurrent, explicit judgment at the moment of action that the chosen option is worst overall, and that hyperbolic discounting tracks time-inconsistent preferences rather than this specific normative failure. Camp Two, the intertemporal structuralists, holds that hyperbolic discounting reveals agents lack any stable unitary judgment across temporal slices, that apparent weakness of will is best explained by present-biased optimization under a non-exponential discount function with a present-bias parameter β < 1, and that the traditional concept of akrasia should be reconstructed in terms of dynamic consistency conditions and multi-period welfare aggregation rather than judgment-violation.

Write the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two camps should produce. Structure it exactly as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: substantive statements both sides can sign without reservation. Cover shared empirical commitments (the observed shape of discount curves, the existence of preference reversals between short and long delays, the phenomenology of resisting temptation, and the efficacy of precommitment devices), shared conceptual distinctions they both accept (even if they weight them differently—e.g., between occurrent judgment and standing preference, between local and global rationality, between descriptive and normative modeling), and shared acknowledgments of limits in current frameworks. Do not include vacuous platitudes; each claim should constrain both sides precisely.

Second, a disagreements table. For each disagreement, provide:
(a) the precise formulation of Camp One's position, phrased in wording Camp One would accept as accurate and not as a strawman;
(b) the precise formulation of Camp Two's position, phrased in wording Camp Two would accept;
(c) a brief neutral gloss identifying whether the disagreement is primarily conceptual, empirical, normative, or terminological, and noting explicitly if any portion is merely verbal.

Third, identify the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would cause at least one camp to shift substantially. There should be at least three. For each crux, specify:
(i) the crux in a single neutral sentence;
(ii) a concrete case, experiment, or class of evidence both camps agree is genuinely probative—do not invent hypothetical data, but reference the kind of evidence both accept (e.g., neuroeconomic correlates of precommitment, cross-cultural variation in discounting parameters, or the structure of all-things-considered judgments in high-stakes versus low-stakes intertemporal choices);
(iii) Camp One's explicit prediction about what that evidence shows or will show;
(iv) Camp Two's explicit prediction about the same evidence.

Keep all formulations technically precise—use terms such as present-bias parameter, dynamic consistency, occurrent judgment, intertemporal preference reversal, and multi-self welfare aggregation where appropriate—but do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not depend on the identification of specific obscure texts. The document should read as a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with residual disagreements made exact rather than dramatized, and with any purely verbal disputes separated from genuine substantive ones.
