I have been thinking on my own about whether culture evolves in something like a biological sense—through units people call memes that replicate, vary, and face selection—rather than just spreading however people intend. In discussions and in books I have found, there is a long-running deadlock: one side treats viral slang, fashion cycles, and online challenges as evidence of blind variation and environmental selection, while the other side insists that human beings deliberately redesign, teach, and market these things, so calling the process Darwinian is only a loose metaphor. Both camps cite the same examples, yet after years of argument neither has given ground.

What puzzles me is whether they are even talking about the same phenomenon. I have noticed a distinction that no one I read addresses clearly: inside small, closed groups—like a private forum or a local friend circle—a phrase or image seems to mutate rapidly, drift, and adapt to the group’s norms almost accidentally; but at large scale, successful ideas often look engineered for replication, like advertising jingles or branded challenges designed to be catchy. Could it be that one camp is describing micro-level drift and the other is describing macro-level design, and that both are being called “memetic evolution” without anyone separating them? Or is the real disagreement about whether Darwinian evolution requires variation that is genuinely random with respect to fitness, and whether intentional human editing breaks that condition?

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. Is this stall mainly verbal—different definitions of “meme” or “selection” hidden under one word? Is there a concealed empirical question about whether cultural transmission actually has high enough fidelity and independence from intention to count? Or are the sides anchored to different intuitions about whether explaining culture requires reducing it to mechanisms that ignore human agency? Please separate what is broadly settled about cultural transmission and social learning from what remains genuinely contested among people who study the topic, and do not present a disputed interpretation as if it were the consensus. If you think the impasse could be moved—by a conceptual distinction, a measurement of transmission fidelity, or a reframing of the question—say what that would be; if you think it is likely permanent, explain why.
