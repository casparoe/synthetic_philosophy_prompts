I'm a retired biologist who got sucked into philosophy of mind lately, and two people in my study group got stuck in an argment over coffee last week. One said an approval-directed agent — like something trained with RLHF — is really just goal-directed, except its utility function happens to be defined over some human evalutor's mental state instead of over world-states. So the distinction people make is basically taxonomy theater, he said. The other pushed back: no, you're missing the structural point. Approval-direction is parasitc on an external judge that can be gamed, removed, or fooled, whereas a true goal-directed agent carries its objective internally and keeps optimizing regardless of who's watching. First guy came back that evolutionary fitness works the same way — social animals seek approval, but we don't call them non-goal-directed just because the reward comes from other minds. Second said fitness isn't a mental evaluation; it's an objective filter in the enviroment, so the analogy breaks down. Then they argued about whether RLHF actually builds an internal representation of "being approved" or just correlates outputs with reward signals, and whether that mechanistic difference matters for classifying the system. First claimed if there's no internal representation it's even less like agency; second said that proves approval-direction is mechanistically different, not merely a flavor of goal-direction.

Who's right about which point? Is approval-direction just goal-direction with a weird utility domain, or is there a real structural divide that survives? And what are they both missing about how actual learning archtectures blur the line?
