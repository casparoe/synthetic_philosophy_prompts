At a philosophy study group I attend, two regulars got into a sustained argument over whether Kant's universalizability tests can be applied coherently to maxims that include an explicit probabilistic escape clause. They anchored the dispute in a concrete case: "Whenever I judge that my individual contribution to a collective good is below the threshold of pivotality—say, with probability less than epsilon—I will withhold it, while still enjoying the full benefit." (1) One argued that universalizing this collapses immediately into a contradiction in conception: if every agent applies the same epsilon-filter, the collective good is never produced, so the maxim's background conditions are destroyed by its own universal form; the maxim literally cannot be thought as a law of nature without undermining the very benefit it seeks to exploit. (2) The other said this mistakes an empirical population-dynamics failure for a logical contradiction: universalization asks what happens if literally everyone adopts the maxim, and indeed the good collapses, but that is not a contradiction in conception—merely an undesirable state of affairs, which belongs to the contradiction-in-will test. He added that if every collective-action failure is labeled a contradiction in conception, Kant's two tests merge and the distinction loses all work. (3) The first replied that the epsilon-clause makes the maxim self-referentially unstable in conception specifically because "pivotality" is defined relative to others' behavior; universalizing it does not merely change outcomes, it changes the truth-conditions of the maxim's own trigger, so the maxim cannot retain its form across universal adoption. That is a formal, not empirical, failure. (4) The second countered that this proves too much: any maxim referencing social practices—promising, contracting—would then fail in conception once universalized, leaving the will-test with no domain, and that the correct treatment is to hold the epsilon-condition fixed in the maxim's form rather than treating it as a variable that shifts with adoption rates.

Please adjudicate round by round: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a principled way to separate logical-formal failure from strategic-interdependence failure in threshold-sensitive maxims—and note whether either has implicitly smuggled in a decision-theoretic framing that Kant's tests are meant to avoid.
