Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the normative status of work and leisure.

Camp A, the “stewardship-of-time” position, updates the Protestant work ethic in conceptual terms: it holds that labor is a form of covenantal self-formation, that leisure is legitimate only as recuperative or celebratory rest earned by prior productive contribution, and that phenomena like karoshi—death from overwork—are tragedies of individual boundary failure and cultural overvaluation rather than structural injustices that warrant rights-based correction. They regard the 35-hour week not as a moral baseline but as a historically contingent compromise that risks eroding the dignity of sustained vocational commitment.

Camp B, the “sovereignty-of-leisure” position, holds that non-work time is a pre-political entitlement rather than a reward, that the Protestant work ethic functions as an ideological mechanism to naturalize surplus extraction, and that karoshi reveals a systemic violation of bodily and temporal sovereignty that cannot be remedied by individual moderation. They treat the 35-hour week as an inadequate floor and argue that any ethic linking human dignity to productivity is conceptually incompatible with recognizing leisure as an independent right.

Your deliverable is a constructive document both camps could sign. Structure it as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. Cover: the empirical realities of modern labor (the documented prevalence and physiological mechanisms of karoshi-related mortality; the historical origins of the 35-hour standard as a labor-market compromise; the psychological and social functions of work in identity formation); what both sides agree needs explaining (why overwork is valorized in some cultures and pathologized in others; why the same labor practices are framed as virtue or as exploitation); and formal economic descriptions or institutional facts that are not in dispute.

Second, a disagreements table. For each row, present Camp A’s precise formulation in wording Camp A accepts, and Camp B’s precise formulation in wording Camp B accepts. Explicitly flag any part of the dispute that is verbal rather than substantive—if ambiguous terms such as “right,” “dignity,” “exploitation,” or “earned” drive apparent disagreement—identify the ambiguity, explain why it is verbal, and propose disambiguated replacements that both sides accept for their own positions.

Third, the cruxes: sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. Include at least:
- Whether leisure can be conceptually independent of prior labor contribution (the “unearned leisure” problem).
- Whether karoshi is best explained by individual-level failure of moderation or by structural incentives that make moderation individually irrational.
- Whether recognizing a “right to leisure” necessarily undermines the moral value of vocational commitment, or whether the two values can be decoupled.

For each crux, provide:
a. The crux stated in neutral language.
b. The specific argument, observation, hypothetical case, or empirical pattern both camps agree is relevant.
c. Camp A’s prediction of what the outcome or implication should be if their view is correct.
d. Camp B’s prediction of what the outcome or implication should be if their view is correct.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not ask the camps to cite specific scholars; frame every disagreement in conceptual terms each side could defend without appeal to authority. Where historical or empirical claims appear (e.g., the 1930s origins of the 35-hour movement, Japanese regulatory responses to karoshi), rely on general knowledge rather than specific archival sources, and do not require verification of page numbers or primary texts.

Conclude by assessing whether any remaining disagreement is merely verbal, specifying the term involved and the disambiguation both sides accept, and state what—if anything—both camps agree would actually move the debate.
