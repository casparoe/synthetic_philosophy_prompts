I've been trying to understand what kind of political idea allows a modern democracy to keep a hereditary monarch who formally holds all executive power—appointing ministers, giving assent to legislation, and serving as commander-in-chief—while also requiring that this same person remain strictly neutral and never exercise independent political judgment. In the United Kingdom, Sweden, and Japan, the monarch is said to embody the state itself: criminal proceedings are brought in the Crown's name, and the armed forces pledge loyalty to the sovereign personally, not merely to a constitution or to the people. Yet the actual government is elected and accountable to parliament. What philosophical commitments about authority, representation, and the source of legitimate power does this split require? Does it presuppose that sovereignty can be divided between a symbolic body and a functional one, or that democratic consent applies only to the exercise of power rather than to its formal possession? And if the justification is genuinely symbolic, why must the symbol be a specific, unelected individual rather than an office filled by election or rotation?
