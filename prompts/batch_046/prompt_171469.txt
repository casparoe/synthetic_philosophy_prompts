I sit on the heritage board of a small port city that oversees a maritime museum founded in 1923 with capital from a shipping firm that profited from transporting enslaved people across the Atlantic. The museum's governing charter was revised in 1985 to include what lawyers call a "continuity clause": the institution is deemed the legal and moral successor to its founding entities, retaining their obligations as well as their assets. A citizens' group now proposes that any public funding for the museum's expansion be conditional on the board issuing a formal statement of "inherited culpability" and committing five per cent of future revenue to a reparative education programme. Opponents on the board argue that this mistakes institutional continuity for personal guilt: the current trustees, staff, and local taxpayers neither committed nor benefited from the original crimes, and to speak of the museum or the city as guilty is to apply a moral predicate that only makes sense for individual agents. Others reply that refusing the statement treats moral obligations as if they expire with the death of the wrongdoers, which would let every institution outlive its debts. I am trying to decide whether to vote for the conditional funding package, but I want to understand whether there is a philosophically coherent account on which a community or institution can carry culpability across generations in a way that genuinely obligates present members, or whether the opponents are right that this is a category mistake. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
