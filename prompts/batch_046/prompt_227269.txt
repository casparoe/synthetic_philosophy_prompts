hey so I'm putting together a little reading group for my philosphy of religion course and we got onto Life of Pi last week — the novel and the Ang Lee film where Pi Patel survives the shipwreck and offers two versions of what happened, one with the tiger Richard Parker and one without any animals at all. I want to read it through William James's idea about the will to believe and Clifford's ethics of belief, but not just as Pi picks the prettier version so James wins. That feels too easy and probably misses the point.

The thing that actually puzzles me is whether Pi's choice counts as belief in the Clifford sense. Clifford is worried about beliefs formed on insufficient evidence that then guide action and become habits of mind, but Pi seems closer to adopting a narrative framework — like he's choosing which ontology to live inside, not just asserting a single fact. So does James's defense apply only to live, forced, and momentous religious options, or can it extend to this kind of narrative commitment where the evidence is literally identical for both stories? And where does the work resist the reading? The Japanese insurers accept both accounts but prefer the animal one, which makes it feel like aesthetic preference rather than doxastic courage. I want a reading that takes both the novel and the James-Clifford debate seriously — maybe point to the scene with the insurers or Pi's conversation with the writer — and says whether the book dramatizes something the philosophers miss, like belief being less a single propositional attitude and more a sustained practice of interpretation. Don't reduce the book to an illustration, and don't force James into a box he wouldn't recognize. If you think the work actually undermines James — maybe by showing that choosing the better story is just escapism — say so. What would you tell my group to look for?
