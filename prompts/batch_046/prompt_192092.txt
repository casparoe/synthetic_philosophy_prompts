I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or jurisprudence, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an internal two-day workshop titled 'Self-Modifying Systems: From Code to Constitution.' The audience is deliberately mixed: approximately half are legal scholars, philosophers of law, and policy researchers; the other half are software engineers, site-reliability staff, and product managers with no background in either logic or constitutional theory. I want to frame the workshop around Peter Suber's concept of Nomic — the game in which rule-change is itself governed by rules, and in which the rules include provisions for amending themselves — but I want the idea to do genuine structural work rather than serve as an opening anecdote.

To that end, I want to introduce a concrete, hypothetical framing device drawn from my own workplace: our engineering 'Deployment Constitution,' a living handbook that contains Article 7. Article 7 states that any engineer on the active on-call rotation may propose an amendment to the handbook, including to Article 7 itself, if the proposal secures a two-thirds vote of that same rotation. Crucially, Article 7 also defines what counts as an active rotation, what counts as a valid vote, and what quorum is required; there is no external authority that validates amendments to Article 7. It governs its own conditions of revision. I want to use this scenario to pose the workshop's central, unresolved question: can a system contain the complete authority for its own revision without either freezing into rigidity or dissolving into arbitrary self-modification?

Please write a polished opening address of approximately five minutes that:
- Speaks respectfully to the experts without alienating the non-specialists;
- Uses both Suber's Nomic and the Deployment Constitution as the architectural backbone of the remarks, so that what I choose to open with, what I deliberately leave unanswered, and the tension I ask the room to hold for the next two days all turn on the problem of legal and procedural self-reference;
- Makes clear that the Deployment Constitution crisis is hypothetical — our actual handbook has never faced this breakdown — so that I am not inventing corporate drama;
- Contains no bibliographies, reading lists, or requested citations;
- Includes, either in the closing or in a brief appended speaker's note, an explicit account of where my reasoning is most vulnerable: specifically, identify the weakest point in the analogy between our deployment meta-rules and nomic self-reference, and state precisely what argument or observation would cause me to abandon the comparison altogether.

The tone should be formal, reflective, and appropriate for a professional workshop opening — closer to a carefully composed address than to a lecture. I want the philosophical paradox to shape the speech's boundaries, not merely decorate it.
