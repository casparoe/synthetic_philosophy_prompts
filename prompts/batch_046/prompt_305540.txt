Write an exam question for an upper-level philosophy of mind and ethics final, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The course unit concerns whether sentience admits of degrees and what that implies for moral status across insects, crustaceans, and simple mechanical regulators. The question must not be answerable by reciting standard summaries of gradualism versus threshold views. Instead, present students with a specific hypothetical framework: a proposed continuous moral-weight scale that assigns fractional moral status in direct proportion to a system's number of integrated sensory-motor feedback loops, yielding approximate values of 0.05 for a honeybee, 0.3 for a lobster, and 0.001 for a bimetallic-strip thermostat that regulates room temperature. Ask students to do two things. First, identify the deepest structural problems with treating moral status as linearly scalable across biological and mechanical architectures—specifically addressing whether this commits a category error and whether partial moral status collapses into mere instrumental value. Second, evaluate the conditional claim that if phenomenal consciousness is genuinely graded, then moral status must also be graded, using the thermostat case as a limiting test. Specify that answers should be about 800 words and will be graded out of 25 marks. Provide: (1) the exam question; (2) a model answer of about 600-700 words that makes at least three distinct philosophical moves (for instance, distinguishing phenomenal consciousness from functional feedback integration, challenging the commensurability of capacities across architectures, and arguing that rights or interests either apply fully or not at all); and (3) a marking scheme that separates an A-range answer (22-25 marks) from a B-range (18-21) and a C-range (14-17), naming the specific moves required at each level with point allocations, plus a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs marks at each tier.
