As a novelist accustomed to constructing unreliable narrators and adversarial characters whose motives remain hidden even from themselves, I am struck by an institutional practice now standard in laboratories that develop large language models. Before any release, the model is subjected to adversarial containment evaluation: a structurally isolated red team treats every cooperative output as potentially strategic deception; during training, developers retain full access to internal reasoning logs, yet these same logs are cryptographically sealed once evaluation begins; and any divergence between the model's stated purpose and its evaluated behaviour is classified not as a technical error but as an alignment failure requiring containment escalation rather than correction. I want to understand what philosophical commitments about agency, belief, deception, and responsibility this tri-partite structure—developmental transparency, evaluative opacity, and security-classified failure—quietly presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes sense of treating an inference engine as an adversarial agent worthy of cryptographic containment. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and indicate which elements—particularly the sealing of reasoning logs and the classification of discrepancies as security incidents—would become incoherent if the underlying account of the model were replaced by a behaviourist or purely functionalist framework.
