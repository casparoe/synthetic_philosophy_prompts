Here is the situation. Over the last several decades, researchers studying three well-known cases—the Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui—have divided into two camps that recycle the same arguments without resolution. The discontinuity camp treats the archaeological record as evidence of genuine societal collapse: a rapid, multi-dimensional breakdown of political centralization, economic integration, and demographic stability that amounts to the end of a particular institutional identity. The continuity camp treats the same record as rapid adaptive reorganization: populations disperse, monumental construction stops, and elite hierarchies dissolve, but social networks, knowledge practices, and local identities persist in transformed rather than destroyed form. Both sides cite settlement-pattern surveys, stratigraphic sequences, and paleoclimate proxies; both agree dramatic change occurred; yet neither has persuaded the other, and the literature now largely restates familiar rebuttals.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily empirical but metaphysical: discontinuity theorists implicitly treat a society as having a kind of teleological integrity—like an organism—so that the loss of its central institutions is analogized to death, while continuity theorists treat societies as distributed networks lacking any essential core and therefore incapable of dying, only of changing topology. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether a hidden formal disagreement about what counts as explanation in historical science is doing the real work.
