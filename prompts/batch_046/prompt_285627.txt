I am working through the secondary literature on Wittgenstein's discussion of private language, and I keep encountering a particular defensive pattern in responses to the criterion objection that I cannot quite map onto the standard taxonomy. Here is what it looks like in practice. A proponent of private ostensive definition is pressed on how she can correct misapplications of her internal term without any public criterion. Rather than denying the need for correction altogether—the route that treats the private object as analogous to a beetle in a box that might as well be empty—or retreating to a fully public criterion, she introduces what I shall call a dual-track calibration model: she claims that the private name is anchored to an internal sample accessed directly, but that she also maintains an occasional, non-systematic cross-check against her own public behaviour—say, her verbal reports or bodily reactions—to catch gross drift. Crucially, when the cross-check yields a mismatch, she does not treat it as evidence that the private application was wrong; instead, she treats the public behaviour as having been misinterpreted or distorted by external factors, while the private name retains its original reference. Once the pressure subsides, she resumes speaking as if the occasional cross-check were sufficient to guarantee normative constraint, even though it plays no actual corrective role in the internal practice.

I have observed this structure in at least two settings: in seminar discussions where interlocutors defend the possibility of a purely mental diary, and in recent commentary that attempts to salvage first-person authority by allowing intermittent public verification without granting it normative priority. The move is not exactly motte-and-bailey, because the strong claim—that no public criterion is ever normatively relevant—and the weak claim—that public behaviour can be consulted as a fallible heuristic—are not arranged as a defensible core and an indefensible extension. They are presented as complementary components of a single picture, yet they function in the argument in an alternating fashion: one is invoked to block the objection, the other to preserve the appearance of accountability. Nor is it plain equivocation, since the speaker does not shift between two meanings of criterion; rather, she assigns incompatible functional roles to the same public behaviour—corrective versus merely evidential—without acknowledging the tension.

Is there an established name or literature for this exact argumentative structure—this intermittent calibration without normative correction? If so, I should like to know how it differs from the standard no-criterion-of-correctness objection and from the beetle-in-the-box analogy, and whether it has been diagnosed as a distinct failure mode in its own right. If there is no established concept, please coin one, with a precise definition and clear boundary conditions: what distinguishes it from mere inconsistency, from a genuinely fallibilist internalism that allows public feedback to revise private content, and from strategic ambiguity about the status of public behaviour. I am not looking for a bare label; I want the carving—what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
