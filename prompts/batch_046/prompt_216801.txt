So I was chatting with a coworker over lunch—he's in bio and I'm mostly in backend—about whether DNA is actually a "code" in the same way we talk about protocols at work, since I deal with encoding schemes all the time. I tried to formalize my intuition and now I've built an argument that feels like it proves way too much, maybe even sneaking intelligent design in through the back door. Here are the steps I wrote out:

(1) In computer science, a real code needs three pieces: symbols, mapping rules, and a decoder. Crucially, the symbol-to-meaning mapping is arbitary—ASCII could have assigned 'A' to 65 or 99 without breaking physics, so the convention is independent of the hardware.

(2) DNA has four symbol-bases, codon rules that map triplets to amino acids, and ribosomes with tRNA that decode it. So it checks all three boxes on paper.

(3) But here's the catch: in CS, if the mapping is physically locked—like voltage levels representing bits—it's not a code, it's just direct causation. The DNA-to-amino-acid mapping is chemically fixed; change a codon and you break the ribosome chemistry, not just the convention. So DNA fails the arbitrariness test and isn't literally a code.

(4) Therefore "genetic information" is just a metaphor, like saying the brain runs software. There's no actual information layer in the genome, only chemicals reacting in fixed ways.

(5) If genetic information is purely metaphorical, evolutionary biology can't literally say mutations alter functional information—there's nothing informational for selection to act on besides random chemical variation.

(6) But natural selection requires heritable differences in functional information, or there's no mechanism to preserve advantageous traits across generations.

(7) So either DNA is a true code with an arbitary mapping—which implies someone or something designed the codon table—or evolutionary biology collapses into pure chemistry with no selection mechanism.

(8) Obviously evolutionary biology works fine and doesn't need a designer, so (8) is false.

Where does this go wrong? I really want to hang on to (3) and (7)—the chemical fixity seems rock solid and evolutionary biology is too well-supported to toss. But if I keep both, I'm stuck rejecting (8), which means I have to drop something upstream. What's the least painful premise to reject, and what do I pay for dropping it?
