In philosophical discussions of Madhyamaka, emptiness (śūnyatā) is frequently compared to the mathematical empty set: phenomena lack intrinsic essence (svabhāva) just as the empty set lacks members, yet both operate within well-defined conventional frameworks. Some interpreters push this further, treating emptiness as a formal structural feature rather than an ontological denial. Audit this analogy: lay out the correspondence table (what plays the role of set membership, the empty set itself, the axioms that ground it, and conventional mathematical use); identify where the mapping breaks, especially regarding dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) and conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya); and apply Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (catuskoti) to the analogy itself—does it yield a coherent result, or does it show that emptiness, unlike the empty set, cannot be pinned to any of the four corners without contradiction? Assess whether the structuralist reading survives these disanalogies, and if not, suggest a more accurate analogy. Separate what is settled in Nāgārjuna's rejection of svabhāva from what remains genuinely contested among Madhyamaka scholars—particularly whether emptiness is a metaphysical claim or a therapeutic practice—and do not present any contested interpretation as consensus.
