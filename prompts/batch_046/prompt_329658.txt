I've constructed a kind of private advance directive—not for medical care, but for intellectual work. Eighteen months ago, during what I retrospectively judge to be my most epistemically clear-headed period, I drafted a binding protocol that specifies exactly which long-running research threads I should terminate and which narrower, more tractable problems I should adopt if I ever met a precise conjunction of conditions: sustained burnout metrics, declining marginal output, and a specific kind of motivational shift away from the original framing. I treated the document as a Ulysses contract against my own anticipated weakness of will and present-bias. Those conditions were satisfied three weeks ago. The protocol unambiguously directs me to abandon my current agenda.

Here is the philosophical tension. When I read the charter now, the reasoning behind it feels foreign—not merely wrong, but authored by someone with a different evaluative framework. The shift does not feel like simple rationalization or fatigue; it resembles, structurally, the kind of value transformation people report after recovering from prolonged depressive episodes, where prior 'healthy' preferences come to seem like artifacts of a distorted state rather than genuine commitments. I am not asking for career advice. I want to know whether there is a defensible account on which my earlier self has authority over my present judgment, or whether treating the charter as binding commits me to a kind of diachronic tyranny. Specifically: if my current resistance is not merely present-bias but a coherent revision of my values, does that undermine the authority of the document? And is there a middle position—perhaps one that distinguishes between instrumental commitments and constitutive ones—that allows me to honor part of the protocol without treating my past self as sovereign?
