For my postgraduate seminar on the ethics of collective memory, I wish to clarify a conceptual tension that sits at the intersection of remembrance and reconciliation. Avishai Margalit frames shared memory as an active, communal practice that binds moral agents across generations, whilst David Rieff defends the normative legitimacy of strategic forgetting in post-conflict societies. The standard framing opposes a duty to remember against a permission to forget. Yet this seems conceptually asymmetrical: remembering can be cultivated, but forgetting is usually a passive omission rather than an intentional action. Please explain whether there is a coherent conceptual distinction between "active forgetting"—a deliberate withdrawal of mnemonic investment or institutional archiving—and "passive forgetting," the natural decay of collective attention. In particular, address whether a "duty to forget" can be a positive normative claim rather than merely the absence of a duty to remember, and examine how this interacts with the idea that certain traumatic memories become structurally embedded in social practices and are therefore "unforgettable" not because they are preserved, but because they are continually reproduced by institutional behaviour. I should like the explanation to be rigorous but accessible, and please provide your answer in British English.
