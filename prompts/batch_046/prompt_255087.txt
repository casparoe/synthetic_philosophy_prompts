You are an expert in moral philosophy and philosophy of action, with particular familiarity with collective-action problems and causal reasoning in ethics. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm the standard literature on the inefficacy objection to individual climate action and meat consumption—especially the tension between marginal causal contribution, expected-utility replies, and signaling—so that your questions can press beyond those familiar moves. Then write eight to ten interview questions for a podcast with an unnamed philosopher who defends a precise variant of the inefficacy objection: she claims that in large-scale collective harms, individual acts are not merely causally weak but are “structurally screened off” by institutional thresholds, making appeals to expected utility or signaling fail because they wrongly treat the agent’s contribution as probabilistically independent rather than redundant within a fixed causal architecture. Your questions should turn on the actual mechanics of this “structural redundancy” claim—how it differs from ordinary causal overdetermination; whether it applies symmetrically to voting in large democracies and to meat consumption versus fossil-fuel use; what empirical conditions would have to hold for an individual action to break through the screening; and whether the view risks collapsing into moral quietism. For each substantive question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—for example, if she insists thresholds are exogenously fixed, ask what happens when they are endogenous to aggregate behavior; if she distinguishes voting from consumption, ask why democratic aggregation does not produce the same redundancy. Do not include generic biographical or “what drew you to philosophy” questions. Conclude with one question asking what conceptual or empirical finding would cause her to abandon the view. Keep the tone rigorous but accessible to an informed non-specialist audience.
