I'm putting together notes for my grad seminar on well-being and I keep walking myself into this corner with tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering actually matters, and everything else (joy, knowledge, love, achievement) is just axiologicaly neutral noise. I think the argument proves way too much, but I can't find the exit. Here's the chain I wrote out:

(1) Tranquilism: only states that lack craving and suffering have positive value; presence of pleasure, etc., adds exactly zero.
(2) So compare world A, where people live rich flourishing lives with art and friendship but some risk of future craving, and world B, where everyone is in a permanent serene blankness—no suffering, no craving, but also no experiences at all, like a conscious void.
(3) By (1), A and B are exactly tied for well-being; the richness in A contributes nothing.
(4) But B is easier to maintain, uses less resources, and eliminates all risk of future disturbance. So rational well-being promotion should preffer B, or at least not object to replacing A with B.
(5) Worse, since flourishing activities carry risk with zero compensating value, tranquilism seems to actively recommend stripping away richness—maybe even moving toward extinction if that removes all risk.
(6) That can't be right; tranquilism was meant to end suffering, not turn existence into featureless blankness. So the argument must fail somewhere.
(7) Therefore some premise is false.

But which one? I really want to keep (1)—there's something compelling about the idea that only the absence of badness counts—but I don't want to bite the bullet on the void. Is the error in (3), where I treat "zero value" as "disposable"? Or is there a hidden quantifier shift between (3) and (4) that lets me slide from "equal well-being" to "should replace"? Or does (5) smuggle in a probabilistic premise tranquilists can reject? I'd rather keep (1) than (6), but I need to know what I'm paying if I reject (4) or (5) instead. Where does the chain actually break?
