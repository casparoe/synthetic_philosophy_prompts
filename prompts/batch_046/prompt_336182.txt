I cover neuroscience and philosophy of mind for a science publication, and I sit on my university’s Clinical Research Ethics Subcommittee for Interoceptive Studies. We meet Thursday to finalize our written opinion on Protocol 447, a proposed multi-site trial recruiting adults with congenital pain asymbolia—individuals who report intact sensory discrimination of tissue damage yet lack the negative affect and withdrawal motivation that typically accompany nociception. The investigators argue that, because these participants do not suffer, standard analgesic safeguards and unconditional withdrawal rights are ethically redundant; they propose replacing them with an informational-consent framework that treats nociceptive signals as neutral interoceptive data rather than harm-events. One faction of the board finds this persuasive, reasoning that if pain’s badness resides in its phenomenal affect—which is absent here—then protective protocols designed for suffering subjects are misapplied. The opposing faction insists that pain’s normative weight derives from its imperative, motivational structure—its command-character—and that stripping safeguards instrumentalizes a neurodivergent population while ignoring the possibility that sensory discrimination without affect still carries intrinsic disvalue. Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday’s vote: it should recommend approval with binding conditions, rejection, or deferral within a concrete timeline; it must treat the strongest philosophical objection to the recommendation with genuine seriousness rather than dismissal; and it should be written so the researchers can respond to it directly, without asking for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific named texts.
