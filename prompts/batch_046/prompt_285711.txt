I have been thinking about the conceptual structure of secession claims, particularly a framing that moves beyond the familiar remedial-right versus primary-right dichotomy. Consider what we might call the “double authorization” problem: for a secessionist movement to establish full democratic legitimacy, it appears to need both (i) a positive democratic mandate from the population of the territory seeking independence, and (ii) a defensible claim that the parent state’s constitutional framework does not already provide adequate democratic representation for that population’s distinct interests—in other words, that the exclusion from sovereignty is not itself democratically justified by the larger polity. This framing is designed to capture a tension often overlooked in standard accounts: the seceding group must claim democratic authority against a state that also claims democratic authority over the same territory, and the interaction between these two claims is rarely examined in detail.

Take the 2014 Scottish independence referendum as a real-world case study. The salient facts are well known: the Scottish National Party secured a parliamentary majority in 2011 on a platform that included an independence referendum; the UK government agreed to the Edinburgh Agreement in 2012, which authorized a legally binding referendum; the vote on 18 September 2014 produced a majority against independence on an unusually high turnout; and the UK government subsequently resisted further referendum demands, particularly after the 2016 EU referendum altered the constitutional context in ways that many Scottish voters rejected. The actors, options, and institutional sequence are part of common knowledge, so the analysis should turn on conceptual mapping rather than historical excavation.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the double-authorization framework described above. First, map the actors and options with precision: who possessed democratic authority to authorize the referendum, what the “Yes” and “No” options represented in terms of democratic self-definition, and how the Edinburgh Agreement altered the usual constitutional dynamics of secession. Second, assess whether the Scottish case satisfies both conditions—did the 2014 vote constitute a clear positive mandate (even though it failed), and did the post-2016 Brexit context change the second condition regarding adequate representation within the UK? Third, identify exactly where the framework explains the episode well and where it breaks down or remains silent—for instance, does it illuminate why the UK government granted the referendum voluntarily, and does it have anything to say about whether a second referendum would be legitimate? Finally, consider whether the Scottish episode supports, undermines, or simply fails to engage the double-authorization view, and compare its explanatory power with that of a standard remedial-right account applied to the same facts.
