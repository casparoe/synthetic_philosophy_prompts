Been digging into Molinism since I retired from the lab, and I think I talked myself into a trap I can't talk myself out of. Here goes.

(1) Middle knowledge means God knows every true counterfactual of freedom: for any person P and situation S, God knows whether P would freely do A or not-A in S.

(2) These counterfactuals are true before creation, and God can't change them—he just uses them to pick which world to actualize.

(3) So in world W, P is in S and freely picks A. God knew: "If P were in S, P would freely pick A."

(4) But libertarian freedom means in S, P genuinely could have picked not-A. So there's a nearby possible world W' that's identical to W right up to the choice, except P picks not-A.

(5) In W', God knows the counterfactual: "If P were in S in W', P would freely pick not-A."

(6) So far okay. But notice: in both W and W', the circumstances S are literally the same. The only difference is P's choice. So God's middle knowledge assigns opposite outcomes to the exact same circumstance S, just indexed to different worlds.

(7) But if the circumstances are identical, then the counterfactual "what P would do in S" ought to be the same across worlds—otherwise it depends on which world we're already in, which makes it circular, not explanatory.

(8) So for any given S, there can't be two worlds with identical S but different choices. Once you fix the prior conditions—the boundry conditions, like we'd say in physics—the free choice is fixed across all worlds.

(9) That means middle knowledge doesn't just record freedom; it globally determines every choice from prior conditions, like a deterministic physical law.

(10) The Molinist wants God to "choose" which world to actualize, but if every choice in every world is already fixed by the counterfactual profile, God isn't selecting among open possibilities—he's just reading off the one feasable partition.

(11) Worse, apply it to God himself. If God has middle knowledge about his own free decisions—say, whether to create W—then his own choice is fixed by prior conditions too. So divine freedom collapses the same way.

(12) So Molinism proves too much: it turns every free agent, including God, into a deterministic system, which kills the whole point.

I feel like the slip is somewhere between (6) and (8), maybe I'm smuggling in a uniqueness condition that doesn't belong? Or maybe (4) is wrong because libertarian freedom doesn't need "identical worlds" with different outcomes? I can't quite name it. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if possible, since the middle-knowledge idea is what got me interested in the first place.
