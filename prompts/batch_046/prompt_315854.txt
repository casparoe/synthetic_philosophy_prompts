Write a dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors, Althea and Barnaby, about whether Henry Sidgwick’s sustained engagement with psychical research is intellectually continuous with, or in tension with, the method and ontology underlying his moral philosophy. Althea should defend continuity: she should argue that Sidgwick’s openness to telepathic and survival-related evidence reflects the same empirical caution and anti-dogmatic attitude that shapes his ethical reasoning, particularly his refusal to ground moral principles in metaphysical speculation about an immaterial soul. She should further claim that investigating post-mortem consciousness need not disrupt hedonistic aggregation, since the calculus concerns experienced welfare regardless of substrate. Barnaby should defend tension: he should argue that Sidgwick’s ethical framework—especially its reliance on quantifiable pleasures and pains and its methodological analogy between ethics and the physical sciences—presupposes a causally integrated world in which mental states are systematically correlated with physical conditions, and that admitting discarnate mental causation or survival introduces explanatory entities that break the measurement conditions required for utility comparison. The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: Barnaby should press Althea directly on whether a hedonistic calculus can accommodate pains or pleasures that lack any physical or intersubjective verification conditions, and Althea should respond by distinguishing methodological naturalism from an ontological commitment to physicalism, arguing that ethics needs only observable correlations, not metaphysical closure. Then Althea should press Barnaby on whether Sidgwick’s well-known distinction between the dualism of practical reason and metaphysical dualism can be used to insulate ethical method from psychical ontology, and Barnaby should reply by asking whether that separation remains stable once survival of death is treated as a live epistemic possibility that extends the temporal horizon over which utility must be aggregated—potentially requiring ethical agents to weigh distant post-mortem states they can neither observe nor causally influence. Conclude without resolution, but with both identifying a specific conceptual crux—whether the conflict lies in the ontology of mental causation, in the verification conditions for welfare states, or in the temporal scope of ethical aggregation—that determines where the disagreement actually lives. Neither interlocutor should be a strawman; each should concede the force of the other’s strongest point before sharpening the objection.
