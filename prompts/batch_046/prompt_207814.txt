I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, and I have been studying the long-standing disagreement over the backward induction paradox in the centipede game. The dispute is familiar: one camp holds that common knowledge of perfect rationality forces both players to defect immediately, since each can foresee the other’s reasoning all the way back to the first node; the other camp points to actual experimental play, to intuitions about trust, and to the apparent rationality of cooperation for several rounds, and concludes that immediate defection is not required, or that the induction argument proves too much. Decades have passed without resolution, and the same moves are replayed.

What puzzles me, coming from a natural science background, is that neither side treats the induction chain like a transmission problem. In physics, local conditions can be intact at every point and still fail to determine a global state if there is attenuation, noise, or imperfect coupling between nodes. Applied here, this suggests a possibility that neither camp fully articulates: that each agent might be locally rational—correctly computing payoffs and best responses at every stage—while the global induction still collapses, not because any agent is irrational, but because the common-knowledge condition demands a structurally fragile, lossless transmission of rationality across stages that real reasoning may not support. Defenders of immediate defection seem to assume that local rationality, if present everywhere, necessarily propagates globally; critics seem to treat any breakdown of global induction as evidence of local irrationality or bounded cognition. I have not encountered an established term or framework for this middle ground—graded or probabilistically attenuated common knowledge that preserves local rationality without guaranteeing global convergence—and I suspect the stalemate may partly stem from both sides implicitly treating rationality as an all-or-nothing global property rather than a locally distributed one.

My request is for a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this disagreement substantive—about whether rationality is holistic or atomistic, or about what common knowledge actually requires? Is it verbal, with both sides using terms like “rationality” and “common knowledge” to mark different concepts? Or are they answering different questions—one normative and idealized, the other descriptive and predictive—under a single formalism? If the “attenuation” analogy identifies a real conceptual gap, is there an existing approach in decision theory or epistemic game theory that captures graded failures of common knowledge without collapsing into either full rationality or full irrationality? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would actually move the debate: a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a refined experimental design, or an acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed?

Please answer in American English.
