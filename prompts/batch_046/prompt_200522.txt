I am a retired comparative ethologist who spent three decades studying social cognition in primates and corvids, and who, since leaving the laboratory, have been working carefully through Strawson's arguments on reactive attitudes, moral responsibility, and the stance he terms the objective attitude. Next Thursday I am delivering the opening remarks—approximately six minutes—at a workshop titled Agency, Affect, and Explanation, hosted jointly by our university philosophy department and its institute for behavioral science. The audience will be divided evenly between philosophers specializing in moral responsibility and behavioral scientists with little philosophical training.

The remarks must do real structural work. I wish to introduce a conceptual distinction I have developed, which I term calibrated attenuation: a state in which reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, moral expectation—are neither fully present as in ordinary interpersonal relations, nor fully suspended as in the objective attitude that treats a person as an object to be managed, cured, or strategically predicted, but are instead selectively and contextually dampened. Consider the researcher who interprets a colleague's repeated errors as symptoms of cognitive overload rather than as moral failures, without thereby adopting the clinical or strategic stance that eliminates moral address entirely.

This framework should organize the address. First, I need to explain Strawson's binary—between the participant reactive attitude and the objective attitude—in language fully accessible to scientists, assuming no prior familiarity with the literature and providing no bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations. Second, I need to use calibrated attenuation to frame the workshop's central tension: if this intermediate register is coherent and widespread in scientific and clinical practice, then the boundary of moral responsibility may not align with the simple presence or absence of reactive attitudes in Strawson's binary form; responsibility might survive in gradations, or it might depend on whether attenuation is reversible and context-bound. Third, the speech must respect two constraints that the framework imposes: I must not claim that scientific explanation always eliminates moral address, but I also must not claim that reactive attitudes remain invariably intact in every interpersonal context.

Please write the address in a voice that sounds like a scientist who has genuinely engaged with philosophical argument but remains outside the discipline. Include two or three concrete questions for the workshop that arise directly from the calibrated-attenuation framework. Do not include direct quotations from philosophical texts, bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
