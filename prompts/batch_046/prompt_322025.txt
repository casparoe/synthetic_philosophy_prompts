I keep noticing a judgment pattern in ordinary moral talk that I don't have a word for. Picture two factory managers who approve the same cost-cutting change, both knowing it will have a side effect. In one case the side effect is toxic runoff that harms a nearby river; people say the manager intentionally caused the harm, that it was part of her decision. In the other case the side effect is an accidental wetland restoration that helps wildlife; people say the restoration was just a lucky byproduct, that she never intended it. The mental state is identical—she knew, she went ahead, she could have chosen differently—yet people assign intentionality to the harm and deny it to the benefit, and then use that assignment to explain what the decision was "really about." This sounds like territory experimental philosophers have mapped, but I want to know whether there is an established concept for this exact configuration, how it differs from ordinary outcome bias, and whether the intentionality judgment is treated as primary or as a rationalization of a prior moral evaluation. Does it have a precise name, and if not, how should its boundaries be drawn? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
