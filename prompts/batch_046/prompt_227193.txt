I was arguing with a coworker about Ryle's knowing-how vs knowing-that after a philosophy of mind seminar, and it actually matters for my job: I audit training modules for our state's rope-access technician cert board. New reg says any module tagged "knowing-how" must come with a supervised physical assesment, which costs us real money and pushes exam dates back. So I need a reusable decision procedre I can run in about 15 minutes on any module outline—questions in order, what counts as passing each filter, where I have to write "judgment call—board decides" instead of hiding behind a fake rule, and where the whole thing just stops and admits values are in play. Ground it in whatever the philosophy actually distinguishes: the regress argument, the difference between being able to do X and being able to state the rules for X, and why manual-based learning hits a wall.

Then apply it to three modules: (A) "calculating load tension on a carabiner system"—mostly equations, but trainees also learn to hear when a gate sounds wrong; (B) "recognizing hypothermia in a partner via posture and speech patterns"—taught with a symptom checklist, but experienced climbers say it's a gut read that doesn't reduce to the list; (C) "history and ethics of high-angle rescue"—pure knowing-that except for a role-play where trainees must decide whether to abort a rescue in high wind, which instructors claim builds moral skill. I want the hypothermia one (B) to come out tagged K-H in a way I'll genuinely hate, since that forces an expensive drill on every class. Also flag the exact point where the procedure breaks—like whether tacit knowledge can ever be fully manualized, or if the role-play in (C) is embodied skill or just applied reasoning. And be honest about what it can't do: don't pretend this replaces the board's value judgments about risk, and say clearly which ambiguities are just built in.
