Here is an argument about Kierkegaard's Abraham that strikes me as suspicious because the conclusion seems to destroy what it means to defend.

(1) In Fear and Trembling, the ethical is the universal: moral norms bind every rational agent without exception.
(2) Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac violates the universal—both the prohibition on murder and the father's specific duty to protect his child.
(3) Kierkegaard praises Abraham rather than condemning him, explaining that he performs a "teleological suspension of the ethical": he sets aside the universal for the sake of a higher telos, his absolute relation to God.
(4) This suspension is justified not by any reason accessible to others, but solely by the privacy and immediacy of Abraham's relation to the absolute.
(5) But if privacy and immediacy are sufficient to justify suspending the universal, then any agent who sincerely claims an unmediated, private command from an absolute authority can invoke the same structure.
(6) There is then no principled distinction between Abraham and someone who hears a private voice commanding harm to an innocent and acts on it in the name of faith.
(7) So either Kierkegaard provides a criterion to distinguish legitimate suspension from delusion—thereby mediating the absolute relation and destroying its absoluteness—or his position collapses into the view that any subjective conviction justifies violating universal ethics.
(8) The first option betrays the concept of faith; the second makes ethics contingent on private revelation.
(9) Therefore Kierkegaard's account of the teleological suspension is incoherent.

Which step should I reject? I want to keep (1) through (4) and reject (5), but I worry that (4) genuinely entails (5) and that saving Abraham requires reintroducing some form of mediation or universal criterion into the absolute relation. If the flaw lies elsewhere, name the step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the cost of giving it up—especially whether it forces us to concede that the knight of faith is, in principle, indistinguishable from the deluded fanatic.
