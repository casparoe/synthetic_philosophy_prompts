I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I occasionally discuss conceptual questions with colleagues. Recently, two members of my study group—I'll call them Riley and Sam—had an extended argument about G. E. Moore's account of intrinsic value, specifically concerning friendship and beauty. I stayed neutral, but they asked me to help adjudicate the exchange. Here is the back-and-forth as I recall it.

Riley began by stating that Moore treats friendship and the appreciation of beauty as paradigmatic goods that are valuable entirely in themselves. On this view, if a person genuinely participates in friendship or experiences something truly beautiful, those states carry positive intrinsic value regardless of what else is occurring in the surrounding context. Even if the broader situation is otherwise miserable, these elements remain good parts of reality.

Sam objected by invoking Moore's doctrine of organic unity. Sam argued that Moore does not allow us to isolate a friendship or a beautiful experience and assign it value independently, because the value of any state of affairs depends on the relations among all its constituent parts. A friendship embedded in cruelty or deception might contribute to a bad overall whole, and a beautiful object might form part of a malicious design. Therefore, Sam claimed, Moore does not grant friendship and beauty unconditional intrinsic value; rather, he grants value only to minimal wholes that include the appropriate mental states and relational conditions.

Riley conceded that total value depends on organic relations, but insisted that Moore still classifies friendship and aesthetic appreciation as good in themselves—meaning they always contribute positively when present, even if other parts reduce the total value of the situation. Riley offered an analogy: a well-written function inside a buggy codebase remains correct in itself, even though the system as a whole fails.

Sam replied that this analogy imports a parts-based metaphysics that Moore rejects for values. For Moore, goodness is not a property inhering in isolated parts like a software module; it is a property of the entire state of affairs. Calling friendship good in itself is therefore not a claim about a portable token of value, but shorthand for the claim that there exists a minimal organic unit—say, two people with appropriate mutual affection and awareness—that is good. Strip away the mental states and you no longer have friendship, only proximity.

Riley then presented a specific case: imagine a person who maintains one deep, reciprocal friendship but otherwise lives in total isolation with no other goods. If Sam's interpretation is correct, then in the absence of other parts to form a rich whole, the friendship might carry almost no value, which seems absurd. So Riley argued that Moore must allow the friendship to bear substantial value on its own.

Sam answered that this case actually supports the organic-unity reading. Even the apparently isolated friendship already contains internal relations—mutual awareness, emotional reciprocity, shared understanding—so it is already a minimal whole with its own internal organic unity. Its value derives from that internal structure, not from friendship being an atom of goodness that can be dropped into any context. Sam added that reducing the relation to mere physical proximity removes the mental states that constitute it, so the case does not demonstrate that friendship is independently valuable.

Riley's final point was that if Sam is right, then Moore's well-known lists of great goods become incoherent. Moore names friendship and aesthetic appreciation as goods without constantly specifying the accompanying mental states every time he mentions them. That pattern suggests he treats them as identifiable goods in their own right, not as minimal organic packages.

Sam closed by noting that Moore does specify them: friendship requires appropriate emotions and consciousness, and beauty requires a particular kind of appreciative awareness. Thus the list is not of bare external relations but of minimal organic unities. Sam concluded that the disagreement is ultimately about whether good in itself applies to a bare relation or to a relation combined with the requisite mental state.
