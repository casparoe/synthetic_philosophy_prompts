I have been tracking a pattern in organizational and educational settings that adopt what are usually framed as liberatory assessment practices—self-evaluation portfolios, peer feedback loops, transparent rubrics, and reflective journaling intended to replace opaque hierarchical judgment. In the cases I have watched closely, these mechanisms initially appear to distribute authority downward: students or employees define their own goals, document their progress in their own terms, and participate in shaping the criteria by which they will be judged. Yet over successive cycles, the categories embedded in these reflective instruments—how collaborative capacity is operationalized, what counts as evidence of growth, which emotional responses are coded as appropriate—grow more granular and more normatively loaded than the older implicit standards ever were. The subjects begin to perform intensive self-monitoring not against an external observer but against the taxonomy they themselves have helped populate, and the institution acquires a high-resolution map of deviation without issuing direct commands. The phenomenon is not quite the panopticon, since the gaze is internalized through participatory structures rather than imposed by an architectural or supervisory presence, and it is not exactly bureaucratic rationalization, because the norms remain fluid and are continuously updated by the very data that subjects generate about themselves. Is there an established concept in the study of power, knowledge, and discipline that captures this precise inversion—where technologies of self-assessment are repurposed into instruments of finer-grained normalization? If not, can it be named and defined in a way that clearly separates it from panopticism, governmentality, and plain institutional mission creep? I am less interested in a bare label than in a precise carving: what exactly this mechanism is, where its edges lie, and which cases belong inside it rather than in neighboring concepts.
