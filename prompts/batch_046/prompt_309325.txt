Write a short research proposal (suitable for a one-term philosophy-of-mind independent study or a workshop pitch) that frames and answers the following precise question: does pain asymbolia — the clinical presentation in which subjects report the sensory qualities of pain without the usual motivational aversion, affective distress, or avoidance behaviour — demonstrate that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to its phenomenal character, but rather depends on a contingent, second-order normative commitment to treat the interoceptive signal as action-relevant?

Position your proposal within a rationalist-style analytical framework: operationalise the distinction between first-order sensory registration and second-order “normative binding”, explain why the standard philosophical responses (that asymbolia reveals a dissociation between sensory and affective pathways, or that it masks hidden distress through compensatory mechanisms) fail to resolve whether pain’s aversiveness is analytically tied to its sensory profile, and propose an alternative conceptual approach that models the aversive force of pain on the structure of interoceptive calibration errors — analogous to predictive-processing accounts of error signals — without importing empirical neuroscientific commitments that would take the project outside conceptual philosophy.

State exactly what would count as a determinate answer. If one can construct a logically coherent description of a subject who correctly applies the concept “pain”, reports its qualitative character, yet experiences no normative pressure to avoid or terminate the state, then badness is extrinsic to the sensory profile. If every such description necessarily collapses into either conceptual confusion or an undetected affective disturbance, then badness is essential. Identify the intermediate possibility — that badness is a default but overrideable calibration setting — and say what conceptual considerations (for instance, the analogy to perceptual beliefs that guide action only under specific attentional conditions, or the logical possibility of “neutral” interoceptive reporting) would decide among the three.

Outline your method: map the logical space of interoceptive reporting, distinguish “feeling a pain-sensation” from “treating that sensation as a reason for action”, and test whether the latter can be suspended while preserving the former through structured conceptual experiments rather than clinical case reviews. Specify the deliverable: a principle that captures the relation between sensory registration and normative force, together with its verdict on at least one unconsidered case (for example, a hypothetical subject who registers injury-signals with perfect accuracy but assigns them zero decision-theoretic weight).

Give an honest assessment of scope and risk. Note which parts of the project threaten to expand into empirical neuropsychology or into a full normative theory of practical reasons, and explain precisely how you will contain them — for example, by restricting arguments to conceptual coherence conditions and by treating predictive-processing language as a modelling heuristic rather than an empirical hypothesis.

Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or reading list; refer to existing philosophical positions and arguments in general terms, and indicate where you will insert specific references during writing. Write the proposal in British English, using precise philosophical terminology but avoiding unnecessary jargon.
