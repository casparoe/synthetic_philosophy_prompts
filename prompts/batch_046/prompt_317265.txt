I have just finished a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about Tolstoy's aesthetic theory, and I am trying to settle a specific interpretive point rather than reopen the whole debate. We were discussing his essay What Is Art?, particularly the section where he argues that true art is defined by its capacity to infect the audience with the artist's feeling, and that this requires the artist to have genuinely experienced the emotion being transmitted. In that section, Tolstoy seems to claim that if the artist does not sincerely feel what is being communicated—if the work is calculated, conventional, or produced merely to please—then however skillful it may be, it lacks the essential quality of art and becomes instead a form of deception or counterfeit.

My friend insists that this criterion collapses under its own weight: she points out that many works Tolstoy himself condemns, such as Wagner's operas, clearly move audiences powerfully, and that collective or anonymous works—medieval icon painting, folk ballads—seem to transmit feeling without any identifiable solitary artist whose private emotional state can be verified. She also raised a more niche objection that I could not answer: if art is essentially emotional contagion from a conscious source, then Tolstoy's framework seems structurally incapable of accommodating art produced through distributed, procedural, or non-intentional processes, not merely modern algorithmic generation but even traditional collaborative forms where authorship is dispersed. I want to understand whether the passage actually commits Tolstoy to the view that a single, identifiable, sincerely feeling artist is a necessary condition for genuine art, or whether he allows that emotional transmission can occur even when the feeling is not localized in one individual's present consciousness.

Could you help me parse the logical structure of that passage? Specifically: does Tolstoy present sincerity and infectiousness as two independent conditions, or does he treat sincerity as merely the usual causal mechanism that produces infectiousness? How does he distinguish between a work that fails because it is insincere and a work that succeeds despite being produced by someone who does not currently feel the emotion—say, through craft or tradition? And if the answer depends on whether the audience receives a real feeling or only an appearance of one, how is that distinction supposed to be detected by a viewer or listener who has no access to the artist's inner state? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of scholarship; I simply need the conceptual machinery of the passage clarified, with attention to the points where Tolstoy's procedure for identifying real art breaks down or requires judgment that his own text does not provide.
