Having spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics before retiring and turning to moral philosophy, I have been attempting to integrate what I encounter in cross-cultural moral psychology with a realist framework that I adopted early in my reading. In a recent seminar with colleagues from my university’s philosophy program, I articulated a cluster of commitments that I now wish to audit for coherence before developing them into a longer essay. Specifically, I hold: (i) that moral judgments express objective truths about human welfare that are not constituted by any culture’s beliefs about them; (ii) that there is robust, non-artifactual divergence across cultures in first-order judgments about concrete cases—such as obligations to distant strangers versus loyalty to kin—where the divergence cannot be reduced to differences in non-moral background information; (iii) that this divergence is best explained not by genuinely incompatible moral norms, but by a normative calibration effect: different cultures share an underlying moral grammar but apply different contextual weights (to proximity, visibility of harm, social role, and so on) in a manner analogous to differently calibrated measurement instruments reporting different values for the same underlying physical state; (iv) that reflective equilibrium remains the correct normative method, and that its initial inputs ought to be one’s own culturally calibrated intuitions, since discarding one’s baseline calibration renders subsequent reasoning unmotivated and epistemically unstable; and (v) that a principle which remains endorsed across systematically varied calibrations—one that survives when contextual weights are deliberately shifted—should carry greater evidential weight for objective moral truth than a principle that holds only within a single calibration regime.

I am particularly concerned that (iv) privileges precisely the kind of biased baseline that (iii) diagnoses, and that (v) risks collapsing the objectivity asserted in (i) into a form of intersubjective consensus that realism ought to reject; at the same time, I am unsure whether (ii) and (iii) stand in genuine tension or whether the calibration model dissolves the appearance of conflict between them. Please examine this package systematically: identify which pairs are in real tension and which only appear to conflict, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally damaging, specify for each the commitment that bears the burden and the least costly revision available, and indicate which single adjustment—if I were forced to make only one—would clean up the greatest number of inconsistencies without abandoning the core realist and calibrationist structure I wish to preserve.
