I was discussing Spinoza's political philosophy with a friend who had just listened to an introductory podcast episode, and she offered this summary of his democratic theory: Spinoza argued that democracy is the best regime because in it every citizen is simultaneously ruler and ruled, which completely dissolves the tension between individual freedom and state power. He believed the multitude naturally converges on rational laws through shared reason, so democratic sovereignty does not need coercive enforcement—agreement emerges organically. That is why he supported full universal suffrage with no exclusions for property, gender, or education, since rational capacity is identical in all people. Moreover, she tied this to his metaphysics: democratic law expresses the common notions of the multitude, making the state a direct expression of adequate ideas rather than merely a practical compromise among conflicting passions.

Something about this sounds too neat, especially given what I remember about Spinoza being wary of the multitude's passions and placing significant restrictions on who could participate in democratic citizenship in his later political writings. But I am not sure which parts are genuine Spinozism and which are modern liberal projections. Could you audit this summary claim by claim? Please tell me what Spinoza actually held about democracy being the most natural form of government, about the relationship between being ruler and ruled, about the conditions for citizenship and suffrage, and about whether democratic agreement is rational or passionate. Also, is the link drawn between his epistemology of common notions and democratic sovereignty something Spinoza actually develops, or is it an anachronistic overlay?
