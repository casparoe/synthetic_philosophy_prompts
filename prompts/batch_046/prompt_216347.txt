I am a retired physicist who spent thirty years in statistical mechanics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science. Recently I have been studying formal accounts of inductive inference—Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and the various mathematical precisifications of Occam's razor—and I find myself troubled by a tension that standard treatments rarely address directly. I have taken to calling it the problem of indexical leakage: whenever we fix a universal description language in order to compute algorithmic probabilities, we implicitly encode prior observational commitments about which features of the world are easily representable, commitments that are not themselves warranted by the data under explanation. To test whether this leakage can be neutralized rather than merely relocated, I have sketched four competing normative criteria for adjudicating between scientific theories that share an identical unnormalized descriptive length but distribute their internal complexity differently across observable and unobservable parameters.

The four criteria are as follows. (A) A pure Solomonoff prior that weights hypotheses strictly by 2 raised to the negative Kolmogorov complexity, with no normalization for the choice of universal machine. (B) A resource-bounded Kolmogorov measure restricted to a polynomial-time universal machine, which penalizes hypotheses that are descriptively short yet require intractable computation to decode or apply. (C) A thermodynamic variant of parsimony—my own tentative construction—which penalizes any hypothesis in direct proportion to the reduction in entropy that it demands of the prior belief distribution, on the assumption that a genuinely simpler theory ought not require a sharply peaked prior in order to generate accurate predictions. (D) An epistemic-utility framework that explicitly trades predictive accuracy against the cognitive compression cost incurred by a human interpreter who must deploy the theory in real-time reasoning. Would you please rank these four criteria from most to least defensible as normative principles for scientific theory choice, assigning each a score between 1 and 10, and provide a brief justification for each placement? I am especially interested in whether criterion C genuinely resolves indexical leakage or only displaces the problem into the thermodynamic formalism.
