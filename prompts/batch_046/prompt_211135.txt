After a long argument with a friend about whether clinical mindfulness actually imports Buddhist metaphysics or is just secular psychology in disguise, I want to pin down what a specific institutional practice really commits us to philosophically. In standard mindfulness-based clinical protocols used for depression relapse and trauma, patients practice decentering: they are taught to observe thoughts, emotions, and sensations as transient, impersonal events, explicitly framing mental content as lacking inherent ownership in line with the Buddhist doctrine of anatta (no permanent self). Yet within the same protocol, patients complete structured exercises in values clarification and autobiographical reconstruction, identifying enduring core commitments, constructing a coherent narrative of recovery, and making long-term behavioral choices justified by reference to what "truly matters to me" or "who I am becoming." The manual treats these values as genuinely belonging to the patient across sessions, carrying normative authority that should resist momentary mental states.

What theory of personal identity does this practice presuppose, if any? Does the decentering module presuppose a reductionist or eliminativist view of the self, while the values-reconstruction module presupposes a narrative or constitutive view? Or does the protocol rely on a single consistent framework—perhaps a conventional-versus-ultimate distinction, or a pragmatic two-level account—that licenses both moves without contradiction? Can a strict anatta position explain why certain values should be treated as authoritative for this particular patient over time, or does the practice quietly smuggle in a persistent self that serves as the owner and bearer of those values? And is the practice philosophically underdetermined: could rival theories of personal identity (psychological continuity, narrative unity, reductionism, or even a qualified essentialism) license the very same therapeutic structure, so that the protocol rules out far less than its Buddhist framing suggests? If the implicit metaphysics were replaced—for instance, by a purely secular cognitive-behavioral framework that never invokes impermanence or non-self—which parts of the practice would have to change, and which could remain intact?
