Recently, while sorting my late grandfather’s papers, I found an unfinished essay from 1978 proposing that philosophy advances not by accumulating true doctrines, like science, but by progressively mapping the precise boundaries of what cannot be consistently maintained—what he called the “error space” of human reasoning. I am now preparing a lecture on whether philosophy makes progress at all, and I would like you to write a short story dramatizing this tension. The narrative should center on a protagonist who uncovers an intergenerational philosophical correspondence—perhaps her own ancestor’s—that forces her to ask whether philosophical change is cumulative, revolutionary, or primarily a matter of dissolving old confusions and charting new ones. Write with literary precision but conceptual clarity, treating the “error-space” view as one live possibility among others. Please include a brief author’s note explaining where the story takes liberties with standard frameworks—such as Kuhnian, Lakatosian, or conceptual-engineering accounts of philosophical change—or relies on contested interpretations of whether philosophy is genuinely progressive. In that note, clearly separate what is broadly settled about the comparison between philosophical and scientific progress from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any single contested view, including the “error-space” thesis, as the consensus position.
