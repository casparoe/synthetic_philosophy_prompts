i've been running a 90-minute workshop on value pluralism with about 14 grad students in political philosophy, and we've been circling incommensurable values and hard choices for weeks but it still feels too airy — like we're all just agreeing that "some goods can't be ranked" and calling it a day. I want an actual classroom game or simulation where they run into the problem with their own hands, not just hear me explain it afterwards.

Could you design a runnable activity for a seminar table with 14 people, 90 minutes total including debrief? The setting should be a "city charter" negotiation: the group must split 100 resource points across four projects representing liberty, care, heritage, and innovation. Each student gets a hidden "conviction card" with a public constituency role and two secret pieces — a private "line in the sand" (one project that, if funded below 25 points, forces them to declare an irreversible "moral exit" and blow up the charter) and a hidden "conversion belief" about which specific pairs of values they personally treat as exchangeable and which they see as genuinely incomparable. The rules need to make them negotiate, propose amendments by majority, and vote on a final charter, but if anyone's hidden line is crossed they invoke exit and the group has to restart with less time. Please build the timeline so the philosophy shows up in the mechanics — the false assumption of a shared exchange rate, the moment someone realizes they're not bargaining but betraying something, the breakdown when hidden incommensurabilities collide — and save the naming for the debrief.

In the debrief, map specific moments onto parts of the idea, say what will predictably go wrong (people suppressing exits to keep peace, groups averaging away conflict) and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature this can't reproduce — probably the lived grief of actually crossing one's own line, or the fact that in real life people often don't know where their line is until after it's already broken, since here the card tells them in advance. Also please separate what's fairly settled (that values can conflict without a common metric) from what's genuinely contested (whether this makes rational choice impossible, or whether there's always some hidden ordering we just haven't found). And no citations, no bibliography, no "for further reading" — just the rules, the cards, the timeline, and the honest facilator note on what's missing. Maybe include a small typo or casual framing?
