In the course of co-teaching a graduate seminar on historical moral responsibility, I have catalogued the consistent judgments rendered by a senior colleague—an historian of ethics—regarding whether the “man of his time” defence exculpates a figure from moral blame. Her track record runs as follows. She rejects the defence for Thomas Jefferson’s slaveholding, on the grounds that Jefferson personally articulated universalist principles of equality that his conduct directly contradicted. She grants the defence to Christopher Columbus for his enslavement and violent subjugation of Taíno peoples, holding that no credible institutional or intellectual alternative to Spanish colonial practice was practically accessible to him during the 1490s. And she grants the defence to Winston Churchill for the racial hierarchies embedded in his imperial administration, noting that Churchill never personally endorsed anti-racist norms he then violated, and that anti-imperial dissent remained institutionally marginal within his governing circles.

Next week we turn to a case I fear will fracture her implicit framework. The case concerns Dr. J. Marion Sims, who in the 1840s conducted repeated experimental surgical repairs of vesicovaginal fistulas on enslaved Black women—Anarcha, Betsy, and Lucy—without administering ether, despite the fact that he had access to the anesthetic and shortly thereafter used it routinely for white patients undergoing comparable procedures. I ask you to predict whether she will extend the defence to Sims. More importantly, lay out the two most plausible general rules that jointly explain her three prior verdicts—for instance, one centered on personal hypocrisy and one centered on the practical accessibility of dissenting practices—state precisely what each rule predicts about Sims, and then offer your best-guess prediction with explicit reasoning. Finally, propose a single further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish these two rules. In your response, please indicate how confident you are in each component of your analysis and explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain.
