My community garden has twelve shared plots and a single tool shed. For years, an unwritten pattern governed borrowing: anyone can take a tool, but it should be cleaned and hung on the left wall, never the right. New members learned this by observing regulars. The shed also contains an old paper logbook that technically records every withdrawal, though nobody has consulted it in years; coordination relied entirely on visible, mutual awareness of where things belonged.

Two months ago, two new households joined. One began leaving borrowed spades on the right wall because it is closer to the gate. The other, interpreting the left wall as now "claimed," started storing her personal trowels there with a small lock. Long-time members are split. Some insist the left-wall rule is an established convention that binds newcomers regardless of whether they were told. Others argue that a convention requires mutual benefit and common knowledge of that benefit, which collapsed the moment the newcomers entered without being socialized into the pattern. A third faction wants to abandon silent coordination entirely and vote on a formal charter with explicit penalties.

As the coordinator drafting our group message, I am trying to determine whether the old pattern should be treated as still binding, whether it has already dissolved, or whether we should deliberately replace it with designed rules. I am not looking for generic conflict-mediation tips; I want an analysis that treats this as an instance of how arbitrary coordination patterns become norms, what sustains them, and under what conditions they legitimately evolve or expire—especially given that objective records exist but were never part of the shared awareness that sustained the practice.
