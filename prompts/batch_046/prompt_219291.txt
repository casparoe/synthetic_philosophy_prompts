I've been reflecting on a course I took recently in early analytic ethics, and it has left me uncertain about a decision my neighborhood association now faces. We maintain an old estate that includes a decaying but extraordinarily beautiful glass conservatory filled with rare ferns; for years, the collective labor of repairing panes and tending plants has been the backbone of the closest friendships in my life. Now our budget forces a concrete choice: we can restore the conservatory properly, which requires hiring outside specialists and reducing our communal workdays to a small supervisory committee, or we can preserve the shared labor but allow the structure to continue deteriorating until it becomes unsafe and the visual experience that draws people in is lost. Several members argue that we should simply calculate which option produces the greatest total satisfaction or well-being and vote accordingly, but something about that approach strikes me as fundamentally inadequate—almost as if it assumes that the value of the place and the value of the relationships can be separated, weighed on the same scale, and summed. I cannot shake the intuition that the friendships and the beauty are intertwined in a way that makes the whole more valuable than either part alone, yet I worry that insisting on this just sounds like sentimental resistance to rational planning. Is there a principled way to think about this trade-off that takes such interdependence seriously rather than treating both goods as interchangeable units? If so, how should it shape our decision, and what should I say to the members who believe any deviation from the satisfaction calculus is merely irrational attachment?
