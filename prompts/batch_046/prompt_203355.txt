In historical and popular accounts of societal collapse — whether the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, or Easter Island — one frequently encounters the analogy that a civilization is like a living organism. The claim is that societies possess a kind of organizational homeostasis, that they can suffer trauma and fail to reproduce their own institutions, and that collapse is therefore not merely change but death: a terminal, irreversible loss of coherence. This analogy is often used to license the stronger conclusion that collapse represents a genuine catastrophic failure rather than a neutral transformation, a graceful degradation, or a protocol shift to a different but viable state.

As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or history, I tend to think about resilience in terms of distributed systems: some architectures crash catastrophically when a critical node fails, while others reroute and continue operating with reduced capability. That framing makes me skeptical of the organism analogy, but I want to audit it properly rather than dismiss it.

Can you lay out the correspondence table in detail — what corresponds to the body, the vital organs, metabolic homeostasis, reproduction, and death in this analogy — and then identify the specific points where the mapping breaks, particularly with respect to the Bronze Age, the Maya, and Easter Island? Does the analogy quietly import the assumption that there is a single correct organizational state that counts as being alive, so that the loss of writing, monumental construction, or centralized administration is treated as organ failure rather than feature reduction? And if we apply the analogy consistently, does the conclusion that collapse is genuinely catastrophic survive, or was the emotional weight of death carrying the argument?

Finally, if the organism analogy fails or is misleading, is there a better analog — perhaps drawn from software architecture, ecological succession, or something else — that captures the dynamics of these three cases without smuggling in the same normative commitments? If so, what does that alternative analogy imply about whether societies fail or merely change?
