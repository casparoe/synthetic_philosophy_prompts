"The discovery/invention distinction is central to IP debates. Discoveries, like a physicist formulating E=mc² or a mathematician proving a new theorem, involve uncovering something that already exists in nature or logic. These goods are non-rival: my knowing the theorem doesn't stop you from knowing it too. Inventions — say, a new engine or a software algorithm — are artifacts humans create, and they're rivalrous in implementation, since only one factory can run the machine at once. This difference seems to explain why we patent inventions but don't patent discoveries.

But critics say discovery requires enormous cognitive labor. Locke's labor-mixing theory holds that we acquire property rights when we mix our labor with the commons. If mental effort counts as labor, then the discoverer should own the result just as a farmer owns crops. Copyright law already does something similar with the idea/expression dichotomy: you can't copyright an idea, only its specific expression. So the law already separates discovery-like ideas from invented expressions.

Because both discovery and invention involve labor, the distinction collapses. We should either extend IP protection to discoveries when the mental labor is substantial, or deny it to both categories. The non-rivalry point doesn't matter once we see that labor deserves reward regardless of the good's type."
