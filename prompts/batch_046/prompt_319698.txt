Consider the “Stoic Resilience Protocol” recently mandated for managers at several large consulting and technology firms. Under the protocol, managers must complete weekly “control audits” that sort workplace setbacks—lost contracts, team conflicts, product failures—into events within direct agency (judgment, effort, attitude) and events outside it (market forces, colleagues’ actions, strategic decisions made above them). Managers are rated on emotional composure, measured by the degree to which they have withdrawn concern from the second category. Simultaneously, however, these same managers are evaluated for bonuses and promotion on metrics—revenue targets, retention rates, delivery timelines—that depend overwhelmingly on precisely those external conditions. What philosophical commitments about moral agency, responsibility, and welfare must this arrangement presuppose in order to treat both the audit and the evaluation system as legitimate? Reconstruct the implicit theory of control and accountability that binds them together; distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits with; assess whether that theory is internally coherent given the tension between detachment and accountability; and specify what would have to change in the evaluation or training regime if the firm replaced its Stoic framework with a view that assigns responsibility for outcomes an agent can influence but does not fully determine. Conclude with a single line beginning “Verdict:” that summarizes the reconstructed view and states whether it is coherent.
