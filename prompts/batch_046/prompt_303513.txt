I have been running a graduate reading group on objective chance and rational belief this semester, and for our final session we are working through the Principal Principle—the claim that if one knows the objective chance of an event is x, one's rational credence should be x. The discussion has stalled in exactly the place I hoped it would: some of us read the principle as a substantive constraint that ties epistemic rationality to physical propensities, while others read it as a coherence condition that risks being either tautological or metaphysically overcommitted, especially once we start asking whether objective chance refers to anything more robust than a summary of frequencies or dispositions. Rather than assigning a concluding paper, I want to close the session with a poem that holds this tension open. Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a final quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge—structured so that the form itself enacts the standoff. The first refrain should be voiced by the deference position, something like knowing the physical chance fixes the belief; the second should be the skeptical pull, questioning what weight that knowledge carries when chance itself might be nothing but a pattern. The refrains should take turns through the tercets, passing each other without resolution, and in the closing quatrain they must sit side by side, unreconciled. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with Lewis's formulation recognizes the specific problem—how deference to objective chance can seem both mandatory and mysteriously empty—without becoming an expository summary. It should be suitable for reading aloud at the end of a seminar, leaving the group in the same unresolved but clarified place where our conversation ended.
