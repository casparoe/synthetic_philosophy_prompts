I've been working through a problem about verisimilitude that seems to collapse into something absurd, and I want a committed diagnosis—no survey of options, but a definite line on where the chain breaks and what that costs. Here is the argument.

(1) A false theory can be closer to the truth than another false theory only if there is a metric that ranks worlds or propositions by similarity to the actual world.
(2) Any such metric must treat logical equivalence as identity: if two theories entail exactly the same propositions, they describe the same possible state of affairs and must occupy the same point in the metric space.
(3) Now take any two false but empirically adequate theories T₁ and T₂ in a mature science—say, one posits continuous fields, the other discrete action-at-a-distance mediated by hidden mechanisms—both saving every observed phenomenon but differing in unobservable ontology.
(4) By construction, T₁ and T₂ are empirically equivalent: they entail the same observational consequences.
(5) If the similarity metric is restricted to observable consequences, then T₁ and T₂ are equidistant from the truth; there is no fact of the matter about which ontology is closer.
(6) If the metric is not so restricted but allows comparison over unobservable structure, then we must be able to measure the distance between a field-ontology world and an action-at-a-distance world.
(7) But any well-behaved metric over ontological structures will either rank every pair of distinct ontologies as equally distant (if symmetry is preserved) or will arbitrarily privilege one structural feature—continuity, locality, number of entities—as the axis of measurement.
(8) Therefore, either verisimilitude is undefined for empirically equivalent false theories, or it depends on an unmotivated choice of ontological metric.
(9) But if it is undefined for empirically equivalent false theories, then when scientists replaced the caloric theory with kinetic theory, or geocentrism with heliocentrism, they were not moving closer to the truth unless the replaced theory was already empirically inadequate.
(10) That makes scientific progress either purely instrumental—better predictions, not closer truth—or requires that every theoretical advance be accompanied by an empirical crisis in the old view.
(11) Both options seem wrong: we want to say kinetic theory was closer to the truth even before decisive experiments, and we want to allow progress through theoretical refinement without waiting for anomaly.

My study partner says the sleight of hand is in (7), but I think the real pressure is between (5) and (6). Give me a committed answer: which specific step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to about whether verisimilitude requires an observable-only metric, an ontological metric, or neither? If you think the argument is valid and we really must swallow (11), say so and defend that conclusion. Take a definite position on whether empirically equivalent false theories can differ in verisimilitude, and price the alternatives honestly.
