As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying moral philosophy in earnest, I have become absorbed in luck egalitarianism—the view that distributive justice requires neutralizing the effects of brute luck while permitting inequalities that flow from genuinely responsible choices. In my former laboratory work we constantly separated measurement outcomes into systematic error, which traces back to the design, calibration, and deliberate choices of the experimenter, and random error, the irreducible noise that strikes regardless of planning. The analogy to distributive justice is almost seductively neat: option luck seems comparable to systematic error, since it arises from risks one knowingly assumes with full command of the apparatus, whereas brute luck resembles random noise, falling upon individuals irrespective of their will. Yet pressing this comparison exposes what I fear is a recursive collapse. The “apparatus” of human agency—our preferences, risk tolerances, cognitive capacities, and even the psychological machinery that evaluates trade-offs—is itself assembled from genetic endowments and social conditions that are paradigmatic instances of brute luck. If every choice is executed through machinery that was never chosen, then the boundary between option luck and brute luck begins to look less like a morally salient distinction and more like an arbitrary slice through a continuous causal chain.

Write an essay examining whether luck egalitarianism can survive this kind of scientific analogy intact. Specifically, weigh the strongest defense—that one can identify a threshold of “sufficient causal contribution” at which choice genuinely takes over—against the strongest objection, that any such threshold is necessarily stipulative and collapses the distinction into a broader, undifferentiated egalitarianism. Do not merely survey these positions; I want a committed response that reaches and defends a definite verdict on whether luck egalitarianism remains coherent once we rigorously apply the measurement-error analogy, whether it must be abandoned for a different framework, or whether it can be reconstructed. Provide explicit reasons for your conclusion, and avoid ending on a non-committal note that leaves the verdict to me.
