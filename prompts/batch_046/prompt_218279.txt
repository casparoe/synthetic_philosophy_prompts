I am preparing an opening piece for a philosophy seminar that has spent the term on moral motivation and the sociology of religion, and we have arrived at a stubborn disagreement: whether secular societies can sustain what philosophers and sociologists call "thick" interpersonal trust — reliance on strangers that exceeds contractual or reputational assurance — or whether such trust necessarily draws on shared transcendental commitments that secular frameworks cannot replicate. The group is also stuck on the measurement problem: claims that religious people are "more moral" conflate behavioral metrics (charitable giving, honesty in economic games), characterological metrics (virtue, humility, forgiveness), and systemic metrics (institutional corruption, social cohesion), and no one agrees on which metric, if any, captures what "more moral" should mean.

Please write an English ghazal of six couplets — twelve lines, each couplet autonomous, closing with the same refrain phrase without the witness — using a single rhyme sound (for example, -ear, -ain, or -ust) that immediately precedes the refrain in each rhyming line. The refrain should do structural work: in the first couplets it should evoke the absence of divine oversight; in the middle couplets, the absence of any objective observer who could adjudicate competing moral metrics; and in the final couplet, the positive condition of secular trust, which must operate precisely because no transcendent witness guarantees it. Each couplet should pair one concrete moral metric (e.g., donation rates, promise-keeping, care for non-kin strangers) with one mechanism of social cohesion (contract, ritual, institutional transparency), binding them through the rhyme so that the formal coupling mirrors the conceptual claim that measurement and social glue are not separable. The poem should not resolve into a sermon for religious restoration or a secular shrug, but should end with the refrain holding both meanings — absence as deficit and absence as condition — simultaneously. Render the distinction between thick and thin trust, and between behavioral and virtue-based moral assessment, with enough precision that a specialist in philosophy of religion or sociology of secularism would recognize the concepts rather than see them named.

After the poem, include a brief authorial note — no more than a short paragraph — stating how confident you are that the framing maps onto actual debates (for instance, whether the literature on secular morality and social trust genuinely poses the problem in these terms), and explicitly identify the point at which you are least sure: specifically, whether the ghazal's formal repetition genuinely illuminates the epistemic difficulty of measuring morality without shared metaphysical commitments, or whether that reading is an imposition that risks distorting the philosophical disagreement. If your interpretation of any particular scholar's position is contested, flag it.
