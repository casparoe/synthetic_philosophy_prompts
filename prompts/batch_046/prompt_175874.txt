Since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have been auditing graduate seminars in moral philosophy and metaphysics, and I recently found myself in a vigorous discussion with colleagues about whether metaphysical assumptions about identity over time should alter our moral evaluation of long-range policies. I am now assisting in drafting the final examination for an upper-level seminar on intergenerational ethics, and I need a single essay question—suitable for a ninety-minute response, carrying twenty marks—on the nonidentity problem. Rather than allowing students to recite the familiar climate-change or reproductive-choice examples, I want the prompt anchored in a less common conceptual framing: the interaction between the nonidentity problem and four-dimensionalist (perdurantist) accounts of personal identity. Ask the students to evaluate whether adopting a spacetime-worm ontology dissolves the problem, transforms it into a different kind of comparative harm, or leaves it intact but shifts its moral significance. Use as the carrying case a hypothetical long-term geological carbon-sequestration protocol that alters regional climate patterns and thereby determines which future individuals come into existence, so that students must ask whether anyone is harmed when there is no enduring subject who exists in both the actual and counterfactual worlds.

Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately six hundred words that identifies the crux—whether harm requires a continuous enduring subject or can be reconstructed across temporal parts—and a marking scheme built around five weighted criteria totaling twenty marks: (1) correct distinction between origin-affecting and identity-affecting actions; (2) accurate application of the perdurantist framework without conflating it with stage-theoretic variants; (3) recognition that the problem shifts from "who is harmed?" to "which modal history is worse?"; (4) explicit avoidance of conflating the nonidentity problem with Parfit's repugnant conclusion; and (5) a reasoned conclusion about whether four-dimensionalism offers a genuine resolution or merely a redescription. For each criterion, specify the marks awarded and the characteristic misconception or omission that should cost points—particularly the error of assuming that if no enduring individual is harmed, then no moral wrong has occurred.
