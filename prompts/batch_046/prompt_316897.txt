I'm noticing a recurring argumentative and institutional pattern in layered systems—the US, the EU, and Germany—where lower-level governments retain formal authority over a domain, exercise it in genuinely different ways, and then that very divergence is treated by central advocates not as a successful expression of local autonomy but as proof that the matter must be moved upward. The reasoning is rarely that the local units failed at a shared task; rather, they succeeded at incompatible ones, generating patchwork compliance costs, cross-border mobility effects, or enforcement gaps that uniform central rules are said to be the only remedy for. I see it when US states adopt stricter vehicle-emission or labor standards and industry coalitions then lobby for federal preemption precisely to avoid fifty regimes; when EU member states maintain divergent data-protection or digital-market rules and those national differences become the principal justification for Brussels-level harmonization rather than for mutual recognition; and when German Länder design distinct school curricula or examination systems and federal actors cite the resulting student-mobility friction to press for national framework standards, despite education being formally reserved to the Länder. What strikes me is that the argument treats autonomous pluralism itself as a systemic malfunction and uses the fact of lower-level choice as leverage for removing it—almost as if subsidiarity is being invoked in reverse, not to defend local decision-making but to justify its preemption by framing divergence as an unavoidable externality. Is there an established term for this specific configuration—where the exercise of decentralization is reinterpreted as evidence that decentralization has reached its functional limit? If so, how exactly does it relate to competence creep, regulatory arbitrage, or race-to-the-top dynamics, and where does it sit relative to genuine coordination failures that no single lower-level actor can solve? If it lacks a precise name, what are its nearest conceptual neighbors, and what would mark its boundaries—for example, distinguishing strategic divergence intended to trigger centralization from uncoordinated local failure?
