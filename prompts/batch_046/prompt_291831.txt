I'm a retired physicist who only started taking philosphy seriously after I left the lab. I've been mulling over actual infinities and I wonder if I'm mixing categories or onto something. Here's the gist of an essay I'm sketching out.

Hilbert's hotel is always presented as a purely logical puzzle: there's always room for one more. But if you try to physically instatiate it, every shift of guests up a floor requires work — information has to be updated, positions changed, maybe signals sent. If the hotel is genuinely infinite, then accomodating a new guest requires an infinite chain of updates, hence infinite energy or time. So in any physical universe with conservation laws, the hotel can't actually operate. Cantor's diagonal argument has the same issue: it asks us to complete an infinite procedure of comparing digits, but a physical agent or computer can only run for finite time and with finite precision. Finitism, then, isn't just a philosophical bias against the actual infinite; it's an emergent constraint from the fact that representation and computation are physical processes. Mathematical infinity might still exist as an abstract object, but it can't be instantiated or realised.

Does this hold up? I'm particularly unsure whether I'm committing a category error by dragging physics into pure maths. Is it interesting, or just a tired physicalist reduction?
