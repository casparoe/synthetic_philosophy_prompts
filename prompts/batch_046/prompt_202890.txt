I have recently been asked to advise on the conceptual framing for a comparative genocide-studies curriculum, and I keep encountering the same impasse in the literature: the debate over Holocaust uniqueness versus comparability—particularly in the wake of the Historikerstreit and Bauman’s thesis that modern bureaucratic rationality made the Holocaust structurally possible rather than a premodern aberration—has not moved in decades despite extensive archival and sociological work. Both sides seem to accept the same empirical claims about bureaucratic structures, technological administration, and the role of ordinary perpetrators, yet they draw opposite conclusions about whether comparison with other state atrocities is conceptually appropriate or morally permissible. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about historical evidence but about what “modernity” is doing in the explanation: one camp treats it as a causal mechanism that can be instantiated in other contexts, the other as a moral-ontological category that renders the event singular. Is the stall actually a hidden empirical dispute about institutional forms, a difference over what counts as an adequate historical explanation, or a bedrock commitment about whether moral uniqueness is compatible with causal comparison? And if your diagnosis is correct, would distinguishing explanatory uniqueness from normative uniqueness resolve anything, or is this disagreement structurally unresolvable?
