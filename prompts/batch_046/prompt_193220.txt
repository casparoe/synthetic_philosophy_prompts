As a novelist constructing characters whose moral fates depend on the reader's sense of justice, I have been testing my own intuitions about punishment and desert against the philosophical divide between compatibilist and skeptical accounts of responsibility. Here are my verdicts. A character with a brain tumor that compels him to violence merits containment, not punitive desert. A character raised in absolute deprivation who steals to survive has diminished but not abolished desert. A wealthy character who defrauds others purely for stimulation merits full blame—perhaps heightened, precisely because his circumstances afforded him the reflective space to become someone else. Now suppose neuroscience reveals that every human choice is causally determined in exactly the same mechanistic fashion as the tumor, merely with more intermediate steps; I still want to maintain that the wealthy fraudster deserves punishment, despite lacking any robust ability to have done otherwise. Yet I also hold that a child soldier who kills with full cognitive awareness, but was systematically indoctrinated, deserves no punishment whatsoever. These judgments seem to require a principle that locates desert not in bare alternate possibilities, but in whether an action flows through an agent's reflectively endorsed evaluative structure—something like "authorship through internalized reasons"—yet this pulls me toward a compatibilist position I am unsure I hold, while my determinism intuition and my indoctrination intuition pull in opposite directions. Is there a precise principle, distinguishing causal pathways that bypass an agent's reflective capacities from those that run through them, that captures all five verdicts? If not, which verdict must I abandon? And apply your principle to a character I have not yet written: an artist raised in a culture that venerates destruction over preservation, who deliberately ruins a rival's masterpiece not from personal malice, but because her fully internalized aesthetic framework genuinely demands it.
