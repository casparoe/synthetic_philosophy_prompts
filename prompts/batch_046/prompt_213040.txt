I've never studied history formally, but I've spent years repairing mechanical watches and tending a small orchard, so I think naturally in terms of escapements, rootstock, soil fatigue, blight cycles, and the difference between a failure caused by neglect and one caused by a flaw in the parts themselves. Lately I've been reflecting on the idea that germs, long climate shifts, and volcanic winters are sometimes treated not as passive background but as forces that actively shape events — almost like characters in a story — and on the opposing worry that this kind of framing makes human choices look like nothing more than reactions to disease and weather. Please restate that entire tension in the vocabulary I actually live in: gears and springs that wear or seize, trees responding to grafting and drought, maintenance schedules that succeed or collapse, and the boundary between repairing a system and being controlled by its inherent design. For each element — attributing agency to non-human forces, the risk of reducing people to environmental effects, and the remaining space for deliberate human decision — tell me what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in the language of repair and cultivation. Finally, say whether the residues that disappear point to a genuine limitation in thinking through maintenance and growth, or whether they suggest the original historical framing was less precise than it seemed.
