Write a deadpan parody of an internal corporate policy manual—about 600 words, in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—addressed to all staff and titled in its opening line as Directive 47-B: On Institutional Utterances. Treat every sentence in the manual itself as a literal performative act that enacts organizational reality rather than describing it. Ground the satire in genuine features of speech-act theory: state explicit felicity conditions (authorized speaker, correct procedure, sincere intention, proper execution), then show failures—misfires that void the act, abuses that perform it insincerely, and perlocutionary effects listed as quarterly deliverables. Invent specific bureaucratic scenarios: an unauthorized intern attempting to declare a company holiday, a manager attempting to terminate an employee by speaking the wrong formula, a sincere apology delivered to the wrong department so that it misfires entirely. Let the distinctions among locutionary content, illocutionary force, and perlocutionary consequence govern office logistics—meetings are convened by the force of the invitation, budgets are altered by the utterance of approval, morale is tracked as a perlocutionary output—without ever breaking the deadpan bureaucratic voice. The target is the literalization of Austin and Searle's framework: a reader familiar with the literature should recognize that every absurd rule maps onto a real distinction, while a general reader should still grasp the joke from context alone. Maintain strict internal consistency; never allow a character to suggest that words are merely descriptive. End the memo with a clause that attempts to sign itself into effect but fails because the signatory has already been dismissed by an earlier misfired clause.
