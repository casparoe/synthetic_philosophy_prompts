During a seminar on historical ontology, a colleague dismissed my objection to treating 1492 as a conceptual watershed by repeating the standard narrative in one breath: "The Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution broke the long darkness of the Middle Ages to birth modernity." I want to understand exactly what claim she was defending. Please separate the precise theses this sentence could be expressing: (i) a purely organizational claim about how textbooks and curricula should be divided; (ii) a descriptive claim about abrupt, synchronized shifts in art, politics, and natural philosophy centered on 1400–1700; (iii) a normative claim that modern values represent progress over medieval ones; and (iv) an ontological claim that these labels correspond to real, unified epochs in the world rather than to retrospective constructions. For each thesis, say whether it is trivial, defensible but contested, false, or question-begging. Then apply your disambiguation to a specific conceptual tension: practices conventionally treated as distinctive of the Scientific Revolution—quantitative modeling of change, systematic empirical observation, and the sustained institutional study of nature within universities—have well-documented antecedents in medieval scholastic and Islamic Golden Age contexts that resist placement in a "pre-modern" box. Does this misfit show that the three-act narrative is merely a useful fiction, or can it be refined—for instance, by positing multiple localized renaissances and treating "the Scientific Revolution" as a gradual, geographically dispersed process—without collapsing entirely? Be precise about which of the four readings survives such a refinement and which is destroyed by it. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation; work only from the conceptual distinctions.
