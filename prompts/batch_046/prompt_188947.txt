In my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind we have been stuck for months on whether the meta-problem of consciousness is a genuine philosophical puzzle or a recurring cognitive misfire that we keep redressing in new vocabulary. One faction holds the standard view: the task is not to explain subjective experience itself, but to explain why we judge that physical processes fail to account for it — in other words, why there seems to be an explanatory gap between brain states and phenomenology. The opposing faction, which a few colleagues have started labeling the epistemic humility position, contends that the apparent gap arises not from ontology but from an asymmetry in evidential standards: we demand a kind of first-personal transparency for explanations of consciousness that we never require for other scientific domains, and this stricter norm makes reduction feel impossible regardless of whether it succeeds. The debate has stalled precisely because both sides keep framing the issue as being about the hard problem, yet one group is asking an ontological question about what exists while the other is asking a normative question about what counts as adequate explanation for introspective reports. I have begun to suspect that the disagreement may not be about consciousness at all, but rather about whether philosophical progress should aim to dissolve an intuition or merely to map the conditions under which it arises.
