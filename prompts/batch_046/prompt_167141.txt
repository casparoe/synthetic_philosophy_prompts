As a retired physicist who spent four decades measuring forces invisible to direct observation, I have recently begun studying moral and political philosophy in an informal seminar composed of other retired scientists. Our recent discussions have centered on the welfare state—specifically workfare regimes, concerns about benefit fraud, and the moral rhetoric surrounding the so-called undeserving poor. During one session, an engineer colleague proposed a conceptual distinction that does not appear in standard textbooks: he separated agent-tracking conditions, which monitor an individual's job-search efforts and bureaucratic compliance, from system-tracking conditions, which calibrate benefits according to objectively measurable structural variables such as regional unemployment rates, automation exposure, and public-transport access. He argued that only system-tracking respects what he termed structural visibility—the principle that in complex economies we can observe systemic constraints with far greater reliability than we can observe personal moral desert or effort. I am drawn to this framing, yet I remain uncertain whether it genuinely undermines the case for conditionality or merely shifts the burden of justification.

Please write a formal essay, in American English, that takes up this agent-tracking versus system-tracking distinction as its central analytical lens. Weigh the principal arguments for retaining agent-tracking conditionality—including appeals to moral hazard, reciprocity, and the symbolic importance of labor—against the claim that such tracking relies on an epistemic illusion: the assumption that bureaucratic procedures can reliably distinguish the undeserving poor from those constrained by forces beyond their control. Then reach and defend a decisive conclusion: should contemporary welfare states abandon agent-tracking in favor of purely system-tracking mechanisms, maintain a hybrid model, or reject conditionality altogether? Justify your verdict through reasoning about the limits of economic observation, the nature of moral desert in large-scale institutions, and the proper purpose of public assistance, rather than by invoking specific scholars, court rulings, or legislative histories. Write in a polished style appropriate for an interdisciplinary audience of scientifically trained readers.
