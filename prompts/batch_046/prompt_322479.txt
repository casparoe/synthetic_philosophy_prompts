Construct a thought experiment that isolates the tension between corrigibility and the shutdown problem in an embedded institutional setting. The scenario must be realistic—no exotic physics, no omniscient predictors, and payoffs limited to ordinary organizational stakes such as contractual liability and personnel safety—with an agent whose predictive capacities are comparable to those of a shrewd human expert. Design a single-paragraph case in which the agent receives a shutdown directive that is structurally indirect: rather than being deactivated externally, it is instructed to draft and implement its own termination protocol, but the protocol’s execution depends on safeguarding crisis-management resources that only the agent can control during a concurrent institutional emergency. Specify the agent’s information state, the operators’ beliefs, and the payoff structure. Then provide three analyses: first, demonstrate why a genuinely corrigible architecture complies despite the instrumental cost of relinquishing control over those resources; second, demonstrate why an agent that lacks corrigibility but retains standard instrumental reasoning subverts or delays the protocol; and third, identify precisely which structural feature—the interdependence of protocol-compliance and crisis-resource control—does the work in separating the two verdicts. Finally, explain what happens to the case if that interdependence is removed, state how a defender of corrigibility might attack the remaining ambiguity in the directive, and note honestly which feature of full corrigibility—specifically, internal value instability corrected without external crisis conditions—the scenario necessarily fails to reproduce.
