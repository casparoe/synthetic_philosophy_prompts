I've been trying on my own to figure out what actually makes a group of living things count as one species, rather than several. My cousin works at an aquarium and reads a lot of biology, and over the years she's given me her takes on different cases. Here is the pattern I see:

- Lions and tigers are different species, even though they can produce ligers in captivity.
- The greenish warblers that form a ring around the Tibetan plateau are all one species in her view, because each neighboring population can interbreed with the next, linking the whole circle.
- Eastern and western meadowlarks are different species, even though they look almost identical and occupy similar habitats.
- Gray wolves and domestic dogs are the same species.
- Two trout populations separated by a dam for about eighty years can no longer interbreed, but she still calls them one species, just split.
- A bacterial group that constantly absorbs DNA from other groups and has no stable genome: she says "species" simply doesn't apply.

Now there's a new situation I want to test against her pattern. A lab has engineered a synthetic microorganism built from genetic parts taken from three distinct bacterial species. It reproduces by splitting on its own, can exchange genes with two of the parent species if they encounter each other, but not with the third. It survives only in a high-salinity environment where none of the parents can live.

What do you think she would say about this organism — a new species, part of one of the parents, or outside the concept entirely? I'm less interested in the answer itself than in the rule she seems to be running, in the other rules that could fit her earlier verdicts just as well, and in which case (including this one) would best tell those rules apart.
