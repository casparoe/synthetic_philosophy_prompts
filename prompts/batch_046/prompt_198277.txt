As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of technology, I have been struck by what is called the responsibility gap for autonomous systems: situations in which a harmful decision emerges from algorithmic processing without any clearly identifiable human agent who can be held to account. In physics, conservation laws are not optional metaphysical additions but structural constraints—if charge or energy appears to vanish locally, we postulate an unseen field or particle to restore global balance. Applying this structural intuition to ethics, I find it difficult to accept that moral responsibility can genuinely disappear into a gap rather than being redistributed across the network. If responsibility is not a conserved quantity across human-machine interactions, then its absence at one node requires no further explanation; but if it behaves analogously to a conserved current, then an autonomous system that produces harm without a corresponding bearer of blame introduces something like a divergence in the moral field.

My question, therefore, is whether the standard philosophical response—usually framed in terms of distributed or shared accountability—implicitly treats responsibility as a non-conserved scalar that can be diluted to zero. Would it not be more coherent to model it instead as a kind of vector potential that assigns a definite, if non-agential, moral potential to the system itself, analogous to electric potential assigning energy without requiring charge to reside in empty space? Or is the fundamental error precisely this analogy—namely, that responsibility lacks the formal properties (linearity, locality, conservation) that make physical field theories applicable? I am especially interested in whether anyone has developed a formal framework—perhaps drawing on gauge-theoretic structure or information-theoretic causality—that distinguishes a true absence of responsibility from a mere transformation of its bearer, and whether such a framework could close the gap without attributing literal moral agency to machines.
