I write literary fiction, and I have agreed to lead a single three-hour session for a small group of fellow writers—novelists, poets, and editors—none of whom have studied philosophy of mind beyond casual reading, though they think constantly about narrative consciousness, unreliable memory, and the architecture of plot. The session's subject is the dispute over whether dreams are genuine experiences, specifically Norman Malcolm's argument that they are not, since there can be no criterion for identifying a dream as occurring during sleep other than the waking report itself, set against Daniel Dennett's proposal that the narrative of a dream may be assembled only at the moment of awakening rather than lived through in real time. I do not want you to adjudicate the dispute; I want you to design the pedagogical architecture for the session. What conceptual distinction—perhaps between immediate experience and retrospective reconstruction, or between physiological state and narrative content—must be in place before the disagreement can even be formulated clearly? Choose one concrete scenario to carry the entire explanation; describe precisely what it must include, such as a sleeper who reports a long, emotionally coherent narrative after only a brief arousal with no intermediate physiological markers, and what it must deliberately exclude so that it does not smuggle in either Malcolm's verificationist worry or Dennett's cassette-like assembly by accident.

Then map the misconceptions these writers will almost certainly import from their own craft: I anticipate they will confuse "not an experience" with "fictional" or "deliberately invented," or imagine Dennett is saying we compose dreams with authorial intent like plotting a short story, or conflate the temporal span of the dream report with the temporal span of sleep itself. For each misconception, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and defused. I also need one precise check—perhaps a single question I can pose near the end—to determine whether they have grasped the conceptual tension rather than merely adopted a literary preference for one side. Finally, tell me what parts of this debate—the technical literature on REM physiology, the finer points of verificationism, or the subsequent empirical literature—I should openly set aside as beyond the scope of one session, and how to frame that omission honestly without making the philosophical problem seem smaller than it is.
