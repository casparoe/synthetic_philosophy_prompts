I'm taking a course on cross-cultural moral psychology, and I've been trying to formalize a worry about whether guilt is genuinely morally superior to shame or whether that judgment is merely a Western prejudice dressed up as philosophy. I ended up constructing the following chain, but now it feels like it proves far too much. (1) Moral emotions can be distinguished by their "structure of address": guilt is autocentric—the agent addresses herself—whereas shame is allocentric—the agent is addressed by the real or imagined gaze of others. (2) If moral autonomy requires that the agent be the primary source of moral evaluation, then any emotion that makes her standing depend on an external audience subordinates autonomy and is therefore morally inferior. (3) So guilt is the better moral emotion in any culture that takes autonomy seriously. (4) But if guilt is universally better, then any culture that systematically cultivates shame—through honor-based coordination, for instance—is not merely different but is actively corrupting the moral agency of its members. (5) That conclusion seems intolerably imperial: it condemns the moral architecture of most pre-modern and many contemporary societies. (6) The only escape is to deny that autonomy is universal and treat it as a culturally specific value; but then (2) loses its normative force, and we are left with no reason to prefer guilt except local preference. (7) Alternatively, one might reject (4) by saying that cultivating shame does not necessarily corrupt autonomy, but then the distinction in (1) stops doing the work we wanted it to do. I would most like to preserve (1) and (2)—the idea that the internal structure of address has real moral weight—but I cannot see how to do so without either accepting (5) or collapsing into relativism. Which step is the weak link, and what does abandoning it cost?
