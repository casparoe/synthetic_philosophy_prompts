I'm writing an essay for my seminar on pragmatism and democratic theory, and I'd like your comments on both its coherence and whether the central distinction actually holds water. My working thesis is that Dewey's experimentalist account of democracy contains an underappreciated asymmetry between what I am calling first-order policy experiments and second-order democratic experiments. In the first case—say, testing a new vocational training program or a public-health intervention—the democratic community treats a social arrangement as a working hypothesis with relatively stable success conditions: employment rates shift, mortality curves change, and the community revises its institutional commitments in light of those results. But in the second case—say, redesigning the deliberative forum itself, altering who counts as part of the affected public, or renegotiating what kinds of reasons count as legitimate inputs—the success conditions are not given in advance. The experiment is reflexive: the inquirers are simultaneously the object of inquiry, and the metric by which they judge success is itself being reconstructed through the very process they are evaluating. I am arguing that Dewey's analogy between scientific inquiry and democratic politics tends to obscure this reflexivity, because scientific communities (on Dewey's own account) already share a provisional but functioning framework of methods and standards, whereas democratic politics is precisely the arena in which that shared framework is being forged and contested.

The essay then asks whether this asymmetry undermines Dewey's claim that democracy is best understood as continuous social experimentation, or whether it can be reframed as a feature rather than a bug—perhaps by treating the evolution of success conditions itself as the primary experimental outcome. I also try to show that if democratic inquiry lacks any external or pre-political criterion of success, then Dewey's experimentalism risks collapsing into a form of procedural self-validation in which any outcome that survives the deliberative process is automatically warranted. I am not convinced I have resolved this tension, and I worry that my distinction between first-order and second-order experiments may be sharper on paper than Dewey's actual writings allow.

Could you comment specifically on three things? First, is the distinction between these two levels of democratic experimentation coherent within Dewey's framework, or does he already have conceptual resources—perhaps in his notion of intelligence as a method rather than a content—that dissolve the asymmetry? Second, does the reflexivity problem I have identified genuinely threaten the normative force of Dewey's experimentalism, or can it be accommodated by treating the reconstruction of evaluative standards as the central democratic achievement? Third, I am particularly unsure about my claim that democratic success conditions are internal and evolving; I would appreciate suggestions on whether that part needs sharpening, qualification, or abandonment, and whether the overall argument strikes you as philosophically interesting or as an artificial imposition on Dewey's view.
