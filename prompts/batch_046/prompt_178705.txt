Hey, I spent thirty years doing field biology and only got into philosophy of mind after I retired. Last Tuesday our little discussion group got sidetracked onto heterophenomenology -- Dennet's idea about taking first-person reports seriously but treating them like fiction describing a heterophenomological world. Two regulars, Mara and Jake, went back and forth about whether this actually buys consciousness science anything, and I couldn't tell who was winning.

Here is the exchange as I remember it, though my notes are a bit messy:

1. Mara said heterophenomenology is basically redundant. Once you bracket all first-person authority and treat reports as texts, you're just doing behavioral science with extra literary steps. She said you could study the same mechanisms with cognitive ethology and skip the fictional-world framing entirely.

2. Jake said that's wrong because the framing matters methodologically: you're not just observing behavior, you're constructing a model of the subject's own report-world, which lets you map inconsistencies and patterns inside that world without claiming they mirror objective reality. He said that's a distinct research program, not a redundant one.

3. Mara pushed back: if the report-world is genuinely fictional, then contradictions inside it are just narrative errors, like a novel where a character is both six feet tall and five feet tall in different chapters. She said scientists don't study plot holes; they discard unreliable instruments. So why should inconsistencies in heterophenomenological reports be scientifically interesting?

4. Jake replied that inconsistencies are exactly where the science happens -- they show you the generating mechanisms behind the reports, like memory distortion or attentional limits. He said treating the world as fictional is what frees you to study those mechanisms neutrally, instead of arguing over whether the contradiction really happened in consciousness.

5. Then Mara brought up AI. She said Jake had mentioned extending heterophenomenology to language models, but she argued there's no heterophenomenological world there at all -- just statistical prediction. Jake said you can still treat the outputs as reports about the model's own functional state, constructing a fictional world of machine experience that you study without ontological commitment.

They kept talking but I zoned out a bit.

Can you adjudicate each of these five moves? Rate them from clearly wrong to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. And specifically, is Jake's AI extension a natural development of heterophenomenology or does it break the method? I'm not asking for citations or a reading list, just the conceptual logic.
