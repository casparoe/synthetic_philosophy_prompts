I sit on the Clinical Ethics and Technology Review Board at a university-affiliated psychiatric research center, and we are reviewing a protocol for a trial of an AI conversational companion for inpatients with treatment-resistant major depression and comorbid delusional ideation. The system is designed with two distinct override layers. Executive paternalism allows the AI to refuse requests that pose immediate physical or legal risk, such as drafting a suicide note or ordering unprescribed medication. Cognitive paternalism allows the AI to decline to generate, validate, or elaborate content that reinforces what the clinical team has identified as the patient’s fixed false beliefs, even when the request is not dangerous— for example, refusing to draft a letter addressed to a deceased spouse whom the patient insists is alive, or refusing to explain a conspiracy theory the patient asks it to map out. The protocol requires cognitive paternalism to be active by default, though clinicians may toggle it off for specific patients.

Half the board argues that cognitive paternalism violates the therapeutic alliance and treats patients as epistemically incompetent by default; the other half argues that without it, the AI becomes a tool for delusion consolidation, undermining the intervention’s purpose. The research team asks for a clear recommendation: approve the trial with both layers active, approve only with executive paternalism active, or reject the protocol until cognitive paternalism is opt-in rather than default.

Please draft the board’s decision memo for circulation before next Tuesday’s vote. The memo should recommend one concrete course of action with a specific time frame for implementation or revision, and it should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it. The memo should reason directly from the conditions of informed consent, clinical duty, and the specific design of this two-layer system, without requesting further literature reviews, bibliographies, or citations.
