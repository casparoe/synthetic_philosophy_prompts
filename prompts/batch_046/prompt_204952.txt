Defenders of the precautionary principle frequently liken it to a smoke detector installed in a large building: you calibrate the system for extreme sensitivity, accepting routine false alarms, because missing even one genuine fire is catastrophic. The comparison is used to rebut the charge that precautionary regulation—restricting technologies or chemicals before harm is conclusively demonstrated—is anti-scientific alarmism; instead, it frames such policy as ordinary, rational risk management. Audit this analogy with explicit correspondence: what in the target domain plays the role of the detector, the false alarm, the actual fire, the building's occupants, the maintenance budget, and the single authority who sets the threshold? Then identify the points of breakdown—particularly whether false alarms in democratic policymaking distribute costs, erode institutional trust, and generate political backlash in ways that false alarms in a private building do not; whether the analogy presupposes that we already know the building's floor plan (that is, the causal pathways and exposure mechanisms of the suspected harm); and whether it smuggles in a technocratic, single-decision-maker model that sits uneasily with pluralistic governance and competing values. Finally, determine whether the analogy's intended conclusion—that the burden of proof should rest on the innovator and that precaution is therefore rational—survives these disanalogies intact, or whether an alternative analogy, such as epidemiological screening or portfolio diversification under deep uncertainty, would serve the same argumentative purpose without importing the same hidden premises.
