Following a long and somewhat unresolved argument with a friend about the procreation asymmetry—the view that bringing a suffering being into existence is morally wrong, while failing to bring a happy being into existence is not wrong—I find myself wanting a poetic form that captures the structural imbalance rather than merely stating it. My friend kept returning to the intuition that absence feels different on the two sides: the absence of pain is a relief that needs no recipient, while the absence of pleasure is not a deprivation that demands redress. I want a poem that treats this not as a puzzle to be solved but as a formal feature of moral space, precise enough that someone familiar with the underlying debate recognizes it, yet readable without prior study.

Please write a poem—no fixed meter, but governed by a strict asymmetrical architecture. Use two stanzas separated by an abrupt white space with no transitional line. The first stanza should be ten lines long, with a regular rhyme scheme (ABAB CDCD EE), and should render the suffering side through embodied, named, or concrete images—perhaps congenital pain, parasitic infection, or a life that asks for mercy—so that the stanza feels structurally complete and normatively insistent. The second stanza should be exactly four lines, entirely unrhymed, with each successive line shortening in syllabic length (beginning with roughly ten syllables and ending with one), depicting the never-existing happy life—someone who might have loved music or sunlight—whose hypothetical presence dissolves as the lines contract. The formal disparity—ten rhymed, weighty lines against four unrhymed, diminishing ones—should do the philosophical work: one side generates a fully voiced obligation, the other fades into an absence that carries no counterweight.

To keep the idea precise without relying on standard textbook formulations, embed the distinction between person-affecting and impersonal reasons implicitly in the imagery rather than in explicit jargon: the subjects in the first stanza should have bodies and identities that ground a grievance, while those in the second should lack any anchoring self, so that the poem illustrates why non-existence harms no particular subject when pleasure is missing, yet can be a profound wrong when suffering is actualized. Do not invoke any philosopher by name, do not provide citations or a reading list, and do not resolve the tension in a concluding couplet or summary; let the white space between the stanzas stand for the gap itself. The result should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the asymmetry recognizes the conceptual move, but should read as a coherent poem even to someone encountering the idea for the first time.
