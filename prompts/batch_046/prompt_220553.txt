I'm drafting a short seminar essay on mental causation and want critical comments on its coherence and accuracy—especially where I may misrepresent standard positions. The sketch runs as follows. Kim's causal exclusion argument holds that if every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, and mental properties are not identical to physical properties, then mental properties are causally redundant. I challenge the uniqueness premise behind "sufficient cause" by proposing a "functional pluralism" about causation: a single token process can simultaneously instantiate distinct causal profiles—say, an energy-transfer profile and a reason-guided organizational profile—without those profiles competing as separate causes. On this view, mental properties are not epiphenomenal; they describe an organizational aspect of the very nexus that physics describes differently. My worry is that this collapses mental causation into mere redescription, leaving mental properties causally inert in any framework-independent sense, and that I may be conflating causal exclusion with explanatory exclusion. Please comment on whether this misrepresents Kim's argument, whether functional pluralism is coherent in this context, and where the sketch is weakest. Separate what is settled in the mental-causation literature from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on it, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as framework-relative or pluralist causation—as if it were the consensus view.
