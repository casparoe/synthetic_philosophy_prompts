Within theoretical discussions of symbolic causation, a long-running division separates substrate-primacy theorists—who hold that the careenium, understood as the base causal-physical layer of a cognitive system, fully determines all behavioral outputs—from advocates of simmball efficacy, who maintain that structured symbolic representations (simmballs) can exert downward organizational pressure on the careenium in ways not reducible to substrate dynamics alone. The substrate-primacy camp typically argues that every apparently symbol-driven phenomenon, from predictive-error correction to representational binding, decomposes into careenial mechanisms, whereas simmball proponents point to cases in which the relational structure of symbols constrains substrate states in patterns that only make sense if the symbols themselves are treated as causally relevant organizers. Despite repeated exchanges across philosophy of mind and cognitive architecture, the debate has not moved: the same examples recur, the same reductionist moves are countered with the same appeals to organizational autonomy, and neither side accepts the other’s criteria for what counts as explanatory success. Is this stalemate substantive—concerning real differences in how causation operates across levels—or is it verbal, rooted in competing uses of terms like 'cause' and 'substrate'? Might the sides be answering different questions, one about ontological reduction and another about explanatory indispensability, under the single heading of symbolic efficacy? Or is the impasse a sign that the framing itself is ill-posed, perhaps because 'careenium' and 'simmball' are defined within incompatible ontological commitments that prevent direct comparison?
