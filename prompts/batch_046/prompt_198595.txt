In debates over whether large language models genuinely have beliefs, philosophers often invoke Dennett’s thermostat: we say it “wants” to keep the room at 20°C not because there is inner thirst, but because treating it that way predicts its behavior, and the same heuristic, some argue, licenses attributing beliefs to an LLM if doing so predicts its outputs. Consider a twist on that picture. Imagine two architecturally identical models, one trained on an Earth-corpus in which “bank” tracks financial institutions, and one on a Twin-Earth-corpus in which “bank” tracks river edges, with the two corpora arranged so that the internal weight configurations are isomorphic and every observable token output is indistinguishable across the pair. Both systems emit “The bank is secure” in parallel contexts, yet the environmental anchor of the term differs. Audit the thermostat analogy in this setting: lay out the explicit correspondences between the feedback loop and the model’s predictive dynamics, identify where the mapping breaks—especially regarding whether belief content is fixed internally, behaviorally, or by an external training environment—say whether the analogy quietly imports an internalist assumption about mental content that the Twin-Earth split undermines, and determine whether the conclusion that AI “beliefs” are merely predictive heuristics with no further metaphysical weight survives once that disanalogy is on the table. If the analogy collapses in a way that matters, what would be a better comparison for thinking about externally anchored representational states in artificial systems?
