I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a three-day interdisciplinary workshop on deep-time ethics and stratigraphic authority, convened jointly by a philosophy department and a geology faculty. The audience is evenly divided: on one side, stratigraphers who understand the International Commission on Stratigraphy’s formal protocols, the Anthropocene Working Group’s mid-twentieth-century proposal marked by radionuclides and plastic deposits, and the competing boundary hypotheses linked to 1610 and 1784; on the other, philosophers, historians, and policy scholars who grasp that declaring an epoch carries normative weight but do not know the technical criteria for a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point. As a philosopher who works on social ontology and collective responsibility, I need a fifteen-minute address that treats the question “When did the Anthropocene start, and who has the authority to decide?” not as a preliminary to ethical analysis but as its core structural problem. To prevent this from collapsing into familiar pretraining patterns, please inject a specific, somewhat niche conceptual framing—call it stratigraphic performativity—according to which fixing an epochal boundary does not merely record a geological transition but enacts a particular distribution of historical responsibility, rendering some human impacts visible in deep time while systematically erasing others. The speech must do real rhetorical work: this framing should prevent me from hiding behind scientific neutrality to avoid moral judgment, and it should prevent me from issuing moral condemnation without accounting for the institutional authority that actually governs geological nomenclature. The specialists should recognize that their vocabulary is being used with precision, while the non-specialists should understand that the debate over plutonium versus nitrogen versus colonial-exchange markers is simultaneously a debate over whose suffering becomes geologically legible. The address should contain no bibliographic recommendations, no citation lists, and no appeals to texts the audience ought to read; it should rely solely on ideas articulable without external search. Conclude not with resolution but with a live tension—the speaker recognizing that to fix the start date is to fix a moral ledger, and that the authority to do so is distributed between scientific committees and the collective actors they seek to name. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to an academic occasion, and do not place any passage from an actual text inside quotation marks unless it has been verified against a source; paraphrase freely otherwise.
