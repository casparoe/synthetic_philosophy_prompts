I am planning a single 90-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group of graduate students in science and the humanities with no prior philosophy training. The topic is Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction is catastrophically harmful not primarily because of the deaths it involves, but because of the afterlife conjecture: that much of what gives present activities their value depends structurally on the expectation that humanity continues. I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. Specify the dependency order: which preliminary distinction—between instrumental and constitutive reliance on a collective future—must be in place before the conjecture can sound like a claim about present value rather than sentimental projection. Identify the one concrete carrying example drawn from long-term ecological restoration or intergenerational cultural preservation that should anchor the discussion, and say exactly what it must include and what it must lack—specifically, why it cannot be a merely unfinished task—to make the structural dependency visible. Map the misconceptions in the order they will likely arise—I anticipate first that this is just fear of personal death projected onto strangers, then that it assumes present people can care about individuals they will never meet—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. Provide one end-of-session diagnostic question that distinguishes someone who grasps the structural collapse of present meaning from someone who has only adopted a melancholy posture. Also specify which part of Scheffler's parallel argument about individual death should be explicitly bracketed and why, and name one related issue—such as whether the conjecture implies obligations to distant generations—that should be openly refused at this level.
