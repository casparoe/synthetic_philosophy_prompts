I am a final-year undergraduate in a seminar on comparative church-state regimes, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—approximately four minutes—at our department’s workshop, “Fiscal Secularism and Its Mechanisms.” The audience is divided: on one side are constitutional-law scholars who have worked extensively on the German Kirchensteuer and the remnants of the English parish-rate system; on the other are first-year graduate students whose exposure has been limited to the First Amendment and basic laïcité. Rather than survey the field, I want the remarks to turn on a conceptual distinction I have been developing: what I am calling “default-membership secularism” versus “authorized-establishment funding.” In the first model, the state collects religious taxes automatically and requires citizens to take formal administrative steps to exit, thereby generating a precise state registry of religious identity that appears neutral but operates as a census of belief; in the second, religious funding requires either local affirmative authorization or direct state allocation, which demands less bureaucratic knowledge of individual conviction. I want to claim that the German system exemplifies the first and produces what I term “administrative establishment,” while the American model—despite its rhetoric of separation—actually demands less state knowledge of belief, and that this reverses the usual comparative judgment. The speech must allow me to advance this claim for the experts without alienating the newcomers, and it should shape what I can say about neutrality rather than serving as ornament. Please write the remarks in no more than 500 words. Include one concrete institutional detail from Germany, one from England, and one from France or the United States, but do not name specific scholars, court decisions, or texts; refer to them only by institutional form. Conclude with a single line that frames the workshop’s purpose without resolving the tension.
