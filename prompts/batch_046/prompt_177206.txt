(2) So if h² for IQ in a sample is about 0.80, then 80 percent of the observed differences between people are statistically attributable to genetic differences, leaving 20 percent for environment. (3) That means for any individual drawn from that population, changing their environment can shift their trait value by at most 20 percent of the observed range—there's a hard structural ceiling at 1−h². (4) The same logic applies to height in well-nourished societies or to any trait with high heritability, so policy interventions are basically capped by whatever variance environment still holds. (5) But this implies that if h² ever reached 1.0, environment could do literally nothing, which is false: phenylketonuria has near-perfect heritability of the metabolic defect yet its phenotypic expression is almost entirely controlled by dietary environment. (6) Therefore the chain must be broken somewhere, though my friend insists PKU is just a weird outlier and the ceiling in (4) still holds generally.
