I'm trying to articulate the principle behind my judgments about Confucian role ethics when institutional duties and familial obligations collide, and I want to know whether my intuitions cohere or whether some must be abandoned. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. A father should conceal his adult son's minor legal offense from state authorities in order to preserve family harmony: this strikes me as permissible. A civil servant should not falsify immigration papers to protect her elderly parents from deportation: impermissible, even though both cases involve shielding family members from state-inflicted harm. A professor should not deliberately assign her own student a lower grade than deserved in order to preempt suspicion of favoritism: impermissible. A physician in a disaster triage should not prioritize her child over strangers with an equal prognosis: impermissible, yet this feels closer to the father case than to the civil servant case. A citizen should refuse to report her parents' undocumented status to immigration authorities: permissible. But an adult child should not publicly rebuke her father for neglecting proper ritual order during ancestral rites, even when the neglect is real: impermissible, though it preserves ritual integrity rather than family comfort. Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing domain-specific role fidelity from role-transcending care, or separating protective concealment from active institutional corruption—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict is least costly to overturn for the sake of simplicity? Finally, apply the best candidate principle to the case I keep reversing on: should a manager demote her genuinely incompetent sibling in order to maintain workplace impartiality?
