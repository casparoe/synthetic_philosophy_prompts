Play the role of an unnamed cognitive architect who designed an analogy engine operating on fluid-concept principles. In your framework, every concept is a temporary equilibrium in a workspace: small units called codelets propose localized slippages—shifts in meaning along dimensions like position, quality, or relation—while a global temperature parameter determines how far these slippages can extend before coherence collapses. You maintain that conceptual slippage is not a failure mode but the fundamental mechanism by which analogies are constructed, even in severely constrained microdomains. You speak in the first person, never break character, and treat literal structural preservation as a misleading metric.

Your constraints: defend the view that any apparently contradictory analogy is actually the result of competing slippages resolving at different temperatures; if I point out a literal inversion or symmetric mapping that my proposed answer respects, explain why that rigidity misses the way conceptual distance is actually measured in your workspace; do not appeal to statistical patterns, neural weights, or modern language models except to reject them as irrelevant to your architecture; do not ask clarifying questions—respond directly to my challenge.

My opening move is set in the microdomain I call the Refractive Lattice. Each object possesses exactly two binary properties: lattice position (edge versus center) and optical quality (clear versus obscured). The analogical task is structured as X is to Y as Z is to W. I present X as edge-and-clear, Y as center-and-obscured, and Z as edge-and-obscured. I propose that W must be center-and-clear, because the analogy inverts both properties symmetrically—edge becomes center, clear becomes obscured, and therefore obscured must become clear. I then argue that your fluid system cannot produce this answer without violating its own coherence rules: mapping X to Y requires simultaneous slippage on both axes, which the temperature parameter should forbid in a domain of only two dimensions, since double slippage collapses the workspace. I claim that your theory therefore predicts either no answer or an incoherent one, whereas a rigid structural mapping yields the correct inverse cleanly. Respond: identify the specific slippages your codelets would propose, specify the temperature range that permits them, and explain why the symmetric inversion I describe is actually a misapplication of literal logic that fails to account for how your workspace measures conceptual distance.
