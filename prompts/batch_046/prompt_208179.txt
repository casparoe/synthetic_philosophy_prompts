Philosophers of mind have been arguing since Malcolm and Dennett whether dreams are experiences, and despite decades of refinement—plus sleep science that can distinguish REM-stage phenomenology from post-awakening narrative consolidation—the dispute feels frozen. The standard moves are by now canonical: Malcolm presses a verificationist worry that sleeping subjects lack the wakeful criteria needed to ascribe mental states; Dennett responds that the waking report is itself a reconstruction, leaving no independent access to a "pure" sleeping experience. More recently, researchers have trained lucid dreamers to send predetermined eye-movement signals from within REM sleep, and some neuroscientists propose direct, report-independent neural markers for conscious states, yet these empirical developments have not dislodged either philosophical camp. I suspect the stall is not about missing data but about a structural misalignment: Malcolm seems to be asking an epistemic question about what authority a subject can have over her own mental states without wakeful verification conditions, while Dennett seems to be asking a metaphysical-causal question about whether a morning narrative tracks prior neural events. Both call the target "experience," but one may mean phenomenal presence and the other accessible memory, with neither side recognizing that they are answering different questions under one heading. Is this diagnosis right—specifically, is it a verbal dispute over "experience," a substantive disagreement about first-person authority, or a bedrock clash of intuitions about whether consciousness requires temporal continuity of a self-model? And given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress? Would a formal distinction between phenomenal occurrence and retrospective attribution dissolve the impasse, or is there a hidden empirical question—say, about whether direct neural markers can be interpreted without behavioral reports—that could move one side? Finally, if the disagreement is indeed structural rather than factual, is there any reason to expect it to resolve, or should we treat it as a permanent crux that tells us more about our concepts of subjectivity than about sleep?
