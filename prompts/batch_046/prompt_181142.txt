I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind working on interface metaphors for consciousness, and I have constructed an argument that seems to show the user-illusion view undermines its own rational acceptance. On the predictive-dashboard model I am examining—where conscious mental states are high-level representational formats that integrate lower-level neural computations but include no downward causal pathways back into the subsystems they summarize—the following reasoning appears valid. (1) If a mental state lacks downward causal pathways to the neural processes that realize it, then that state is causally inert not merely with respect to those subsystems, but with respect to behavior generally. (2) Therefore conscious beliefs, pains, and intentions are causally inert. (3) If a mental state is causally inert, then it cannot cause speech acts or transitions in reasoning. (4) So the conscious belief that consciousness is a user illusion cannot cause anyone to accept or defend that theory. (5) But rational acceptance of a theory requires that one's belief in it causally contribute to the reasoning that produces acceptance; otherwise acceptance is only an unconscious neural byproduct with no rational standing. (6) Hence, if the predictive-dashboard model is true, there is no rational basis for accepting it—any apparent acceptance is driven solely by unconscious dynamics. (7) A theory that eliminates the rational basis for its own acceptance is self-undermining. (8) Therefore either the predictive-dashboard model is false, or rational belief-formation is impossible. I would prefer to keep (1) intact, since abandoning downward-causal closure seems to compromise the core architectural claim of the interface metaphor. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If the flaw is in (1), does that force me to allow some form of mental causation I had hoped to avoid? If it lies elsewhere, what picture of rational acceptance survives?
