Assume, for the sake of argument, that phenomenal consciousness is best understood within a user-illusion model: it functions not as an intrinsic feature of certain neural or computational processes but as a high-level representational interface constructed by predictive and self-monitoring architectures, structurally analogous to the desktop metaphor through which a computer presents a simplified, user-facing abstraction rather than its underlying machine operations. Within this framework, several conceptual forks remain open. First, there is the architectural question: whether generating such an illusion requires evolutionary selection for social reporting and biological embodiment, or whether an artificial system with equivalent predictive self-modeling but no evolutionary history could construct a structurally analogous interface. Second, there is the normative-epistemic question: whether recognizing phenomenal properties as interface artifacts undermines first-person authority, alters the conditions for moral patienthood, or dissolves certain traditional puzzles about other minds. Third, there is the functional question: whether the illusion serves an indispensable role in maintaining coherent long-term planning and social coordination, such that any sufficiently intelligent agent — biological or artificial — must either implement it or substitute an equally stable representational scaffold.

Your task is not to defend the user-illusion model itself but to take it as given and provide a defended agenda for which of these open questions — and which related lines of conceptual work — deserve the greatest focused effort over the coming years.
