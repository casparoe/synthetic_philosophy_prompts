I am grading take-home exams for an advanced undergraduate seminar on Indian epistemology, and our unit on Jain philosophy has focused on keeping metaphysical descriptions of the object strictly separated from the linguistic and epistemic strategies used to articulate them. The students had spent two weeks on the jīva’s simultaneous permanence and modal change, the logic of naya, and the internal distinction between false and correct standpoints, but we did not cover comparative taxonomy with Western pluralism. Question 4 asked: Explain the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda and its relationship to syādvāda, specifically addressing how the tradition resolves the apparent contradiction that the jīva is both permanent (nitya) and subject to change (parivartana) through karma. In your answer, (a) distinguish whether anekāntavāda concerns the object itself or the knowing subject, and whether syādvāda functions at the same level; (b) use dravyārthika-naya and paryāyārthika-naya to show how the contradiction is dissolved rather than merely tolerated; (c) discuss whether an omniscient being (kevalin) remains bound by the seven-fold schema (saptabhaṅgī) and why; and (d) briefly evaluate whether anekāntavāda is better read as an ontological pluralism or as an epistemic limitation thesis, referencing the distinction between durnaya and samyag-naya.

The student answer reads as follows. Anekāntavāda holds that every object possesses infinite attributes, so reality is inherently multi-faceted. Syādvāda is the practical expression of this: since no finite knower can grasp all sides at once, every judgment must be prefixed with conditional qualification to mark it as partial. The jīva illustrates this well. From the substantial standpoint (dravyārthika-naya) the soul is eternal and unchanging in essence; from the modal standpoint (paryāyārthika-naya) it is constantly modified as karma attaches and falls away. These descriptions seem contradictory, but anekāntavāda resolves this by saying both are true of the same object from different angles—there is no single correct view that cancels the other. The seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī) formalizes this by allowing seven combinations of affirmation and denial for any predicate, which means contradictory claims can coexist without violating non-contradiction, provided each is tied to a specific naya. Even an omniscient being must use this framework, because the object’s infinite complexity means no single unqualified assertion can ever be complete, regardless of who makes it. This suggests anekāntavāda is really an ontological pluralism: the world actually contains contradictory properties, and knowledge merely tracks different slices. As for false standpoints (durnaya), they occur when someone mistakes a partial perspective for the whole truth, like claiming the soul is only eternal and never changes. A right standpoint (samyag-naya) avoids this by acknowledging its own partiality. So syādvāda and anekāntavāda work together—one describes the world, the other regulates speech about it.
