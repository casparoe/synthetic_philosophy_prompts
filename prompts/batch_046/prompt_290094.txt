At a seminar on value theory, two graduate students debated whether G. E. Moore's doctrine of organic unities, originally developed for aesthetic value, can coherently be extended to friendship without yielding morally repugnant results.

(1) Alex argued that a friendship is precisely the kind of whole Moore describes: the intrinsic value of a friendship is not reducible to the sum of the intrinsic values of the two friends taken separately plus their interaction effects. Just as a beautiful painting's being appreciated by a suitable mind forms a valuable whole greater than the value of the painting plus the value of the mental state in isolation, so too two friends' mutual affection and shared history form an organic unity whose value can exceed the moral worth of either friend considered alone. Therefore, Alex concluded, Moore's framework allows that a friendship involving a deeply flawed person might still be intrinsically excellent if the relational structure is rich and harmonious.

(2) Jordan objected that the analogy fails structurally. In aesthetic appreciation, the appreciator and the object are ontologically distinct and neither is a moral agent within the whole; in friendship, both parties are constitutive parts of the whole and remain full moral agents. To treat the friendship as an organic unity whose value can outweigh the moral defects of its parts is, Jordan claimed, to commit a category mistake that risks instrumentalizing persons—treating them as value-bearing components rather than as ends in themselves.

(3) Alex responded that Jordan smuggles in Kantian premises that Moore explicitly rejects. Moore's ethics is non-naturalist and teleological, not deontological; for Moore, the only intrinsic goods are certain mental states and their appropriate objects, arranged in organic unities. There is no Moorean prohibition on a part's value being subsumed into a greater whole, provided the part retains its own value where appropriate. Alex added that if Jordan's objection were sound, it would undermine not just the friendship case but Moore's entire aesthetic theory, since the appreciator in aesthetic experience is also a person.

(4) Jordan countered that even within Moore's own framework, there is a distinction between parts that are themselves bearers of intrinsic value and parts that are merely instrumental conditions. The friends, Jordan said, are not like brushstrokes or color-patches in a painting; they are beings with intrinsic worth. So applying organic unity to friendship does not merely analogize aesthetic appreciation—it collapses the distinction between relational goods and the goods of the individuals who constitute them. Jordan concluded that Moore should either restrict organic unities to non-agential wholes or abandon the claim that friendship's value is non-additive.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall about whether Moore's doctrine can be extended to friendship, and identify the specific conceptual crux—whether about the ontology of parts, the scope of organic unity, or the compatibility of Moorean value theory with respect for persons—on which the dispute ultimately turns.
