You chair the acquisitions committee of a municipally funded contemporary art museum. Every month you must vote yes or no on works proposed for the permanent collection and file a one-page public rationale that may not cite market price, artist reputation, or institutional prestige. Your board includes a lawyer, an educator, and a retired painter; none are professional art historians or philosophers. You want to replace personal preference with defensible judgment, but you also reject the claim that aesthetic value is purely subjective. Design a reusable decision procedure—executable in about forty-five minutes per proposal—grounded in Hume’s account of taste: the distinction between sentiment and judgment, the conditions that qualify a critic (practice, comparison, delicacy of imagination, freedom from prejudice), the idea that internal coherence and proportion matter, and the claim that some disagreements reveal a defect in the observer rather than in the work.

Structure the procedure as ordered steps with explicit markers (pass / high-medium-low confidence / stop for human judgment). For each step specify: what information is needed, how a non-specialist can verify it without outside consultation, and the exact point at which aesthetic evaluation ends and a value judgment (moral, political, representational) takes over. Include a small set of six test questions that an editor of the written rationale can apply to detect when the writer has slipped from judgment back into bare preference.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:
A. A large-scale interactive digital installation that requires viewers to navigate a simulated refugee camp; prominent critics split between calling it emotionally manipulative and calling it profoundly moving. The board expects rejection because of the political content and anticipates public controversy.
B. A hyper-realistic bronze sculpture of an ordinary garbage bag, technically flawless in proportion and surface, that evokes neither pleasure nor displeasure in most viewers and has generated almost no critical discussion.
C. A series of images produced by a generative model and then manually altered by a human artist; several well-known critics deny the series can be beautiful because they claim it lacks the kind of intentional authorship that aesthetic judgment presupposes.

For each case, show the step-by-step result, flagging where the procedure produces an answer the committee will not like. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot settle—particularly regarding moral offense, demands for cultural representation, and works that require specialized disciplinary background to understand their aims—and identify the conditions under which it will systematically misfire, such as when a work’s intended sentiment is itself contested. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
