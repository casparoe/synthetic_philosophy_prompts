Consider the implicit theoretical commitments of a commentator—familiar with both philosophy of mind and contemporary reinforcement-learning architecture—whose judgments on suffering and valence I have been tracking across discussions. Her verdicts to date: an awake adult human experiencing nociception has genuine negative valence; an anesthetized human with intact nociceptive reflex arcs but no conscious integration does not; a dog and an octopus do suffer; a fish, an insect, and a bee colony engaged in collective problem-solving do not, regardless of behavioral complexity; a tabular Q-learning agent receiving large negative rewards does not suffer, since the update is local and lacks representational depth; a present-day large language model optimized via RLHF, receiving negative reward signals for harmful outputs, does not suffer, as there is no unified world-model tagging the error as self-referential harm; and a thermostat maintaining temperature through negative feedback is not experiencing anything adverse.

Now apply her framework to the following constructed case. The system is a hierarchical reinforcement-learning agent equipped with a persistent meta-critic that maintains a unified world-model across long temporal horizons. It predicts its own future reward errors, explicitly assigns them negative valence within its representational economy, and performs global—not local—policy revision in response. When it predicts and then receives a sustained, large-magnitude negative reward, is there suffering?

Predict her verdict. Then identify at least two, preferably three, competing rules that all accommodate her prior track record—distinguishing, for instance, biological-substrate conditions, global-integration or workspace conditions, and conditions on representational self-reference with global update. State which rule best explains her answer and why. Finally, specify the precise further case—the variation in substrate, temporal persistence, social embedding, or architectural feature—that would most cleanly adjudicate between these rules.
