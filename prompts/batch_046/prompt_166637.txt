I am working through the metaphysics of consciousness in split-brain cases, and I want to stress-test a specific position I have been developing. I hold that the corpus callosum should not be understood as a passive information conduit but as the physical substrate of an interhemispheric predictive-coding loop that is necessary and sufficient for the diachronic and synchronic unity of consciousness. On this view, complete commissurotomy does not merely divide access within a single persisting subject; it dissolves the loop that constituted the federation, leaving two distinct conscious subjects. Crucially, I deny that the pre-operative patient was ever a metaphysically unified substance: the apparent unity was an emergent, functional achievement of that loop, not an underlying substantial identity. This is meant to navigate between the standard "one divided subject" account and the naive "two subjects all along" thesis, since the federation was ontologically real but not substantial. What would count against this view? I want the empirical observations, conceptual distinctions, and formal arguments that should move me, ranked by how decisive they would be. In particular: are there documented cases of partial callosal damage in which unity is preserved despite disruption of interhemispheric predictive signaling? Could predictive-coding mechanisms be localized subcortically in a way that renders the callosal loop redundant? Has my position been insulated into unfalsifiability by allowing federation to be realized at multiple scales, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical vice? I also want an honest assessment of what defenders of global-workspace theories of unity would themselves concede as damaging, not merely what critics allege. Finally, distinguish evidence that would be merely awkward—forcing a qualification—from evidence that would be fatal, and say whether the view risks collapsing into either the single-subject or the dual-subject rival if its central mechanism is abandoned.
