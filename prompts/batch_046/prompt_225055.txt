I am a documentary editor who recently completed a project in which I reconstructed a narrative sequence by inserting B-roll between two separately filmed events; the audience experienced a seamless transition that never occurred in any continuous reality. That experience sent me back to a debate about the color phi phenomenon—specifically the chromatic variant in which a red dot followed by a green dot at a different location produces the conscious impression of a single dot moving and changing color in between. In popular explanations, this is analogized to film editing: sensory input is discrete “footage,” conscious experience is the “final cut,” and the interpolated color-motion is like an inserted transition frame. The analogy is often used to license a relaxed conclusion: since film audiences do not demand that every transition frame existed in the raw dailies, we should not treat the brain’s interpolated phi-content as metaphysically suspicious, whether it is inserted retrospectively in an Orwellian fashion (rewriting the past record) or prospectively in a Stalinesque fashion (directing the live exhibition).

Audit this analogy precisely. Second, identify where the mapping fractures—particularly with respect to temporal access (whether the subject ever has access to the “dailies”), the presence of an external director or editor, and whether consciousness involves a representational product consumed by a separate viewer rather than an unmediated process. Third, examine whether the analogy quietly imports a homunculus (an internal audience watching the final cut) or a false separation between an acquisition timeline and an exhibition timeline that conscious experience does not actually exhibit. Fourth, determine whether the conclusion that interpolated experience is “just editing” and therefore unproblematic survives these disanalogies, or whether it depends on treating consciousness as a finished film rather than as an ongoing temporal flow. Fifth, if important dimensions of the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction collapse under this framing, propose a more apt analogy—perhaps drawn from live broadcasting, architectural construction, or musical performance—and explain why it handles the revision distinction more cleanly without smuggling in a separate consumer of experience.
