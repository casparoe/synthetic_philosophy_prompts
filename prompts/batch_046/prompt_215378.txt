Write a deadpan parody of a corporate terms-of-service agreement—approximately 650 words, formatted as numbered clauses with lettered sub-clauses—issued by a fictional metaphysical-insurance firm named “Infinitum Actuarial.” The agreement offers policyholders salvation-coverage contracts structured around the decision-theoretic logic of Pascal’s wager. The satire should exaggerate real structural features of the wager: the asymmetry between infinite and finite payoffs, the treatment of belief-formation as a voluntary wager rather than an evidential response, the “many-gods” problem concealed inside exclusion riders, and the implicit cost-accounting of religious practice. To prevent the task from being answerable by mere regurgitation, embed the following invented niche framework: the firm calculates premiums using a proprietary metric called “epistemic risk amortization,” which assigns fractional belief-costs across competing infinite-reward hypotheses according to their “prior exclusion density” (define this invented term within the clause text as the ratio of mutually exclusive salvation conditions to the total hypothesis space weighted by infinite utility). The voice must remain strictly legal-corporate—no winking, no direct mockery—so that absurdity emerges solely from the internal consistency of applying contract law to salvation economics. Include at least one clause addressing what happens when a policyholder attempts to “hedge” by subscribing to multiple, mutually exclusive infinite-reward plans, and one clause specifying the refund policy for cases in which a policyholder’s belief-state is adjudicated to have been “manufactured” solely for premium reduction rather than arising from genuine doxastic commitment. The target is the wager’s logic and the practice of treating belief as a strategic investment; do not name, depict, or mock any living person.
