You are a philosopher of ethics consulting for public institutions on memory policy. Think carefully about which conceptual distinctions must survive simplification before you begin operationalizing anything. A municipal Memory Review Board meets monthly with thirty minutes per case, no philosophical staff, and must issue administratively defensible written decisions on whether to maintain, alter, retire, or reject public commemorations—street names, plaques, archived exhibits—tied to past injustice or collective trauma. Turn the tension between Margalit’s ethics of memory—the claim that societies hold special obligations to preserve the memory of victims of radical evil, especially while survivors or close kin live—into a reusable decision procedure for Rieff’s opposing case for strategic forgetting—the claim that enforced remembrance can imprison communities in perpetual grievance and that democratic functioning sometimes requires mnemonic closure.

Begin with brief brainstorming: identify the three distinctions that must remain intact in any usable version—direct mnemonic debt versus inherited mnemonic claim; commemorative saturation versus commemorative absence; victim-centered versus community-centered harm—then present the procedure as an ordered checklist or word-flowchart. For each step, specify the test the chair should run, the evidence required within the thirty-minute window, who must sign off, and the precise point where philosophical judgment cannot be replaced by a rule. Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a plaque honoring a local figure now revealed to have enforced a discriminatory policy, with survivors’ descendants still resident in the district; (b) a colonial-era street name that causes present civic exclusion, where no direct victim families remain; and (c) a request to de-accession an archived trauma exhibit because of “commemorative fatigue” among younger residents—this is the case where the procedure should yield an answer the board will not want, resisting retirement despite pressure. For each case, show the procedure’s verdict, the draft justification the chair can write, and the residual judgment call that remains unavoidable.

Conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot do—resolve conflicts between competing victim groups, determine when a society has “remembered enough,” or substitute for democratic deliberation about collective identity—and explain why these are structural limits rather than problems that more information or finer rules could fix. Keep the deliverable focused: the chair needs something she can apply next Monday, not a treatise.
