As a novelist who habitually revises protagonists across radically different drafts and parallel narrative timelines, I am drawn into a metaphysical dispute that bears directly on craft. One position—counterpart theory—holds that the hero of Draft B is not literally identical to the hero of Draft A, but rather a counterpart linked by narrative resemblance and functional continuity; the opposing view—transworld identity—maintains that one and the same individual persists across storylines, bearing different properties in each possible version. Please describe the most important implications of this disagreement for the practice of fiction. In particular, address how an author should conceive moral responsibility for characters across alternate endings, the coherence of sequel planning when a protagonist perishes in one timeline but survives in another, and the emotional authenticity readers experience upon recognizing an enduring self within a counterfactual incarnation. Treat the revision process itself as a modal space in which each draft constitutes a possible world, and examine whether adopting counterpart theory entails that destroying a character in Draft B annihilates no genuinely real being, whereas transworld identity might imply that such an act genuinely harms the single self that also lives in Draft A. Keep the analysis conceptual rather than purely literary-critical, and do not request citations, bibliographies, or references to specific scholarly texts; I seek reasoning that can stand on its own for a writer without library access.
