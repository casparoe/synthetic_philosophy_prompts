Here are my verdicts on what counts as a silence or an exercise of power in an archive, and I want to know whether one principle captures all of them or whether some verdict must be overturned.

Case A: A colonial land registry from the 1920s records individual freehold titles with survey-grade precision but contains no field for customary communal tenure; the customary system continues in practice but is legally invisible. My verdict: this is not mere omission but structural categorical exclusion—the archive actively produces silence by defining what can be registered.

Case B: After a conflict, a community maintains an oral history archive but elders enforce a binding rule never to speak the names of villagers who betrayed the community to militias; outsiders who interview them report that "there is no record of collaboration." My verdict: the silence is an active ethical boundary and a form of archival agency, not an absence of memory; treating it as a gap to be filled by researchers is itself a violation.

Case C: A national library digitizes every newspaper from 1900 to 1950 with perfect fidelity, but the OCR, metadata, and search interface were built by a private contractor using proprietary tagging; scholars without the contractor's access key can search only by date, not by topic, so they literally cannot discover what is preserved. My verdict: this is silencing through retrieval infrastructure—total preservation coexists with effective invisibility, and the power lies in the architecture of access, not in destruction or exclusion of content.

Case D: An ethnographic museum displays ritual objects acquired during a 1930s expedition with complete scientific provenance—collector, date, material analysis—but the wall text describes only aesthetic form and ritual function in the abstract, never mentioning the coercive conditions of acquisition or that the objects were removed from active ceremonial use. My verdict: the archive is complete in one dimension and radically silent in another; the silence operates through curatorial framing rather than missing records.

Case E: A historian writing a social history of a textile mill uses only the factory owner's correspondence and official reports, deliberately excluding workers' private letters that she has read and catalogued in a separate deposit, on the grounds that they are "too emotional" to be reliable economic evidence. My verdict: this is a deliberate epistemic exclusion that creates silence, but it differs from Case A because the excluded material exists elsewhere and the exclusion is a judgment about evidentiary weight, not a structural impossibility of registration.

My further intuitions: Cases A and D both involve framing that hides violence, yet A seems systemic and D seems presentational. Cases B and E both involve deliberate exclusion, yet B feels like protection and E feels like distortion. Case C seems unique because nothing is hidden or destroyed—everything is there but unreachable.

Is there one precise principle—perhaps distinguishing structural, ethical, infrastructural, curatorial, and epistemic modalities of archival power—that captures why all five are silences without collapsing their differences? Or does accommodating all five require a principle so complex that I should overturn one verdict—perhaps treating B as non-archival refusal rather than silence, or treating E as legitimate methodological choice rather than power? State the principle, say which clause handles which case, predict what it says about a new situation I have not listed—specifically, a case in which an AI training dataset scrapes all available public text from a language community but applies "quality" weights that systematically downrank oral-style narratives, leaving them technically present in the corpus but never surfaced in model outputs—and be honest about which of my five verdicts the principle cannot save.
