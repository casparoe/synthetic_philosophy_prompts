I have been trying to work out the precise relationship between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation, and I believe I have identified a tension that the standard literature glosses over. As I understand Woodward's account, a causal claim is grounded in an invariant relationship that holds when a variable is subjected to an actual intervention by an external agent, which means that the causal structure is only genuinely revealed in contexts where physical manipulation is feasible—controlled experiments, engineered systems, or clinical settings. Lewis-style counterfactual theories, by contrast, seem purpose-built for observational and historical cases precisely because they do not require anyone to actually do anything; they only ask what would have occurred had the cause been absent. From this, it seems to me that interventionism is not a general theory of causation at all, but rather a specialized epistemology of the laboratory, and that whenever we apply it outside experimental contexts—say, to ask whether the 1918 influenza pandemic caused a sustained drop in urban birth rates in a particular city—we are smuggling in counterfactual reasoning about what those rates would have been without the pandemic. So my question is this: if interventionism is genuinely independent of counterfactual dependence, as its defenders claim, then how can it avoid the conclusion that causal knowledge is impossible in history, epidemiology, or any field where we cannot literally manipulate the past? And if, on the other hand, it quietly relies on hypothetical or modeled interventions—what Pearl formalizes with the do-operator—then is it not merely a notational variant of counterfactual analysis that conceals its metaphysical commitments behind talk of exogenous variables and modularity?
