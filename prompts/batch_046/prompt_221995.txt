If one adopts the distinction—sketched in recent informal discussions of collective agency rather than in canonical literature—between linearly aggregative harms, in which each individual contribution adds a discrete marginal quantum to a continuous damage function, and configuratively collective harms, in which the outcome depends on a non-additive pattern of participation such that no single act possesses independent causal weight, then the familiar probabilistic reply to the inefficacy objection applies with full force only to the first category. Anthropogenic climate damage and, on one analysis, industrial meat consumption appear aggregative; democratic voting in large electorates, by contrast, appears configurative, since an individual ballot alters the result only in conditions of near-tie. Given this taxonomy, does the standard moral defense of voting—which imports expected-utility reasoning developed for climate ethics—commit a category error by treating a configurative case as if it were aggregative? Or does the distinction collapse upon closer inspection, because even threshold-governed outcomes retain well-defined marginal expected values?
