I have no formal philosophy training, but I have been working through some popular accounts of the Buddhist teaching of anattā — that there is no permanent self, only the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness) arising and ceasing in dependence on conditions. Separately, I have encountered the Western philosophical idea that personal identity over time consists in psychological continuity — a smooth, connected chain of memories, intentions, and mental states linking a past stage to a present one. I would like you to restate that psychological-continuity view of personal identity using only the vocabulary of the aggregates and dependent origination; do not import terms like "numerical identity," "substance," or "psychological continuity" into the restatement. Once you have done that, provide a residue report: indicate precisely which features of the psychological-continuity account translate cleanly into the language of aggregates and causal conditions, which features require distortion or omission to be expressed that way, and whether any significant problem in the philosophers' debate — such as what happens when mental life seems to branch or split — lacks any genuine counterpart in the Buddhist framework. I am especially interested in whether the Buddhist vocabulary genuinely dissolves these puzzles or merely obscures them by leaving out the machinery that makes the puzzles visible.
