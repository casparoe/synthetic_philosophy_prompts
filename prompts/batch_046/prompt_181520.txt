You are an expert in Marxist political economy and in the design of philosophical classroom simulations. Before you design anything, take a moment to brainstorm how a physical token economy can represent Marx's distinction between formal subsumption, real subsumption, and the expulsion of living labor without reducing the idea to a simple board game. I am running a 90-minute graduate seminar on Marx's theory of capital with 12 participants who have already read selections from Capital, Volume I, specifically the chapters on machinery and large-scale industry, and the corresponding section from the Grundrisse on machinery and automation. I want them to feel, rather than merely discuss, the structural contradiction Marx describes between automation as a vehicle for relative surplus value and automation as a tendency to expel living labor—the source of value—thereby generating what later interpreters call a valorization crisis. To keep the exercise from feeling like a standard review, inject a niche framing: treat modern algorithmic management—predictive scheduling, real-time performance metrics, and dynamic task allocation—as a contemporary form of what Marx calls real subsumption of labor under capital, where capital no longer merely appropriates the results of labor but actively reshapes the labor process itself through data-driven feedback loops.

Design a runnable in-class simulation, complete with assigned roles, materials, round-based rules, and a payoff scheme using only point cards or colored tokens with no real money involved. The simulation should have three rounds: first, formal subsumption, where workers sell labor-power for a fixed working day and capitalists extract absolute surplus value by extending hours; second, mechanization, where capitalists can invest in machinery cards that reduce necessary labor time per unit but require capital outlay, generating relative surplus value; third, algorithmic real subsumption, where a new role—the algorithmic supervisor—uses data from prior rounds to dynamically pace and allocate tasks, deskilling remaining workers and expelling others into a reserve pool. The payoff rules must be arranged so that capitalists initially celebrate automation, then face a hidden crisis mechanism: once too many workers are expelled or deskilled, total consumptive demand collapses, and capitalists discover they cannot realize the surplus value they have produced, while workers experience first intensified exploitation and then redundancy. Participants should run into this contradiction through their own strategic choices before any debrief names it.

Provide a debrief that maps each round onto Marx's conceptual distinctions—formal versus real subsumption, absolute versus relative surplus value, the reserve army, and the tendency of the rate of profit to fall—without naming these terms until the mapping occurs. Also include an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run this, such as capitalists refusing to invest in machinery because the payoff structure is opaque, or participants missing the demand-crisis link, along with a concrete fix I can apply in the moment. Finally, be explicit about which feature of Marx's analysis—perhaps the specific quantitative law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, or the historical specificity of nineteenth-century factory conditions—the activity cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally after the simulation.
