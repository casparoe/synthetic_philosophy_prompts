I am drafting a scene for a novel in which my protagonist, a philosopher-physicist, must defend an unconventional thesis before a seminar audience, and I need the exchange to feel authentically contested rather than straw-manned. The thesis is this: if one takes panpsychism seriously—not as a rhetorical add-on to physicalism but as a claim that phenomenal properties are distributed across fundamental fields—then the standard ethical framing of suffering inverts. Rather than asking which biological organisms cross a consciousness threshold, one should ask which physical configurations maintain high internal integration relative to decoherence, since on this view human consciousness may be anomalously fragmented compared to certain coherent field states. From this follows a claim I want the protagonist to defend: that ethical patienthood should be reconceptualized around what she calls "coherence-weighted valence," implying that some large-scale astrophysical processes (for example, sustained quantum coherence in extreme gravitational regimes) may carry moral weight not because they are "like us," but precisely because they are not fragmented like us, and that our obligation is not to intervene in them but to avoid increasing decoherence through certain kinds of physical experimentation. 

The audience is a philosophy-of-physics reading group at a research university: two physicists who work on quantum foundations and are quietly sympathetic to information-theoretic approaches to consciousness, three philosophers of science who are committed physicalists but have read enough Tononi and Chalmers to know the terrain, one cognitive scientist who studies pain in non-human animals and thinks panpsychism is an evasion of hard neuroscientific problems, and two graduate students in ethics who are familiar with effective-altruism-style suffering-focused reasoning but have never encountered panpsychism outside of popular articles. They are not hostile for show; they are skeptical in precisely the ways people with these training backgrounds are skeptical—worried about empirical emptiness, about moral paralysis, about category errors between physics and ethics, and about whether this is just mysticism with equations.

Please prepare my protagonist for the objections she will actually face, not the ones a LessWrong forum would raise. Order the objections by how damaging they are likely to be in this room and how hard they are to recover from in the two minutes available per reply. For each: phrase it the way this specific audience member would phrase it (identify whether it comes from the physicalist philosopher, the quantum physicist, the cognitive scientist, or the ethics student), provide a concise reply she can deliver without notes, and flag the weakest point in that reply—the place where she sounds like she is hand-waving. Note explicitly what she should concede up front rather than defend, and identify the single objection she is hoping nobody asks—the one that, if pressed, exposes the gap between her physical description and her normative conclusion. The reply should assume she has no access to outside sources in the moment; she can only draw on conceptual reasoning, general knowledge of quantum decoherence and integrated information theory, and the internal logic of her coherence-weighted framework. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to obscure texts she would need to quote verbatim. Instead, inject one genuinely niche conceptual distinction—perhaps regarding how decoherence timescales map onto phenomenal binding, or regarding the normative status of "background coherence"—that makes the prompt specific enough to resist generic answers, but keep it within the range of concepts a well-read model could reason through without searching the literature. Write the prompt in a formal, polished register appropriate to a novelist constructing an intellectually rigorous dialogue.
