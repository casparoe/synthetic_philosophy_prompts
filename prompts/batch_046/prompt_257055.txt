I'm running a philosphy seminar next week, about 10 grad students, one 90-minute session, and I want them to feel the paradox of phenomenal judgment in epiphenomenalism rather than just recieve it as a familiar puzzle. Design a classroom game or structured role-play that bakes in what I'll call "mirror-judgment failure" — the idea that if my own reports about phenomenology are physically caused but causally isolated from the mental states, then analogical reasoning about other minds collapses too, not just first-person access. So the activity needs both first-person reporting and third-person attribution stages.

The setting: 10-12 people, no lecture up front. They should construct the problem themselves through rules and incentives.

Design the full thing for me:
- Stage one: a brief induced state (maybe a color patch or low sound) followed by an "inner report" — but half the participants have their report wording secretly fixed by a hidden card draw, not by their experience. The other half report genuinely. No one knows which group they're in, and they have to defend whether their own report was "authentic" without inspecting the causal chain.
- Stage two: they pair up and try to assign mental states to partners using only those reports plus visible behavior, attempting to seperate the "real experiencers" from the "report zombies." Include a coordination rule — maybe they only get a shared payoff if both partners correctly identify the causal structure behind the other person's report.
- The rules should force them to discover they can't tell the difference, and that resisting this costs them analogical reasoning about others.

Then the debrief: map each moment onto the epiphenomenalist picture — causal closure, the mental state floating free, the judgment as purely physical, and teh mirror-judgment failure where other-minds reasoning breaks down too. Be frank about what the activity can't reproduce (probably the actual metaphysical possibility of zombie worlds, or the specific causal closure argument from physics) and say exactly what I have to supply verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first run — people cheating the hidden mechanism, or confusion over the card draw — and how to handle it.

Keep it casual, don't ask for readings or citations, and make sure they run into the problem before anyone names "epiphenomenalism."
