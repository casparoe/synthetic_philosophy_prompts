I'm taking a seminar on comparative moral psychology, and after arguing with friends about whether a society could deliberately become a "guilt culture" or a "shame culture"—not merely describe itself that way, but rationally engineer an emotional shift through institutions—I realized most discussions collapse into name-dropping without actually testing what people believe about concrete cases. I want a formal decision tree, structured as a branching taxonomy rather than a scored quiz, that sorts positions on two intertwined questions: whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can intentionally choose to cultivate one over the other.

The tree should begin from commitments about the object of moral failure—does wrongdoing primarily violate a discrete, reparable principle, or does it expose a global, socially visible feature of the self?—and about institutional malleability—can rituals, legal structures, and educational systems deliberately shift which emotion dominates a moral climate? Each internal node must be a concrete scenario or policy choice answerable without knowing philosophical labels: for example, whether anonymous restitution should be preferred to public apology, whether moral education should target internal conscience or social reputation, or whether a society should reward private remorse over communal reintegration. The leaves must include at least these positions: (A) universalist guilt-privileging, holding that guilt is always the healthier moral emotion regardless of cultural context; (B) strong cultural constructivism, holding that cultures can and should choose, with no cross-cultural standard of correctness; (C) shame-rehabilitation, holding that shame is actually superior for communal repair and that guilt is alienating; (D) integrative calibration, holding that both are necessary but that cultures can legitimately weight them differently; and (E) emotional minimalism, holding that neither should dominate and that attempts to engineer emotional culture are coercive. Map every combination of branch answers onto one of these leaves or onto an explicitly empty cell; for any unoccupied combination—such as the view that guilt is universally best yet institutions are powerless to shape emotional norms—briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. The instrument should sort respondents by what they say about cases and institutional possibilities, not by which theorist they have read, and should flag any pair of leaves that the tree's logic cannot genuinely separate.
