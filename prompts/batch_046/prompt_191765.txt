I've recently felt caught in a loop of identical workdays, yet I notice I'm slowly accumulating skills and shifting in temperament without feeling replaced by a new person. This made me revisit Groundhog Day with fresh eyes. Read the film through the endurantism versus perdurantism debate: Phil Connors lives the same February 2nd repeatedly, gaining piano mastery and moral insight, yet each morning he wakes in the same bed with the same body. If endurantism is correct and he is wholly present on every loop, how can he possess properties—like knowing a specific chord progression—that he only acquired in earlier iterations of the very same day? If perdurantism is correct and he is a four-dimensional worm of temporal parts, which slice escapes into the "real" February 3rd, and are the failed loops abandoned segments or parts of one continuous object? I want a reading that takes both the metaphysics and the narrative seriously: does the loop structure make temporal parts visible in a way ordinary life conceals, or does Phil's enduring moral growth vindicate a single self that can radically change? And be honest about where the film resists either framework—particularly the fact that the townspeople seem unchanged, which might imply that only Phil persists across loops, raising the further puzzle of what his interactions with them are interactions with.
