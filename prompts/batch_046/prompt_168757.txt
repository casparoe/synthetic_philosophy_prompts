Design a structured 90-minute seminar simulation for ten graduate students in philosophy of mind that forces them to inhabit the specific structural vulnerability at the core of higher-order theories of consciousness—not the generic claim that consciousness requires a second-order representation, but the more precise asymmetry that a higher-order awareness can misrepresent or even fabricate its target mental state, and that the subject has no internal mechanism to detect or correct this error because the introspective apparatus itself is the source of the misfire. Arrange the room for paired interaction; split the group into five "experiencers" and five "adjudicators," rotating pairs every eight minutes across four rounds. Give each experiencer a hidden instruction card assigning one of four conditions, kept secret even from the partner: (1) an aligned case, where a genuine first-order perceptual state is paired with an accurate higher-order awareness of it; (2) a misdirected case, where a genuine first-order state is present but the experiencer is instructed to sincerely report awareness of a different state; (3) an absent-target case, where no relevant first-order state is present but the experiencer is instructed to sincerely report awareness of one; (4) a bare first-order case, where a genuine state is present but the experiencer receives no higher-order instruction and must respond only with minimal behavioral cues. Experiencers may communicate only through standardized phrases—"I am aware of X," "I am aware of nothing specific," or a single permitted non-verbal cue—while adjudicators must classify each interaction as "aligned awareness," "misdirected awareness," "fabricated awareness," or "first-order only," using only the verbal report and one permitted observation. Build a scoring system with real (small) stakes: adjudicators earn points for correct classifications, but experiencers in conditions 2 and 3 earn points if their misaligned report is accepted, creating a genuine tension between truthful disclosure and strategic success. After 40 minutes of rotation, reveal all cards in a 10-minute "transparency phase," then run a 40-minute debrief. The debrief must map condition 1 onto the baseline case of first-person authority, condition 2 onto target misrepresentation (the higher-order representation points to the wrong state despite the presence of some first-order content), condition 3 onto the absence of a target with an apparently authoritative report (the higher-order representation lacks any corresponding first-order state), and condition 4 onto the possibility of awareness failure without subjective notice. Be explicit about predictable failures: participants in condition 4 will likely invent higher-order language out of social pressure, adjudicators will over-weight behavioral cues when verbal reports are ambiguous, and some experiencers will confuse strategic deception with genuine misrepresentation—explain how the facilitator should handle each. Crucially, state frankly which feature the simulation cannot reproduce: because every experiencer knows their card's instruction, the activity cannot replicate the phenomenal invisibility of a missing or misdirected first-order state from the inside; the facilitator must supply this limitation directly, explaining why, on the higher-order view, a subject in condition 3 would not experience themselves as "missing" anything. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, references to specific empirical studies, or lists of theorists; frame the entire design conceptually around the distinction between being in a mental state and being aware of being in it.
