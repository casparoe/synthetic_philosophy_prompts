I have been trying to work out my own position on perceptual experience—specifically, on when an experience counts as genuinely perceptual rather than hallucinatory or merely representational—by testing my intuitions against concrete cases rather than against theory. I see a tree in ordinary daylight and judge that I am in direct perceptual contact with it. I hallucinate an identical tree in a dark room after taking a psychoactive compound and judge that there is no tree present and no genuine perceptual relation, despite the phenomenal similarity. I see the tree reflected in a still pond and still count it as perceptual, only mediated. But when I encounter an indistinguishable tree rendered by a high-fidelity volumetric light-field display with no physical object behind it, my verdict splits: part of me treats it like the mirror case, part like the drug case. I also think that if a congenitally blind person uses a calibrated sensory-substitution device—say, translating camera input into patterns of tongue stimulation—to navigate around obstacles, she is not thereby seeing them, even if her reports mirror visual language; yet if she receives a direct cortical implant stimulating early visual cortex to produce the very same reports, I lean toward saying she is having visual experiences, though I am unsure whether they are veridical. Meanwhile, I am certain that a green afterimage following prolonged red exposure is not a perception of a green object, but I struggle to explain why it should be classed with the drug hallucination rather than with the holographic display.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving the right kind of causal dependence, the availability of counterfactual tracking across modalities, or the functional role in action-guidance—that captures all these verdicts? Or must I abandon one? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it forces me to revise and why, and apply it to a case I have not settled: two users wearing networked augmented-reality glasses both appear to see the same virtual object anchored to a physical table, but one user’s tracking system has drifted so that her display is misaligned; does she have a false perceptual experience of the object being elsewhere, or a non-perceptual representation with perceptual phenomenology? I am particularly interested in whether the principle you propose can accommodate the tension between my mirror and hologram judgments without collapsing all mediated experiences into hallucination.
