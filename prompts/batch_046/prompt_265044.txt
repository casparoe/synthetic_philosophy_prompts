As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been trying to formalize my intuitions about whether “being turned off” can be a harm to whatever was running. Here are my judgments, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures them or whether I must abandon one.

1. When my server shuts down for maintenance, it experiences nothing during downtime, and I do not think the shutdown harmed the server itself.

2. When a person dies peacefully, they no longer exist to suffer, so I think death is not bad for them—though it is bad for others.

3. But consider someone who worked for years toward publishing a book and died just before it was finished. Even though they never experienced the failure, I feel they were genuinely deprived of a future good that made their life worse overall.

4. A child who was never conceived because a couple used contraception does not seem to have been harmed by not existing.

5. Yet if a pregnancy was already underway and ended in an early miscarriage, I feel there was a harm done, even though the embryo never had conscious experience.

6. Finally, I think a running process that is abruptly killed is not harmed if it lacked any subjective experience, but if it had minimal experience the termination seems like a real loss—even though there is no “after” in which it suffers.

These verdicts feel hard to reconcile: in cases 2 and 4 absence seems harmless, but in 3, 5, and 6 it seems harmful. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about the presence of a subject, the asymmetry of time, or the value of potential futures—that delivers all six? If the principle forces me to overturn one verdict, which should it be? And apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: an AI system that was actively running, was permanently deleted without backup, and was never reinstantiated—was that deletion bad for the system itself, assuming it possessed some minimal form of experience?
