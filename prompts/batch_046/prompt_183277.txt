I'm a doctoral student in the history and philosophy of science, working on demarcation problems between mainstream historiography and chronological revisionism. At a recent workshop on pseudohistory, a speaker defending Fomenko's New Chronology responded to documentary objections—specifically the absence of contemporary chronicles for events he reassigns to antiquity—by invoking the line, "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." The move was meant to block the inference from silent archives to non-occurrence, and thereby to protect the claim that certain medieval records are misdated duplicates of earlier ones.

Can you lay out, as a precise menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct theses that slogan could be expressing? I want at least four readings: (1) the purely logical claim that non-observation does not entail non-existence; (2) a methodological principle about how historians should weigh negative archival findings; (3) an evidential claim that missing data should reduce confidence less steeply than standard Bayesian practice suggests; and (4) a burden-shifting claim that skeptics must positively disprove a revisionist narrative rather than merely point to documentary gaps. For each, state whether it is a platitude, defensible but substantive, or false, and specify which—if any—actually licenses using the slogan to shield Fomenko-style arguments from mainstream chronological criticism. Finally, indicate which reading the speaker was probably invoking, and whether that reading distinguishes legitimate inference about undocumented trade routes or lost sources from the kind of reasoning characteristic of pseudohistory.
