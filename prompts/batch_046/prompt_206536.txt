I am a high school student currently taking an independent study course in philosophy of science, and I have become particularly interested in what might be called the competence-without-comprehension inversion that appears in both evolutionary biology and early theoretical computer science. In Darwin’s framework, natural selection produces exquisitely adapted structures—eyes calibrated to light, immune systems tuned to pathogens, echolocation refined for navigation—without any comprehension of optics, immunology, or acoustics on the part of the environment that does the selecting. In Turing’s framework, especially as developed in his reflections on universal computation and machine intelligence, a purely formal system can generate correct outputs, solve complex problems, and reproduce reasoning-like behavior without possessing anything like semantic understanding, conscious grasp, or internal representational transparency. I am trying to understand the most important philosophical implications of these parallel inversions, particularly for how we ought to evaluate claims about intelligence, knowledge, or cognitive achievement in systems—whether biological, artificial, or hybrid—where high functional competence is systematically decoupled from internal comprehension.

Could you outline the most significant implications? I am especially interested in implications that bear on three specific concerns. First, how should these inversions affect our standards for attributing genuine cognition or understanding to non-human entities, including both evolved organisms and algorithmic systems? Second, do they undermine the explanatory power of evolutionary accounts by reducing them to descriptions of mechanical success, or can evolutionary explanation retain a distinctive epistemic status even once we acknowledge that adaptation requires no designer-like comprehension? Third, is there a meaningful and stable distinction between what I will call “performative success”—the reliable production of correct or adaptive outputs—and “epistemic achievement”—a state that involves not merely correct behavior but some form of justified grasp or representational coherence—and does that distinction survive intact once we accept that competence can be fully mechanized without comprehension?

I want a focused conceptual analysis rather than a historical survey or bibliographic overview; please treat these inversions not as curiosities from the history of science but as active constraints on contemporary reasoning about mind, mechanism, and explanation. In particular, I am curious whether accepting both inversions together forces us to revise our intuitive assumption that understanding is the deep cause of successful performance, or whether it instead reveals that our concept of understanding was always partly a projection onto functional patterns we could not fully explain.
