I direct taxonomy audits for a consortium of natural-history and contemporary-art museums that share a unified digital catalog. Objects routinely migrate between domains—scientific specimen, legal evidence, aesthetic artifact, cultural relic—and I need a reusable decision procedure to detect what I will call copycat-fluid slippage: the migration of a concept across domains such that it retains hidden procedural, normative, or evidentiary baggage from its origin, producing misclassification that standard checklists miss. Ground the protocol in the philosophical view that concepts are fluid, family-resemblance structures rather than sets of necessary and sufficient conditions, but translate that view into an audit flow an auditor without philosophy training can run in under thirty minutes per object, with small teams and no external consultant sign-off required. Deliver a sequential checklist—questions in fixed order, criteria for a sufficient answer at each step, explicit stop points that flag slippage, and explicit hand-off points where the audit stops and a curator or legal officer must decide because the conflict is institutional or ethical rather than taxonomic. Include rules for high-frequency, high-stakes migration paths (e.g., biological material that may also be a sacred object; manufactured items that may be either evidence or installation art). Apply the procedure to three cases: one in which it correctly flags hidden baggage that curators had overlooked; one in which it yields an unwelcome reclassification the museum will resist; and one ambiguous case in which the protocol stops without resolution. Conclude with an honest account of where the procedure will misfire—especially under time pressure or with under-resourced teams—and what kinds of slippage it is structurally unable to catch. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the deliverable must be fully operable for an auditor working without web access.
