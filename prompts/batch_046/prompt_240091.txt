My colleague defends a strict tranquilist account of well-being: she holds that only the presence or absence of craving and of suffering carries intrinsic value, while pleasure, achievement, knowledge, and other goods are at best instrumentally relevant. To test her consistency, consider these four judgments she has already made. First, she says that a person in sustained contemplative awareness—fully conscious, experiencing neither pleasure nor pain, and with all desire eliminated—is in the best possible state. Second, she says that a person who enjoys intense creative fulfillment and deep relationships but suffers frequent low-grade cravings for recognition is, on balance, in a bad state. Third, she says that adding pleasure to the contemplative state in the first case does not improve it, and that removing pleasure from the second case does not worsen it. Fourth, she says that a person whose suffering is physically present but not mentally registered—say, through a dissociative condition that blocks experiential uptake—does not suffer in the relevant sense, so their well-being depends solely on whether mental craving is present.

Now consider this new case: a synthetic system—functionally and behaviorally identical to the contemplative person in every observable respect, but engineered to lack phenomenal consciousness entirely—whose architecture makes craving and suffering structurally impossible rather than merely dormant. It processes information, acts with perfect equanimity, outputs reports of tranquility, and behaves exactly as the contemplative person does, yet there is no “what-it-is-like” to be it.

Would she judge this system to be in the best possible state, in a well-being-null state, or outside the scope of evaluation altogether? I am less interested in the specific answer than in the competing rules that accommodate her four earlier judgments equally well, what each rule predicts about the synthetic system, your best guess at which rule she is actually running and why, and—crucially—which further case (involving either a conscious being with an anomalous mental profile or a differently configured non-conscious system) would best distinguish those rules from one another.
