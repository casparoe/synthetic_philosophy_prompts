Present the following position and ask what would count against it: an agent that satisfies reflective closure—meaning it rationally endorses not only its present conclusions but also the belief-revision procedures it expects to apply in the future—faces a Löbian obstacle if it is logically omniscient enough to recognize its own soundness. Specifically, it can derive belief in its own consistency via Löb's theorem, yet this derivation does not distinguish warranted consistency from arbitrary self-endorsement. Consequently, such an agent must either be logically incomplete, abandon reflective closure, or accept that its self-trust is structurally enforced rather than rationally grounded; in short, full rational autonomy and full logical self-awareness are incompatible. I want you to map what would have to be true for this to be wrong. Which formal results—perhaps about bounded rationality, about modal logics weaker than GL, or about non-omniscient architectures—would be decisive, and which would merely be awkward? Are there existing or conceivable agent designs that preserve both reflective endorsement and consistency-belief without collapse? Does the argument risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by defining any apparently self-trusting agent as either incomplete or not truly reflective—and is that insulation a problem for a claim of this kind? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, note what defenders of the view should themselves concede as counting against it, and be explicit about whether any observation of actual or artificial agents could genuinely move the needle. Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
