Quick question from someone who reads old biology texts for fun. I've been working through Darwin's Descent of Man and some secndary literature, and I keep noticing a pattern I can't name. Darwin spends long chapters explaining moral feelings—sympathy, guilt, the feeling that you ought to help strangers—as instincts that got selected because cooperative groups outcompeted others. Plain evolutionary origin story. But then, in other places, he treats morality as genuinely getting better over time: he describes the moral sense as summed up in the word ought, he praises people who risk themselves for strangers as reaching the highest development of human nature, and he even suggests that belief in God might grow out of moral reasoning instead of the reverse.

The specific pattern isn't just that Darwin is ambiguous. It's that whenever people try to classify him, they split the text in half. One camp grabs the evolutionary mechanism and says morality is debunked—just inherited instinct with no real authority. The other camp grabs his praise and progressive language and says he clearly believed in real moral improvement that evolution happens to track. Each camp treats the other half of the book as either rhetorical window-dressing or a mistake Darwin didn't notice. I see this split happen over and over, with both sides citing the same work but treating it like two different authors.

How is it different from ordinary ambiguity, or from a thinker being flat-out inconsistent?
