Recently, I was appointed director of a regional infrastructure and governance initiative that operates across several municipalities where formal bureaucratic procedures overlap with dense networks of kinship, patronage, and customary reciprocity. My professional background is in public administration within a liberal-democratic framework that treats anti-corruption compliance as largely universal, yet my daily work has forced me to recognize that the boundary between corruption and legitimate social obligation is far more contested in practice than my training suggested. I am trying to determine what I actually believe about this boundary, and I need help testing whether my intuitions cohere.

Below are my verdicts on six concrete situations I have faced or observed. I want to know whether there is a precise principle—or a small family of competing principles—that captures the pattern, what each principle predicts for a case I have not listed, and which of my verdicts I must abandon if I adopt it.

Case I. After we awarded a road-repair contract through competitive bidding, the winning firm's director sent an expensive watch to my private residence with a handwritten note praising the fairness of the selection process. I returned the item and documented the incident. My verdict: this is unambiguous corruption.

Case II. A senior municipal official, whose office had delayed a necessary environmental clearance for months, personally intervened to release it shortly after I attended a community meeting at his invitation. One week later, he invited my family to his daughter's wedding, where guests customarily present cash in an envelope. I gave the customary amount—approximately one week's local wages. My verdict: this is social reciprocity, not bribery, though the timing creates an uncomfortable conflict of interest.

Case III. Our accounting clerk explained that invoice processing requires an unofficial "facilitation contribution" of roughly twenty dollars, pooled among clerks and shared collectively, without which payments to subcontractors stall indefinitely. Local staff describe this as a standard workplace custom, not private extortion. I authorized the payments to protect local laborers from harm. My verdict: technically corrupt and wrong, but structurally and morally different from Case I—less an extraction than a dysfunctional institutional tax.

Case IV. A respected elder asked me to direct a small community-development grant to a registered village association that includes his nephews. The association meets all technical criteria, and other eligible groups are no more deserving. The elder had previously mediated a land-access dispute that saved the project months of delay. I approved the grant. My verdict: this is patronage or the consolidation of social obligation, but not corruption, because there is no private enrichment—only the translation of reciprocal trust into an administrative allocation.

Case V. Our headquarters mandates strict adherence to Western-derived anti-bribery protocols: refusal and documentation of all gifts, invitations, and informal payments. Local partners have responded by withdrawing cooperation, arguing that the framework treats every social bond as a potential transaction and thereby destroys the trust required for governance. I enforce the policy, but I believe it is wrong if treated as a culturally neutral universal standard. My verdict: necessary as an institutional safeguard, yet imperial if presented as context-independent.

Case VI—the one I keep revisiting. A long-standing supplier, who has never received a contract from us, offered in writing to pay the full tuition for my child at a private school, framing the offer as "corporate social responsibility" and "investment in local human capital." There is no explicit quid pro quo, but the supplier makes clear they hope this will place them on a future preferred-vendor list. I refused. Yet I remain unsettled: if reciprocity and long-term relationship-building are legitimate in Case II, and if collective institutional practices are excusable in Case III, why is this particular form of reciprocity categorically corrupt? Is the distinction based on directness of exchange, on the private versus public nature of the benefit, or on something else?

Please propose a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures the distinction between corruption and legitimate reciprocity or patronage across these cases. For each principle, specify precisely which clause is motivated by which case, state its verdict on Case VI and on at least one further hypothetical case I have not listed (for instance: an anonymous donation made to a community fund that happens to be administered by my office, with no donor identification and no expectation of recognition), and be honest about which of my verdicts—especially Cases II, III, and IV—it cannot accommodate without strain. If the best principle forces me to drop one of my intuitions, say so explicitly, and explain whether the dropped verdict was the outlier all along.
