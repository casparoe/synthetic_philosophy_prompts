As a journalist who covers philosophy of science and ethics, I keep returning to a version of the scale argument about insect sentience that seems to prove far more than its advocates intend, and I cannot locate the fault line. The reasoning runs as follows.

(1) Even if the prior probability that any particular insect is sentient is low—say, one part in a thousand—the global population of insects, estimated near 10^19, implies an enormous expected number of sentient subjects alive at any moment, likely exceeding sentient mammals by several orders of magnitude.

(2) If moral consideration should track expected sentience-weighted harm rather than certainty alone, then routine human activities—agriculture, pesticide application, highway driving—generate vastly more expected suffering than many human-scale moral catastrophes, and should therefore dominate our ethical priorities.

(3) The same aggregation logic applies downward without obvious stopping point. Soil ecosystems contain on the order of 10^28 bacteria; if each carries even a vanishingly small probability of minimal experiential states, the expected moral weight of microbiota dwarfs that of all animal life combined.

(4) It applies upward within a single organism as well. During sleep spindles, transient coalitions of neurons form and dissolve; if each coalition has a non-zero chance of constituting a minimal subject, then altering one's own sleep architecture could carry the same moral weight as altering the lives of thousands of strangers.

(5) But it is plainly false that taking an antibiotic or entering REM sleep is morally comparable to genocide, and it is equally implausible that every commute is a moral atrocity on the scale of mass violence. So the argument must go wrong somewhere between (1) and (5).

(6) Therefore, either the scale principle collapses into absurdity, or we must accept that ordinary human life is a continuous, unacknowledged moral disaster.

Which step should be rejected? I am especially reluctant to abandon (2), because without some form of expectation-weighted consideration the urgency of insect welfare seems to evaporate entirely; yet if I keep (2), I seem forced into (3) and (4). Is there a hidden equivocation between epistemic probability and ontological aggregation—between counting expected subjects and weighting moral claims? Or is the error a scope shift, applying linear expectation across categories where individuation itself breaks down? If the argument is in fact valid and the burden falls on the premises, I want to know what each standard response pays: the threshold view that demands a confidence cutoff before moral status activates; the pluralist view that denies probabilities should be summed across ontologically ambiguous entities; and the view that accepts (3) and (4) but redefines harm so that microbiota and neural coalitions fall outside its domain. Where is the weakest link, and what does breaking it cost?
