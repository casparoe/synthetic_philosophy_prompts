Case 2: The 1784 Watt steam-engine patent initiates fossil-capitalism and a long, accelerating CO2 rise. Verdict: not the boundary. The transformation is gradual, regionally staggered, and produces no single stratigraphic layer.

Case 3: Agriculture and pastoral methane release begin altering biogeochemistry around 8000–5000 BCE, eventually affecting every continent. Verdict: conceptually important, but I reject it as the official start because the change is diachronous—there is no global layer marking it.

Case 4: Around 1610, the Columbian Exchange and New World depopulation generate a globally detectable CO2 dip in ice cores. Verdict: despite the global signal, I reject this. The mechanism is biological exchange and unintended demographic collapse, not direct technological transformation of Earth systems.

Case 5: The post-1950 Great Acceleration—plastics, concrete, isotopic spikes, biodiversity collapse—creates a dense cluster of markers. Verdict: my best candidate, though I think fixing the year exactly to 1950 is somewhat conventional; 1945 is sharper, but I accept 1950 if a commission prefers socio-economic correlation.

Case 6: Imagine that in 2100 an autonomous geoengineering AI takes over carbon and nitrogen cycling, maintaining them for ten millennia with minimal human oversight. Verdict: this should not extend the Anthropocene continuously; it inaugurates a distinct epoch—call it the Syntropocene—because the dominant geochemical agent is no longer human.

Case 7: Suppose future stratigraphers discover the 1945 plutonium layer was localized, not globally uniform, leaving 1950 as the only sharp marker. Verdict: the Anthropocene concept survives; we relocate the boundary rather than abandon it. This suggests the underlying reality matters more than any particular marker.

Case 3 eventually achieves global dominance but fails (a).
