As a journalist who reports on the philosophy and neuroscience of consciousness, I am drafting a long-form essay on methodological frameworks in the science of mind, and before publication I want to audit a cluster of positions I have come to hold, since they feel coherent in isolation but may not survive joint scrutiny. I accept Dennett's heterophenomenology as the proper methodological stance for consciousness science: first-person reports should be treated not as direct, incorrigible access to private phenomenal states, but as texts describing a subject's belief-world about experience, to be interpreted with the same hermeneutic neutrality we apply to fiction or autobiography. I am also a physicalist: I hold that consciousness is entirely realized by physical processes, and that there is no ontologically distinct phenomenal substance or property. Nevertheless, I maintain that the explanatory gap is a real and enduring feature of our epistemic condition rather than a mere confusion to be dissolved—complete neuroscientific understanding will still leave us with the persistent sense that subjective experience is somehow left out of the account. At the same time, I believe that achieving a complete heterophenomenological catalog—every report a subject could produce regarding dreams, pain, color phenomenology, or meditative states—would amount to a full explanation of that subject's consciousness, leaving nothing unexplained. I also take phenomenal concepts to be theoretical rather than specially direct, and I think heterophenomenology renders them dispensable. Finally, I wish to extend this framework to non-human animals and potentially to advanced artificial systems, interpreting behavioral outputs as heterophenomenological texts without requiring that these entities possess explicit beliefs or linguistic self-report. Can you examine whether these commitments form a consistent package? In particular, I sense a potential conflict between my physicalism and my insistence on a real, never-bridged explanatory gap, and another between that gap and my claim that a complete catalog of reports exhausts explanation. I am also concerned that treating reports as belief-texts may block the extension to entities lacking beliefs, though I am not certain this is more than apparent friction. Please order any genuine tensions by severity, specify for each what must be revised and at what philosophical cost, distinguish the tensions that are merely apparent and dissolve on inspection, and indicate which single revision would resolve the greatest number of conflicts.
