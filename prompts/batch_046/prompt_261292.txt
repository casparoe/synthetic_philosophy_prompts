My colleague in philosophy of science has ruled on five cases involving epistemic bootstrapping and easy knowledge. (1) A technician calibrates a scale by weighing objects of unknown weight and adjusting outputs until they cohere, then weighs a new object: verdict, no knowledge of the weight, vicious circularity. (2) A chicken sexer reliably sorts chicks by touch, is told by experts she is highly accurate, then sorts a new chick: verdict, she knows the sex despite lacking explanatory access—easy knowledge is genuine. (3) A climate model’s parameters are estimated from satellite temperature records, an independent measurement, then the calibrated model forecasts temperatures in unsampled regions: verdict, legitimate, not bootstrapping, because calibration was anchored externally. (4) A mathematician proves a lemma using intuition, then cites the lemma’s success as evidence that intuition is reliable in that domain: verdict, illegitimate circular justification. (5) A neuroscientist validates an fMRI protocol against behavioral tests independent of brain scans, then uses fMRI to study cognition: verdict, legitimate.

Next case: a protein-folding algorithm is initially validated against experimentally determined structures. Once deployed, it predicts structures for proteins lacking experimental data (Batch A). Those Batch A predictions—weighted by high internal confidence—are fed back into the training pipeline. The retrained system then predicts structures for a second set of uncharacterized proteins (Batch B). There is no independent experimental check for either batch, though internal consistency metrics improved after retraining.

Predict my colleague’s verdict on whether the researchers have knowledge of Batch B’s structures. Identify at least two rules consistent with her first five verdicts—for instance, a strict anti-circularity principle and a baseline-reliability transmission principle—state what each predicts about Batch B, and give your best guess at which rule she runs, with reasons. Then specify one further case that would clearly distinguish those two rules.
