I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal that can turn a half-formed preoccupation into a tractable, conceptually focused project. Since completing a seminar on Plutarch's Parallel Lives, and since returning—after a recent family bereavement—to late antique hagiography as a way of thinking about how lives become instructive, I have become fixated on a structural feature that neither classicists nor moral psychologists seem to isolate. The standard debate treats biography and hagiography as different genres serving different ends—historical comparison versus devotional identification—and asks whether biography teaches virtue by presenting imitable models. Contemporary exemplarist accounts in moral psychology tend to treat the exemplar as a more or less static bundle of traits, while Plutarch scholars debate whether the paired structure is genuinely pedagogical or merely rhetorical display. What both frameworks miss, I suspect, is the role of narrative residue: the unassimilated details of a subject's character—flaws, contradictions, or actions that resist full integration into the work's explicit moral framework. In Plutarch's paired lives, the comparative architecture creates an evaluative gap in which the reader must adjudicate competing virtues without a definitive synthesis; in hagiography, by contrast, every pre-conversion vice is retrospectively dissolved into a single redemption arc, leaving almost no residue. My precise question is whether moral learning from biography requires this residue as a structural feature—whether the pedagogical value lies precisely in the reader's forced engagement with unresolved moral friction—or whether hagiography's cleaner teleology achieves a different, perhaps more durable, form of practical reasoning that modern exemplarism should prefer.

The approach would be comparative and conceptual rather than archival or reception-historical. Months one through three would involve close structural readings of three Plutarchan pairs, focusing not on the explicit moral judgments but on the precise narrative moments where the evaluative voice withholds synthesis—what I am calling the residue—alongside three late antique vitae of figures such as Anthony of Egypt and Martin of Tours, analyzed specifically for their suppression of post-conversion ambiguity. Months four through six would map these architectural differences onto current work in narrative moral psychology, asking whether the cognitive work demanded of the reader differs in kind between the two forms, and whether that difference explains why biography is often praised for training judgment while hagiography is praised for shaping identity. The deliverable would be an argument that either (a) narrative residue is necessary for biography to function as practical moral training, in which case hagiography is pedagogically deficient for secular ethics and modern exemplarism must deliberately choose its narrative form, or (b) the two genres serve incommensurable cognitive ends, in which case the revival of exemplarist virtue ethics cannot treat all lives as equivalent moral data. The project fails if the structural distinction collapses—if Plutarch's pairs turn out to be as teleologically closed as hagiography, or if the psychological literature already contains an equivalent distinction that I have merely renamed. The scope is deliberately narrow: no new editions, no broad survey of ancient biography, and no attempt to resolve the broader debate about Plutarch's intent; I will supply all citations myself, and the fellowship period is sufficient precisely because the argument depends on conceptual framing rather than exhaustive cataloguing.
