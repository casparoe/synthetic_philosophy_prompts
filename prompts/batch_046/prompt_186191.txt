As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading seriously in moral psychology and metaethics, I keep encountering the same argumentative move in popular and semi-technical discussions of cross-cultural morality. Critics of moral objectivity—especially those who invoke the WEIRD-sample problem—frequently liken deep moral disagreement across cultures to disagreement about taste: whether a fermented preparation smells appetizing or repellent, whether a particular harmonic interval sounds consonant, or whether cilantro registers as fragrant or soapy. The analogy is meant to license a specific conclusion. If moral judgments are structurally like gustatory or olfactory judgments, then the documented divergence—Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic populations treating harm and fairness as nearly exhaustive of morality, while many other societies weight loyalty, authority, and sanctity as equally fundamental—is not a case of some groups being right and others mistaken, or of all grasping different parts of a single objective structure. It is merely different palates, shaped by local ecological and social conditions, with no fact of the matter to adjudicate between them.

Please audit this analogy with the rigor I would expect in a peer review of a physical model. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: in the moral domain, what plays the role of the taste receptor, the chemical ligand, the private qualia, the evolutionary function of aversion, the possibility of expert connoisseurship, and the absence of public reason-giving? Second, isolate the disanalogies that matter for the anti-objectivist conclusion. Moral judgments are typically advanced with reasons, are revisable under reflection, and carry a prescriptive force that seems to bind not only the speaker but anyone in relevantly similar circumstances; taste reports do none of these consistently. Does the analogy therefore smuggle in a private, non-cognitive picture of moral judgment that the cross-cultural data—say, the systematic differences in how societies rank the five moral foundations—do not actually entail? Third, examine whether the WEIRD/non-WEIRD divergence maps better onto taste variation or onto something like disagreement over the calibration rules of a shared measuring instrument, or over the axioms of a cooperative game. In particular, if harm and fairness dominate in one population not because those populations lack other receptors but because their social ecology makes those foundations particularly salient, does that support subjectivism or rather a context-sensitive objectivity? Fourth, state whether the conclusion—that there is no objective moral fact—survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the taste comparison was carrying the argument all along. Finally, if the analogy collapses, propose a more apt comparison—perhaps moral disagreement as disagreement about the weighting of incommensurable parameters in a multi-objective optimization—that captures the phenomenology of cross-cultural conflict without presupposing the very subjectivism it is meant to establish.
