Schrödinger was profoundly shaped by Vedanta, especially the doctrine that there is only one consciousness—Atman identical with Brahman—and that the appearance of many separate minds is a perspectival illusion rather than a metaphysical fact. He insisted on a sharp distinction between the empirical multiplicity of subjects, which science can investigate, and the underlying unity of awareness, which he took to be singular and indivisible.

Consider a hypothetical research program, call it distributed subjectivity, in which high-bandwidth brain-to-brain interfaces allow two people to share sensory streams in real time, producing a temporary state in which each reports direct access to the other's qualia. When the link is severed, the shared field dissolves back into two apparently separate streams. Philosophers of this program divide: some argue that the phenomenon empirically validates a partitioned-unity view—one underlying field that can be locally divided like a wavefunction—while others claim it proves consciousness is fundamentally modular, capable of being spliced and recombined like biological tissue.

Given Schrödinger's specific commitment to Vedantic monism—not generic mysticism, but the claim that multiplicity belongs only to the empirical perspective and never touches the metaphysical substrate—how would he likely have interpreted these results? Would he have accepted that technology can genuinely blur the boundary between separate minds, or would he have argued that even perfect neural coupling creates only an illusion of shared awareness, leaving the singularity of consciousness untouched? Please address both the empirical framing (what the experiments seem to show) and the metaphysical framing (whether consciousness can be partitioned at all), and indicate where he would likely draw the line between practical coupling and ontological division.
