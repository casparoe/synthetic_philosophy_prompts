I'm leading a session for a reading-group of cognitive-science graduate students who have no formal philosophy training. They've just encountered popular writing on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and they are stuck between two misunderstandings: half interpret it as the claim that we literally have no visual experience at all, and half treat it as functionalism repackaged with provocative branding. I need a teaching plan—not the lecture script itself, but the explanatory architecture. You are an expert philosopher of mind and an experienced pedagogue; think carefully, and begin by laying out the conceptual dependency structure before addressing delivery.

Specifically, map the order in which components must be introduced so that illusionism becomes intelligible rather than immediately absurd. Identify the precise distinction—likely between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, or between the representational content and the purported intrinsic vehicle of experience—that must be firmly in place before the illusionist thesis can even be heard correctly. Choose one carrying example or experimental finding, such as change blindness or the peripheral-vision richness illusion, and specify exactly what it must demonstrate about introspective fallibility and what it must avoid implying about total sensory absence. Then chart the misconceptions these students will predictably generate in sequence: that illusionism denies all experience, that it requires a homuncular audience being fooled, that it collapses into behaviorism, and the more subtle worry that an illusion cannot exist without a real phenomenal substrate to be misrepresented. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the arc where it should be anticipated and neutralized.

Next, propose a rigorous check for understanding that distinguishes genuine comprehension from mere agreement or discomfort—something that requires applying the distinction to a novel case rather than repeating slogans. Also state explicitly what should be left out at this level and why: for instance, should I set aside the phenomenal concept strategy, the strong versus weak illusionism distinction associated with Frankish and Dennett, and technical debates about the metaphysics of qualia? Finally, keep the plan entirely self-contained: do not propose readings, bibliographies, or citations of any kind, and design it so that it can be executed by someone without web access to verify obscure sources.
