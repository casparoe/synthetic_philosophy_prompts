Assume Thomas Carlyle, having articulated his doctrine of hero-worship and the world-shaping power of singular great men, is presented with the modern historiographical counterfactual: could Nazi Germany have arisen without Adolf Hitler? In framing the challenge, set before him both the familiar structural explanations—the economic destabilisation of the Weimar Republic, the punitive architecture of the Treaty of Versailles, the bureaucratic rationalisation of violence, and the institutional fragility of interwar democracy—and a more recent, hypothetical finding from agent-based modelling of interwar German political networks, which indicates that authoritarian consolidation was path-dependent yet that no individual node was strictly irreplaceable: in simulations where the central charismatic figure is removed but paramilitary organisations and administrative structures remain intact, comparable regimes still emerge. How would Carlyle reply? Would he dismiss the modelling as a mechanistic fallacy that confuses statistical aggregation with spiritual destiny, or might he grant that structures can incubate a tyrant while insisting that only a particular genius of evil could supply the specific demonic energy, rhetorical magnetism, and historical direction that defined the actual regime? In your reconstruction, examine how Carlyle’s notion of the hero—simultaneously shaped by and transcending his epoch—interacts with modern distinctions between necessary and sufficient conditions, and with the idea that causation might be distributed across networks rather than concentrated in a single will. Do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reconstruct the position solely from Carlyle’s known philosophical commitments, treating the network-modelling scenario as a conceptual challenge he must answer directly.
