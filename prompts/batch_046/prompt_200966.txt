Consider the following reconstruction of an argument drawn from Nietzsche’s second Untimely Meditation, usually rendered in English as On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life. (1) Nietzsche distinguishes exactly three ways that history can serve life: monumental history, which supplies heroic models for present action; antiquarian history, which preserves origins and fosters piety toward the past; and critical history, which judges and destroys oppressive traditions to liberate present life. (2) Each mode, when it dominates consciousness, produces a specific pathology that undermines life: monumental history breeds delusional self-aggrandizement through false analogies with distant greatness; antiquarian history breeds paralyzing mummification and an inability to act; critical history breeds destructive rootlessness by severing all ties to origins. (3) The psychological drives that generate these three orientations—the need for exemplars, the need for ancestral belonging, and the need for liberation—are structurally incompatible: to fully honor origins conflicts with destroying them, and to model oneself on remote heroes conflicts with preserving local continuity, so that satisfying one drive necessarily suppresses the others. (4) Therefore, any living being that relates to the past through history must be dominated by exactly one mode, and hence must suffer its corresponding pathology. (5) Nietzsche nevertheless claims that a healthy culture requires all three modes, integrated in proportion to its plastic power—its capacity to metabolize the past without being determined by it. (6) But if the three drives are structurally incompatible, then such integration is impossible: either one drive dominates, which restores the conclusion of (4), or the culture holds contradictory orientations simultaneously, producing not health but a fractured consciousness. (7) Furthermore, if plastic power is itself a non-historical faculty that merely selects, digests, and discards historical material, then history is never the direct servant of life; the service is performed by the non-historical, which implies that the three modes are not genuinely ways that history serves life but rather ways that life protects itself from history. (8) So Nietzsche’s position collapses into a dilemma: either history always harms life, contradicting his claim that it can serve life, or there exists a fourth, non-taxonomic relation to the past—perhaps a purely metabolic or unhistorical assimilation—that escapes the three-mode framework, contradicting his claim that these three are exhaustive.

This conclusion seems absurd because it renders Nietzsche’s central project incoherent. Identify which premise or inferential step should be rejected, explain why that step initially looks compelling, and then price the cost of abandoning it: does it require weakening the claim that the drives are structurally incompatible, conceding that domination is not inevitable, admitting that plastic power is actually one of the three modes in disguise, or accepting that the non-historical plays a more fundamental role than Nietzsche allows? If you think the argument is actually valid and the real problem lies in accepting one of the premises, say which premise you would least want to surrender and what that choice implies for Nietzsche’s broader view of cultural health.
