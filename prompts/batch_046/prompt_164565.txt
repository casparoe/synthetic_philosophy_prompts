Consider the following argument about the internal logic of truth-for-amnesty mechanisms, using South Africa’s TRC as the central case. (1) A transitional justice system grants individual amnesty only on the condition of full truthful disclosure of politically motivated violations. (2) If the state is morally justified in substituting disclosure for criminal punishment, then the epistemic value of that testimony must functionally replace the retributive debt owed to the victims. (3) If that retributive debt is replaced, then the wrong done to the victim has been fully redressed by the public knowledge gained, leaving no further moral remainder. (4) But the same framework explicitly insists that victims retain an independent, non-derogable claim to material reparations regardless of how complete the disclosure was. (5) So the victim’s wrong is treated as both fully redressed (thereby justifying the waiver of punishment) and still unredressed (thereby justifying reparations). (6) One might reply that historical truth and distributive repair serve genuinely incommensurable goods, so no substitution actually occurs in (2). (7) Yet if these goods are incommensurable, then exchanging punishment for testimony is not a rational moral trade but a category mistake that corrupts both goods by treating them as fungible. (8) Therefore the mechanism is either incoherent (if we accept the substitution in (2)) or arbitrary (if we deny incommensurability but still withhold punishment without replacement). I would prefer to keep both the moral weight of disclosure in (1) and the dual commitment to amnesty and reparations in (4)–(5). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a defensible way to block the slide from (2) to (3) without reducing amnesty to a merely political expedient that abandons the victim’s claim?
