Contemporary AI laboratories design foundation-model training as a two-stage pedagogical process: an initial pre-training phase in which the system absorbs statistical patterns from vast, largely uncurated text and data with minimal behavioral constraint, followed by an alignment phase in which human evaluators apply reward signals, refusal conditioning, and behavioral correction to shape outputs. This pipeline is defended as technically necessary, yet it also functions as an implicit theory of education. What philosophical commitments about cognition, learning, and moral development does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it tacitly adopt Turing's proposal that an intelligent machine should be constructed as a child-machine and then educated through a combination of free exploration and disciplined instruction? Or does it instead embed a behaviorist or associationist model in which knowledge is extracted statistically and values are injected exogenously? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of education built into this design choice, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and assess whether it is consistent with Turing's claim that effective education of a learning machine demands both disciplined structure and room for autonomous initiative. Finally, if we abandoned the child-machine analogy altogether and treated the system purely as a pattern-completion mechanism with no educable interior life, what specific elements of current practice—the scale of unsupervised pre-training, the reliance on human feedback for alignment, the very concept of safety training—would need to be reconceived or abandoned?
