I've been tracking debates over commemorative monuments—statues, street names, building names—and I keep encountering a structural pattern I lack vocabulary for. Two concrete instances.

First, a municipality keeps a statue of a colonial administrator in its central square but installs a prominent plaque describing his role in a massacre. The intended message is critical education, yet visitors still pose for photographs beside the statue, and local guides call the site "the statue of X" rather than "the monument to the massacre." The plaque becomes a footnote to the figure.

Second, a different city removes the statue entirely and replaces it with an abstract work honoring local resistance. Nevertheless, tour guides and news coverage consistently describe the new work as "the replacement for the statue of X," and visitors report that the absence makes them think about the removed figure more intensely. The empty pedestal functions as a negative portrait.

In both cases, an intervention meant to diminish or reframe a commemorative presence—contextualization, replacement, or absence—instead makes that presence the unavoidable reference point. The figure seems to exert a kind of gravitational pull on interpretation that local redesign cannot escape.

Is there an established concept in aesthetics, political philosophy, or the study of public memory for this specific failure mode, in which attempts to decenter a commemorative object reinforce its centrality? If there is no precise term, how should one distinguish this phenomenon from simpler explanations like public resistance to change or the figure's independent historical importance? And what are its boundary conditions: does it require that the original figure was widely known beforehand, or can it occur with obscure local names as well?
