In Hume's essay on the balance of trade, he argues that a nation's real wealth lies in its labor and industry rather than in its stock of gold or silver, comparing mercantile anxiety about an unfavorable balance to the fear that all the water in the Thames might flow into the ocean and never return; he insists that money, like water, will circulate back according to commercial need, so a deficit in coin is self-correcting. Yet in his essay on money he grants that a sudden increase in the circulating medium can temporarily stimulate industry and employment before prices fully adjust, which sounds as though monetary representation can cause real economic effects rather than merely reflect them. I am trying to understand whether these claims sit together comfortably, or whether they reveal a deeper tension in Hume's philosophy of conventions: is money a purely artificial sign—like a promise or a rule of justice—whose value is entirely conventional, or does its short-run capacity to affect labor mean that it functions more like a psychological mechanism? Specifically, could the stimulus effect be read not as a mechanical economic law but as an instance of the confidence effects Hume associates with artificial institutions, so that money operates as a kind of collective intentionality in which representational status and causal efficacy are inseparable?
