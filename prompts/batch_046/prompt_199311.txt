Consider the record of the University Monument Review Board, which issues rulings on whether public commemorations of historical figures should stand, be retained with contextualization, or be removed, explicitly weighing actions and beliefs against a possible 'man of his time' defence.

Case 1 — Thomas Jefferson: The Board ruled that monuments should remain but be accompanied by extensive contextualization. It noted that slaveholding, though widespread, was actively contested by abolitionists within Jefferson's own political and intellectual circles, so the temporal defence is weakened; nevertheless, his role in drafting democratic institutions is conceptually separable from the harm.

Case 2 — Winston Churchill: The Board retained monuments without qualification. It held that Churchill's imperial policies and expressed racial views reflected dominant norms within British governing circles of his era, with no meaningful internal opposition on those specific grounds; his leadership during the Second World War is commemorated in a domain distinct from those policies.

Case 3 — Christopher Columbus: The Board recommended removal or substantial reframing. It rejected the temporal defence because the enslavement and devastation of Indigenous populations were documented and condemned by contemporaries within the Spanish clerical establishment; moreover, Columbus's legacy is symbolically inseparable from those events.

Case 4 — Immanuel Kant: The Board retained monuments with modest contextualization. It observed that Kant's anthropological writings on race were peripheral to his philosophical system, that racial hierarchy was largely unchallenged in 18th-century Königsberg academic culture, and that the commemorated achievement—critical philosophy—can be detached from those writings.

Case 5 — Leopold II of Belgium: The Board recommended removal. It found that the atrocities in the Congo Free State were deliberately concealed and not normative even within contemporary European imperial practice; the defence fails, and the harm is inseparable from the figure's primary legacy.

The record acknowledges that Linnaeus applied this same taxonomic structure to human beings, embedding moral and intellectual rankings among racial categories; that this application was contested at the time by naturalists such as Blumenbach, who advocated monogenism and rejected such rankings; but also that the human classification was structurally integrated into the system he published, even if the commemorated achievement is usually identified with non-human taxonomy.
