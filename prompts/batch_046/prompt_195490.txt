After a long and rather heated debate with a friend last night about whether the Western Roman Empire actually fell or merely underwent a prolonged institutional metamorphosis, I find myself wondering how Edward Gibbon would respond to the conceptual framework of modern late-antique scholarship. If he were alive today and confronted with the accumulated evidence and interpretation that has emerged since his History—particularly the shift away from catastrophic narratives toward models of gradual transformation, the role of climate volatility and epidemic disease in undermining fiscal-military capacity, and the claim that Roman legal and bureaucratic structures persisted well into the centuries traditionally called the Dark Ages—how do you think he would revise, defend, or qualify his original thesis? I am especially interested in whether he would accept the distinction now drawn between collapse, understood as a rapid and irreversible loss of socio-political complexity, and reconfiguration, understood as a slower redistribution of authority across hybrid Romano-barbarian polities that retained Roman administrative law and tax structures. Would he regard the modern reluctance to speak of fall as a legitimate historiographical correction, or as a form of semantic evasion that obscures a genuine decline in urban population, long-distance trade, and centralized taxation? Would he still maintain that the transformation of civic virtue by Christianity was a decisive internal cause, or would he concede that environmental and epidemiological factors deserve co-equal causal weight? Please do not provide a bibliography or list of sources; I am asking for a reasoned reconstruction based on his known commitments—his emphasis on internal decay, his skepticism of monocausal explanations, his comparative method, and his particular hostility to religious explanations of worldly events—applied to debates he could never have encountered. If you think some of his original categories, such as the idea that Rome succumbed to a combined victory of barbarism and religion, become incoherent when barbarian kings adopted Roman titulature and Christian bishops administered Roman tax rolls, say so explicitly.
