I am drafting an essay for an interdisciplinary philosophy seminar, and the topic has acquired unexpected personal urgency: three weeks ago I was tasked with curating a small archival donation for which every provenance record had been destroyed, and I found that my attempts to identify the objects kept collapsing into the very absence of information I was trying to overcome. Rather than treating this as a practical failure, I want to explore whether systematic informational inaccessibility can ever be constitutive of ontological identity rather than merely an obstacle to it.

Below are the notes and structure of my argument. I would appreciate comments specifically on philosophical coherence, on whether I am committing an epistemic fallacy, and on whether my central distinction holds.

1. I distinguish incidental inaccessibility—where information exists but is temporarily hidden—from constitutive inaccessibility, where the unavailability is structural and irreversible, and where any recovery of the information would alter the entity's relational properties.

2. I propose a category of entities I call trace-objects: items whose particular identity depends on being the unique remnant of a process, source, or event that is now permanently inaccessible. For example, consider an artifact whose material composition indicates it was produced by a manufacturing technique that left no other surviving examples and no recoverable documentation. Its status as the sole remaining instance of that technique seems partly constituted by the very impossibility of recovering the technique; if the process were reconstructed, the artifact would become one of many, and its particular ontological profile would shift.

3. I argue that this does not reduce ontology to epistemology. The claim is not that our ignorance creates the object, but that the object's identity conditions include a relational property—being the unique trace of X—where X's inaccessibility is a necessary condition for the relation to hold as a singular relation.

My main concern is whether step 2 confuses a feature of our knowledge with a feature of the object. I also worry that my distinction in step 1 relies on an underdeveloped notion of structural irreversibility. Could you assess whether the reasoning avoids collapsing into anti-realism, and whether there is a more precise way to formulate the claim without invoking knowledge conditions?

I am not looking for a bibliography or references to specific literature—only an evaluation of the argument's internal consistency and its vulnerability to obvious objections.
