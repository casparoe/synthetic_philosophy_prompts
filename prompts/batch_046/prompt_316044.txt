I'm on the rules committee of a consensus-governed housing cooperative. Our shared workshop has an industrial 3D printer that members maintain collectively, and I hold what feels less like a factual belief and more like a practical commitment: treating that printer as if it were private property erodes the collective identity we've deliberately built. I want to propose a formal guideline—"If treating the printer as private property erodes our collective identity, then members must disclose non-communal print jobs to the assembly"—but I've noticed something unsettling. When I voice the "if" clause to justify the rule, I'm not expressing that commitment with the same force I feel when I say it outright; I'm treating it hypothetically, as a premise for reasoning. Another committee member argues that this shows I'm not genuinely committed at all: if my stance were real, I couldn't suspend it inside a conditional and still derive obligations from it, so either I assert it everywhere or I don't hold it. I think there's a deeper tension about whether commitments of this kind can function inside reasoning structures—conditionals, disjunctions, or planning counterfactuals—without being expressed in full force at every occurrence, but I can't articulate where the consistency problem actually lies. Is my colleague right that I'm being incoherent, or is there a principled way to embed a practical commitment in a rule's logical structure without expressing it identically in every embedded position? What would resolving that depend on?
