Read the film 12 Angry Men through Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry: democratic deliberation as cooperative, fallibilist social inquiry in which judgment emerges from shared communication rather than pre-fixed preferences. The jury room appears to model this—one juror treats the verdict as open, tests physical evidence experimentally, and gradually shifts collective understanding—yet the film also introduces tensions Dewey rarely addresses: anonymous jurors lacking a shared public identity, an adversarial rather than cooperative structure, and a binary final vote rather than an ongoing experiment. Examine specific scenes—the knife demonstration, the eyeglasses revelation, and Juror 9's reversal—to determine whether each illustrates Deweyan inquiry or shows that democratic judgment depends on personal identification, shame, or authority rather than reason alone. Does the film ultimately vindicate Dewey's claim that democracy works best when it functions like scientific inquiry, or does it suggest that democratic judgment requires individual moral transformation that collective reasoning alone cannot secure? End by saying what the film contributes to this philosophical debate that purely theoretical argument tends to overlook.
