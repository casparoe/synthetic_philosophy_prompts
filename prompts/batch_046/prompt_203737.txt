As a novelist attempting to render the moral texture of public life in an era of perpetual digital publication, I have found myself defending a position I take to be essentially Kierkegaardian: that the modern press, and now its algorithmic descendants, do not merely corrupt discourse but structurally dissolve the conditions under which an individual can stand in authentic, responsible commitment. Where the singular self relates directly to the divine with the full weight of its choice, the public—whether the nineteenth-century crowd or the contemporary feed—offers anonymity without risk, opinion without ownership, and presence without accountability. I treat this not as antiquarian nostalgia but as an active diagnosis of our present. But I want the view genuinely exposed rather than merely ornamented. What would count against it?

Specifically, I ask you to distinguish the defeaters that would be fatal from those merely awkward, and to rank them accordingly. Which observations about collective moral agency, which literary or philosophical counter-examples, and which internal tensions in Kierkegaard's own authorship—his deployment of pseudonymous voices, for instance—would force a defender to concede real damage? Which escape routes remain live: could the view be saved by restricting the public to a specific technological form, or by arguing that pseudonymity is itself a mode of indirect communication rather than a contradiction? And does the diagnosis risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every manifestation of collective opinion is read as further proof of leveling, has the view become a narrative posture immune to evidence, and is that insulation appropriate for a normative theory of this kind? I am not asking for bibliographies or citations I could not verify without access to a library; I want an honest map of where the position is vulnerable, including what its defenders themselves would have to admit if the evidence turned.
