As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and neuroscience, I am assembling a feature on engineered conceptual experiments—scenarios built not to illustrate familiar puzzles but to force specific theoretical verdicts about the architecture of consciousness. I need you to design one such case to order.

Construct a realistic, clinical (not science-fictional) thought experiment in a single paragraph. The subject, designated Case K, is an adult human who, following a precisely localized neurological intervention that spares all primary sensory cortices and intra-modal association areas but eliminates the long-range pathways linking them, retains fully rich conscious experience within each of four modalities—visual, auditory, tactile, and proprioceptive—yet reports and behaves as if there is no phenomenal unity whatsoever: no sense of being a single subject undergoing them together, no cross-modal phenomenal associations, no experiential “togetherness,” though she can intellectually infer that all four streams occur in the same body.

Engineer the case so that the following four positions deliver exactly these verdicts:

- Integrated Information Theory judges that there is not one conscious subject but four distinct minimal conscious entities, because each modality forms its own maximally irreducible integrated complex and the exclusion postulate applies separately to each.
- Global Workspace Theory judges that there is still one conscious subject, but with a fragmented workspace architecture: broadcasting remains robust within each modality yet fails to achieve the cross-modal global integration that normally produces unified experience.
- The phenomenal-concept strategy—the view that the hard problem arises from special phenomenal concepts rather than from an explanatory gap in nature—is forced either to deny that phenomenal unity is a real feature (counterintuitive) or to concede that the physical-functional story is genuinely incomplete regarding unity.
- A property dualist about consciousness finds the case directly supportive: all physical and functional conditions for each modality are satisfied, yet a real phenomenal feature—unity—is absent.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification by walking through the reasoning for each theory. Identify the precise structural feature doing the work—selective preservation of intra-modal neural integration combined with complete abolition of cross-modal phenomenal binding pathways—and explain what happens if this feature is removed (if the pathways are intact, all four theories converge on unified consciousness, collapsing the case). Then present the honest counter-attack available to the defender of the theory that comes out worst—namely, the phenomenal-concept strategist—and state clearly what this experiment is designed to test: whether phenomenal unity can be explained away as a conceptual artifact or whether it requires an irreducible explanatory principle. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the case should be answerable from general philosophical knowledge alone, with no appeal to niche medical literature or exotic physics.
