I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I recently observed a disagreement between two acquaintances—Maya, who has studied comparative religion informally, and Leo, who works in distributed systems design—about whether karma should be understood as a theory of moral desert. The discussion was nuanced enough that I could not referee it myself, so I want a careful adjudication that treats both sides charitably rather than reducing either to a strawman.

Maya opened by arguing that karma is essentially a form of cosmic accounting: the moral quality of an intentional action determines the quality of future conditions, including rebirth and life circumstances, which is precisely what “getting what you deserve” means, even in the absence of a personal deity or judge to administer rewards. Leo countered that this framing imports Western juridical concepts into a framework that is purely causal; he compared karma to a state-transition protocol in a distributed database, where an input state deterministically yields a subsequent state according to fixed rules, with no agent assigning “merit” or “desert.” Maya replied that the rules are not symmetric—only ethically charged actions, not arbitrary physical events, determine the relevant future conditions—so the system is normative rather than merely mechanical. Leo responded that the asymmetry is psychological, not cosmic: ethical actions reshape mental dispositions that then condition experience, just as malicious code introduces technical debt that degrades system performance over time, but we do not say the degradation is “deserved”; we merely describe a structural correlation. Maya pushed back that in karma the suffering or flourishing is specifically indexed to the moral valence of the prior act, not merely to its physical or mental mechanics, which makes the normative “desert” interpretation unavoidable rather than projected. Leo then claimed that this begs the question by assuming the correlation is evaluative rather than descriptive, and that labeling it “desert” is an interpretive overlay we impose because the outcomes feel appropriate to us.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: say who is right about which specific claim, identify what both parties are missing or assuming without argument, and indicate which parts of the dispute are settled among scholars who study Buddhist philosophy versus genuinely contested. In particular, separate settled descriptions of how karma operates in classical sources from contested philosophical interpretations of whether that operation amounts to moral desert, and do not present either interpretation as the consensus view.
