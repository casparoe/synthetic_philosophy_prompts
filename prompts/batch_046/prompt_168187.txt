In debates over whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, I keep seeing a specific argumentative maneuver that I cannot place. One camp holds that guilt is superior because it targets discrete actions and leaves open the possibility of repair; they illustrate this with a person who breaks a promise, apologizes, and restores the relationship. The opposing camp holds that shame is superior because it targets the self and drives deeper moral transformation; they illustrate this with a citizen recognizing complicity in an enduring institutional injustice that no single apology can fix. The move I am tracking occurs when each camp is confronted with the other’s strongest case. The guilt advocate, faced with the institutional-complicity example, does not concede that shame has its own domain; instead she redescribes the feeling as “collective guilt” or “structural guilt,” insisting that because policy change is still possible, the emotion remains action-oriented and reparable. The shame advocate, faced with the broken-promise example, does not concede that guilt has its own domain; instead he redescribes the feeling as “integrity-shame” or “character-based shame,” insisting that the apology is really a form of self-reformation and that the discrete act was only ever a symptom of a flawed self. In both directions, the advocate expands the definition of her preferred emotion to absorb the rival’s paradigm case, rather than allowing a division of emotional labor.

Is there an established name for this exact pattern—not generic motivated reasoning or conceptual stretching in general, but this specific taxonomic absorption in moral-emotional debate, where rival categories are persistently redefined to preserve the supremacy of one? If there is, I want more than the label: I want you to say exactly what makes this the right fit and where its edges lie, including what would count as legitimate conceptual refinement rather than this move, and what case would best expose whether the absorption is happening. If there is not, coin a precise term and define its boundaries, distinguishing it clearly from simple equivocation, from motte-and-bailey-style retreat, and from ordinary theoretical revision. Give me a committed answer: state definitively whether you think this is a genuinely distinct phenomenon or merely an instance of a broader rhetorical failure, defend that judgment with reasons, and identify the nearest existing concepts together with the considerations that separate them from the thing I have described.
