I was discussing collective authorship with two fellow writers during a residency in the Loire Valley — a conversation that began over wine but quickly turned philosophical. One of them, a playwright who edits an annual anthology, argued that any group producing shared work eventually needs what she called a party structure: a defined membership, a shared aesthetic manifesto, and a mechanism for excluding pieces that violate it. Without that, she said, the collective is just a loose network with no identity, incapable of defending its vision against dilution. The other, an essayist who had been reading Simone Weil, objected that the very moment you formalize a shared line and give the group boundaries, you create what Weil saw in parliamentary politics: a machine that manufactures collective passion and replaces individual moral judgment with the warmth of belonging. The playwright replied that Weil was analyzing state parties and mass democracy, not a voluntary cooperative of twelve writers; the scale and the stakes are entirely different, and without editorial discipline — without saying this does not belong — the anthology becomes incoherent noise. The essayist insisted that the mechanism is not about scale but about structure: once there is an us, members begin to police themselves not out of conviction but out of fear of exclusion, and the editorial standard becomes an identity rather than a tool. The playwright asked whether coherence is not worth some social cost, and whether the essayist was not romanticizing isolation; the essayist answered that coherence achieved through what Weil called the gravity of collective belonging is not coherence at all, but conformity disguised as collaboration, and that true collective work requires each contributor to maintain independent attention rather than surrender it to a group passion.

Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about each specific claim — not just who wins overall? In particular, address whether Weil's critique genuinely applies to small voluntary groups, whether some disciplinary boundary is necessary for collective output, and whether there is any form of shared editorial judgment that avoids the dynamics both of them describe. Also, say what, if anything, both parties are missing — for instance, whether there might be intermediate structures that preserve individual judgment without dissolving into incoherence, or whether they are both assuming a false dichotomy between total formalization and pure anarchy.
