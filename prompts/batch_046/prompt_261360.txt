I am preparing material for a graduate seminar on personal identity, and I want to probe origin essentialism—the view that each person necessarily originates from the particular sperm and egg they actually came from—without falling back on overused cloning or teleportation scenarios. To that end, please construct a thought experiment built around tetragametic chimerism, a real but rarely discussed embryological condition in which two separately fertilized embryos fuse in utero so that the resulting individual carries two complete, distinct genomes across different tissues. The scenario should be realistic and confined to one paragraph: imagine an adult who discovers, through a routine clinical blood-type or genetic mismatch during an unrelated medical procedure, that her body is composed of cell lines tracing back to two different conceptions, yet she has lived with a single continuous consciousness, memory, and biography. Your case must pit strict origin essentialism against a rival I will call functional-organism continuity, which holds that personal identity tracks the uninterrupted functioning of a single biological organism regardless of how many genetic lineages contributed to it. The divergence between the two views must emerge naturally from their core commitments rather than being stipulated by fiat, and the scenario must involve no science-fiction technology.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification: state precisely what origin essentialism says about whether the adult is identical to embryo A, embryo B, both, or neither, and explain the reasoning that leads there; then do the same for functional-organism continuity. Identify the exact structural feature of chimerism—namely, a single functioning body with two complete, non-derivative origins—that forces the split between the theories, and explain why altering that feature (for instance, if one genome were merely a mutation-derived subset of the other) collapses the case back into a standard origin-essentialist intuition. Finally, anticipate how a defender of origin essentialism might try to block the counterexample—perhaps by arguing that only one genome counts as the true origin, or that fusion is metaphysically impossible for a single person—and assess whether that response succeeds without begging the question against the rival view.
