You are an expert philosophical educator with deep familiarity in Stoic ethics and its contemporary applications. Before designing anything, briefly brainstorm three structurally different ways a group could be made to experience the instability of an internal/external boundary through rules or assigned roles alone, without being told the concept in advance. Then proceed with the design.

Design a runnable 90-minute workshop activity for 16 graduate students and practitioners in applied philosophy. The theme is Stoicism's modern revival and the dichotomy of control, but approached through a tension rarely foregrounded in popular revival literature: the classical view holds that only our judgments, impulses, and assent are genuinely "up to us," yet modern Stoic practice systematically outsources emotional regulation to journaling apps, workplace resilience protocols, and collective advocacy structures. This creates what we might call a delegation paradox—what is treated as internal (my emotional state or assent) becomes mediated by institutions, algorithms, and group norms that are themselves outside my direct control.

Your deliverable must be a structured role-play or deliberation under constraint—not a lecture, discussion guide, or thought experiment narrated by the facilitator. Specify the exact setting, group division, roles, rules, timeline across the 90 minutes, material needs, and a payoff or incentive scheme that relies on social or procedural stakes rather than money. The rules must be arranged so that participants personally encounter the collapse of the internal/external boundary and feel the practical instability of outsourcing their own assent, all before anyone names "the dichotomy of control" or the delegation paradox.

In particular, include:
- The division of roles and what each role can and cannot control within the simulation
- How the timeline moves from individual choice to mediated/collective constraint
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto (a) the classical dichotomy, (b) the modern outsourcing of emotional regulation, and (c) the precise point where participants discovered they could not locate the boundary between what was theirs and what was not
- What will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, and exactly how the facilitator should handle it without rescuing the experience
- One feature of the classical Stoic view—such as the ideal of complete internal sovereignty over assent—that this activity structurally cannot reproduce, which you must supply explicitly in the debrief, together with an honest explanation of why the activity necessarily fails there

Participants should come away seeing that the dichotomy is not a stable ontological map but a practical achievement that fractures under institutional and technological mediation.

Give a committed response: take a definite position on whether the workshop should conclude that the dichotomy must be abandoned, reconstructed as a collective rather than individual boundary, or retained as an aspirational fiction despite its instability. Defend that choice with reasons tied directly to the structure of the rules and roles you designed. Do not present a balanced menu of options and leave the choice to the facilitator; argue for one.
