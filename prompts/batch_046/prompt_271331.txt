I am preparing background materials for a graduate reading group on political economy and colonialism, and I have become frustrated with the way standard surveys flatten Adam Smith's scattered remarks on empire into a single "anti-mercantilist" line. In my own reading I can distinguish at least three families of interpretation: one that treats Smith as a fiscal realist who opposed the American colonies and the East India Company mainly because they were expensive and monopolistic; one that reads him, especially through the lens of his fears about standing armies and court patronage, as a republican who believed territorial empire would corrupt the liberty of the metropole; and a more recent family that sees in his condemnation of territorial conquest in India and his remarks on Ireland an incipient cosmopolitan or justice-based objection to domination as such. But I also notice that these families split differently depending on whether an interpreter thinks Smith had one unified principle or a context-sensitive stance that varied by the kind of dependency involved—settler colonies versus extractive company territories versus adjacent subjugated kingdoms—so I want to map the logical space rather than just list named scholars.

Please construct a taxonomy of interpretive positions arranged as a decision tree, with each internal node phrased as a concrete claim about Smith's texts or intentions that someone could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to. Start with whether the reader takes Smith's anti-imperial remarks to be governed by a single principle or to be genuinely context-dependent across different kinds of territorial control; then branch on whether the dominant concern, if unified, is fiscal (cost to taxpayers and consumers), constitutional (danger to domestic liberty and representation), or moral-political (injustice to subject populations). Include a further split on how the interpreter handles the distinction between settler colonies, extractive company territories, and adjacent subjugated kingdoms, since this is precisely where several readings diverge without being clearly named in the literature. Every leaf should carry either the name of an established interpretive school or the label "unnamed," together with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—for example, that Smith endorsed "free trade imperialism" as an adequate substitute for formal rule, or that he had no coherent view of empire whatsoever. Where two leaves are genuinely indistinguishable given the available textual evidence, flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than inventing a distinction. In doing so, please keep settled textual points—such as Smith's argument that Britain should voluntarily relinquish the American colonies despite the emotional and strategic cost, and his sharp criticism of the East India Company's territorial acquisitions—clearly separated from genuinely contested judgments, such as whether the Lectures on Jurisprudence contain a developed theory of territorial sovereignty, and do not present any one contested reading as the consensus view among specialists in the history of economic thought.
