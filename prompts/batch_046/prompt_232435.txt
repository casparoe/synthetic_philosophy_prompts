I am designing a single seminar session for political-philosophy graduates familiar with liberal contractarianism but with no background in ancient metaphysics, on Aristotle's argument in the Politics that some people are slaves by nature. The concept is easily flattened into either a racist doctrine or a crude apology for power, and I want to avoid both. I need a teaching plan, not the lecture itself: the dependency order in which the components—teleological function, the soul's rational and non-rational parts, the analogy to household management, and the claim about functional completeness—must be introduced so that the conclusion emerges from internal premises rather than appearing as an alien moral failure; the one concrete scenario or analogy that should carry the explanation, with explicit instructions on what it must include and what it must deliberately exclude to prevent anachronistic misreading; the misconceptions these students are likely to import from modern liberal frameworks (for instance, reading biological determinism into the phrase by nature, or assuming that Aristotle is denying universal human dignity rather than operating with a different conception of human flourishing) and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; a brief check for understanding that would distinguish genuine comprehension from polite acceptance; and what part of Aristotle's broader teleological biology I should openly refuse to unpack at this level. If my framing rests on a confused premise—if, for example, this argument cannot be taught without first establishing his hylomorphism, or if the distinction I am drawing between natural slavery and racial ideology is itself unsustainable—please state that plainly rather than accommodating the request.
