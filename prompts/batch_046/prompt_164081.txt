Read Charlie Chaplin’s *Modern Times* (1936) through Hannah Arendt’s tripartite distinction among labor, work, and action, but apply the lens specifically to the problem of automation rather than to alienation in general. I want a reading that asks whether the film treats mechanization as primarily the automation of labor—the cyclical, biological process of sustaining life—or the automation of work—the linear, world-building fabrication of durable objects. Take seriously the sequence in which the Tramp is swallowed by factory gears and the scene with the automatic feeding machine: do these images suggest that automation collapses Arendt’s clean separation between the laboring body and the fabricating hand, producing a single process in which human movement is reduced to machine-part motion? And in the final scene, where the Tramp and the gamin walk hand in hand toward an uncertain horizon, does their dance represent a recovery of action in Arendt’s sense—appearing in public, initiating something genuinely new—or does it remain trapped within the logic of survival and escape, never achieving the plurality and worldliness that action requires?

At the same time, I want the reading to resist reducing Chaplin’s visual comedy to an illustration of philosophical taxonomy. Does the film in fact know something Arendt’s categories miss? The feeding-machine episode, for instance, treats eating—paradigmatic labor for Arendt—as an engineered, linear, efficient operation, almost as if the body itself were being fabricated rather than sustained. If so, does *Modern Times* suggest that automation does not merely eliminate jobs but dissolves the conceptual distinctions—between labor and work, between body and tool, between repetition and initiation—that make “the meaning of work” philosophically tractable in the first place? I am preparing this for a discussion on automation and human flourishing, and I want a criticism that takes both Chaplin’s bodily rhetoric and Arendt’s ontology seriously: where the framework illuminates the film’s horror and its fragile hope, where the film’s slapstick logic forces the philosophy to accommodate a blurrier ontology of mechanized existence, and whether the Tramp’s final gesture adds to the debate something that argument about labor and automation alone cannot supply.
