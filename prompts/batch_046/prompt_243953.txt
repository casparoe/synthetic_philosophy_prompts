Exam question: In Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," he attacks two interrelated assumptions of logical empiricism. First, explain his argument against the analytic–synthetic distinction, focusing specifically on why attempts to ground analyticity in synonymy, definitional reduction, or interchangeability salva veritate fail or are circular. Second, apply this critique to the claim—common in some cognitive-science research programs—that there is a sharp boundary between "innate representational formats" and "acquired inferential roles." Would Quine treat this boundary as analytic, synthetic, or framework-relative? In your answer, clearly distinguish Quine's position from Carnap's principle of tolerance regarding linguistic frameworks.

Student answer: Quine's first dogma is the belief in a fundamental analytic–synthetic split. He argues that philosophers have tried to define analytic statements as true by meaning alone, but explaining "meaning" requires synonymy, and synonymy is usually explained by saying two terms can be interchanged salva veritate in all contexts—except that identifying those contexts already requires knowing which substitutions preserve truth, which is just the analytic–synthetic distinction in disguise. Definitional reduction fares no better: reducing an analytic claim to a logical truth plus definitions requires that we already know which definitions are legitimate, and legitimacy is usually cashed out in terms of analyticity. So the circle is unavoidable. Quine concludes that there is no sharp boundary; all statements belong to a web of belief that confronts experience as a corporate body.

Applying this to the innate-format versus acquired-role boundary: Quine would deny it is analytic. The distinction is a theoretical posit embedded in our psychological framework, not a truth fixed by linguistic convention. Here Quine's second dogma—the rejection of reductionism—is also relevant. Just as individual empirical hypotheses cannot be tested in isolation but only within a larger theory, so the claim that a particular representational format is innate cannot be evaluated independently of the broader cognitive architecture in which it sits. Therefore the boundary is synthetic and holistically revisable.

That said, Quine recognizes that some beliefs are more central to the web than others. This centrality is pragmatic: we resist revising logic or mathematics not because they are analytic, but because altering them would cause massive disruption elsewhere. In this respect Quine is close to Carnap, who in his principle of tolerance allows us to adopt linguistic frameworks and make certain distinctions analytic by explicit stipulation within them. Both thinkers allow practical distinctions to guide inquiry. However, Quine rejects Carnap's idea that stipulation creates a genuinely analytic class, since for Quine even conventional rules are part of the total empirical system and are revisable in principle for overall theoretical economy. So while Carnap might allow the innate–acquired boundary to be analytic within a chosen framework, Quine insists it remains synthetic—deeply entrenched, perhaps, but never conventionally fixed.
