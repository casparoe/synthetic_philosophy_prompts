I am a student in an advanced seminar on cross-cultural moral obligations, and we have been examining whether visitors are bound by local conventions—particularly those lacking legal force but structuring social interaction, such as tipping norms, queueing protocols, and pedestrian rules. Our instructor has delivered consistent verdicts on the following cases, yet I believe her reasoning may depend on a distinction that is not fully explicit: specifically, whether an obligation derives from the functional structure of an interaction (which I have been calling instrumental deference) or from the affirmation of shared social membership through ritual (which I am calling expressive deference).

Her recorded verdicts are as follows:

1. A Canadian tourist refuses to leave a fifteen percent gratuity at a full-service restaurant in the United States, arguing that in her home jurisdiction tipping is purely discretionary and not embedded in wage structures. Verdict: she acts wrongly; the convention operates here as a de facto compensation mechanism, and a visitor who participates in the transactional framework is bound by its terms.

2. A visitor in London advances past an orderly queue for a bus during peak hours. Verdict: clearly wrong; queueing in this context solves a collective coordination problem, and violation imposes unfair costs on others.

3. A tourist in Singapore crosses a multi-lane arterial road against a red signal during heavy traffic, noting that the street is physically crossable. Verdict: wrong; the convention serves a direct protective function, and disregard creates both danger and disorder.

4. A pedestrian in Berlin crosses an empty residential street against a red light at two in the morning, despite the presence of local pedestrians who wait. Verdict: not a moral failure—the behavior is perhaps socially marked, but in this context the rule serves no protective or coordinative end.

5. A houseguest in Tokyo declines to remove footwear upon entering a private residence, citing hygiene preferences. Verdict: wrong, though the wrongness is specific to the breach of hospitality norms; the obligation arises from the host's authority over the domestic space rather than from any universal moral law.

The case for our next session involves a traveler in a provincial French town who enters a small, family-run bakery, places the correct coins on the counter for a baguette without verbal greeting, conducts the exchange entirely in English, and exits without acknowledgment. Local practice requires a brief vocal greeting upon entry—bonjour—and minimal reciprocal acknowledgment between customer and proprietor, even in brief transactions. The traveler argues that the exchange was efficient, respectful in its precision, and that imposing a linguistic ritual is an arbitrary imposition.

Please address the following:

First, predict the instructor's verdict on the bakery case, explaining which features of the interaction she would likely emphasize.

Second, articulate at least two distinct general principles—each fully compatible with all five verdicts above—that could ground her reasoning. One should align closely with instrumental deference; the other should align closely with expressive deference. For each, show how it explains both the positive obligations (cases 1, 2, 3, and 5) and the absence of obligation (case 4).

Third, state which principle you believe her predicted verdict relies upon, and then design one further hypothetical case—clearly distinct from those listed—that would force these two principles to yield opposing judgments, thereby revealing which framework she actually employs.
