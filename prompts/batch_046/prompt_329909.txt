At a recent philosophy of mind colloquium, two colleagues—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—became locked in a disagreement about split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness that I found genuinely difficult to referee, and I should be grateful if you could adjudicate their exchange with precision. The dispute concerns not merely whether callosotomy produces two minds, but what criterion of unity is appropriate and whether recent interpretations of confabulation data have been misread. Please proceed move by move: rate each contribution from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, identify any hidden premises, say what each party is missing, and conclude with an overall assessment of who is more right and what a defensible middle position might look like. The exchange should be treated as reported by a bystander; do not treat me as taking sides.

Vasquez opened by challenging what she called the “Sperry default”: the assumption that unity of consciousness requires direct interhemispheric information transfer. She proposed that unity is better understood as a functional property of narrative integration. If a system can generate a single, coherent autobiographical narrative that incorporates information originating in either hemisphere—even if the raw perceptual data are initially processed separately—then consciousness remains unified. She noted that in standard divided-field tasks, the left hemisphere routinely confabulates explanations for right-hemisphere behavior, which she takes not as evidence that a second subject is being silenced, but as evidence of a single integrating mechanism working with incomplete data.

Chen granted that confabulation occurs, but argued that Vasquez’s narrative-integration criterion is both too permissive and descriptively inaccurate. He claimed it demonstrates precisely the absence of the right hemisphere’s experiential perspective within the left’s narrative, not its successful incorporation. To make the point concrete, he described a hypothetical but experimentally plausible variant of the divided-field task: a split-brain subject is presented with a red circle in the left visual field (right hemisphere) and, simultaneously, with the spoken word blue presented auditorily to the left hemisphere. The right hemisphere is then asked to point to a matching color card, and points to red; the left hemisphere, asked verbally, reports blue. Crucially, when the left hemisphere is shown the right hand’s selection and asked to explain it, it does not confabulate a bridge—say, that the circle looked purplish—but instead insists that the right hand made an error or was not responding to the task. Chen took this stable, non-integrated contradiction—two hemispheres holding incompatible attitudes toward the same object without either being revised—to show that there are two distinct subjects of experience, not one divided subject.

Vasquez countered that Chen was illicitly reifying the right hemisphere’s belief from a behavioral response. She argued that pointing is not belief-attribution; without linguistic articulation, we have no grounds to ascribe a propositional attitude to the right hemisphere, only a subpersonal processing disposition. By her lights, the left hemisphere’s dismissal of the right-hand response is not the suppression of an alien subject, but the normal operation of a dominant control system filtering out noise. She added that if we applied Chen’s criterion of stable, non-revisable contradiction within an intact brain—say, to a person with strong implicit bias who explicitly denies it—we would be forced to conclude that there are two people inside every ambivalent agent.

Chen replied that Vasquez’s demand for linguistic articulation stacks the deck in favor of the left hemisphere and ignores the possibility of non-linguistic phenomenal consciousness. He proposed a more modest criterion: if a cognitive subsystem can guide goal-directed behavior in a way that is systematically decoupled from another subsystem’s goals, and if this decoupling is stable across contexts rather than momentary, then we have plural consciousness. He noted that this does not require language, only functional autonomy. Vasquez then asked whether, on this criterion, the enteric nervous system or the cerebellum might also count as separate subjects, since they guide behavior with some autonomy. Chen conceded that his criterion needs a boundary condition—perhaps the presence of a unified perceptual field or a minimal self-model—but insisted that split-brain hemispheres meet it, whereas the gut does not.

Please evaluate Vasquez’s initial narrative-integration proposal, Chen’s counterexample and his criterion of stable contradiction, Vasquez’s charge of reification together with her ambivalence analogy, Chen’s functional-autonomy reply and his concession about boundary conditions, and Vasquez’s final challenge involving the enteric nervous system. In particular, say whether Chen’s hypothetical task genuinely isolates the issue or smuggles in its conclusion, whether Vasquez’s analogy to implicit bias is apt, and whether Chen’s boundary condition is ad hoc. Finish with an overall verdict and a brief sketch of what a more careful position on both sides might retain.
