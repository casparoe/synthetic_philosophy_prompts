I am preparing a graduate seminar on moral worth and the conditions under which acting for the right reasons confers credit upon an agent. I have constructed the following argument, which strikes me as valid yet drives towards a conclusion I find intolerable, and I should like to know precisely where the misstep occurs.

Consider these premises:

(1) An agent possesses moral worth with respect to an action only if the reason for which she acts is identical to the normative feature that makes the action right—the 'right reasons' condition.

(2) If the agent's motivating reason is not identical to that normative feature, she lacks moral worth, even when her reason reliably tracks or causally covaries with it.

(3) Take an agent who relieves suffering. Her motivating reason is that the suffering disturbs her own equilibrium. The normative feature is the intrinsic badness of suffering. These are not identical, so by (1) and (2) she lacks worth.

(4) Suppose she reforms her motivation through moral education: she now acts because she judges suffering to be intrinsically bad.

(5) Yet her grasp of that badness is mediated entirely by testimony and conceptual inheritance—she holds it de dicto, as 'what my moral community asserts is bad', rather than de re, through direct normative insight.

(6) If moral worth requires not merely the correct reason but a non-derivative, autonomous grasp of it—otherwise testimony could transmit worth, which seems wrong—then she still lacks worth.

(7) By extension, anyone whose moral cognition depends on education, language, or social transmission lacks the requisite autonomous grasp.

(8) Therefore, only agents with entirely self-originated, conceptually transparent moral insight possess moral worth.

(9) But such agents are either mythical or indistinguishable from ideal moral philosophers; ordinary benevolent agents, including those we most admire, are excluded.

(10) This is absurd.

(11) Hence (1) must be false, or at least (6) must be.

My difficulty is that I should prefer to retain (1)—it captures the intuition that acting from self-interested discomfort is not creditworthy—yet I also wish to preserve the idea that moral knowledge can be transmitted without dissolving worth. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? Does the argument confuse the content of a reason with the mode of its cognitive access, or is there a hidden slide between normative identity and epistemic autonomy? I should be grateful for a diagnosis that treats each step with charity.
