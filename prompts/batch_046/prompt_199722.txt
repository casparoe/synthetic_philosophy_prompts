I've been trying to map out the implicit decision rules behind how people in my philosophy-of-mind discussion group judge personal-identity cases in gradual-replacement protocols—not the standard Ship of Theseus, but specifically substrate-change scenarios that involve temporal gaps, redundancy, and distributed architecture. To keep things concrete, I've recorded the verdicts one particularly consistent interlocutor has given across five hypotheticals, and I want to see how her framework extends to a structurally novel case.

Her track record is as follows. First, gradual neuron-for-neuron replacement with synthetic equivalents over a decade: she says the original person survives, because causal-functional structure is preserved continuously. Second, a destructive scan-and-upload, original brain destroyed and copy booted on silicon: she calls this a duplicate, not survival, even with perfect psychological fidelity. Third, instantaneous total replacement with an identical synthetic brain: again, no survival, because the causal bridge is missing. Fourth, a replacement that proceeds for five years, pauses entirely for another five years with a half-natural, half-synthetic brain living normally, then resumes: she still counts it as the same person, so temporal discontinuity in the replacement process itself doesn't break identity. Fifth, a cloning-style protocol where synthetic neurons are inserted alongside biological ones, then the originals are pruned away, so that for months there are two fully coupled brains supporting one stream of consciousness: she says the original survives and the extras are not competing persons, which suggests her view tolerates temporary redundancy as long as there's a primary causal stream.

The new case introduces a niche structural feature. Here replacement is still neuron-by-neuron and causally continuous, but the synthetic substrates are not localized in the skull. They form a distributed mesh of nodes linked by low-latency connections; during transition some nodes sit outside the body, and some processing modules are briefly shared resources—hardware that also serves other cognitive systems—though the specific information-flow topology mapped to this individual's brain is preserved uniquely. By the end, the cognitive architecture is fully distributed across multiple physical locations with no single container, every node traces back causally to the original replacement sequence, there is never more than one complete functional topology instantiated, but the system operates with heterogeneous temporal dynamics—some nodes running at accelerated clock speeds, others decelerated—while remaining causally coupled. The subjective experience, if any, is presumably unified.

Given this track record, what should she say—does the original person survive, or is this a discontinuity? I'm less interested in my own intuition than in extracting the competing rules that fit her first five verdicts equally well. One rule might hold that identity requires spatiotemporal contiguity within a single bounded physical system; another might hold that only the preservation of a specific causal-informational graph matters, regardless of spatial distribution or internal temporal heterogeneity, provided the graph never branches into two independent full copies; a third might add a uniqueness constraint that forbids even temporary shared substrate. Say which rule best explains her pattern, what she should predict for the distributed mesh case on that rule, and—crucially—what further hypothetical case would actually distinguish the spatiotemporal-contiguity rule from the graph-preservation rule. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, be explicit about what principle she would be adopting with each choice, identify the closest analog among her earlier five, and state what further verdict would settle it.
