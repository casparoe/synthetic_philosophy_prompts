I'm working on a feature about anticipations of modern speech-act theory in eighteenth-century philosophy of mind. I've been studying a passage in Thomas Reid's Essays on the Active Powers of the Human Mind (1788) in which he argues that a promise does not merely report an existing intention but actually creates a new moral relation between speaker and hearer—something that sounds remarkably like J.L. Austin's later account of performative utterances. Reid adds, however, that this creative power depends on a specifically social mental faculty that cannot operate unless both parties recognize each other as rational agents. I'm struggling to determine whether Reid is proposing an early conventionalist theory—where the binding force comes from shared institutional rules—or whether he believes it derives from natural rational capacities that happen to be social in form. Could you help me understand the passage? In particular, is Reid's position closer to Austin's claim that performatives depend on conventional procedures, or to John Searle's view that institutional facts require collective intentionality? And given Reid's requirement of mutual recognition, how should we assess performative utterances directed at entities that lack rational agency—say, a corporate legal fiction treated as the sole addressee of a contract, or an oath administered in the presence of an artificial system? Would Reid regard these as defective promises, as category mistakes, or as something else entirely?
