I am drafting a short analytical essay on the French Revolution that reframes the classic question of whether revolutions devour their children. Rather than treating the Terror as either a metaphysical law of revolution or a purely ideological phenomenon, I propose modeling it as a specific institutional cascade: once the Legislative Assembly collapsed into the Committee of Public Safety, the mechanism of political accountability shifted from deliberative representation to what I call predictive security logic, in which internal dissent was interpreted not as legitimate disagreement but as structural vulnerability to be preempted. I then ask whether this cascade was overdetermined by class conflict—the bourgeois revolution needing simultaneously to suppress aristocratic reaction and contain sans-culottes pressure—or genuinely contingent on a particular sequence of crises, such that a different ordering might have preserved plural deliberation.

The essay sketches three interpretations. First, the class-struggle view holds that the cascade was functionally necessary for the bourgeois revolution to complete itself, with the Terror representing the logical institutional form of that completion. Second, the ideological view holds that the cascade was driven by the internal logic of revolutionary virtue, in which demands for transparency and suspicion became inseparable regardless of social base. Third, the contingency view holds that the cascade required specific trigger events—the Brunswick Manifesto, the September Massacres, the trial and execution of Louis XVI, and the defection of the Girondins—that could have been absent or differently ordered, and that without them predictive security logic would not have achieved hegemony.

Against these, I propose a hybrid framing I call contingent overdetermination: class conflict created the pressure gradient that made institutional collapse likely, but it did not specify the form of resolution; that form—predictive security logic replacing deliberative representation—was contingent on the crisis sequence. Once adopted, however, it became self-reinforcing in ways that made reversal impossible without external military defeat.

- Does my framing of contingent overdetermination genuinely differ from standard contingency arguments, or is it merely a relabeling that adds no explanatory power?
- Are there obvious historical countermechanisms—such as the economic context of assignats, price controls, and war supply—that undermine an institutional-focus narrative?
- Does the framing unfairly neglect the ideological dimension, specifically whether revolutionary virtue was not just rhetorical cover for security logic but an independent causal force that shaped which institutional forms were thinkable?
- Is there a missing fourth interpretation—perhaps one centered on geopolitical encirclement and war emergency—that my triad suppresses?
