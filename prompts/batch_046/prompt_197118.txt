I'm designing the final exam for an upper-level seminar on Enlightenment political thought. Write one 90-minute essay question on Rousseau's critique of theater in the Letter to d'Alembert on Spectacles (1758). The question must be unanswerable by reciting his standard objections—vice, luxury, and the substitution of appearance for reality. Instead, it should require students to analyze the structural claim that theatrical representation severs judgment from action by making the spectator a passive consumer of moral images, and then to test this claim against Rousseau's own account in The Social Contract of the legislator's use of symbolic ceremony and public visibility to establish the general will. Ask whether this creates an internal tension: can the legislator's staged authority avoid the very theatrical corruption Rousseau condemns?

Provide: (a) the exam question, calibrated for a 1,200-word answer; (b) a model answer of about 700 words that identifies the conceptual crux—what conditions representation must satisfy to avoid severing judgment from action—and resolves it without collapsing into either a defense of modern theater or a simple repetition of Rousseau's civic nostalgia; and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 points that separates an A from a B answer by listing the three specific moves an A answer must contain (with point values), noting the failure mode typical of a B answer, and identifying two misconceptions that should cost marks—such as conflating Rousseau's critique with blanket anti-art sentiment, or treating the legislator's ceremony as unproblematically theatrical without addressing the consistency problem.
