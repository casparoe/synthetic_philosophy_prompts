I recently spent several hours arguing with a close friend about tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while pleasure, knowledge, achievement, or beauty are at best instrumentally valuable insofar as they prevent or dissolve those negative states, and at worst neutral distractions. She insisted that if one takes this seriously, normative ethics collapses into a single project: minimize dukkha and tanha. I pushed back by suggesting that even within a tranquilist framework, genuinely open conceptual questions remain that determine whether the view is coherent, actionable, or merely a restatement of familiar negative-utilitarian commitments. I am now trying to decide where to direct my own thinking, and I want a defended agenda rather than a survey.

Specifically, I see at least four lines of work that could be pursued. First, there is the metaphysical-psychological question of whether craving and suffering are sufficiently unitary states to serve as sole axiological anchors, or whether a finer taxonomy—anticipatory versus consummatory craving, existential versus somatic suffering, second-order cravings about craving itself—fractures the view into an unmanageable pluralism that undermines its practical guidance. Second, there is the normative-implication question of whether tranquilism entails a strong form of anti-natalism or only a weak preference for non-creation, and whether that distinction survives once one allows for the possibility of lives that contain minimal suffering but also minimal awareness, raising the problem of whether absence in an unaware being counts at all. Third, there is the comparative-axiological question of how tranquilism interacts with asymmetry debates in population ethics: does the absence of suffering in a non-existent being provide a reason not to create them, and if so, does that reason dominate all others or must it compete with structural considerations? Fourth, there is the practical-political question of whether tranquilist commitments should lead us to prioritize institutional reforms that reduce structural craving—for example, economic systems designed to minimize status competition—over direct suffering-alleviation, and whether that ordering depends on empirical claims about causal efficacy that conceptual philosophy can evaluate only indirectly.

Given that I have limited time—roughly the equivalent of one focused semester—which of these four should I treat as upstream of the others, and have I overlooked a line of inquiry that is more fundamental? I want the reasoning to be explicit about criteria: tractability for conceptual rather than empirical work, how many other questions depend on resolving this one, and what kind of argument or distinction over the next year would cause you to reorder the priorities. I am skeptical that the population-ethics branch is tractable without first settling whether craving can be compared interpersonally, but I am open to correction. Please do not provide a bibliography or citation list; I am interested in the architecture of the problem and in knowing whether tranquilism stands or falls on its own conceptual feet, not in mapping it onto Buddhist ethics or Schopenhauerian pessimism.
