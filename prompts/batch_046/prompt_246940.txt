As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun serious study of philosophy of mind, I am designing the final examination for a graduate seminar I am auditing on physicalism and mental causation. I want one essay question that students cannot answer merely by reproducing the standard summary of Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument together with the familiar menu of responses—supervenient dualism, emergentism, and so forth. Instead, I want the question to press on a tension that I, coming from a background in complex systems and experimental design, find particularly acute: whether an interventionist conception of causation, of the kind developed in the philosophy of science for analyzing experimental manipulation, can be applied to mental properties in a way that escapes the exclusion argument, or whether it simply relocates the problem.

Specifically, the question should ask students to evaluate the following claim: if mental properties exhibit stability across microphysical perturbations—that is, if they remain invariant under a range of interventions on underlying physical states—then they satisfy the conditions for being genuine causes within an interventionist framework, without violating the causal closure of the physical domain. Students should be required to determine whether this stability genuinely distinguishes mental causation from mere correlation or epiphenomenal shadowing, and whether invoking it forces the non-reductive physicalist to abandon the standard supervenience relation or to accept a form of downward causation that conflicts with closure.

Please write:

- One essay question appropriate for a 90-minute examination, expecting approximately 1,200 to 1,500 words.
- A model answer of roughly 800 words that demonstrates the precise moves required for a top mark: a rigorous reconstruction of the exclusion argument's premises, including the closure principle and the non-overdetermination condition; a clear explanation of how interventionist causation differs from nomological or counterfactual accounts; an analysis of whether stability-under-intervention can be coherently attributed to mental properties given physical closure; an identification of the exact point at which the argument either succeeds or collapses; and a defended verdict.
- A marking scheme allocating 20 marks total, with explicit point values for each of the five moves listed above, a one-line distinction between an A answer, which integrates the moves into a critical argument with independent judgment, and a B answer, which covers the moves competently but with weak integration, and a note on the most common conceptual error—specifically, conflating interventionist stability with statistical robustness or correlation—indicating how many marks it should forfeit.

The question must be answerable without access to external sources, should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should avoid dependence on highly specialized literature that a graduate student in philosophy of mind might not have encountered in standard coursework. Please present the material in a polished, formal register suitable for an examination committee.
