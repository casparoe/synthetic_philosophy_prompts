In classical Islamic jurisprudence, legal liability for harm is assigned through human actions that are treated as stable, effective causes: a person who digs an uncovered pit in a public path is held responsible if someone falls in; one who leaves an unattended fire is liable when it spreads to neighboring property; poison introduced into food is traced back to the hand that administered it. These judgments presuppose that natural processes—gravity, combustion, digestion—operate with sufficient regularity to link an agent's deed to its outcome, making compensation and retribution intelligible. Yet the dominant Ash'arite theological framework, articulated with particular rigor by Al-Ghazali, denies that any created thing possesses inherent causal efficacy. On this view, fire does not burn cotton by its own nature; God creates the burning directly in each particular instance, and what we call cause and effect is only a divinely ordained habitual conjunction that carries no necessary connection.

What philosophical commitments about causation, moral agency, and institutional authority does this legal practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that allows jurists to treat human acts as genuine sources of liability for outcomes that, according to the official metaphysics, God alone produces. Does the practice depend on a tacit doctrine of secondary causation that Al-Ghazali's occasionalism rejects, or can occasionalism accommodate legal reasoning by treating human actions merely as occasions for divinely created harm? Examine whether the practice is philosophically consistent with the theology, or whether it relies on a quiet partition—metaphysics for theologians, causal attribution for judges. Say which rival views of causation the legal system rules out, which it merely fits well with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, describe what would have to change in the rules of liability—how blame, compensation, and punishment would be reconceived—if the legal system genuinely adopted Al-Ghazali's occasionalism without this partition, treating every harm as directly authored by God and human acts as coincident rather than causative.
