As a novelist constructing a near-future society governed by a peculiar deliberative institution, I need to understand what philosophical commitments I am quietly smuggling into its design, and I would value your careful, rigorous analysis. You are an expert in moral philosophy, political theory, and institutional design; please think step by step, begin with some brief brainstorming about the implicit conceptual framework, and then reconstruct it with precision. The practice is this: citizens do not vote on long-term constitutional amendments according to their present, possibly uninformed or contradictory preferences. Instead, they enter a structured “reflective arbitration” protocol—guided by synthesized expert testimony, formal consistency-checking exercises, and a model of what they would endorse if they were more informed, more coherent, and closer to the people they aspire to become. The output is treated as the citizens’ own “extrapolated volition” and is binding for multi-generational policy. The architects insist this is not paternalism because authority derives from the citizens’ deeper values, yet critics observe that the protocol silently defines “deeper” in terms of cognitive coherence and long-range consistency rather than emotional authenticity, present suffering, or relational attachment. What theory of value and personal identity does this institution strictly presuppose? Must it assume there is a fact of the matter about one’s “true” preferences, or can it be sustained on a purely constructivist view in which coherence merely produces a decision procedure? Is it compatible with liberal neutrality, or does it embed a perfectionist conception of the good? And if we replaced its underlying view of idealized preference with a relational or care-ethical framework that treats present attachments as non-substitutable, which parts of the institution would collapse and which could be rebuilt? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, note where it is philosophically underdetermined, and indicate what a policy built on present, non-extrapolated welfare commitments would have to change.
