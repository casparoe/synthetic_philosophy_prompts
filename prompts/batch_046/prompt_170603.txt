Leibniz defines justice as caritas sapientis, the charity of the wise, which directs rational beings to love God and to promote the happiness and perfection of other rational creatures according to their merit. Because God selected the actual world through the principle of sufficient reason—applying a metaphysical calculus that weighs the total perfection and simplicity of each possible world—the actual world is not merely the best option but the only rationally admissible one, selected with the same modal necessity that governs geometric truths. Within this system, moral improvement does not involve changing the global quantity of perfection—since the world is already optimally arranged—but rather consists in the agent's intellectual recognition that her own motives are necessary links in the pre-established harmony. For Leibniz, a free choice is spontaneous: it proceeds from the agent's internal nature rather than from external coercion, yet it remains fully determined by prior mental states with complete inevitability. Consequently, caritas sapientis requires that the wise possess a comprehensive grasp of how every local action integrates into the universal calculus of sufficient reasons; without such omniscient insight, human beings can only approximate justice imperfectly.
