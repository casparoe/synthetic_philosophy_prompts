The school is putting on a debate-night thing in his honour, and they've roped me into giving the opening remarks, maybe four minutes if I read it slowly. The audience is split right down the middle: half are family members who just want a nice send-off and won't know Malthus from Malthouse, and the other half are his old students plus a couple of local lecturers who will definately catch me if I fudge the philosophy. I want the whole speech structured around whether a theory can be 'right but early' — specifically Malthus's population-against-food prediction, which Mr Davies always taught even after everyone said it was exstinguished by the Green Revolution. It can't just be decoration; the idea needs to shape what I can say about why he kept teaching it, and also put a hard limit on what I'm allowed to claim about the future without sounding like I'm predicting the buffet will run out.

Can you write it in these four parts, please? First, the hook — about 100 words — starting with that 2019 lesson where he drew the asymptote on the board and half the class laughed because we'd just covered industrial agriculture in history. Second, the rescue argument — around 220 words — explaining how falsification in 1900 doesn't kill a long-run prediction if the boundary conditions shift; keep it honest enough that the economists nod, but don't lose his sister-in-law with jargon. Third, the structural gap — about 120 words — where the philosophy actually bites: if we rescue Malthus by saying he was only wrong about the timeline, what are we now commited to about resource limits, and what must I leave unsaid in the toast? Fourth, the toast itself — about 80 words — tying back to Mr Davies affectionately, without turning into a lecture. Write it in British English, keep my voice casual, and don't tidy up the typo I made with 'definately' — just leave it in.
