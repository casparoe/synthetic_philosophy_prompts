I'm putting together my commitments on privacy for a seminar consistency audit and want to check whether they actually hang together. Here is the set:

(1) I understand privacy structurally as "contextual opacity"—the maintenance of boundaries that keep information disclosed in one social sphere from being automatically accessible in others—rather than as individual control over data flows.
(2) I think the value of privacy is entirely instrumental: it protects autonomy, prevents discrimination, and makes intimacy possible, but carries no independent moral weight of its own.
(3) I hold that fully informed, uncoerced consent eliminates privacy wrongs: if I authorize a broker to merge my medical and financial records, I have not suffered a privacy violation, even though contextual opacity is destroyed.
(4) I regard state surveillance as categorically graver than corporate data extraction, but solely because it is non-consensual and backed by coercive power, not because it violates opacity in any distinctive way.
(5) I deny any "right to be forgotten," maintaining that the integrity of collective archives should generally override individual requests to erase prior disclosures.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real conflict is. For each tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal. Then tell me which single revision to one commitment would clean up the most problems with the least damage to the overall view, and whether any remaining friction is merely apparent.
