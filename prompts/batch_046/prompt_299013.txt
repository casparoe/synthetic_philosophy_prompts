After a long argument with a friend last night about whether phenomenal consciousness could be an illusion, I tried to formalize the worry that illusionism collapses into self-refutation, but now the chain feels suspiciously tight and I want to know exactly where it breaks. Here is the argument I sketched. First, illusionism holds that phenomenal consciousness does not exist; instead, brain states merely represent themselves as having qualitative character, and this representation is false. Second, for any false representation to produce an appearance of consciousness, there must be something it is like for the subject to entertain that representation; otherwise there is no experiential appearance to be mistaken about. Third, that “something it is like” just is phenomenal consciousness directed at the representational vehicle itself, not at the represented world-state. Fourth, so illusionism eliminates phenomenal properties from the represented content but necessarily retains them in the vehicle that does the representing. Fifth, this means illusionism is not a radical denial of phenomenal consciousness but only a relocation of it onto the act of misrepresentation. Sixth, since relocation is fully compatible with the existence of phenomenal consciousness somewhere in the system, illusionism fails to establish its advertised conclusion that phenomenal consciousness is absent altogether. Seventh, therefore illusionism either contradicts itself or collapses into a harmless relocation thesis, neither of which achieves its intended eliminative goal. Where does this go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to defend the second step, but I am willing to concede the slide from vehicle-consciousness to general existence if that is the price of consistency.
