At a philosophy of science discussion group, two regulars argued about whether Popper's problem of verisimilitude can be solved structurally. Maya claimed that without an objective metric over an infinite-dimensional theory space, any false theory can be arbitrarily close to the truth by tweaking irrelevant parameters, so talk of one false theory being closer than another collapses into convention. Leo responded that scientists do not weight all dimensions equally: false Newtonian mechanics retains correct symmetry groups and conservation laws, while a curve-fitted Ptolemaic model retains neither, so the structural distance is genuinely smaller. Maya then raised what she called partition dependency — the judgment that Newton retains structure depends on how we partition theory-space; if we slice by predictive accuracy at quantum scales, Newton scores worse than a finely tuned epicyclic model. Leo conceded the dependency but argued it merely forces us to justify our weighting scheme rather than abandon verisimilitude, proposing that we weight dimensions by intervention stability — the robustness of predictions under active manipulation — which privileges structural theories. Maya countered that intervention stability is itself theory-laden, since identifying an intervention requires a prior causal ontology, so Leo has not escaped circularity but hidden it in his metric.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate each round: is Maya correct that parameter tweaking undermines metric realism, or does Leo's structural-retention reply hold? Does partition dependency defeat him, or can it be managed by justified weighting? Is intervention stability a legitimate escape, or does Maya's circularity objection stand? Say who is right about what, and what both miss — especially whether a middle path exists between metric realism and conventionalism. Separate settled results from genuinely contested positions among specialists, and do not present a disputed view as the consensus.
