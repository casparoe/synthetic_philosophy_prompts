In integrated pest management protocols adopted across North American and European regulatory frameworks, invertebrate organisms are sorted into three functional categories—pests, beneficials, and non-targets—rather than classified by phylogenetic proximity to humans or by any explicit metric of sentience or capacity for suffering. A broad-spectrum insecticide may be licensed despite causing high mortality among non-target soil arthropods, provided population-level recovery is projected within a growing season; the same aggregate mortality applied to a vertebrate population of comparable biomass would typically trigger immediate suspension. Meanwhile, recent amendments to these protocols have introduced pollinator-protection provisions, yet these apply almost exclusively to bees and butterflies—species whose ecological service value is visible and quantifiable—while soil-dwelling invertebrates remain outside the protective scope. The category of pest itself is defined not by any intrinsic property but by its relation to human agricultural interest, allowing the same species to be protected in one region and exterminated in another. What philosophical conception of moral status, harm, and ecological value does this tripartite regulatory scheme presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes the distinction between acceptable aggregate harm to soil fauna, protected status for pollinators, and exclusionary targeting of pests appear coherent. Then distinguish what this practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify whether it rests on a single consistent view—such as a consequentialist aggregation weighted by ecosystem services—or whether it silently combines incompatible commitments, and indicate what would have to change in agricultural policy if the underlying view were replaced by one that assigned moral consideration uniformly according to sentience capacity or uniformly according to ecological role regardless of human utility.
