They need a reusable decision procedure grounded in lifeboat ethics, but I want it to move beyond standard utility-maximising triage by incorporating the concept of "moral remainder" — the idea, familiar from debates on moral luck and dirty hands, that justifiably excluding a survivor still imposes a genuine moral cost that ought to be formally acknowledged rather than erased by the decision's correctness.

The checklist should run in ordered steps that a coordinator can complete in under fifteen minutes without legal counsel present, with each step specifying what counts as a sufficient factual answer, marking the precise point where the procedure stops with a mandatory "no allocation" result, flagging the junctures where escalation to a senior officer is required because the issue turns on values rather than empirical assessment, and identifying which steps are irreducible judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Apply it to two cases: first, deciding whether to reserve the final two spaces in a life-raft for uninjured passengers when three injured survivors are present and including any injured party risks capsizing; second, distributing a single effective dose of antivenom between two crew members who have received identical bites, knowing that splitting the dose saves neither. Ensure one case yields an answer the coordinators will not wish to accept, and state explicitly what the procedure cannot resolve — particularly whether it can adjudicate between agent-neutral welfare maximisation and agent-relative duties to prioritise one's own crew. If this task rests on a confused premise — for example, if lifeboat ethics is too indeterminate to support any procedural checklist, or if the moral-remainder requirement is incoherent under emergency time constraints — please say so plainly rather than constructing the checklist politely.

Please present your response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
