Here is an argument I have been turning over about whether self-deception is genuinely possible, and I am troubled because its conclusion seems to sweep away more than I want to lose. (1) If an agent S is self-deceived about p, then S must both possess the true belief that not-p and simultaneously maintain the false belief that p; otherwise there is nothing being hidden and no deceived party within the same mind. (2) A single mental economy cannot rationally sustain two beliefs that are direct logical contradictories at the same time and in the same reasoning context. (3) Therefore self-deception is strictly impossible. (4) But if self-deception is impossible, then every case that looks like it—say, a skilled clinician who systematically discounts her own diagnostic errors in her professional frame while privately acknowledging them in reflective journaling, with no external audience—must be reconstructed as either simple ignorance of the errors or as ordinary other-directed deception. (5) She is not ignorant, since she records the errors accurately in her journal; and there is no other person she is trying to mislead. (6) So denying self-deception forces us to deny that an agent can ever suppress knowledge she already possesses when no observer is present. (7) That denial does not merely eliminate self-deception; it also eliminates "motivated framing," the broader phenomenon in which identical evidence is weighted differently depending on whether it touches one's self-image or strategic interests. (8) Without motivated framing we cannot explain commonplace compartmentalization—e.g., endorsing hard determinism in scientific contexts while presupposing libertarian agency in moral deliberation—without treating the agent as either ignorant of her own determinist commitments or as deliberately lying to others. (9) Hence the argument proves too much: it turns every partitioned belief-state into either contradiction or deception.

Which step should I reject? I am inclined to keep (8), since compartmentalized reasoning seems unavoidable, but I want to understand what I pay if I instead reject (1), (2), or (6). In particular, does rejecting (2) require adopting a "partitioned mind" model that makes rationality local rather than global, and does that undermine the very idea of a unified agent? Or can (1) be saved by distinguishing between occurrent belief and dispositional acceptance, so that S "has" the truth without actively believing it?
