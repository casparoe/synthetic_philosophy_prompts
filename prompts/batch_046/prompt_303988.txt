You are a professional philosopher of action and practical reason with experience in seminar design. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm how the internalist/externalist debate about reasons for action actually feels in lived practice—where agents can recognize a normative demand without feeling any motivational pull, or where private motivational architectures seem irrelevant to what one is genuinely accountable for—rather than treating it only as a theoretical taxonomy. Then, design a runnable 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 14 graduate students that makes participants feel the force of this tension directly, not through lecture.

Set the activity in a simulation called “Cooperative Accountability”: the group must collectively design and then defend binding rules for distributing a shared, limited resource (e.g., a pool of “credit hours” that determine access to a real but minor shared privilege, like choosing the next seminar’s optional text). Divide participants into three roles—four Deliberators, four Affected Parties, and six Observers—but assign each Deliberator a hidden “motivational architecture” card that describes not mere preferences but a second-order structure: which first-order desires they treat as reason-giving, which they regard as noise, and whether they accept or reject the idea that external normative claims can override those structures. The public framework they must use to justify decisions—“cooperative accountability”—is a synthetic normative system you define in the rules: it permits appeals only to mutual recognizability, consistency across roles, and harm-avoidance, explicitly forbidding reference to private motivational architectures. The Affected Parties receive hidden cards describing their own architectures, which may or may not align with the justifications offered. Observers score each deliberation on whether the justification genuinely addresses the affected party’s architecture without violating the external framework.

Structure the timeline precisely: 15 minutes for role assignment and silent reading of architecture cards; 30 minutes for three rounds of deliberation, each ending in a binding vote that assigns real (small-stakes) commitments; 15 minutes for a “reversal” round in which Deliberators must defend the opposite allocation using the same external framework; and 30 minutes for debrief. The stakes should be social and structural rather than monetary: use a collective reputation score visible on a board, plus a binding rule that the group’s final decision determines a tangible feature of the next session (e.g., who presents first, or which case study is mandatory).

In the debrief, map specific moments onto the philosophical positions: when a Deliberator recognized the normative force of an argument but reported no internal motivational uptake, that maps to the externalist claim that reasons are genuinely normative independent of internal accessibility; when an Affected Party rejected a justification precisely because it failed to connect to their second-order architecture, that maps to the internalist claim that a reason must be capable of motivating through the agent’s subjective motivational set. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time: Deliberators will try to smuggle architecture references into “neutral” language, and Observers will conflate disagreement about outcomes with disagreement about justification—have a facilitator script ready to interrupt and redirect.

Finally, include an honest limitation: the activity can reproduce the asymmetry between first-personal motivational access and third-personal normative assessment, but it cannot reproduce the metaphysical question of whether normative facts exist independently of all human architectures altogether—this feature must be supplied by the facilitator in closing remarks. Keep the prompt formal, avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not depend on specific named theories beyond the standard internalist/externalist framing. Ensure the niche injection—the “motivational architecture” cards with second-order attitudes and the synthetic “cooperative accountability” framework—drives the design rather than serving as decoration.
