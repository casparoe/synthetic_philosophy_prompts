As a novelist accustomed to mapping the hidden architectures of belief, I wish to commission a conceptual taxonomy — a decision tree rather than a list of named schools — for the epistemic regress problem, sorting thinkers by their actual commitments on justification rather than by the labels they prefer.

The root node should ask whether justification requires a terminating point or may extend indefinitely without loss of value. From there, branch on concrete, answerable commitments: whether circular mutual support among beliefs is permissible provided it is non-vicious; whether a belief can function as basic without being infallible or self-evident; whether an infinite chain of reasons retains epistemic standing if no single link is ever fully secured; and whether coherence demands global maximisation or only local cluster consistency.

Each internal node must be phrased as a case or claim that a person could respond to without knowing which philosophical camp it selects — for example, whether one would treat a belief as provisionally basic in practical reasoning while simultaneously recognising it as theoretically defeasible. The leaves must include foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism, but the tree should also expose unoccupied logical combinations — such as a view that accepts unending regress yet insists on provisional halting points for practical life — and briefly diagnose each emptiness (unstable, incoherent, or merely unexplored). Any pair of leaves that the tree’s logic genuinely cannot separate should be flagged explicitly rather than concealed.

To keep this from becoming a regurgitation of standard summaries, please inject at least one novel distinction into the branching logic: distinguish “structural coherence” (mutual entailment within a belief cluster) from “procedural coherence” (mutual survival through successive revision), and locate where each belongs; or introduce “ephemeral foundations” — beliefs that operate as basic for reasoning but are acknowledged as permanently open to defeat. Show how these distinctions alter the tree’s shape.

The intended audience is an intellectually curious reader familiar with basic epistemological vocabulary but not with specialised literature. Write in formal British English, without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Ensure that every diagnostic question can be answered by someone who does not already know the names of the positions.
