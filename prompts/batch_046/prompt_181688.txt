Please audit the consistency of the following package of commitments about advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. (1) I take the binding force of a refusal-of-treatment directive to derive from narrative ownership rather than from bare consent: the author remains the protagonist of the life-story that includes the directive, and this authority holds as long as there is substantial psychological continuity. (2) I also think severe dementia can break psychological continuity so radically that the current patient is no longer the narrative protagonist of that earlier story. (3) Even so, I believe proxies should honor such directives in severe dementia, including cases where the patient expresses sustained contentment with living. (4) At the same time, I hold that bodily continuity by itself is sufficient to bind the present body to past commitments, quite apart from any narrative or psychological link. (5) Finally, I think that if values are revised gradually through a long illness—without a sharp discontinuity—the revised values should override the old directive, but not if the change is abrupt and disorienting. Do these hang together? In particular, can (3) be justified once (1) and (2) are granted, or does (4) have to do all the work—if so, does (4) conflict with the narrative framing in (1)? And is there a hidden tension between (4) and (5) regarding what kind of continuity actually matters? Rank the tensions by severity and indicate, for each, which commitment is the cheapest to revise.
