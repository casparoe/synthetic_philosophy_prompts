For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on strategic reasoning (18 students, 75 minutes), design a structured, paper-and-card classroom game—playable without computers or external materials beyond printed cards and envelopes—that makes participants feel the fragility of iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies in mixed-strategy reasoning, particularly when private payoff uncertainty collides with common knowledge of rationality. The participants should come away seeing that elimination is not merely a mechanical computation but an epistemic process that can become irreversible and locally misapplied when players do not share identical payoff structures.

Specify the setting precisely: students sit in a circle, each receives at the start a private 3x3 payoff matrix card (use only the integers 0, 3, 5, and 8) against a hypothetical opponent, plus one hidden parameter called a shadow coefficient (drawn uniformly from 0.2 to 0.8 and printed on a separate card kept face-down). This coefficient modifies the payoff of exactly one off-diagonal cell—say, the lower-left—by multiplying its base value. Thus two students may face structurally similar matrices but compute dominance differently. Each student also receives three sealed envelopes labeled Strategy A, Strategy B, and Strategy C.

Construct the rules and timeline exactly. In Round 1 (15 minutes), students examine their own matrix alone and place any strategy they judge strictly dominated—given only their private knowledge—into its corresponding envelope, which is then sealed and deposited in a central box; unsealed strategies remain available. Before Round 2, the facilitator reveals only the aggregate count of eliminated strategies across all players, without revealing which strategies or whose coefficients produced the result. In Round 2 (15 minutes), students may exchange exactly one piece of information with each of their two immediate neighbors—either their coefficient range (low, middle, high) or the identity of one strategy they eliminated—but they may not show their cards. After this exchange, they must decide whether to seal additional envelopes. Crucially, in this round the revealed aggregate distribution creates a situation in which one strategy is strictly dominated for the subset of players whose shadow coefficient exceeds 0.5, but no player knows whether they belong to that subset. They must therefore decide whether to eliminate without knowing whether the dominance relation applies to them personally.

In Round 3 (15 minutes), the facilitator reveals all coefficient ranges, allowing every student to compute the true dominance relations for their specific matrix. However, the envelopes sealed in Round 2 are irreversible; students may only seal remaining unsealed envelopes, not reopen previous ones. After Round 3, the facilitator collects all envelopes and reveals the class-level pattern of correct versus incorrect eliminations.

Then provide the debrief (20 minutes). Map Round 1 onto strict dominance with complete information; Round 2 onto near-dominance and private-payoff uncertainty under common knowledge of rationality; and Round 3 onto the irreversibility of iterative steps once taken. Be explicit about what feature of formal iterated elimination this game cannot reproduce—specifically, the infinite regress of higher-order beliefs about rationality in continuous strategy spaces—and state clearly how the facilitator must supply that concept verbally after the activity. Also identify one predictable failure mode (for example, students coordinating on a focal strategy rather than computing dominance) and explain how to handle it without breaking the rules.
