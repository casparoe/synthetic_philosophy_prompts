Consider the institutional practice of a synthetic-biology parts registry that licenses engineered gene circuits under open-source software agreements: each DNA construct is catalogued with version history, users who express it in a novel organism must include an attribution header in their lab reports, and the resulting phenotype is routinely described in the registry’s documentation as a “compiled output” of the source sequence. At the same time, the registry refuses to license naturally occurring alleles, classifying wild-type sequences as unpatentable discoveries rather than authored works. What philosophical theory of genetic information does this bifurcated treatment presuppose? Must it treat DNA literally as a symbolic code with authors, versions, and compilations, or can the practice be sustained by a purely metaphorical analogy? Reconstruct the implicit ontology—distinguishing informational, chemical, and teleological conceptions of the gene—and evaluate whether the licensing framework is internally consistent with each. Then say what the registry’s rules and its scientific rhetoric would have to change if DNA were understood not as an instructional program but as a continuous material cause that shapes biological form through direct biochemical interaction without discrete encoding.
