Here's an argument I sketched for a philosophy-of-social-science seminar and now can't shake, because if it's sound it seems to undermine not just Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis but any attempt to explain economic behavior with cultural values. (1) If cultural values are genuine causes of economic behavior—rather than post-hoc rationalizations—then they must be identifiable and measurable independently of the economic outcomes they produce. (2) But values, unlike prices or population figures, have no observable manifestation except through the very behaviors and interpretations they are invoked to explain; there is no instrument that registers a value without reading behavior. (3) So in practice, identifying the value of worldly asceticism requires observing patterns of rational accounting, deferred consumption, and disciplined labor—precisely the economic behaviors that define early capitalism. (4) Therefore, any claim that this value caused those behaviors collapses into the claim that those behaviors occurred, leaving the causal arrow unestablished. (5) To avoid this circularity, one might argue that values are transmitted through institutions—churches, texts, sermons—that exist prior to the economic shift, so we can identify the cause independently through institutional history. (6) But if institutional transmission is the real causal mechanism, then the causal work is being done by material organizational structures—networks of churches, literacy rates, printing technologies—not by the subjective values themselves; the values become epiphenomenal descriptions of what those structures produced. (7) Hence either values are not independently measurable and the explanation is circular (denying (1)), or they are transmitted materially and are not genuinely autonomous causal forces (also denying (1)). (8) So if we take Weber's anti-reductionist claim seriously—that values are not reducible to material interests—we seem forced into the conclusion that values can never be established as causes in economic explanation. This proves too much, since it would rule out not only Weber's thesis but any value-based explanation in sociology or history. Where does the argument fail? I would prefer to keep (1)—that values can be genuine causes—along with (5), that institutional transmission offers an escape route, but I am unsure whether saving both requires abandoning (2) or finding a hidden mistake in the slide from (5) to (6).
