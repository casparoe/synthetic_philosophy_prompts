I'm currently working on a paper about algorithmic narrative synthesis—specifically, whether automated extraction and sequencing of primary-source fragments can yield something deserving the name historical narrative—and in every seminar someone eventually invokes Ranke's old formulation wie es eigentlich gewesen (conventionally rendered "how it actually was" or "as it really happened"). The difficulty is that the slogan does wildly different work depending on who speaks: one colleague uses it to dismiss any synthetic reconstruction that lacks direct archival contact, implying that machine-assembled chronology necessarily violates the Rankean mandate, while another deploys the exact same phrase to defend bare empirical extraction, arguing that if an algorithm sticks strictly to documented events without imposing interpretive framing it is actually being more Rankean than human historians, who inevitably inject narrative form. Meanwhile, Carr's scepticism that historical facts are not self-evident data points but selections pulled from an ocean of available material, and Hayden White's argument that all historical writing employs literary tropes, are mentioned in passing but rarely integrated into the dispute over what Ranke meant.

Treat the slogan not as a settled doctrine but as a piece of language hiding several distinct theses. Lay out the precise claims it could express, arranged from the near-tautological to the radically substantive, and for each indicate whether it is defensible, false, or merely platitudinous. Specifically, distinguish: (a) the minimal methodological reminder that historians should not invent sources; (b) the stronger epistemic claim that there exists a single, fully determinate past recoverable without interpretive residue; (c) the institutional claim that direct contact with primary documents is both necessary and sufficient for objectivity; and (d) any further precise reading you can isolate that might actually be doing the argumentative labor in my seminar. For each reading, say whether it survives Carr's and White's critiques, and—crucially—indicate which interpretation actually supports the anti-synthesis position versus which supports the pro-extraction position. Present the readings as a disambiguation menu rather than selecting one, and do not provide a bibliography or list of further readings; I need something I can bring back to the discussion to show exactly which thesis is being smuggled in when someone ends an argument with Ranke's phrase.
