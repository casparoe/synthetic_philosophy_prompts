I am currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of consent, and I am writing a paper that examines whether the standard autonomy-based framework adequately captures why deception and coercion undermine consent, or whether a structural account of fair option-sets does independent normative work. Rather than asking you to adjudicate, I want you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration might produce between the two camps we have been discussing.

Camp A—the autonomy-threshold camp—holds that consent is valid just when an agent exercises autonomous authorization over an act, where autonomy requires both procedural independence and an epistemic position adequate to the decision; on this view, deception vitiates consent by corrupting the informational basis of the agent’s will, and coercion vitiates it by substituting another’s will for the agent’s, regardless of the background distribution of options.

Camp B—the structural-fairness camp—holds that consent is valid just when it occurs within a structurally fair choice architecture, where fairness requires not merely the absence of direct interference but also the absence of domination and the presence of genuinely open exit options; on this view, deception and coercion are defective primarily because they distort the shape of the available option set, and an agent can be fully informed, uncoerced, and internally autonomous yet fail to consent if the background conditions are unfair.

Please produce the document both sides could sign. Structure it as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—covering what consent does in legal and moral contexts; the shared judgment that standard cases such as medical consent obtained through omission of material risk and labor agreements formed under severe economic asymmetry are defective; the agreement that both information and option-sets matter; and the agreement that no-go results about pure proceduralism do not by themselves resolve whether structure has independent weight.

2. A disagreements table with the precise formulations of the residual divergence, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording—specifically: (a) whether the normative work is done by the quality of the agent’s internal authorization or by the fairness of the external structure; (b) whether a fully informed, uncoerced agent in an unfair or dominating structure genuinely consents; and (c) whether competence conditions should be evaluated individually or relative to the structural accessibility of alternatives.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I take these to include: (i) whether structural fairness or naturalness of the choice environment has independent normative weight in consent evaluation, apart from its effect on internal authorization; and (ii) whether institutional or ecological predictive models can reliably identify domination in complex settings well enough to ground consent judgments. For each crux, provide a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it—such as a monopsonistic labor market with transparent contracts, or a medical trial in a resource-scarce region with full disclosure—and record in advance what each camp predicts the case demonstrates.

If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—for example, about whether “autonomy” should be defined in purely internal terms or can be constituted partly by structural relations—identify exactly which part and explain why.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation request; the task is to make the disagreement and its potential resolution exact, not to survey the literature.
