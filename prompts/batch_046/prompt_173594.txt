Having retired from a career in experimental neuroscience and only recently begun serious study in philosophy of mind, I find myself returning repeatedly to the problem of epiphenomenalism and to what philosophers call the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if qualitative experience lacks all causal efficacy, then any judgment we make about our own experience seems to be produced entirely by physical mechanisms that never interact with the experience itself. To clarify my own thinking, I want a formal taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—of the positions one might take on this problem, but I want it built from logical commitments rather than from a list of familiar names.

Please construct the tree from three diagnostic splits, each phrased as a concrete commitment rather than a label. The first split concerns the status of phenomenal reports: treat them as direct observational measurements analogous to instrument readings, or treat them as theoretical inferences drawn from behavioral and neural data? The second split introduces a distinction I have been using in my notes, which I call causal shadowing: does one allow that phenomenal properties might be systematically correlated with neural states in a non-causal, structural manner—like a shadow cast by an object that does not affect the shadow's motion—or does one deny even this correlation? The third split concerns a divided-attention scenario: if the left hemisphere verbally denies feeling warmth while the right hemisphere behaviorally seeks warmth in response to the same stimulus, does one privilege the verbal report, the behavioral expression, or conclude that there is no unified phenomenal fact?

Each branch must be labeled with the specific claim that divides it from its sibling. The leaves should locate, at minimum, the following named positions: strict epiphenomenalism, type-identity physicalism, eliminativism about phenomenal properties, interactionist dualism, and a higher-order representational view. Any combination of answers that does not correspond to a named position should appear as an unnamed leaf carrying one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—whether incoherent, unmotivated, or merely unexplored. If two named positions fall on the same leaf, flag them explicitly rather than merging them. The tree should be arranged so that the logical space of answers comes first and the named positions are mapped onto it afterward; please do not present it as a quiz about which philosopher one likes. I do not need bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—this is for private study without database access—nor should the prompt depend on any highly specialized theory unknown outside recent literature. The result should be polished enough to serve as a handout for a small discussion group of scientifically literate amateurs.
