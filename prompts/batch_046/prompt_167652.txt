After a prolonged and rather circular debate with a close friend over dinner about whether consent can ever be genuinely valid in asymmetrical expert-patient relationships, I have realised that we were speaking past each other on precisely what vitiates consent and whether procedural cleanliness is enough. I should like you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two camps might produce. The first, which I shall call the Procedural Integrity camp, holds that consent is valid if and only if three conditions are jointly satisfied: the agent possesses domain-specific competence, no material information is actively concealed or misrepresented, and refusal is not rendered unreasonably costly by an explicit threat. They recognise a phenomenon they term epistemic scaffolding — the framing of choices by an expert so that one option appears as the natural continuation of the agent’s prior commitments — and they argue that this is neither deception, because it introduces no false belief, nor coercion, because no penalty attaches to refusal, but rather a permissible facilitation of autonomous deliberation.

The opposing camp, which I shall call the Substantive Authorship camp, accepts that competence, non-deception, and non-coercion are necessary, yet denies they are sufficient. They maintain that consent is genuinely valid only when the agent’s assent expresses a reflectively stable preference that survives what they call the diachronic identity test — that is, the preference would still be endorsed after the agent has considered it outside the immediate framing context and in light of their broader life narrative. They contend that epistemic scaffolding can undermine consent not by deceiving or threatening, but by bypassing higher-order reflective capacities, thereby producing an assent that is procedurally clean yet substantively alienated from the agent’s authentic will.
