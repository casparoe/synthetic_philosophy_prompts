You serve on the allocation board of a philanthropic foundation holding a fixed ten-year endowment. The board must vote next month on a single portfolio recommendation, and you have been asked to draft the formal decision memo for circulation beforehand. The case concerns two competing grant proposals, both evaluated within an importance-tractability-neglectedness framework, but with a philosophical twist that threatens the coherence of applying that framework to itself.

Portfolio A seeks funding for direct technical containment protocols addressing an unprecedented catastrophic-risk pathway—specifically, autonomous replication safeguards in synthetic biology—where the potential importance is plausibly unbounded, yet tractability is near-zero and the relevant probability distribution remains essentially undefined. Portfolio B proposes what it terms a “meta-cause architecture”: institutional grants to decision-science and philosophy departments aimed at reducing systematic moral uncertainty about how importance and tractability weights ought to be assigned in the first place, including formal protocols for revising those weights as evidence accumulates. The proponents of B argue that without calibrated meta-level machinery, any object-level allocation is merely an unexamined projection of present intuitions.

The board is split. One faction holds that Portfolio B commits a reflexive category error: the framework is designed to rank object-level interventions, not to fund the calibration of the ranking system itself, and assigning B its own tractability score collapses the evaluation into circular self-reference. Another faction counters that refusing to fund meta-level calibration implicitly assigns infinite weight to current normative intuitions, which is itself a hidden allocation decision that should be made explicit. A third concern, raised by the foundation’s fiduciary counsel, is that Portfolio B yields no observable beneficiaries within any standard reporting horizon, potentially violating obligations to demonstrable impact.

Draft the board’s written opinion recommending a concrete course of action—approve A, approve B, approve a conditional split with explicit time frames, or reject both with an alternative restructuring plan. The memo should present a clear bottom line, include honest reasoning that treats the strongest argument for the portfolio you do not recommend with genuine seriousness rather than dismissal, and specify any conditions, review milestones, or sunset provisions attached to the recommendation. Write it as a formal document the board could act upon, without appealing to external bibliographies or citation authorities.
