In a philosophy of mind reading group, a fellow student recently dismissed a formalist account of reasoning by invoking what she called "the Gödel constraint." She summarized it as follows: Gödel showed that in any consistent formal system, there are true statements it cannot prove; therefore, since the brain is presumably a physical system running on fixed rules, there must always be mental truths it cannot access or represent, which makes complete computational modeling of cognition impossible. The move struck me as suspiciously quick, but rather than dismissing it outright I want to understand exactly where the sleight of hand occurs.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses hiding inside that one-line summary? Please present them as a menu rather than selecting a single reading: (i) the literal arithmetical result about recursively enumerable axiomatizations of arithmetic; (ii) the analogical claim that physical or neural systems are formal systems of that specific kind; (iii) the specifically epistemic claim, associated with Lucas and Penrose, that human mathematicians can recognize Gödel sentences for the formal systems they inhabit; and (iv) any stronger metaphysical claim that consciousness necessarily transcends all formalization. For each reading, indicate whether Gödel's first or second incompleteness theorem actually delivers it, what hidden premises about consistency, self-reference, or representation are being smuggled in, and whether the conclusion that cognitive science must fail genuinely follows. I am especially interested in whether the argument confuses syntactic unprovability within a fixed axiom set with a broader semantic or causal limitation on a physical brain, and in whether the brain can be modeled by successively strengthened formal systems without falling prey to the same diagonalization.
