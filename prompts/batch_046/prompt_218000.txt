Draft the joint statement that a cross-cultural convergence universalist and an institutional-embedding constructivist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on what WEIRD psychology and cross-cultural moral data imply for moral objectivity.

The universalist holds that, despite well-documented WEIRD sampling biases in comparative psychology, the replication of core harm and fairness judgments across small-scale societies—together with structural parallels in how violations are justified—supports a modest moral objectivity in which cross-cultural variance is mainly in weighting, contextual salience, and expression, not in basic normative ontology. The constructivist holds that the survey categories used to establish this convergence (e.g., harm, purity, loyalty, fairness) are themselves embedded in Western legal, religious, and educational institutions; apparent replication is largely an artifact of translation, framing, and institutional scaffolding, and the residual variance is not noise around a universal core but evidence that moral categories are locally constituted rather than discovered.

Your deliverable should be the document they would co-sign. Structure it exactly as follows:

1. Joint Agreed Claims (numbered list; approximately 250 words). List claims both parties accept about the empirical landscape: the scope and limits of WEIRD sampling, which moral judgments replicate reliably outside WEIRD populations, which ones show high contextual variance, what counts as a well-controlled cross-cultural comparison, and what either side would treat as genuine counterevidence. Do not include claims that either party would reject, and do not appeal to authority or citation—describe findings in sufficient detail that the reference is clear without one.

2. Residual Disagreements (table format; approximately 300 words). Use four columns: Crux, Universalist formulation (worded so its proponents accept it as their own view), Constructivist formulation (worded so its proponents accept it as their own view), and Neutral framing of the precise divergence. Cover at least: (a) whether purity-related violations in non-WEIRD societies are best interpreted as harm-avoidance expressed through a different cultural idiom, or as genuinely independent normative domains; (b) whether institutional embedding affects only the expression and salience of norms, or also partly constitutes their content and boundaries.

3. The Cruxes and Predictive Tests (approximately 350 words). Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case, hypothetical comparative design, or conceptual test that both sides agree bears on the issue, and record each side's explicit prediction in advance. At minimum, include a test involving ritual pollution taboos that lack direct interpersonal harm, examining whether participants treat the violation as a distinct wrong or as an indirect harm to communal welfare; and a test examining whether fairness judgments remain structurally stable when institutional context—such as market integration or legal formalization—is ethnographically or experimentally varied.

4. Verdict (approximately 150 words). Take a definite, committed position: state whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal (for example, about the definition of universal, objective, or constituted), identify which crux is the deepest and why, and specify what particular finding or argument—short of full conversion—would genuinely update both sides. Do not hedge by laying out options and leaving the reader to choose; make definite choices, justify them, and explain what the adversarial collaboration actually reveals about the state of the debate.

Write in a formal, polished register. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. If you invoke specific empirical patterns, describe them precisely enough to be recognizable without attribution. Commit to exact wording for each camp rather than using tentative phrasing like "might say" or "could argue," and ensure the document reads as one both parties could genuinely sign, with the remaining disagreement rendered exactly rather than smoothed over.
