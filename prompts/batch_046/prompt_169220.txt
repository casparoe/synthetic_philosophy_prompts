As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of religion and moral psychology, I have followed with interest a sustained disagreement between two research-minded camps. The first holds that religious commitment supplies a non-substitutable commitment mechanism—what they term transcendent accountability—which uniquely stabilizes costly cooperation when reciprocity and institutional monitoring are weak. The second holds that secular societies can generate equivalent or greater moral stability through transparent institutions, reputation economies, and normative education, with religious affiliation being one substitutable implementation among many. Both sides agree that the question is not whether religious people report more prosocial attitudes—cross-national surveys are well-known—but whether secular societies can sustain what I will call operational moral resilience: the persistence of costly cooperative behavior under conditions of high anonymity, absent direct reciprocity, and minimal institutional surveillance, rather than merely maintaining high scores on declarative morality indices.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Format it as follows: first, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, including about what empirical patterns are established, about what "more moral" must mean if the comparison is to be meaningful (distinguishing declarative norms, observable prosocial signaling, and operational moral resilience), and about the formal requirements any adequate explanation of cooperative stability must meet. Second, a disagreements table stating the precise formulations of their residual divergence, each phrased in wording its own side would accept. Third, a cruxes section identifying the sub-questions—such as whether secular institutional design can fully replicate the accountability density of religious community structures, and whether moral resilience decays measurably faster in secular populations under experimentally induced anonymity—such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify a concrete case, study design, or empirical pattern both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's explicit prediction in advance. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable should be assessable without external lookup. Maintain a formal, analytical tone throughout, and ensure no claim is presented as agreed unless both formulations in the table genuinely permit it.
