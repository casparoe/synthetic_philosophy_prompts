In a seminar on early analytic philosophy I have been working through Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay on the meaning of life, in which he contends that meaning attaches to activities pursued for their own sake—what he describes as "play"—rather than to instrumental "work." During a recent discussion a classmate kept returning to the one-liner "life is play, not work," using it first to dismiss careerist ambition as inherently hollow, then to reject religious purpose as self-deception, and finally—somewhat bizarrely—to praise "gamified" labor as proof that productivity can recover its playful core. The phrase seemed to shift its meaning each time: sometimes it meant only that enjoyment is essential, sometimes that any goal-directed action is futile, and sometimes that the distinction between means and ends is itself an illusion.

Could you lay out, as a precise menu, the distinct theses that slogan might actually be expressing? I want the tautological reading, the psychological claim about motivation, the axiological claim about intrinsic versus instrumental value, the metaphysical claim about human nature, and any other interpretation that genuinely belongs in Schlick's neighborhood. For each, say whether it is trivially true, defensible, false, or actually incompatible with Schlick's broader logical-positivist commitments. I am especially interested in whether Schlick's "play" refers specifically to a kind of youthful, creative spontaneity rather than to mere pleasure or leisure, and in which of these readings that specificity matters. Then tell me which version of the slogan my classmate was relying on when they used it to end an argument about whether someone who spends decades building schools for poor children has found meaning in life, and whether that version actually reaches the conclusion they wanted.
