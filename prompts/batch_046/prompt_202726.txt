I am preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on normative architecture, and I want a consistency audit before I present. Here is the package I have settled on. First, I accept indirect normativity in a specific form: what I have reason to do is fixed not by my actual occurrent judgments or desires, but by what I would endorse after a process of idealized deliberation—full empirical information, logical coherence, emotional equilibrium, and the absence of self-deception and motivated reasoning. Second, I insist that this idealization is thin rather than thick, and I characterize the retention condition as structural: it corrects instrumental errors and fills informational gaps, but it preserves both my first-order evaluative framework and my second-order procedural commitments, such as valuing autonomy in practical choice. If my core orientation is hedonistic or deontological, my idealized counterpart retains that orientation, applied only more consistently. Third, I take this framework to support a form of moral realism about practical reasons: there are correct answers to what I ought to do, determined counterfactually by the outcome of this deliberative procedure, and these answers hold independently of whether I currently recognize them. Fourth, I hold that in cases of deep normative disagreement—say, between a committed utilitarian and a committed Kantian—indirect normativity yields non-convergent, agent-relative reason-sets; there is no neutral standpoint that adjudicates between them, only different counterfactual deliberative outcomes anchored in different retained structures. Fifth, despite that absence of convergence, I maintain that in collective practical contexts—democratic deliberation over climate policy, for instance—I should treat the normative judgments of my fellow participants as possessing direct authoritative weight for the collective decision, not merely as data about what they would endorse under idealization. Sixth, my account of epistemic obligation is strictly derivative and non-aggregative: I have reason to acquire information precisely when, and only when, my structurally retained idealized self would judge that information capable of altering the ranking of available actions; I deny both that knowledge has intrinsic value and that information is valuable in proportion to its global reduction in uncertainty.

Please audit these six commitments for joint tenability. I am particularly worried about whether my realism in the third commitment can survive the agent-relativity that my structural-retention condition and my treatment of disagreement in the fourth seem to entail, and whether my claim about collective authority in the fifth can be sustained given the lack of a shared normative framework implied by the fourth. I also want you to examine whether my sixth commitment about information introduces hidden tensions with the idealization procedure itself—especially if gathering information can sometimes change the very structural preferences that are supposed to be retained. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, specify exactly which commitment must be qualified or abandoned, and identify the least costly revision that restores consistency. In addition, flag at least one pairing that looks as though it ought to conflict but actually dissolves on closer inspection—perhaps involving the relationship between structural retention and the divergence of reason-sets—so that I do not over-correct. Do not treat all conflicts as equally damaging; I want to know which single revision would eliminate the greatest number of problems, and what that revision costs me in terms of the overall view.
