Design a 75-minute classroom activity for 12 graduate students in philosophy of biology or social epistemology — no prior game theory required — that makes participants experience, through their own strategic choices, why Zahavi’s handicap principle is conceptually distinct from Spence-style costly signaling. The specific distinction to embody is that in a true handicap the cost is not merely high but differentially unbearable for low-quality senders, so pool separation emerges because weak agents literally cannot absorb the burden rather than merely rationally declining a flat fee.

Set up rules, assigned roles, and concrete materials (cards, tokens, or brief physical tasks) so that in Round One students attempt to signal quality with cheap or uniform-cost displays and the pool fails to separate; in Round Two introduce a mechanism where the identical display carries a burden that only high-quality agents can bear without catastrophic loss, and arrange the incentives so that participants feel the equilibrium shift in real time before anyone names “handicap principle” or “separating equilibrium.” Specify exactly who holds hidden quality assignments, how information is revealed, and what the non-monetary payoff scheme is (e.g., points convertible to a small collective reward or individual rank).

Write a debrief that maps each round onto the formal distinction between flat-cost signaling and quality-dependent-cost signaling, identifying which player actions correspond to signaling, which correspond to assessment, and where the “waste” that makes the signal honest appears. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time you run this — for example, students gaming the physical-cost mechanism, or confusion about why rational agents don’t simply opt out — and say exactly how to recover without breaking the pedagogical point. State frankly which feature of evolutionary handicap dynamics (such as genetic correlation between quality and cost-bearing capacity, or population-level selection over generations) the activity necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally by the facilitator.

Take a committed position on one design choice: whether the handicap burden should be represented by abstract point losses or by embodied physical costs (e.g., maintaining a posture, completing a timed manual task). Defend that choice with reasons tied to whether you want participants to experience the cost as strategic calculation or as visceral incapacity, and explain how that shapes what they come away seeing. Finally, include a brief note identifying any point where your design simplifies Zahavi’s original formulation or leans on a contested interpretation of how handicap signals stabilize in evolutionary game dynamics.
