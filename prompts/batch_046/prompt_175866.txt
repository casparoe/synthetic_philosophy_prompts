You are a philosopher of action and comparative cognition with particular familiarity in mechanistic accounts of behavior and the boundary between rigid and flexible response patterns. Before you answer, take a moment to map out the conceptual terrain: distinguish first-order behavioral routines from second-order revision capacities, and clarify what makes a mechanism "sphexish" in the relevant sense. I hold the view that human free will should not be understood as an exception to mechanistic causation, but rather as a specific departure from what philosophers of mind have called "sphexishness"—the extreme behavioral rigidity exemplified by the digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus, which will continue to execute a prey-storage routine even when the prey has been experimentally removed and the action has become functionally pointless. On my account, free will consists not in breaking causal chains, but in possessing a recursively structured capacity to interrupt, revise, or redirect first-order behavioral programs in light of representations that are not themselves embedded within those programs. The wasp lacks this meta-level architecture; humans possess it, though still within a fully deterministic framework. The position therefore claims that flexibility and rigidity are both mechanistically implemented, but they differ in hierarchical depth rather than in the presence or absence of mechanism.

What would genuinely count against this position—not standard objections to determinism, but defeaters that specifically undermine the sphexishness/free-will distinction?
