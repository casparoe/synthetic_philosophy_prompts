As a novelist who has spent years thinking about how narrative structure shapes belief, I have arrived at the following set of convictions about philosophical intuition pumps, and I want to know whether they form a coherent package or a hidden contradiction. Here is my profile.

First, I hold that every intuition pump is essentially a compressed narrative, and that its philosophical force derives from focalization—the restriction of information to a particular perspective—rather than from logical form alone.

Second, I think that the danger in pumps comes not from false premises but from editorial suppression: the author excises counterfactual details that would complicate the scene, and the reader accepts the excision because it mimics the coherence of edited fiction.

Third, I maintain that narrative coherence within a well-constructed scenario is a fallible but genuine guide to metaphysical possibility: if a situation can be rendered with full internal consistency, that counts in favor of its possibility, unless there is independent reason to think the coherence has been manufactured.

Fourth, I also hold that a pump should be rejected if it genuinely requires a specific focalization or emotional register to generate its intuitive result—if, for instance, the veil of ignorance only produces its egalitarian pull when narrated in Rawls's detached, bureaucratic tone—because then the result is an artifact of staging.

Fifth, I believe that conceivability arguments, such as the zombie scenario, are especially dangerous because they present narratives that are internally coherent but metaphysically empty—like novels with perfect plot logic and no external referent—yet they exploit the coherence criterion in my third commitment by trading on that very internal consistency.

Sixth, and finally, I think that a novelist's trained eye for authorial manipulation—recognizing where a scene has been edited for emotional effect—is an intersubjective skill rather than mere subjective taste, and should serve as a standard check in philosophical evaluation.

Are these jointly tenable? I am particularly unsure about the relationship between my claim that focalization does the philosophical work and my claim that pumps requiring specific focalization are artifacts; about whether my coherence-to-possibility principle can survive the zombie case without circularity; and about whether my reliance on editorial judgment collapses into subjectivity despite my denial. Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, state the least costly revision for each genuine tension, and tell me which single change to one commitment would clean up the most problems overall. Do not treat all conflicts as equally damaging.
