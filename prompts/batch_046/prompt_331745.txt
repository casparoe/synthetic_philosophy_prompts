I'm a PhD student in moral psychology and philosophy, currently in a graduate seminar on virtue ethics and the situationist critique—twelve students, ninety minutes, all familiar with Aristotle's account of hexis and at least with Doris's or Harman's arguments against global character traits. Last week, over coffee with two other students, we got into a long argument about whether the situationist findings are just artifacts of extreme experimental conditions like Milgram-style obedience, or whether they also appear in mundane, low-stakes "moral bookkeeping" where people fragment their honesty across personal and institutional frames. I want an in-class activity for this seminar that doesn't begin with a lecture on cross-situational consistency, but instead makes the participants feel the dissonance from the inside: first, the conviction that they possess stable virtues that should guide them uniformly, and then the discovery that structurally identical choices yield opposite behaviors depending on proximity, mediation, and framing. The group should come away seeing why the situationist worry—that virtue ethics overestimates the coherence of moral character—is not just an empirical curiosity but a live threat to first-person moral self-understanding.

Design the full runnable activity: specify the room setup, group divisions, materials, rules, and a precise timeline for the ninety minutes. I want a staged, structured deliberation with forced collective choices rather than a role-play with fictional personas—perhaps involving hidden framing conditions across subgroups—so that participants construct their own reasoning in real time and only afterwards discover that situational variables, not character, drove the divergence. Include an incentive structure that doesn't use real money but creates genuine stakes for consistency (for example, a public consistency score or a binding collective commitment), and build in at least two rounds with morally isomorphic scenarios—say, a direct face-to-face resource dilemma versus a mediated institutional accounting ambiguity—so the framing split is visible. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between global and local traits, the fundamental attribution error, and the virtue-ethical reply that appeals to practical wisdom or trait integration. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time—defensive rationalization, framing leakage between groups, or participants refusing to own the divergence—and how to handle it without dissolving the activity's force. Also be frank about which feature of the situationist critique—likely the claim that high-cost or extreme situations overwhelm traits entirely—this classroom simulation cannot reproduce, and therefore which part of the argument the facilitator must supply verbally rather than letting the rules generate it.

In your design, do not present me with alternative structures to choose among. Take definite positions on whether to include an authority-figure cue, exactly how many rounds to run, how to conceal the framing conditions, and how to sequence the debrief. Give me a committed, runnable blueprint with clear reasoning for each choice, not a menu of options.
