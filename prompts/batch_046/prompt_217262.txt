Researchers in cross-cultural moral psychology have documented that WEIRD populations produce systematically different folk-moral judgments—particularly regarding harm, purity, and authority—than participants in many small-scale societies, and philosophers have used this divergence to argue either that moral objectivity is culturally parochial or that only deep structural features of cognition matter. Despite broad agreement on the empirical pattern, the debate over its implications for objectivity has stalled: neither camp has substantially revised its position.

To audit this impasse, distinguish between 'surface variance'—disagreement about particular deontic verdicts, such as whether diverting a trolley is permissible—and 'structural invariance', the claim that every culture employs a similar hierarchical mechanism for norm transmission together with a shared computational sensitivity to cooperative harm, even when parameterised outputs differ. One camp treats high surface variance as direct evidence against objectivity, on the grounds that an objective morality should yield convergent judgments across contexts; the other treats structural invariance as sufficient, arguing that objectivity concerns the underlying architecture rather than its local expressions.

Given this framing, diagnose the disagreement precisely. Is it a hidden empirical dispute about whether structural invariance actually holds across all documented societies, or about whether surface variance correlates with deeper architectural differences? Is it normative—centred on what moral objectivity requires, whether convergence of outputs, convergence of architectures, or some further condition? Or is it partly verbal, with each side defining 'moral cognition' differently so that they answer distinct questions under a shared label?

Further, consider the injected concept of 'normative plasticity': the degree to which a moral architecture can generate divergent surface outputs without altering its core computational commitments. If high plasticity is compatible with objectivity, does the debate collapse into an empirical question about the actual plasticity observed in cross-cultural development? Or does a bedrock intuition about naturalness or purity remain that no structural analysis can dissolve, leaving the disagreement genuinely intractable?
