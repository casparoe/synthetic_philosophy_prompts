I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been following the debate over predictive algorithms and neuroscience-based risk assessments in criminal sentencing—systems that claim to forecast a defendant's future behavior, or even identify a "decision" in brain activity seconds before conscious awareness. In the commentary surrounding these tools, a one-liner keeps surfacing: "You can't hold someone responsible if they couldn't have done otherwise." Speakers invoke it to argue that once an algorithm or scan predicts an action with high accuracy, the person lacked a "real alternative," and therefore moral blame collapses entirely. I need to write precisely about this for readers who don't study philosophy, but the slogan is doing far too much work. Could you lay out, as a clear menu of distinct precise theses, what "responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise" could actually be claiming? For each reading, please indicate: whether it is trivial or definitional; whether it is substantive, defensible, and under what conditions; whether it is false; and, crucially, whether it actually supports the inference from "the model predicted it" to "therefore no blame is warranted." I am especially interested in how the different kinds of "can"—psychological, physical, rational, or counterfactual—change the claim, and in whether the requirement applies to the agent at the exact moment of action, to some prior deliberative stage, or to the system's design. Please also identify which of these readings is most likely being smuggled in when a commentator uses the line to shut down discussion of algorithmic accountability, and explain why that particular reading fails to do the work they think it does. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I need only the conceptual disambiguation, with verdicts, so I can bring accuracy to the reporting without sending my readers into the literature.
