I run a small philosophy-of-decision workshop for rationalist-adjacent adults—typically eight to ten participants, one 75-minute session, no formal background in auction theory required, though everyone is familiar with basic prisoner's-dilemma framing and the idea that rational agents can end up worse off by reasoning correctly. I want a runnable in-person activity that makes the group feel the structural force of escalation of commitment through a modified dollar-auction format, not merely hear a lecture about sunk-cost fallacies. The conceptual target is precise: participants should discover in real time that backing down is locally rational only under common knowledge of mutual rationality, and that every successive bid functions partly as a costly signal attempting to establish that common knowledge, which collapses the moment either party tests it.

Design a game with explicit rules, a payoff scheme using small tangible stakes rather than real money—tokens or points that convert into a minor shared privilege or pool—optional roles if they help, and a clear timeline that lets the group run the auction with minimal facilitator narration. Arrange the rules so that participants construct the escalation sequence themselves, ideally reaching a state where collective committed costs exceed the prize value, before anyone names the phenomenon. Include a debrief that maps each phase of play—the opening bid, the first overbid, the moment of mutual recognition, and the final lock-in or collapse—onto the corresponding logical feature of escalation; note what will predictably go wrong on the first run (for example, one dominant speaker, premature tacit cooperation, or misinterpretation of second-price rules) and how to handle it; and be explicit about which feature of the theoretical model—perhaps the infinite-horizon reasoning chain or the exact common-knowledge hierarchy—the physical activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally.

Please embed one non-obvious structural twist into the auction mechanics: for instance, a hidden budget cap revealed only after the first round, a silent-bidding phase that forces reasoning about beliefs without observed reactions, or a side-payment option that turns the two-player structure into a coalition problem. The twist should be niche enough that the prompt is not answerable by copying a standard classroom dollar-auction script, but explained clearly enough that the activity remains fully runnable without any web lookup. No lecture up front, no request for citations or reading lists, and do not ask participants to prepare anything in advance.
