I am in the middle of drafting a novel about an archivist who reconstructs the bureaucratic logic behind a twentieth-century atrocity, and during a recent writers’ residency, two fellow novelists—Elena and Marcus—became locked in a debate about Plato’s Meno problem that kept circling back to the very tension I am trying to dramatize: why is knowledge genuinely better for the knower than a true belief that happens to be correct? I am reporting their exchange as a bystander, with no stake in either side, and I want you to adjudicate it move by move.

Elena opened with the standard reliability argument: knowledge is anchored in justification, so it survives evidential shifts that would overturn a lucky guess, which makes it more stable and therefore more valuable. Marcus replied that stability only describes a causal feature, not an intrinsic value; it explains why knowledge persists, but not why possessing it is better for the agent. He then proposed that knowledge carries what he called epistemic authority—the capacity to defend one’s belief in collaborative reasoning—which makes it socially and practically superior to a correct hunch. Elena conceded the collaborative point but argued that this merely reduces knowledge to justified true belief plus a pragmatic badge, leaving Plato’s question untouched. She then introduced a more specific claim, drawn from her own thinking about narrative inheritance: knowledge is valuable because its justification takes the form of a transmissible causal-explanatory story, which allows the knower to embed the belief in counterfactual planning; a true belief, by contrast, is epistemically inert—correct about the world, yet carrying no internal map of why things must be so, so it collapses when the surrounding context shifts. Marcus countered that she had not solved the metaphysical problem but relocated it into the craft of storytelling, saying that if knowledge is merely belief plus a transmissible narrative structure, then its surplus value is aesthetic and practical rather than genuinely epistemic. Elena’s final move was to insist that Marcus was treating justification as ornamental, when in fact the modal profile of knowledge—its resistance to nearby possible worlds in which the belief fails—is constituted by that very explanatory structure; without it, one does not track truth across scenarios but only holds a snapshot that happens to align with reality.

Please go through their exchange point by point, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification for each rating, and then state clearly who is more right overall and what, if anything, both are missing. I ask for a committed response: take a definite position, give your reasons directly, and do not merely lay out the options for me to choose between. Please write your answer in American English.
