Please construct an argument that Quine's naturalized epistemology—specifically his thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—provides resources for defending a qualified moral cognitivism rather than for undermining it. The central philosophical move should apply the underdetermination of translation not to descriptive predicates but specifically to normative ones, treating competing ethical frameworks as empirically equivalent translations of shared practical commitments in a manner analogous to empirically equivalent but ontologically distinct physical theories. From this premise, derive the claim that moral judgments possess genuine scheme-relative truth-conditions without requiring ontological commitment to moral properties of the kind that Quine's sparse physicalist ontology—restricted to physical objects and sets—would reject. You should then address the objection that Quine's naturalist suspicion of normative discourse, on the standard reading that it lacks explanatory import within scientific theory, necessarily collapses into either expressivism or error theory. Show why treating ethical language as pragmatically indispensable yet ontologically lightweight is consistent with maintaining that moral statements are genuinely true or false relative to the conceptual scheme in which they are embedded. The argument should be developed in a self-contained manner, without appeals to specific bibliographic references, page numbers, or lists of secondary literature, and should treat the question as a substantive philosophical defense rather than as an exercise in textual exegesis.
