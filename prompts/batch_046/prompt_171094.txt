Question: Consider the debate between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are experiences in the same sense as waking perceptions. Malcolm contends that dream reports are not descriptions of mental episodes occurring during sleep, but rather constructions formed upon awakening, and that the absence of independent verification criteria for sleep-state consciousness means we have no basis for attributing experiential states to the sleeper. Dennett, by contrast, argues that consciousness should not be modeled as a linear stream presented to a Cartesian observer, but rather as a distributed process of narrative drafting in which dream content is assembled post hoc from cognitive fragments generated during REM. In your answer, address the following: (i) Reconstruct Malcolm’s argument precisely, noting why he denies that a first-person report upon waking can serve as evidence for a concurrent experience; (ii) Explain how Dennett’s “multiple drafts” framework applies to dream reports, and whether it allows for any form of phenomenal content during sleep; (iii) Evaluate whether the activation-synthesis model in neurobiology—according to which REM-phase brainstem activation produces random cortical signals that the sleeping brain later integrates into coherent narratives—supports Malcolm’s skepticism, Dennett’s constructivism, or neither; and (iv) Identify one conceptual difficulty for each position that arises specifically from treating dream reports as retrospective narrative reconstructions rather than as direct memories of prior experiences.

Student answer:

(1) Malcolm’s verificationist argument holds that because the sleeper lacks consciousness, there is no criterion for applying the concept of “experience” to the sleeping state. A report given upon waking cannot be corroborated by any concurrent observation, so it does not meet the conditions for a genuine first-person description of an internal episode. Consequently, dreams are not experiences but retrospective narratives constructed to make sense of physiological arousal.

(2) Dennett’s multiple-drafts model denies that consciousness is a linear stream presented to a central observer. In the case of dreams, this means that no single canonical narrative unfolds during REM; instead, the brain produces several competing drafts of content, and the version recalled upon awakening is merely the one that happened to be edited into coherence by post-awakening cognitive processes. Dennett thus allows that some neural activity with phenomenal character may occur during sleep, but he denies that this activity constitutes a unified experiential stream with a definite temporal structure.

(3) The activation-synthesis hypothesis supports Malcolm’s position more closely than Dennett’s. Because the model describes REM sleep as driven by random pontine activation that the cortex attempts to synthesize into meaning, it implies that the experiential content—if any—is fragmented and incoherent during sleep itself, and that the coherent dream narrative is assembled only after the brain has returned to waking levels of integration. This aligns with Malcolm’s claim that there is no continuous experience to report.

(4) A difficulty for Malcolm is that if dream reports are pure constructions, we need an account of why they preserve specific emotional and sensory details rather than generating arbitrary fiction. A difficulty for Dennett is that if all narrative coherence is achieved post hoc, then the phenomenology of being “present” inside a dream—of experiencing events from a first-person perspective in real time—becomes a kind of illusion that requires an explanation his distributed model does not obviously provide.
