In The Honor Code (2010), Appiah examines dueling, foot-binding, and Atlantic slavery, arguing that each practice collapsed once it became incompatible with elite honor codes—anti-slavery and anti-dueling arguments had existed for centuries, but only produced social change when slaveholding and dueling lost prestige among the relevant elites. Williams, in related essays on morality and history, contends that abolition in particular illustrates a stronger point: moral reasoning against slavery was available since antiquity, yet British abolition in 1833 occurred only after slaveholding had become dishonorable for the landed gentry; on Williams's account, economic interests—such as the declining profitability of West Indian plantations—played no independent causal role but were entirely epiphenomenal to this prior honor shift. Student answer:

Appiah argues in The Honor Code that moral revolutions are driven by shifts in elite honor rather than by rational persuasion. In the case of dueling, anti-dueling arguments had circulated since the seventeenth century, yet the practice persisted in Britain until the mid-nineteenth century, when aristocrats began to treat a challenge as vulgar rather than courageous; once that status reversal occurred, dueling disappeared within a generation. Bernard Williams applies a parallel analysis to Atlantic slavery, observing that abolitionist reasoning had existed since antiquity but only produced legal change in 1833, after slaveholding had lost its association with gentlemanly prestige among British elites. Both philosophers therefore reject rationalist histories of moral progress. They differ, however, on the causal status of economic interests. Appiah allows that falling sugar profits and the rise of industrial manufacturing accelerated the honor shift in Britain, functioning as interacting causes. Williams denies this: he holds that economic motives are entirely epiphenomenal, becoming politically decisive only after honor has already delegitimized slaveholding, and he adds that legal abolition therefore followed automatically once elite opinion turned. Consequently, the honor-first model explains both cases without granting economics any independent explanatory weight, and any account that treats material conditions as primary reverses the true causal sequence.
