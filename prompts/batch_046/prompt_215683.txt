I'm running an advanced seminar in philosophy of mind—twelve graduate students, one 90-minute block—where we've spent the past two sessions on non-reductive physicalism and the intuition that mental states do real causal work. Last week, over coffee with a friend who works in cognitive science, she mentioned that her colleagues rarely worry about mental causation because they implicitly treat neural explanations as exhaustive, and I realized my students probably share that complacency without knowing the formal problem behind it. I want an activity that makes them feel the causal exclusion argument from the inside: specifically, the squeeze between the causal closure of the physical domain and the claim that mental properties, though physically realized, are not causally redundant. To keep it from being a generic textbook exercise, please inject a conceptual twist around the distinction between causal production and causal patterning—whether mental descriptions can track irreducible regularities without competing with physical producers—and ask the groups to test that distinction under the rules themselves.

Please design a runnable in-class simulation or structured deliberation: specify the roles or group divisions, explicit rules and any symbolic payoff or budget mechanism, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and at least two staged cases (for instance, voluntary arm-raising and an affective withdrawal response). The rules should be arranged so that students try to allocate causal work between physical and mental explanations of the same outcome and discover the redundancy problem through their own allocations, not through your explanation. Include a debrief that maps each failure mode onto parts of the argument, says what will predictably go wrong—perhaps students smuggling downward-causation intuitions through the rules' back door—and how to handle it. Also be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, particularly the metaphysical status of realization relations, and tell me how to supply that verbally.
