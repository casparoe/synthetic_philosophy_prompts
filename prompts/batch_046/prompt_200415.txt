I'm trying to understand historical counterfactuals and I keep running into the same blind spot. People argue about whether WWI was inevitable - if Franz Ferdinand's driver hadn't taken the wrong turn, maybe no war, versus the alliances were a powder keg and some spark was bound to go off. But what strikes me is that both sides treat contingency like its constant; they don't ask whether 1914 was a moment when the system was brittle and amplifying noise, or whether it was actually pretty resilient and just broke for unrelated reasons.

I see the same pattern elsewhere. The Roman Empire absorbs crazy scandals, bad emperors, plagues, and keeps going for centuries - small shocks just dissipate. Then suddenly one tax revolt in Egypt or one border raid cascades into collapse. Or tech adoption: some inventions sit around for decades with zero impact, then one minor policy tweak and they explode everywhere. Its like history has these phases where its viscous and others where its brittle, and the "for want of a nail" debates ignore that completely.

Is there an established name for this - that the same small cause can be decisive or irrelevant depending on the system's state, not just on the cause itself? I know about path dependence and critical junctures but those feel different, they're about locking in outcomes not about whether noise gets amplified or absorbed. If there's no term, what's the closest concept, and how would you draw the line between this and ordinary structural explanations?
