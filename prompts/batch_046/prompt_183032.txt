For a philosophy-of-mind seminar of about 10-12 students with 90 minutes, design a runnable classroom game called The Sleep Archive Audit that makes the group feel Malcolm's pressure on whether dreaming counts as an experience, but lets Dennett's retrieval-construction idea come out of the mechanics rather than lecture. I want participants divided into Sleepers (hidden cards describing simulated REM or false REM signals), Reporters (who must produce first-person dream narratives under time pressure), and Auditors (who classify each report as genuine experience, confabulated retrieval, or indeterminate using only a public checklist of behavioral, linguistic, and physiological criteria — no access to the hidden cards). Give them payoffs: Auditors earn points for inter-rater reliability, Sleepers earn points if the Auditors misclassify them in the direction they secretly prefer.

Structure it in three rounds with a rough timeline. Round 1 should feel easy — the Auditors apply the checklist and think they've found sharp criteria. Round 2 should break that by introducing contradictory indicators (high emotional intensity paired with incoherent narrative, or perfect narrative coherence paired with flat physiology). Round 3 reveals that some Sleepers were actually awake with induced false REM signals, so the Auditors were applying experience-criteria to people who never slept at all. Before anyone names Malcolm or Dennett, let the group argue about whether theres a fact of the matter.

Then include the debrief: map Round 1 onto Malcolm's claim that experience requires publicly applicable correct/incorrect conditions, Round 2 onto the breakdown of those conditions in sleep reports, and Round 3 onto Dennett's idea that the narrative is assembled at waking rather than played back from sleep. Be honest about which feature the game cant reproduce — probably Malcolm's stricter linguistic point that without public criteria the concept of experience simply doesnt apply — and tell the facilitator to supply that part by hand. Also predict what will go wrong: some students will insist they just need better brain scanners, others will collapse into its all subjective, and the facilitator should let both mistakes happen before stepping in.
