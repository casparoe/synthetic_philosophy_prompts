I have been exploring a position that dissolves the classic problem of verisimilitude not by repairing Popper's logical-content measure but by replacing it with what I will call structural persistence: a false theory T is closer to the truth than a rival T' just in case T's counterfactual dependency graph—the directed causal-relational topology it asserts, abstracted away from specific parameter values and from ontological commitments to particular unobservable entities—remains invariant across a broader range of empirically adequate revisions, with the range weighted by the quantitative precision of T's predictions. On this account scientific progress is not the accumulation of true logical consequences but the preservation of the true system's adjacency structure while local nodes and parameters are updated or expanded.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this position—not merely the familiar Miller-Tichý objections about logical strength and false content, but which specific observations, formal results, or conceptual pressures it is actually exposed to. In particular:

- Which actual or conceivable cases from the history of science would show that high structural persistence correlates poorly with what we intuitively treat as theoretical progress, or with predictive success?
- Does the view risk collapsing into structural realism about relations and instrumentalism about parameters, and if so is that convergence fatal or acceptable?
- What formal conditions must a counterfactual dependency graph satisfy for an invariance metric to be well-defined across theory revisions, and are there results in causal inference showing that such graphs are underdetermined by observational data in ways that make the metric arbitrary?
- Would a defender be forced to concede that two theories with identical graph topologies but radically different underlying ontologies—say, one framed in continuous fields and one in discrete particles—tie for verisimilitude, and does that concession rob the view of its explanatory power?

Please rank these defeaters, together with any others you identify, from fatal to merely awkward. Distinguish the objections that a committed defender of structural persistence would dismiss as missing the point from those they would have to take seriously, and say whether the position is well insulated or genuinely risky. I want an honest map of its exposure—what it predicts that its rivals do not, what it risks, and where it might already be protected by qualification—without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
