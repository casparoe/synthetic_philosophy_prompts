Design the teaching plan for a 90-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of science for students who have completed formal epistemology but have not studied Hume's argument on miracles in depth. The audience will arrive treating the argument as either a disguised theological dismissal or as a bare appeal to prior improbability. The plan should not be the lecture itself; it should specify the dependency chain of concepts that must be in place before Hume's reasoning about testimony and uniform experience can be understood as a structural claim rather than a sneer at religion. Choose the single carrying example that will run through both the historical reconstruction and a modern parallel case— it must be secular, involve explicit quantitative base rates, and be capable of displaying the same logical form in both contexts— and specify what properties it must have and what properties it must lack to prevent students from reducing Hume's point to generic skepticism. Map the misconceptions in the predicted order of appearance: first, that uniform experience is merely an inductive count of past events; second, that the relevant probability is subjective degree of belief; third, that the conclusion entails the metaphysical impossibility of miracles. For each, state the precise point in the explanatory sequence where it should be intercepted and the move that defuses it. Provide one final check question that tests whether students recognize the structural parallel between testimony evaluation and anomalous scientific reporting, not whether they can summarize Hume's verdict. Explicitly identify which elements of the modern parallel—such as institutional peer-review dynamics or media amplification of outliers—must be set aside at this level because they depend on empirical sociology outside the session's scope. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
