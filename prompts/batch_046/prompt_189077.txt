Rank the following four refinements to the choice/circumstance distinction in luck egalitarianism, from most to least successful at addressing the charge that the view is harsh to agents who make genuinely free but catastrophic choices: (A) a responsibility-sensitive cutoff in Arneson's style, which excludes preferences formed under severe deprivation from the domain of voluntary choice; (B) a Cohen-style restriction that extends compensation to option-luck losses only when the underlying choice was made without adequate background information; (C) a hypothetical 'modal-proximity' amendment, not standard in the literature, which treats an outcome as a matter of choice only if the agent could have accessed an alternative in a nearby possible world with the same informational and motivational profile; (D) Roemer's equality-of-opportunity framework, which replaces individual-level choice identification with type-based counterfactual comparisons. For each, state in a sentence or two whether its strength or weakness lies mainly in how it handles informational conditions, modal accessibility of alternatives, or aggregation across individuals.
