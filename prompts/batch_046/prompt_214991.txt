I've been working through some issues in metaphysics of personal identity for a course I'm taking on modal essentialism, and I keep running into what seems like a tension in the origin essentialist position as it's usually presented. The standard line, which I take from discussions of Kripke's argument about Queen Elizabeth and her parents, is that an individual necessarily originates from the particular egg and sperm from which she actually developed, so that had different gametes fused, the resulting person wouldn't be her. But I'm wondering whether this claim is really about intrinsic identity conditions or whether it sneaks in an external relational requirement. After all, the particular sperm and egg are not parts of me now; they are historical entities with which I stand in a causal relation. So it looks like origin essentialism makes my identity depend on a specific past causal chain rather than on any feature I presently possess, whether psychological or physical. That seems closer to a relational or even historical theory of identity than to an intrinsic essentialism.

This leads me to a more specific worry that I haven't seen addressed clearly. Suppose we imagine a counterfactual scenario in which the very same egg and sperm that actually produced me are used, but due to a laboratory intervention during in vitro fertilization, the sperm's nucleus is replaced with the nucleus from a different sperm cell that happens to carry an identical genome — same sequence, same epigenetic markers — before fusion occurs. The causal origin, in terms of which physical cells initiated development, involves a substitute nucleus, yet the resulting genome and developmental trajectory are indistinguishable from my actual origin. Would the resulting person be me? If origin essentialism answers yes because the egg and the initiating sperm cell are the same, then the view seems to care about the container cells rather than the genetic information they carry, which feels arbitrary. If it answers no because the nucleus came from a different sperm, then the view is tracking genetic material rather than the particular gamete cells themselves. Either way, there seems to be a hidden ambiguity about whether origin essentialism is essentially about the continuity of specific biological material or about the causal history of that material.

Given all this, my question is this: doesn't origin essentialism actually collapse into either genetic essentialism or a purely causal-historical account of personal identity, and in either case, hasn't it lost the claim that origins are metaphysically special in a way that doesn't reduce to other properties? Or is there a coherent way to maintain that I necessarily come from these particular gametes without committing to either of those reductions, and if so, what does that view say about my hypothetical laboratory case? I'd appreciate an answer that separates what is broadly accepted among philosophers working on this topic from what remains genuinely contested, and please present it as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a thoughtful letter.
