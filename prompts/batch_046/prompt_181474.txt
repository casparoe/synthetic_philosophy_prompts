My half-baked idea comes from Hofstadter's tripartite split in GEB — frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages — but I think people treat it too statically. The precise question: can the frame/inner distinction survive across embedding levels, or does nesting force us to recognize a fourth message type — maybe a "cross-frame" or "interstitial" message that doesn't belong to either layer?

Why it's open: philosophers citing GEB usually talk about single-level strange loops (like Gödel-style self-reference) to argue for emergent meaning or consciousness. They don't systematically ask what happens when one loop frames another. Formal logicians care about self-reference but rarely use Hofstadter's specific "message location" vocabulary, so there's a gap.

My approach: I'll construct three artificial case studies — a base formal game with explicit rules (frame), explicit symbol-manipulation (outer), and a hidden self-describing property (inner) — then embed it inside a second game where that hidden property sets the interpretation rules. I'll map where meaning "lives" at each level and check whether there's leftover semantic content that can't be assigned to frame, outer, or inner in either system. I'll contrast this with standard internalism/externalism debates but won't do a literature review by citation; I'll fill references in later.

What counts as success: either (1) a defensible fourth category, (2) an argument that nesting collapses inner into frame and kills autonomy, or (3) a precise account of why the distinction is level-relative. Failure would be if all three cases just reduce to "context decides" with no structural difference.

Scope: one year, deliverable a 35-40 page chapter. It's tractable because I'm not solving consciousness, just mapping conceptual geography.
