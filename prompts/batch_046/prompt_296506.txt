I am preparing an essay that reads Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism — particularly the scenario in which an agent hesitates to save his spouse in order to weigh her life impartially against a stranger's — through the vocabulary of formal decision theory, specifically subjective expected utility with ambiguity aversion and non-substitutable preference arguments. I have a working grasp of the Savage framework but little training in axiomatizations of identity-dependent preferences. Please restate the core elements of Williams's objection — the alienating separation between agent and ground project, the demand for impartial calculation, and the sense that a single deliberative step is already morally corrupting — in these decision-theoretic terms. For each element, indicate what translates cleanly, what must be forcibly reshaped to fit (for instance, treating a commitment as a parameter in a state-dependent utility function), and what lacks any formal counterpart at all. Then evaluate whether the untranslatable residue reveals a structural limitation in decision-theoretic representations of practical reasoning, or whether it suggests that Williams's original formulation relied on rhetorical intensity that formalization rightly dissipates.
