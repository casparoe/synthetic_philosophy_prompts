I'm trying to put my finger on a pattern I keep seeing in enviromental history papers and I'm not sure if it's already named. Take a valley where rainfall hasn't changed in eight centuries and olives have always grown there. A historian writes that olives survived because a particular monastery preserved the knowledge through wars, or that a 1347 ordinance protected the groves. The climate and soil are mentioned as background conditions but never as the actual reason nothing else could replace the olives. But then if there's a drought in 1590 and the olives fail, suddenly the same author points to climate shift as the decisive cause. Another case: rice terraces on steep slopes stay in use for generations, and every continuity gets credited to clever local water-sharing agreements, never to the fact that flat land doesn't exist and rice is the only crop that works there. So there's this weird double standard where slow stable geography gets erased from explanations of continuity, but dragged in for explanations of change, and human agency gets the credit for keeping things the same. Is there an established term for this attribution asimmetry? Does it connect to Braudel's longue durée being invisible precisely because it doesn't change, or is it closer to some other fallacy about agency versus structure? If it doesn't have a name, how would you define its edges so it doesn't just collapse into ignoring background conditions?
