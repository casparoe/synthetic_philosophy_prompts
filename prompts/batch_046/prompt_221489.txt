I'm grading take-home exams for a graduate seminar on moral philosophy, and I've encountered a student response that I want a second opinion on before I finalize marks. The exam asked students to analyze a hypothetical case through Thomas Nagel's taxonomy of moral luck—specifically constitutive, circumstantial, causal, and resultant luck—while also addressing Bernard Williams's account of agent-regret. The case presented was as follows: Dr. Elena Vasquez, a field surgeon raised in a tradition emphasizing universal human dignity (a disposition she did not choose), is stationed in an active conflict zone and must decide whether to treat a critically wounded enemy combatant. Unbeknownst to her, an embedded journalist is recording the scene; had she refused treatment, the journalist's footage would have triggered international intervention protecting her unit from retaliation, but because she chooses compassionately, the footage instead documents her as a collaborator, leading to her court-martial.

The student wrote: "This case clearly involves both constitutive and circumstantial moral luck. The constitutive element is Vasquez's moral disposition—her upbringing shaped her character in ways outside her control, which is Nagel's standard example of luck in one's constitution. The circumstantial element is her being placed in this conflict zone with this particular patient. These categories remain cleanly distinct: her character is one factor, the external situation is another.

There is also resultant luck, since the court-martial outcome depends on the hidden journalist's presence, a factor Vasquez could not foresee. This is precisely where Williams's notion of agent-regret applies. Williams holds that when an agent causes harm through no fault of her own, she experiences a distinctive regret different from that of a mere spectator. Vasquez should therefore feel agent-regret about the court-martial, even though her decision was morally correct, because she is the author of the action that produced it.

That said, I think the hidden observer introduces what we might call informational circumstantial luck—the luck of being observed—which is really just a subset of causal luck. Causal luck concerns how actions are caused by prior factors, and the journalist's presence is such a prior factor. Consequently, the situation was determined by prior causal chains, which means there is no genuinely distinct circumstantial luck here. The case therefore involves constitutive luck, causal luck (including the observer), and resultant luck, but not circumstantial luck as an independent category."

The student clearly grasps the broad framework, but there are one or two issues that should meaningfully affect the score.
