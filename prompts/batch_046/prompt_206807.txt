I was at this coffee thing after a talk on the history of physics, and two grad students got into it about whether Galileo's falling-bodies thought experiment actually taught us anything new. Maya said yes, it's basically a conceptual stress-test: if you imagine tying a heavy rock to a light rock, Aristotelian logic forces you into a contradiction—should fall faster because combined weight is greater, but also slower because the light one drags—so the framework collapses from inside. She thinks that's genuine knowledge production, like running a simulation in your head where physics is the code and contradiction is the error message.

Leo wasn't buying it. He said thought experiments in science never run on pure logic; they run on mental models we've calibrated through embodied experience. When Galileo pictures rocks falling, he's not doing abstract reasoning—he's running a counterfactual simulation built from years of seeing things drop, and the contradiction only bites because we've already tacitly imported empirical regularities about weight and motion. Without that hidden empirical substrate, Leo argued, it's just storytelling with extra steps. He brought up Einstein's elevator too—people think it proves equivalence, but it only works because we already know elevators exist, that ropes can snap, that gravity pulls downward. The knowledge isn't born in the thought experiment; it's imported, compressed, and then recieved back out with a new label.

They turned to me since I cover this stuff and asked me to call it. Can you lay out who's right about which specific claim—whether conceptual contradiction alone can break a theory, whether embodied simulation is always smuggling empirics, whether Einstein's elevator proves anything without prior data? And what's missing from both sides? I'm genuinely stuck—feels like there's something about tacit mental modeling that neither of them fully addressed.
