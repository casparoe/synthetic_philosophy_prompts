Consider the Pasadena paradox: a sequential coin-toss game in which payoffs grow exponentially with the round number but are structured—through parity-dependent weights or analogous conditioning—so that the expected value is not infinite but undefined, with truncations at every finite horizon yielding contradictory recommendations and no convergent limit. For decades, decision theorists and philosophers of probability have divided into three camps: those who take the undefined expectation as a reductio of applying expected-utility maximization to genuinely infinite protocols; those who reject the game as decision-theoretically illegitimate on the grounds that no physically realizable agent can execute an unbounded sequence of moves; and those who seek to preserve rational evaluation by replacing linear expectation with bounded-utility transforms, non-Archimedean value representations, or dominance-based rules. Despite this tripartite structure being well mapped in the literature, the debate rarely advances—each camp rehearses its standard objection to the others, and no formal result or conceptual distinction has visibly shifted the stalemate.
