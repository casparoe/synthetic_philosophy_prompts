I'm convening a small interdisciplinary working group—philosophers, cognitive scientists, and a few practicing forecasters—for a single semester to tackle a specific tension at the intersection of inside-view versus outside-view reasoning and epistemic modesty. We are not rehashing the basic distinction; rather, we are stuck on what we have been calling the reference-class selection problem: whenever you try to apply an outside view, you must first identify the appropriate reference class of past reasoners or predictions, yet that identification seems to require precisely the kind of first-person, inside-view judgment that the outside view was meant to discipline. The result is a nested circularity—epistemic modesty tells us to distrust our own reasoning, but deciding how to distrust it demands reasoning we have just been told to distrust. Over twelve sessions, with participants who have philosophical training but not advanced statistics or formal decision theory, we want to produce a usable conceptual framework, not a literature review or bibliography.

Given these constraints, which two or three open conceptual questions should actually structure our agenda, and in what order should we pursue them? Please defend the prioritization with explicit criteria—foundational importance, tractability for this audience, and practical payoff for domains like institutional forecasting or philosophical disagreement—rather than merely listing unresolved issues. For each question, explain why it is upstream of the others (that is, which downstream disagreements dissolve if this one is settled), how tractable it is within a semester, and what kind of argument or limited empirical finding would cause you to reorder the list. I am especially interested in whether the central task should be analytical—clarifying the logical structure of the circularity—or normative—determining how much weight a modest reasoner should assign to her inside view when the reference class is ambiguous, contested, or self-selected. I also want you to identify the main misconceptions or dead ends we should expect—perhaps treating epistemic modesty as unconditional deference to base rates, or conflating calibration with humility—and indicate at which point in the sequence each should be headed off. The deliverable should be a defended agenda for limited resources, including the trade-offs you are making and what you are deliberately leaving out at this level.
