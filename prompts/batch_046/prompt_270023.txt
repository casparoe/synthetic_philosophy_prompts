Write a dialogue in American English between two philosophers of psychiatry, Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen, who hold opposing views on whether Hacking's looping effects ultimately stabilize or destabilize psychiatric classifications. Vasquez defends a "stabilized looping" position: she grants that psychiatric categories are interactive kinds, but argues that institutional standardization—such as structured clinical interviews and inter-rater reliability protocols—eventually "freezes" loops, producing categories that are predictively robust even if not biologically natural. Chen defends a "reflexive dissolution" position: he contends that these very institutional mechanisms create "diagnostic echo chambers," in which clinicians calibrate assessment instruments against prior diagnostic distributions rather than independent phenotypic features, progressively decoupling the classification from the behaviors it purports to track.

The dialogue should center on a concrete historical shift: the DSM-5 consolidation of DSM-IV's categorical autism subtypes (autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified) into a single autism spectrum disorder with dimensional severity levels. Vasquez should present this consolidation as a successful instance of loop-freezing—collapsing unstable subcategories into a more reliable spectrum. Chen should respond that the severity-level system introduces what he calls "severity calibration drift": clinicians adjust severity ratings in response to service-eligibility and resource-allocation thresholds rather than observed trait intensity, thereby opening a new, more hidden loop in which institutional incentives reshape the very phenotypes being measured.

Require that both characters respond directly to each other's arguments rather than talking past one another. Neither should be a strawman. The dialogue should be substantive and somewhat technical—engaging with the mechanics of looping, inter-rater reliability, and institutional feedback—without requesting citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
