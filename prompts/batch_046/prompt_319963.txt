I sit on a municipal heritage-review board that evaluates proposals for maintaining, altering, or removing public monuments tied to national “lost-cause” narratives—memorials to defeated armies, failed uprisings, or exiled communities whose histories have been folded into collective identity myths comparable to those surrounding the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo Field, or Dunkirk. We receive roughly one case per quarter, the stakes are always public and legal, and I must produce a documented rationale within ninety minutes that cannot be outsourced to staff historians or resolved by popular vote. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off opinion: an ordered sequence of tests I can run in writing, with clear stopping rules, explicit flags for where judgment replaces procedure, and an honest account of what the framework cannot settle.

The procedure should be grounded in the ethics of collective memory, specifically the claim that every such monument performs three potentially separable functions—mourning specific dead, advancing a political interpretation of defeat, and providing pedagogical complexity about the past—and that the central conceptual task is “mnemonic decoupling”: determining whether the commemorative object can honor suffering or mark an event without endorsing the ideological cause that originally produced it. Please translate this into a practical flow: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation (preserve, contextualize, relocate, or remove), and where it must hand the decision back to human judgment because the conflict is between incommensurable values rather than resolvable facts. Include the hard cases—where the dead and the cause are symbolically inseparable, where decoupling is technically possible but politically destabilizing, and where the monument has become an active site of present-day exclusion. Then run the procedure on three concrete proposals: (1) a town-square statue of a secessionist commander inscribed with cause-language, where decoupling clearly fails; (2) a proposed civic plaque honoring civilians evacuated during a “glorious defeat” retreat, sponsored by descendants and using mythic solidarity language, where the procedure likely approves with conditions but I personally distrust national-defeat mythmaking and want to see if my discomfort survives the framework; and (3) a monument to a failed religious-nationalist revolt commemorated both by legitimate descendants and by a modern extremist group, where decoupling is genuinely ambiguous. For each, show the step-by-step application, flag the judgment call, and state the recommendation. Conclude by stating precisely what this procedure cannot do—whether it can resolve conflicts between community solidarity and historical accuracy, or whether it merely structures the disagreement without dissolving it.
