I am preparing notes for a philosophy-of-mind seminar in which we are comparing Western personal-identity theories with Buddhist anatta, and I keep returning to the well-documented 1848 railroad accident involving Phineas Gage — the Vermont foreman who survived an iron rod driven through his frontal lobes — and the subsequent reports that, though physically intact, he seemed to become a different person: impatient, profane, and unable to execute long-term plans. Rather than running the usual psychological-continuity or narrative-self analysis, I want to examine the episode strictly through the Abhidharma framework of the five aggregates: rupa, vedana, sanna, sankhara, and vinnana.

Treat the pre-accident and post-accident Gage as two successive configurations of these aggregates rather than as stages of one enduring self. For each aggregate, identify what the historical record plausibly supports having changed, persisted, or become disordered — paying particular attention to sankhara (mental formations) and vinnana (consciousness) — and then say whether anatta explains the contemporaries' intuition that the original Gage had been lost better or worse than a Western narrative-identity account does. Reverse the analysis: does the case expose a silence or weakness in the Buddhist taxonomy, for instance in accounting for the causal role of localized neurological damage, or does it risk reducing a profound discontinuity to something so ordinary that it cannot capture why observers experienced the change as a death of the person? Keep the mapping disciplined — specify which feature of the episode corresponds to which aggregate-level shift, note where the theory is genuinely explanatory versus merely redescriptive, and conclude with whether Gage's transformation counts as an anomaly for anatta or as one of its most vivid historical illustrations.
