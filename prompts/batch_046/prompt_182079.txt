I work on a municipal oversight board — kind of an auditor but for legitimacy — and we keep getting these weird restructuring proposals about who gets to use force in the city. It's not just the usual reform vs defund vs abolish talk; we get stuff like neighborhood co-ops hiring armed responders with city certification, or mental-health teams that can legally detain people but don't carry police badges. I was arguing with a friend in poli-sci and she brought up Weber's monopoly on violence, which I mostly get, but the real headache is this grey zone I'm calling "layered coercion": the state keeps the symbolic authority but outsources the actual physical act, or keeps the badge but outsources the decision about when to use it. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run in like twenty minutes per proposal — ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, clear stop points where the answer is no, and clear hand-off points where it's not a fact question but a values call for the council. It should be grounded in what philosophy and political theory actually know about state monopoly and delegated violence, but translated so colleagues who aren't theorists can apply it without guessing. Then run it on two cases: first, a pilot where unarmed crisis responders can hold someone up to four hours if they wear a city-issued vest; second, a community-contract model where residents pay for private security but the city retains veto power over tactics. One of these should come out against my intuition — I kinda like both, but the procedure should reject at least one on structural grounds, not just preference. Also flag exactly where the procedure fails or misfires, and seperate what's fairly settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested among people who study this — don't present a disputed reading of Weber or of legitimacy as if it's the consensus.
