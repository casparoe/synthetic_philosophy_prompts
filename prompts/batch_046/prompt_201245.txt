Write one final-exam essay question for an upper-level philosophy of science or epistemology seminar on Bayesian epistemology and the problem of old evidence. Do not use the standard Mercury-perihelion, Einsteinian gravitation, or Hesperus-Phosphorus illustrations. Instead, center the problem on this specific scenario: a geophysicist already holds an archival set of raw seismic waveforms from a fault region. She then proposes a new structural model of the fault line. To evaluate it, she reprocesses the same archival waveforms with a newly developed denoising filter; importantly, the filter's hyperparameters were calibrated in part using that very same archival set. Once filtered, the cleaned signals align with the new model's predictions significantly better than with the prior model. She then treats this improved alignment as fresh confirming evidence for her new structural hypothesis.

The essay question should require students to determine whether this counts as a legitimate case of old-evidence confirmation, as spurious double-counting, or as learning the explanatory relevance of an already-known fact—without allowing them to stop at labeling. It should force them to specify exactly where the Bayesian update occurs (prior, likelihood, or posterior restructuring), to identify whether and how the calibration step alters the evidential status of the waveforms, and to explain why one interpretation respects the old-evidence constraint while another violates it. The question should be answerable in about 60 minutes and should not be solvable by reciting the standard distinction between acquiring E and acquiring that H explains E.

Please provide:
1. The essay question itself, precisely worded and focused on the crux described above.
2. A model answer of approximately 600 words that executes the specific conceptual moves an A-level response must contain.
3. A marking scheme totaling 20 marks that assigns points to specific required moves, distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming exactly which moves are present or missing, and includes a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs marks for each point.

Write the response in American English, with no requests for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
