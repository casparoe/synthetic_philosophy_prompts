Construct a decision-tree taxonomy of positions falling under structural realism in the philosophy of science. Begin with the root claim that scientific progress is best understood as the preservation of mathematical or relational structure across theory change, then branch through concrete diagnostic questions that do not depend on knowing academic labels in advance. The branching criteria should be: (i) whether structural knowledge exhausts what can be known about unobservable entities or whether some non-structural knowledge remains accessible; (ii) whether structures are ontologically fundamental, with individual objects derivative or eliminated altogether, or whether objects remain fundamental and structures are merely descriptive; (iii) whether structures require concrete instantiation in particular entities or can exist as free-standing relations; and (iv) whether the structural-continuity thesis applies only to mature physical theories or extends to the special sciences and social structures. Each leaf should name the corresponding position—such as epistemic structural realism, eliminative ontic structural realism, non-eliminative ontic structural realism, or moderate structural realism—together with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward ontological commitment. Identify at least two logically coherent combinations of answers that currently lack named representatives in the literature, and for each provide a brief diagnosis of why the combination remains unoccupied (for instance, tension with other commitments, lack of theoretical motivation, or mere historical neglect). Ensure the tree distinguishes positions that introductory surveys often conflate, particularly those that accept structural continuity across revolutions but deny that structure is ontologically prior to its relata. Present the taxonomy in clear British English, using labelled branches and concise leaf descriptions, and include a scoring guide showing how a reader’s answers map onto the leaves without requiring prior familiarity with the terminology.
