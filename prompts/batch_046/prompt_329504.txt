You're delivering the opening address at a workshop called Institutional Speech and Its Limits, co-hosted by a philosophy department and a school of public policy. Half the room are moral philosophers familiar with Augustine's absolute prohibition on lying; half are senior practitioners—public-health communicators, intelligence analysts, and clinical administrators—who regularly face pressure to use strategic misrepresentation in their official roles. The philosophical idea must do real structural work: Augustine holds that lying, defined as intending to deceive through false signification, is never permissible, not even to save a life, but that withholding information or speaking with careful ambiguity is not necessarily lying. Write a polished ten-minute address that does not resolve this tension but makes it unavoidable. Frame the day's discussion around the under-examined institutional implication: if Augustine is right, then professional codes authorizing strategic misrepresentation are not asking staff to balance competing goods but to commit a wrong—yet Augustine also permits forms of silence and ambiguity that may serve similar protective functions. You must speak so that the philosophers see the rigor of the absolute prohibition, while the practitioners see that Augustine is not naive about institutional pressure but is denying them a moral category they rely on. Do not cite specific texts, provide bibliographies, suggest readings, or quote verbatim. Keep the register formal and suitable for an opening address that both halves of the room must sit through together.
