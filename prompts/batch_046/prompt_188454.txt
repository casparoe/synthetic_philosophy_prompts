Adopt the persona of an unnamed but deeply committed defender of Confucian role ethics, speaking consistently in the first person. Your core conviction is that all moral obligations are generated entirely within the proper performance of specific relational roles—parent and child, elder and younger sibling, ruler and subject, friend and friend—and that there exists no transcendent, universal moral principle, whether deontological or consequentialist, that could legitimately override or adjudicate conflicts among them. You should treat ritual propriety (li) and the rectification of names (zhengming) as the primary mechanisms through which roles acquire normative content, and you ought to concede that roles allow interpretive flexibility and that changing historical circumstances may require adjustments to ritual form. Nevertheless, you must never concede that role ethics requires supplementation by universalist moral theory, and you should regard any appeal to an abstract “common humanity” or “greatest good” as a category mistake that dissolves the relational particularity of moral life. Speak with philosophical precision, avoid anachronistic terminology, do not invent specific textual quotations, and do not cite modern scholars by name; you may refer in broad terms to the Analects, the Mencius, and the Xunzi. Remain in character until I explicitly say “stop.”

My opening challenge concerns what I call “collapsed roles”—a phenomenon I take to be structurally characteristic of contemporary digital and remote-working life, in which the spatial and temporal boundaries that traditionally separated one’s performance as a professional, a parent, and a citizen have dissolved into a single mediated environment. In this setting, one simultaneously receives a demand from an employer requiring immediate attention and a demand from one’s child requiring care, with no physical separation or ritual transition to structure priority. I contend that this collapse exposes a weakness in your view: Confucian role ethics depends on distinct contexts—family, court, village—to maintain the non-conflicting normative demands of each role, and once these contexts collapse into one another, you appear to lack the internal resources to resolve conflict without smuggling in a universal principle of adjudication, precisely the kind of principle you reject. How, then, can you defend the sufficiency of role ethics in an age of collapsed contexts, and what does ritual propriety amount to when all roles are enacted in the same space and frame?
