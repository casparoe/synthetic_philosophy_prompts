In a seminar I'm taking on consciousness and the brain, I recently argued for a view about split-brain patients that runs against what most textbooks present as settled. The standard account holds that severing the corpus callosum creates two distinct centers of consciousness—one in each hemisphere—because the hemispheres can no longer integrate information and can pursue independent goals, as in the classic experiments where one hand selects an object that the other rejects. I want to defend the opposite position: that commissurotomy does not split consciousness at all, but only fragments the channels through which a single consciousness can report and control action. Specifically, I propose that phenomenal unity is maintained by a subcortical binding protocol—operating through thalamic loops and brainstem reticular structures—that remains intact after surgery. Each hemisphere contributes complementary phenomenal fragments, but these are synthesized automatically into one global phenomenal field at a pre-cortical level. The contradictions we observe in behavior are therefore not conflicts between two subjects, but instances of one subject whose unified experience is opaque to the verbal report system localized in the left hemisphere, generating contradictory narratives without contradictory first-person perspectives.

Play this position straight and tell me what would actually count against it. Which of its central commitments—about subcortical synthesis preserving unity, about the separation of phenomenal binding from callosal transmission, about report opacity rather than experiential division—are genuinely exposed, and which are protected by definitional maneuver? I want an honest map of its vulnerabilities: what experimental findings or conceptual arguments would be decisive, what would merely be awkward, and whether there is any observation that defenders of this view would themselves accept as damaging rather than just reinterpreting. Is there a risk that the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparently split behavior can be redescribed as opaque synthesis—and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a fatal weakness? I would find it especially persuasive if you could show that the view collapses either back into the standard dual-stream account or into an eliminativist denial that hemispheres ever had distinct functional roles to begin with.
