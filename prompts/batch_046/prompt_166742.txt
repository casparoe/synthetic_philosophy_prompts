I am a retired physicist who, after leaving the laboratory, has begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mind and cognitive science at a nearby university. In a recent discussion group on classical artificial intelligence, my colleagues and I kept returning to the same frustration: engineers often speak as though the frame problem has been “solved” by larger context windows or better database schemas, while philosophers treat it as an enduring question about relevance, context, and what it means for an agent to know what changes and what does not. To help my study group see the philosophical stakes clearly, I would like you to produce a piece of sustained satire that treats this engineering overconfidence as an ordinary consumer-product failure.

You are an expert philosopher of cognitive science with deep familiarity with classical AI, nonmonotonic reasoning, and the philosophy of mind. Before you write, please take a moment to brainstorm the following conceptual distinctions so that they inform the satire with genuine precision rather than caricature: the difference between the representational frame problem (how to store efficiently which fluents change) and the inferential frame problem (how to determine which fluents are relevant without checking all of them); Reiter’s successor-state axioms as a formal strategy for limiting the axioms required for action descriptions; McCarthy’s circumscription as a nonmonotonic method that minimizes abnormal predicates; and the Yale shooting problem as a canonical failure mode of default reasoning in which a system draws lethal inferences from incomplete temporal updates. Consider, in particular, how each technical idea could be misread by a marketing department as a feature rather than a philosophical limitation.

Then write a deadpan parody of a serious consumer-technology product review—approximately 650 words, in the authoritative, straight-faced voice of a publication like Consumer Reports or Wired—evaluating a fictional smart-home assistant called “Circum” (manufactured by the fictional firm “Fluent Logic, Inc.”) that advertises itself as having “fully resolved the frame problem through proprietary circumscription engines and dynamic successor-state updating.” The reviewer should treat philosophical failures as conventional product defects: connectivity issues, battery drain, and safety hazards. The humor and critique must arise from accurate philosophical content woven into the review’s jargon. For example, the device should correctly apply successor-state axioms so that flipping a light switch does not alter the color of the wallpaper, yet it should then collapse on the Yale shooting problem—defaulting that a loaded firearm remains loaded after an intervening waiting period, with catastrophic domestic results. The company should confuse the representational problem (storing updates) with the inferential problem (knowing what matters without exhaustive search), and the reviewer should praise this confusion as an “elegant minimalism in ontology management.” At one point, the system should attempt to apply circumscription by minimizing abnormal predicates until it concludes that the user herself is an abnormal fluent and should be minimized from the household database. The target is not any named individual but the discursive practice of treating deep philosophical questions about relevance and context as purely engineering optimizations. Maintain sustained irony throughout: never break character, never wink, and ensure that a specialist recognizes the satire as conceptually fair while a lay reader can still follow the domestic disaster. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further literature; the piece should be entirely self-contained. Avoid verbatim quotation marks drawn from real texts; present technical terms only as part of the review’s deadpan corporate jargon. By the end, a reader should understand both what is being mocked and exactly why the mockery is philosophically accurate.
