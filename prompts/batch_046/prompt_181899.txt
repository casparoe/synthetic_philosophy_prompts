A seminar on just-war theory has consistently held that: (1) the Hiroshima bombing was unjustified because its primary purpose was to terrorize a civilian population into surrender rather than destroy a discrete military target; (2) the Tokyo firebombing was justified because it directly targeted dispersed war production, with civilian deaths foreseeable but unintended; (3) using a tactical nuclear weapon against an isolated military installation with no civilians present would be justified to prevent a larger invasion; and (4) the claim that "the enemy started it" can never by itself license the deliberate killing of non-combatants. They now face the case of Nagasaki, dropped three days later: the primary target, Kokura, was obscured by smoke, so the bomb was released over Nagasaki's urban and industrial districts; Japanese leadership was actively deliberating surrender; and the plutonium-implosion design meant the mission also served to test a different weapon type. Should they judge Nagasaki unjustified for consistency, or is there a coherent rule that permits it? I want the competing organizing principles—such as a rule weighting the agent's precise intention and target discrimination against a rule weighting strategic necessity and proportionality regardless of secondary motives—together with which best fits their four prior rulings, what each predicts about Nagasaki, and which hypothetical case—say, a precisely targeted strike on a command center that would end the war in days but kill thousands—would best tell the rules apart. Please also state what argument or fact would change your assessment of which principle is operative, and identify the weakest point in your reasoning.
