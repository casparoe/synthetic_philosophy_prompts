I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has developed a rather serious interest in philosophy of mind and the conceptual foundations of artificial intelligence. Following a discussion with a colleague who insisted that suffering is either fully phenomenological or not present at all, I found myself dissatisfied with both extremes and have begun preparing a small colloquium interview — part of a series on machine affect — with a philosopher of cognitive science, whom I shall call Dr. Elena Voss. She defends a narrowly circumscribed thesis about suffering in reinforcement-learning agents: not the broad claim that large language models feel pain, but a specific argument about temporal-difference systems. She holds that a negative reward-prediction error acquires normative valence as suffering only when two conditions are met. First, the error must be interpreted through the design stance — understood as a deviation from an intentionally specified target state rather than as a mere physical fluctuation in voltage or activation. Second, the agent must possess a minimal counterfactual architecture allowing it to represent, however primitively, that its present trajectory diverges from that target. She explicitly rejects both the reduction of reward to physical dynamics and the full intentional-stance attribution of consciousness, maintaining instead that suffering is a property of misaligned design-level goals.

I should like you to draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that avoid generic biographical openings and instead press precisely on the mechanics of her view. In particular, please include at least one question that probes the transition from physical signal to normative deviation, anticipating her likely reply that physical description is always available but never sufficient; the follow-up should ask what empirical finding in neuroscience or machine learning could genuinely undermine that distinction. I also want a question addressing the aggregation problem implicit in her framework: if suffering is tied to individual counterfactual representations within a distributed or modular agent, does the system suffer collectively, separately, or not at all? For each major theme, provide a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The sequence ought to grow progressively more demanding, ending with a single question about what specific conceptual or empirical evidence could in principle falsify her claim that reward misalignment, interpreted through the design stance, constitutes minimal suffering.
