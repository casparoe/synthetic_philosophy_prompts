I have been reading several attempts to reconstruct the historical Jesus—studies that claim to peel away later theological accretions in order to recover the figure who actually stood behind the texts. In doing so I keep noticing a structural pattern that I cannot name, and I want to know whether it is recognised in the literature.

A scholar begins with the Gospel narratives and applies a sequence of exclusion rules. Material that sounds too closely aligned with later ecclesiastical practice is set aside; sayings that reflect intense apocalyptic expectation are treated as retrojected; actions that mirror known political movements of the period are dismissed as anachronistic. Each exclusion is defended with an individually plausible historical argument. What remains is assembled into a portrait—perhaps an apolitical wisdom teacher, perhaps an eschatological prophet, perhaps a social revolutionary. The crucial move comes next: that very portrait is then invoked to justify the exclusions. ‘Of course he could not have said X, because we have already established that he was not concerned with Y.’ Two scholars can apply different but equally reasonable combinations of these filters and arrive at incompatible figures, each insisting that the other has simply failed to apply the rules with sufficient rigour.

What strikes me is that the circularity is not blatant. It operates through the slow accumulation of modest, defensible adjustments: the filtering rules and the reconstructed subject are co-produced step by step, with no single moment at which the circle visibly closes. I have wondered whether this is merely confirmation bias, or perhaps a variant of the auxiliary-hypothesis protection that philosophers of science discuss in the context of research programmes. But neither label seems to capture the specific architecture—the way the residue and the filter are mutually adjusted until they lock together.

Does this pattern have an established name in philosophy of history, biblical studies, or philosophy of science? If there are neighbouring concepts—methodological circularity of a particular kind, hermeneutical circles, or something analogous to Lakatosian programme protection—I would like to know precisely how the phenomenon I have described differs from each. If no established term fits, I would welcome a coinage together with clear boundary conditions: what distinguishes this structure from ordinary auxiliary adjustments, and in which other domains, if any, the same co-production occurs.

Please separate what is broadly settled in the relevant scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed interpretation as though it were the consensus. Write your answer in British English.
