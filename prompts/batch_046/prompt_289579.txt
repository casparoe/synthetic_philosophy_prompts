I sit on the allocation committee of a small institutional fund that pilots behavioral-intervention programs—workplace dietary shifts, voter-registration tools, staff carbon-offset commitments—where every proposal claims large collective benefits but the direct marginal impact of any single funded unit is philosophically contested. Drawing on the literature about individual contributions to collective harms and the inefficacy objection—particularly the distinctions between causal overdetermination, probabilistic redundancy, threshold-triggered effects, and expressive or symbolic value—please design a reusable decision procedure my committee can execute in a single thirty-minute session without requiring members to parse original papers. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each node, where to flag for discussion, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a person because the issue is normative rather than factual. Include annotations marking which steps are factual assessments versus irreducible value judgments, and state clearly what the procedure cannot settle—for instance, whether symbolic or expressive justification can outweigh direct-impact opportunity costs. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a proposal to fund a university beef-reduction campaign projected to shift about three percent of meals served; (b) a proposal to fund a get-out-the-vote mobile app in a jurisdiction with already-high turnout; and (c) a deliberately hard case in which the causal model shows a technically nonzero but astronomically small individual contribution, the collective-harm framing is strong, and the symbolic argument is equally strong, so that the procedure yields a recommendation that conflicts with a direct-impact, effective-altruist intuition. The procedure should be honest about its failure modes and misfires, especially where small errors in estimating base rates or counterfactual dependencies could flip the outcome, and it should specify the institutional constraints—who must sign off, what data must be present, and what to do when members disagree at a value-laden node.
