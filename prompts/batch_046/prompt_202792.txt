I spent most of last evening in a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about whether collective intentionality genuinely requires a shared mental state or whether interlocking individual intentions can do all the explanatory work. We kept circling back to William Golding's Lord of the Flies as a kind of test case, and I want a philosophical reading that takes both the novel and the conceptual debate seriously rather than reducing one to the other.

Read the novel through the lens of collective intentionality and joint action, with particular attention to the boys' efforts to constitute themselves as a governing "we" through the conch and to the eventual collapse into tribal violence. Does Golding depict a plural subject in Margaret Gilbert's sense — a joint commitment that binds members even when individual preferences diverge — or does he present only an aggregate of I-intentions that disintegrates once Ralph's shared plan fractures? Examine the negotiations between Ralph and Jack over authority, and the collective decision to hunt Ralph: do these scenes dramatize the conditions for shared cooperative activity as philosophers like Michael Bratman analyze them, or do they expose a structural gap between having a collective goal and being genuinely jointly committed to it? I am especially interested in whether the novel suggests that joint action is inherently unstable without some external authority to sustain the joint commitment, and whether that literary insight adds something to the philosophical debate that purely conceptual analysis tends to miss.

Please keep the analysis faithful in both directions: do not treat the boys as mere illustrations of philosophical theories, but also do not distort the theories to fit the plot. Point to specific scenes where the philosophical reading is strongest and where the novel actively resists it. I am preparing to teach this in a philosophy of social action course and want to use the reading without misleading students about either Golding's psychology or the conceptual distinctions at stake.
