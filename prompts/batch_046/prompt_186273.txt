Two colleagues in a philosophy of religion reading group—Arjun and Priya—have been arguing about whether Jain anekāntavāda should be read as an ontological pluralism about contradictory properties or as an epistemic doctrine about the partiality of any single standpoint (naya). They were working through the standard saptabhaṅgī framework applied to a concrete case: the claim that this clay pot is permanent.

Arjun opened by saying that anekāntavāda is fundamentally a theory of epistemic humility. He argued that when a Jain philosopher asserts syād asti and syād nāsti—qualified in each case by syāt—the speaker is adopting a particular naya, such as the substantial standpoint versus the modal standpoint, without making an unqualified metaphysical commitment. On his reading, the object itself is not contradictory; human cognition is necessarily partial, and the seven-fold formula is a linguistic protocol for avoiding dogmatic overreach. He added that this makes anekāntavāda structurally similar to modern fallibilism or perspectival realism: reality is consistent, but our descriptions of it are always ekānta, one-sided.

Priya pushed back that this collapses anekāntavāda into generic fallibilism and erases its distinctive ontological commitment. She insisted that nayavāda is not merely about cognitive limitation but about the object being genuinely anekānta—non-one-sided—such that it actually possesses contradictory properties in different respects. The qualification syāt is required not because reality is hidden, but because language is inherently ekānta and can express only one aspect at a time. She pointed to the seventh predicate, avaktavya (inexpressible), as evidence: if the object merely exceeded our knowledge, there would be no need for a logical category marking certain combinations as literally unsayable within a single statement. Instead, she argued, this category shows that reality itself outstrips any single predicative form, which is an ontological thesis.

Arjun replied that even if reality is complex, calling it ontologically pluralistic risks attributing to the Jains a violation of the law of non-contradiction. He noted that in practical ethical reasoning—say, applying ahiṃsā to environmental policy—Jain thinkers use anekāntavāda to weigh competing considerations without asserting that an action is literally both harmful and harmless in the same respect at once. This practical use, he said, supports the epistemic reading: the method helps navigate a consistent but complex world, not a world containing real contradictions.

Priya answered that this misreads the practical application. The reason multiple nayas are necessary in ethical deliberation is precisely that the situation itself contains genuinely conflicting values—life, livelihood, ecological balance—that are not reducible to a single correct ordering. The method reflects reality's structure; it does not merely paper over our ignorance. She concluded that reducing anekāntavāda to epistemic humility turns it into a banal reminder that things are complicated, whereas its philosophical force comes from claiming that contradiction, when properly qualified by standpoint, is a feature of reality as much as of our descriptions.
