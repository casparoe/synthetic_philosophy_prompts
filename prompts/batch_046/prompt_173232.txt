After a long argument with a friend about whether the European Union is genuinely federalist or merely intergovernmental, I want to check whether the summary he offered holds up under scrutiny. He characterized the EU principle of subsidiarity—enshrined in Article 5 of the Treaty on European Union—as a constitutional rule requiring that every EU legislative act be accompanied by proof that its objective cannot be sufficiently achieved by member states acting individually or in coordination. From this he drew three comparative claims. First, he said this makes the EU structurally identical to Germany’s cooperative federalism and to the post-New Deal United States, because in all three systems the central authority intervenes only where local units demonstrably fail. Second, he claimed that the European Court of Justice treats subsidiarity as a rigorously justiciable standard: if a member state challenges a directive, the Court will invalidate it unless the Commission has produced a detailed comparative-efficiency analysis showing that national action was insufficient—much as the U.S. Supreme Court’s pre-New Deal dual-federalism doctrine struck down federal statutes that encroached on state police powers. Third, he argued that Switzerland’s cantonal autonomy represents the logical endpoint of subsidiarity, since any shift of competences to the federal level there triggers a mandatory popular referendum; therefore, he concluded, the EU’s absence of such direct-democratic veto mechanisms proves that subsidiarity in Brussels is merely rhetorical and that the Union is not genuinely committed to decentralization.

I am inclined to think the comparison to Switzerland is a stretch, but I am less certain about the legal points. Is this a fair summary of how subsidiarity operates in EU law, and of its relationship to American, German, and Swiss federalism? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common but defensible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation—particularly regarding the justiciability of subsidiarity before the ECJ, the analogy to American dual federalism, and the inference from Swiss direct democracy to EU institutional design. Finally, does his conclusion that the EU lacks genuine commitment to subsidiarity actually follow once the summary is corrected?
