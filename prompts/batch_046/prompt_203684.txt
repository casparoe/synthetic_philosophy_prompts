I'm drafting a conceptual paper on the metaphysics of biological function and have been testing my colleague Dr. Aris's intuitions over a series of informal cases. Her verdicts so far have been consistent but leave her underlying principle ambiguous. She grants that a mammalian heart has the function of pumping blood, but denies that the acoustic vibrations it produces serve any biological function; she accepts that the crystalline lens in a vertebrate eye functions to focus light, yet she refuses to assign a function to the particular refractive index of the aqueous humor, treating it as a metabolic by-product rather than a functional trait; she holds that the flat tail of a North American beaver serves both the function of signaling danger to conspecifics and the function of fat storage, while insisting that the tail's brown pigmentation is non-functional; and she maintains that the rotary motion of a bacterial flagellum has the function of propulsion, whereas the thermal energy dissipated by that rotation does not.

The new case I want her to consider is a synthetic chimeric tissue, engineered in vitro and never part of any evolutionary lineage, composed of intermingled cardiomyocyte and hepatocyte clusters. These clusters spontaneously generate slow, coordinated electrical oscillations that do not drive contraction or secretion, but they stabilize local pH in the bioreactor; without them the tissue dies within hours. The tissue was designed for metabolic study, not for oscillation, and no selection process—natural or artificial—has ever favored that electrical behavior. Would Dr. Aris say these oscillations have a biological function? I am less interested in a simple yes or no than in mapping the rules that could generate her verdict: a strict etiological account that requires a selection history specifically for the effect in question; a broad causal-role account that requires only that the effect currently contributes to the survival or reproduction of the system in which it occurs; and a hybrid "systemic maintenance" view that demands evolutionary pedigree for the underlying trait but allows present contribution to determine its current functional profile, perhaps treating novel contributions as non-functional exaptations. For each of these, I want to know what it predicts about the synthetic tissue.

Finally, I want to know which comparative case would best discriminate among these rules if her synthetic-tissue verdict remains ambiguous. I am considering pairing it with a naturally occurring vestigial eye in a cave-dwelling salamander that has lost photoreception but now secretes melatonin in response to temperature shifts, using a cellular mechanism that was never selected for that secretory role. If she assigns a function to the oscillations but denies one to the melatonin secretion, or vice versa, what exactly does that reveal about whether she requires evolutionary origin, present contribution, or some intermediate condition? And if both verdicts align, what further case—perhaps involving an artificially selected trait in a domesticated organism whose original wild-type function has been entirely lost—would force the distinction into the open?
