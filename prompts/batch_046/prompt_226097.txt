I serve on the Intellectual Property and Ethics Committee of the Interdisciplinary Research Consortium. By next Friday we must rule on whether to recognize proprietary control over an abstract conceptual framework—a researcher-developed taxonomy of epistemic dependency chains that maps how normative concepts propagate through collaborative networks—after a private funder that covered thirty percent of the costs demands exclusive licensing rights over its use in commercial AI training protocols. The researcher contends that ideas are non-rivalrous goods and that her contribution amounts to conceptual labor rather than tangible invention; the funder replies that without exclusivity the investment is unrecoverable and that, in applied contexts, the taxonomy operates as a de facto trade secret. Draft the short opinion I will circulate before the meeting: recommend one concrete course of action—grant limited proprietary rights, release the framework into an attribution commons, or deny the claim outright—include an implementation timeline, and treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine seriousness rather than dismissal. Reason from the nature of conceptual goods, the limits of labor-mixing arguments in intellectual space, and the Consortium's institutional purpose, but do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
