I want to apply this lens to the ratification of the Twenty-Second Amendment in 1951, which followed Franklin Roosevelt's unprecedented election to four presidential terms. Rather than debating the wisdom of term limits in general, please treat the Amendment as a collective attempt by one democratic generation to bind its successors against a specific institutional failure mode—the consolidation of executive power through repeated electoral renewal. Analyze how the Amendment's architecture, including its provision that a vice president who serves more than two years of a predecessor's term may be elected only once, maps onto hard and soft precommitment. Does that exception weaken the contract, or does it supply the calibrated flexibility that makes collective precommitment credible over time? I also want the reverse analysis: what does this episode reveal about the theory? In particular, who counts as the future self being bound when the precommitment is enacted by a constitutional majority rather than an individual, and does the Amendment's eventual acceptance suggest that democratic Ulysses contracts require a particular kind of crisis to be ratified? Please be disciplined about where the analogy to individual self-binding holds and where it collapses, and avoid forcing the framework to speak where it is silent.
