I spent much of yesterday evening in a rather heated but enjoyable argument with a close friend about the norms of assertion — specifically whether knowledge, truth, or belief ought to govern what we may properly claim — and we kept circling back to a case neither of us could settle satisfactorily. Imagine a senior museum curator who, in a closed board meeting, asserts that a newly donated ceramic vessel is fifth-century Attic rather than Corinthian, basing her claim entirely on a conservator’s technical report she has not personally evaluated; she then repeats the identical claim in a public press release aimed at potential donors. My friend insisted that belief, fortified by institutional role, is sufficient in both contexts, whereas I felt that knowledge must be required yet struggled to say whether the curator genuinely knows anything when her epistemic position is so thoroughly delegated to another expert. I am writing the final for an upper-level philosophy of language seminar and want an essay question that forces students to go well beyond rehearsing the standard knowledge-norm account or its belief-norm rivals. The question should present this kind of scenario and ask students to analyse whether the correct norm of assertion varies with audience and with the speaker’s dependence on testimony, and whether an institutional role can ever substitute for first-person epistemic achievement. Please provide the essay question itself — suitable for a 90-minute examination and expecting roughly 800 words — together with a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, present exactly five numbered criteria, each expressed in one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; each criterion should identify a specific analytical move that distinguishes an excellent answer from a merely competent one, particularly regarding the distinction between primary and secondary assertion and the problem of expert dependency. Write in British English and avoid asking for citations, bibliographies, or named texts beyond the general debate.
