Consider David Lewis's account of convention—developed in Convention and in subsequent work on the evolution of norms—according to which a behavioral regularity within a population becomes a convention only when it solves a coordination problem, is sustained by common knowledge of the regularity together with knowledge of others' preferences for conformity, and is arbitrary in the sense that an alternative regularity could have served the same coordinating purpose. Apply this framework to a phenomenon that became prominent only after Lewis's death: in federated social-network architectures built on protocols such as ActivityPub, server administrators collectively practice defederation, that is, the refusal to exchange messages with specific server instances that violate shared moderation norms. This is decentralized, lacks any central legislator, and involves a coordination problem among administrators who must align on which instances to isolate without explicit negotiation.

What would Lewis likely have said about whether defederation norms constitute a genuine Lewisian convention? In particular, address: (1) whether the payoff structure among server administrators is genuinely analogous to canonical coordination problems such as driving on the right; (2) whether common knowledge of the regularity is achievable given the opacity and asynchronicity of server-level decision-making; (3) whether the norm is arbitrary in Lewis's sense, given that technical protocols could in principle automate isolation and thereby eliminate the need for mutual expectation; and (4) how his distinction between conventions proper and mere social norms, or between conventions of action and conventions of language, might apply to this digital-governance case. Construct your answer from the internal logic of his theory rather than from external commentary, and identify the specific feature of federated governance that most strains—or most naturally extends—his original apparatus.
