I am writing from our seminar on comparative moral philosophy to ask whether there is an established name for a pattern I keep encountering in discussions of Buddhist ethics, and if not, I would like it precisely carved. The pattern is this: an interlocutor defends Buddhist ethics as non-consequentialist by insisting that the moral quality of an action resides primarily in the agent's mental state—cetana—at the moment of acting, so that outcomes matter mainly for the agent's own karmic trajectory rather than as direct measures of rightness. But then, when the same person is asked to judge a concrete case—say, whether a bodhisattva may violate a precept to prevent widespread harm, or which of two monastic welfare programs is ethically superior—she slides without acknowledgment into evaluating the action by its aggregate effects on sentient beings, sometimes stretching across rebirths, as though welfare maximization were the operative criterion. The shift is not presented as a change of framework; it happens within a single argumentative thread. For example, one classmate recently argued that deceptive speech is wrong because it corrupts the speaker's mind, appealing exclusively to internal mental purity; yet when I raised the case of a physician who lies to a dying patient to prevent despair, she justified the lie solely by citing the net reduction in suffering, never returning to the speaker's mental state. In another discussion, someone claimed that the merit of giving depends entirely on the donor's non-attachment, but then proceeded to rank charitable institutions by the total happiness they produce, treating the donor's intention as an ancillary accounting variable rather than the heart of the evaluation. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration—the unacknowledged oscillation between an agent-centered, mental-state criterion and an outcome-centered, aggregate-welfare criterion, with the criterion chosen according to whether the speaker is defending the tradition's distinctiveness or applying it? If so, how does it differ from plain equivocation, from the doctrine of skillful means, and from a two-level view in which intention governs personal karma while outcome governs social ethics? If not, can you coin a precise term, define its boundary conditions, and indicate what counts as a clear instance versus a borderline case? I care far more about the carving—what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie—than about the bare label. Please respond in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter or an email.
