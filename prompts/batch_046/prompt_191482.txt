I'm on the committee writing this year's philosophy of modality PhD comprehensive exam. Draft one essay question that a candidate could answer in 90 minutes. The question should test whether they can do more than summarize Lewisian counterpart theory and Kripkean transworld identity—specifically, whether they can apply both frameworks to adjudicate identity conditions for a composite, socially distributed object with vague boundaries. Use this constructed scenario: in the actual world there is a continuously revised, collectively authored digital philosophical archive (call it the "Open Ontology Project") with an indeterminate contributor roster and evolving arguments. In possible world W1, the archive has 47 named contributors and contains argument A in formulation F1; in W2, it has 43 named contributors (only 31 overlap with W1), argument A appears in a substantively revised formulation F2, and the institutional framing has changed. The question should ask candidates to determine, under counterpart theory and under transworld identity respectively, whether the archive in W2 is identical to the actual-world archive, and whether argument A in F2 is identical to argument A in F1. They must identify the precise point at which each framework succeeds or breaks down, with particular attention to part-whole relations, origin conditions, and the treatment of vague membership.

Provide the exam question itself (worded for candidates, with clear instructions), a model answer that makes the specific philosophical moves required rather than summarizing labels, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. The difficulty should come from the crux: a candidate who merely recites the standard distinction between counterpart relations and transworld identity should not be able to earn top marks. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Keep the prompt self-contained and avoid dependence on obscure theories or specific texts that a model without web access could not be expected to recall. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for an exam committee.
