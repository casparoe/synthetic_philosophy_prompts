You are a specialist in Thomistic moral and political philosophy, and you are designing assessment materials for an upper-level undergraduate or early graduate seminar on medieval political thought. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm the precise conceptual tension you want students to navigate: Aquinas’s prohibition on private individuals killing a tyrant in Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 42 (grounded in the risk of sedition and harm to the common good) versus his broader natural-law doctrines that an unjust statute is not truly law and that legitimate self-defense is permissible. The exam should force students to work through whether that prohibition still holds when the tyrant has systematically captured every legitimate public authority—senate, magistracy, ecclesiastical hierarchy—leaving only private citizens with no institutional channel for deposition. The point is not to let them recite Aquinas’s standard answer, but to make them test the consistency and limits of his framework from within.

Please draft a single final-exam essay question (to be answered in about 90 minutes, roughly 800–1,000 words) together with a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme. The question must present the captured-institution hypothetical clearly, ask students whether private resistance becomes permissible under Aquinas’s own principles, and require them to weigh the sedition argument against the common-good and legal-validity arguments. It should not be answerable by summarizing q. 42 alone.

Your model answer must contain these specific moves: (1) a precise reconstruction of Aquinas’s reasoning for reserving tyrannicide to public authority, including the sedition risk and the priority of the common good; (2) an accurate account of his view that an unjust law lacks binding force and of his conditions for legitimate defensive action; (3) an identification of the tension introduced when all public authority is co-opted; (4) an analysis of whether the private/public distinction collapses or can be salvaged by appealing to implicit popular authority or to the tyrant’s forfeiture of legitimacy; and (5) a reasoned judgment on consistency, explaining whether Aquinas’s metaphysics of authority and his account of the common good ultimately permit or forbid private killing in this edge case.

The marking scheme should be out of 25 marks, with explicit point allocations for each of the five moves above (suggested: 4, 4, 5, 6, 6), plus a one-line note for each on the typical error that costs marks (e.g., conflating Aquinas with later monarchomachs, treating the unjust-law principle as automatically licensing rebellion, ignoring the sedition argument entirely, or failing to address the hypothetical rather than reverting to the standard case). Separating an A from a B should depend on whether the student actually engages the tension in move 4 and delivers a philosophically argued judgment in move 5, rather than merely describing both sides.

Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from Aquinas; paraphrase his positions without quotation marks. Keep the level appropriate for students who have read the relevant parts of the Summa Theologiae and possibly De Regno, but do not assume knowledge of later scholastic commentators. Start your response with brief brainstorming of the tension, then present the exam question, then the model answer, then the marking scheme.
