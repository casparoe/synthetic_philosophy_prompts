I've been arguing with my study group about whether Friedmans F-twist still holds water for modern macroeconomic models, and I'm kind of stuck on why the debate never actually dies. You know the standard moves: one side says unrealistic assumptions about rational expectations or perfectly competitive markets don't matter as long as you get decent predicitive success, and the other side says that's not real science unless you've got the actual causal mechanisms in the box. We've been going in circles for months, replaying the same objections about IS-LM versus DSGE calibrations, and nobody shifts.

So I'm trying to figure out: is this a substantive disagreement about the aims of economics, or are they using words like 'explanation' and 'realism' to talk about totally different things? I have a hunch part of the stall comes from mixing up representational accuracy — whether the model's parts map onto real economic entities — with what some philosophers call epistemic surrogacy, where the model works as a stand-in that lets you reason about the system without mirroring it. If that's right, are the instrumentalists and mechanists even disagreeing, or just answering different questions under the same label? I want a real diagnosis, supported by what each camp actually commits to, not just another survey. What's the actual crux — a hidden empirical claim about whether false assumptions eventually break predictions, or a bedrock intuition about what science owes us? And be honest: is there anything that would actually move this — a formal result, a failed case study, a conceptual distinction — or should we expect this stall to just keep going forever?
