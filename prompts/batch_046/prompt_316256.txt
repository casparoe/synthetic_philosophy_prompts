Having spent three decades in experimental physics and only recently begun studying philosophy in retirement, I find myself returning to Borges's metaphysics of time—particularly his denial of successive temporal flow and his model of branching, coexistent possibilities. I would like you to read Denis Villeneuve's film Arrival through this specifically Borgesian lens, rather than through the more familiar frameworks of determinism or linguistic relativism alone. The narrative presents a being whose language restructures consciousness so that past, present, and future are perceived as simultaneous, and it poses the question of whether a person can knowingly embrace a painful future without thereby annulling its emotional reality. I want an analysis that takes both the cinematic work and Borges's position seriously: does the film's experience of simultaneity map onto Borges's idealist denial of temporal succession, or onto a physicalist block-universe interpretation? Where does the premise that linguistic structure determines temporal perception align with Borges's argument that time is substantially a mental construct? Please concentrate on Louise's choice to conceive a child despite foreknowing her grief—would Borges interpret this as confirming the eternal presence of all moments and thus dissolving loss, or does the narrative's emotional architecture resist that eternalist consolation? I am preparing this for a philosophy-of-science reading group, so I seek a reading that neither reduces the film to an illustration of Borges nor bends Borges's metaphysics to accommodate the screenplay; it should acknowledge where the work pushes back against the philosophical frame, and conclude by stating what the narrative itself adds to the debate over temporal reality that conceptual argument alone cannot supply.
