Write a short story of no more than 800 words centred on Dr. Elara Voss, a phenomenologist who volunteers for an experimental protocol that induces targeted asymbolia—not for nociceptive injury, but for what the researchers term existential ache: the affective weight of acute mortality awareness. In this near-future clinical centre, the procedure decouples the sensory-cognitive recognition of one’s own finitude from its usual negative valence, leaving the subject able to contemplate death with perfect lucidity but without dread. Through Elara’s first-person account during her first week post-procedure—perhaps during a laboratory debrief, a conversation with a colleague who refused treatment, or while revising her own earlier writings on the badness of pain—explore whether the unpleasantness of this existential state was ever intrinsic to the experience of finitude itself, or merely an avoidable affective overlay. Take a committed philosophical stance: show implicitly through concrete narrative detail rather than expository dialogue that the badness is separable from the cognitive content, so that what remains is genuinely painless awareness; or, conversely, show that stripping the affect transforms the phenomenon into something categorically different, so that painless dread is not dread at all but a hollow simulation. After the story, include a brief author’s note of no more than 150 words stating precisely where you have taken liberties with the clinical literature on pain asymbolia, whether your account of decoupled affect relies on a representationalist or an evaluativist theory of pain, and how your narrative resolution leans on one contested reading of whether affect is constitutive of badness rather than merely contingent to it.
