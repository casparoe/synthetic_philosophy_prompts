I'm drafting a long-form essay for a seminar on comparative ethics, and I'd like feedback on its central argument before I commit to the full structure. I propose that Buddhist ethics, particularly as articulated in the Pali Abhidhamma tradition, is best understood not as a variant of virtue ethics or deontology, but as a form of what I call karmic tranquilism—a soteriological framework in which the only intrinsic disvalue is the presence of tanha and dukkha, and the only intrinsic value is their cessation, with no residual importance attached to the identity of the mental stream in which they occur. Because the doctrine of anatta denies a persistent self that could serve as the unit of aggregation, I argue that Buddhist ethics implicitly rejects the separateness-of-persons assumption that makes standard consequentialist aggregation coherent; instead, moral evaluation tracks whether an action reduces the causal conditions for craving across mental continua, regardless of whose continuum is affected. This yields what I term a process-consequentialism focused on the extinction of root conditions rather than on welfare maximization for discrete agents. I contrast this with agent-neutral consequentialism and with the upaya (skillful means) literature to show that Buddhist moral reasoning prioritizes the dissolution of clinging over outcome optimization.

Please comment on the accuracy of this reconstruction—especially regarding the Abhidhamma account of kamma-vipaka and the role of cetana in moral evaluation—and on whether the comparison with consequentialism is genuinely illuminating or merely an imposition of Western categories onto a system oriented toward liberation rather than normative theory. I also want to know where the reasoning is most fragile: is the inference from anatta to the irrelevance of personal identity in moral aggregation too quick, and does the emphasis on tanha overlook the social and ritual dimensions of Buddhist practice? Please respond in American English, and be self-critical: indicate what specific textual finding or philosophical argument would change your assessment of whether this reconstruction succeeds, and point out the part of my reasoning that is most likely to fail.
