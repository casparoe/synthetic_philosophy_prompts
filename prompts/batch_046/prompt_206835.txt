I was sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar where two graduate students—Mira and Jonah—got into a sharp exchange about the problem of verisimilitude, specifically whether we can rationally rank false scientific theories by how close they come to the truth when we do not possess the true theory against which to measure distance. Mira began by arguing that Popper’s original quantitative definition—counting the true logical consequences of a theory and subtracting its false ones—collapses because logical consequence is monotonic: appending an irrelevant true statement to a false theory can actually diminish its verisimilitude rather than improve it, which shows that syntactic proximity to truth is not a coherent metric. Jonah replied that this objection only defeats a naive syntactic account, and that a structural or content-based measure—one that tracks which core ontological commitments of a predecessor survive in its successor—allows us to make comparative judgments without knowing the full truth. Mira countered that any structural measure smuggles in normative assumptions about which theoretical parts “matter” for proximity, and that without an independent truth-standard those assumptions reduce to conventional preferences disguised as epistemology. Jonah then said that this skepticism is excessive: scientific practice demonstrates convergence on which auxiliary hypotheses to discard and which structural features to preserve, so comparative verisimilitude is operationalized through the dynamics of research programs rather than defined through an a priori formula. Mira pressed the point with a specific comparison, asking whether Jonah’s criterion genuinely shows that classical mechanics is closer to the truth than Cartesian vortex theory, or whether it merely reflects our post-relativistic conviction that spacetime curvature is the “important” feature—something we only know because we already accept Einstein’s framework. Jonah answered that we need not possess the final equations to see that vortex dynamics fails to predict planetary motion in a way that Newtonian dynamics succeeds, so the comparison does not secretly depend on knowing general relativity. They turned to me and asked whether either of them had actually solved the problem, or whether they were both hiding behind different intuitions about what scientific progress requires. Can you go through their exchange move by move, rate each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether there is a coherent middle position that avoids Mira’s conventionalism without letting Jonah implicitly rely on the very truths he is trying to approximate?
