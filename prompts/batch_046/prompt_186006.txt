In a discussion forum I have followed for some years, two regular contributors — call them M and N — have been arguing about whether a nested sortition architecture solves the democratic-epistemic trade-off or only shifts its location. In this design, citizens are randomly selected for local deliberative bodies, and each successive tier (regional, national) is filled by lot drawn exclusively from the tier below. I report the exchange below as a bystander; I am not signaling agreement with either side.

M's first move: Elections suffer from adverse selection. Only people with unusually high risk tolerance, fundraising capacity, and appetite for public scrutiny seek office, so the candidate pool is systematically unrepresentative of the population's judgment and values. Uniform random selection eliminates this filter, and nesting allows local situated knowledge to aggregate upward without being distorted by campaign dynamics.

N's reply: Descriptive representation is the wrong benchmark. What governance requires is epistemic representativeness — a body whose members collectively mirror the distribution of relevant expertise, not just demographics. Elections, however imperfectly, allow voters to select for demonstrated competence; uniform sampling pulls from the full competence distribution, so median technical capacity falls and variance rises. Nesting compounds rather than solves this, because each tier samples from the already-filtered pool of the tier below.

M's second move: Mini-publics are not expected to be experts ex ante. Through structured deliberation with expert testimony, randomly selected citizens learn. The uniform sample captures situated knowledge — lived experience of different social positions — that specialists and career politicians systematically miss. Nesting is designed precisely to integrate these two knowledge types: local bodies process issues requiring local experience, and higher bodies synthesize the filtered outputs.

N's second move: There is an integration asymmetry. Experts advise, but the randomly selected members decide. Situated knowledge concerns values and experience, not causal mechanisms. When values conflict with technical feasibility — say, a locally popular policy that violates macroeconomic constraints — the assembly has no principled resolution mechanism beyond its members' pre-existing cognitive biases. Elections provide a retrospective feedback loop: voters observe outcomes and can remove incompetent representatives. Pure nested sortition lacks this loop unless recall is added, and recall is rarely triggered.

M's third move: The retrospective loop in elections is largely illusory. Voters are rationally ignorant, outcomes are overdetermined, and removal is driven by partisanship and media cycles rather than competence assessment. Meanwhile, strategic distortion from donors and spectacle systematically warps policy. Even if nested sortition lowers mean competence slightly, the reduction in strategic distortion improves expected decision quality. Furthermore, recall can be institutionalized at each tier without destroying the random basis.

N's third move: For high-stakes technical domains — epidemiological modeling, macroeconomic calibration — a small competence gap can produce catastrophic, non-recoverable errors that no reduction in donor influence can offset. Without a mechanism that selectively weights competence, the risk is unacceptable. Moreover, introducing recall creates a hidden selection effect: those replaced are likely substituted by people with higher ambition or social capital, gradually reintroducing the adverse selection sortition was meant to eliminate. The architecture therefore contains an internal tension it does not resolve.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Identify which party is right about which specific claim, and say what, if anything, both are missing — for example, whether nesting changes the epistemic calculus in a way neither addresses, or whether there is an unexamined assumption about how situated knowledge integrates with technical expertise.
