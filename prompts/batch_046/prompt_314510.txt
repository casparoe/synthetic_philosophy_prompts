I've been working out the logical structure of what I'll call strict tranquilism: the claim that only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering possess intrinsic value, and that every apparently positive state—pleasure, achievement, aesthetic absorption, relational intimacy—is valuable only instrumentally, insofar as it prevents or reduces future negative states. I want to compare this with a more subtle opponent, not hedonism but what I'll call the ataraxic-equilibrium view: the claim that there is a sui generis positive good in stable, low-arousal contentment that is not reducible to the mere absence of disturbance.

Can these be distinguished within a formal decision framework? If a utility function assigns negative weights to suffering and craving and flat zero to all other states, does it genuinely represent tranquilism, or does it already collapse into the ataraxic view by treating the baseline equilibrium as a hidden positive coefficient? Put differently, is the disagreement about the ontology of well-being—whether absence and presence are genuinely asymmetrical—or is it partly verbal, turning on which mental states we classify as 'absence' versus 'presence'?

Please approach this as conceptual analysis rather than a literature survey. Separate what is structurally settled—such as whether a purely negative axiology is logically consistent, or whether standard utility representations can capture asymmetry—from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of value and decision theorists. In particular, do not present as settled consensus any claim about whether tranquilism implies antinatalist conclusions, whether it can accommodate agent-relative reasons, or whether negative-utility functions require special boundary conditions; if those questions are live, flag them as live and note where reasonable people disagree.
