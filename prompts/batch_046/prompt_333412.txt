As a retired physicist recently drawn into economic history and philosophy of science, I have come to hold a skeptical position about the debate over why some nations are rich: I think the institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations are not just empirically competing but structurally symmetrized by what I call the macrohistorical identification problem. Because history provides no counterfactual branches—no cases where the same society develops under altered initial conditions—any correlation between inclusive institutions and prosperity can be redescribed as geography shaping institutions, or culture co-evolving with both, without violating any observed fact. Path dependence and equifinality, familiar from evolutionary dynamics without experimental controls, seem to destroy the independence conditions required for causal inference at this scale. So I am inclined to think that knowledge of why nations are rich is permanently underdetermined, not merely practically but in principle. What would genuinely count against this view—not just another regression or comparative case study, but an argument, formal result, or observable pattern—that could show macrohistorical causation is actually identifiable? Which defeaters would a committed skeptic concede as serious rather than dismiss as missing the structural point, and which developments, if any, could reduce the position from fatal to merely awkward? I want the map of its exposure ranked accordingly.
