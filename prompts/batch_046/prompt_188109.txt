I'm a retired physicist who only started reading philosophy after I stopped working in the lab, and last week my study group got stuck on a version of the surprise exam paradox that my friend insists proves too much. Here is his chain, which I copied down as best I could. (1) Call an outcome genuinely surprising for a rational agent only if, right before it happens, the agent's credence in it is near zero. (2) In the classic exam case there are five days, and initially each gets some positive weight. (3) If Thursday evening comes with no exam, and the agent knows it must be Friday and knows she updates correctly, then by Friday morning she assigns near-one to Friday — so she can't be surprised then. (4) By backwards induction the same reasoning wipes out Tuesday, Wednesday, and Monday too, so on no day is her credence near zero. (5) So rationality plus common knowledge of rationality makes surprise impossible in any bounded possibility space. (6) Now generalize: take any future event with a finite list of outcomes and common knowledge that one will occur; as options drop away the survivor gets near-one credence, which my friend calls predictive closure. (7) Therefore in every such closed system genuinely unexpected results are impossible — not just exams, but black-swan experiments, sudden news, anything. (8) But we constantly meet real surprises even when we knew the space was bounded, so (8) can't be right. He says this shows the very idea of rational expectation is self-defeating. I feel like the slip is somewhere between (5) and (6), maybe a scope shift or an equivocation about what "near zero" means across different information states, but I can't name it precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me — do I have to deny backward induction, weaken rationality, or redefine surprise?

One extra twist he added: he defines surprise not psychologically but information-theoretically, as a negative log-likelihood spike that exceeds the agent's prior entropy budget for the day. That makes the backward induction look like a mathematical inevitability rather than a psychological trick. I don't know if that framing is standard or just his own invention, but it makes the conclusion feel harder to dodge. If the flaw is in that framing, tell me where the math overreaches; if the flaw is elsewhere, say so and explain what accepting the fix means for how rational agents should actually update. I don't need citations or a reading list — I just want to know which premise is the weakest link and why it looked so convincing in the first place.
