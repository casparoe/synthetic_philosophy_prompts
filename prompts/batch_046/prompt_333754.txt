I am advising a small interagency audit board that certifies decision protocols for public institutions—hospitals, climate-adaptation boards, and investment committees—before they are approved for multi-year operation. In my graduate seminar on rational choice theory, we have been working through the coherence arguments that underpin expected utility maximization: the Dutch Book arguments against probabilistic inconsistency, the money-pump arguments against cyclical preferences, and the representation conditions that link these together. The board members are not mathematicians; they have roughly thirty minutes per protocol, no access to precise cardinal utility functions, and must sign off on whether a protocol is internally vulnerable to exploitation or contradiction that undermines its stated aims, without being able to outsource the judgment to an economist.

Please write a reusable audit procedure they can apply to any institutional decision protocol. It should take the form of an ordered checklist in words—questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the audit stops with a definite failure, and where it must explicitly hand the decision back to human judgment because the issue involves framing values rather than logical coherence. The procedure must distinguish synchronic violations, where conflicting judgments occur within a single decision frame, from diachronic violations, where a protocol updates beliefs or preferences across contexts in a way that permits a cross-temporal money pump. Be explicit about which steps require numerical precision that real-world protocols rarely possess, and specify what the auditor should do when probabilities and utilities are only ordinal, qualitative, or context-dependent. Then apply the procedure to three cases: first, a hospital triage algorithm that assigns risk weights differently in emergency versus elective surgical frames, such that a given patient’s priority reverses purely based on which administrative context activates the protocol; second, a municipal climate fund that employs precautionary reasoning, refusing to trade present costs for future benefits at any stable rate, which violates the independence axiom across portfolio divisions though not within any single division; and third, a vaccination-allocation protocol that updates risk assessments after each delivery batch, using an update rule that resembles Bayesian conditioning in one administrative context but arbitrary preference reversal in another. For each case, show where the procedure reaches a clear failure, where it flags ambiguity that requires judgment, and—particularly for the climate fund—where it delivers a verdict the board will likely find ethically uncomfortable but must formally record. The deliverable should not be a bibliography or literature survey, but a working document: the procedure with explicit markers for unavoidable judgment calls, followed by the three worked applications, including at least one case where the correct procedural answer conflicts with the institution’s intuitive ethical stance.
