In my philosophy of mind seminar we have been working through functionalism and its discontents, but every discussion of Searle’s Chinese Room quickly collapses into the familiar exchange about syntax and semantics. I want a fresh, engineered case that isolates a specific structural tension rather than replaying the standard script. Please construct a thought experiment—no longer than one paragraph, using only realistic technology and no exotic physics, perfect predictors, or science-fictional devices—in which a symbol-manipulation system is enclosed in a restricted causal-feedback loop with its environment: it receives only thermal-gradient data through a single sensor and outputs classification tokens according to a purely formal rulebook that contains no semantic reference to heat, temperature, or thermodynamics. The scenario should be designed so that (1) Searle-style reasoning—that syntax alone is not sufficient for semantics—delivers the verdict that the enclosed system genuinely lacks understanding of thermal states; (2) a functionalist who appeals to the system reply grants understanding, but only on the condition that the causal loop and sensor are counted inside the system boundary; and (3) a robot-reply functionalist, who requires rich direct causal interaction with the world for intentionality, denies understanding precisely because the interaction is restricted to this single impoverished modality. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning that produces each verdict, identify the exact structural feature doing the work (specifically, how the system-boundary is drawn and how the richness of world-interaction is calibrated), explain whether removing the restricted loop collapses the case back into the standard Chinese Room, and describe how a defender of the position that fares worst—likely the system-reply functionalist—would attack the case by challenging your boundary conditions. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or appeals to obscure literature; write so that someone without web access can evaluate the argument entirely from first principles.
