I'm taking a graduate seminar on comparative constitutional design, and we've been debating whether modern constitutional monarchy is best understood through Bagehot's dignified-versus-efficient distinction or through coordination-theoretic frames that treat the crown as a non-competitive institutional anchor. I want to test the latter idea against a concrete crisis. Analyze the 1936 Abdication Crisis in the United Kingdom — in which Edward VIII insisted on marrying Wallis Simpson, a twice-divorced American, despite the explicit opposition of Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin and the governments of the Dominions, ultimately choosing abdication in December 1936 and being succeeded by George VI — through the lens of the monarch as what we might call a Schelling focal point for democratic continuity. By this I do not mean Bagehot's ceremonial ornament but the specific theoretical claim that the crown occupies a pre-political coordination locus: it fixes national ceremonial precedence and succession in a single, non-elected, non-bargainable point, so that democratic institutions never have to compete over symbolic sovereignty or negotiate the terms of national unity. Any personal choice by the occupant that violates the conventions anchoring this locus therefore threatens not etiquette alone but the stability of the division of labor between democratic government and symbolic statehood.

Map the crisis carefully onto this framework. Identify the actual players in the coordination game — the King, Baldwin and the Cabinet, the Dominions' prime ministers, Parliament, and the public — and specify what moves were available to each and what payoffs attached to preserving versus disrupting the focal point. How does the fact that the crisis resolved through abdication rather than through constitutional amendment, parliamentary defiance, or a referendum illuminate the theory's claim that the monarchy's democratic value lies precisely in its non-negotiability? Then say where the focal-point model breaks: does it misdescribe the Dominions' constitutional role, does it suppress the actual moral and religious objections to Simpson's marital status, and does the rapid resolution without institutional collapse suggest that the focal point was more replaceable — or less structurally essential — than the theory implies? Finally, tell me whether this episode argues for retaining such focal points, for replacing them with an elected ceremonial head, or for abolishing them altogether, and whether the focal-point theory can genuinely distinguish those conclusions or collapses them into an indeterminate preference for stability. I am less interested in the ecology of the crisis than in whether the theory explains the resolution or forces us to invent explanations it cannot supply.
