Student's answer:

In the well-known passage, Zhuangzi describes falling asleep and dreaming that he is a butterfly, then waking and wondering whether he is now a man who dreamt he was a butterfly, or a butterfly presently dreaming that he is Zhuangzi. This establishes that waking experience and dream experience are phenomenologically indistinguishable, which is essentially the same sceptical challenge later developed by Descartes in the Meditations. Because there is no internal criterion that can adjudicate between the two states, Zhuangzi must be committed to ontological pluralism: both the human state and the butterfly state possess equal reality, since neither perspective can be privileged. The reversibility thesis reinforces this conclusion by showing that first-person self-ascription — for instance, saying 'I am awake' — carries no greater normative weight than the corresponding claim made within a dream. Consequently, the sceptical conclusion is strengthened, not weakened, because any attempt to ground knowledge in waking life is undermined by the symmetrical authority of both conditions.
