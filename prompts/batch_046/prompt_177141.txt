I teach an upper-level philosophy seminar on rational choice, about 15 students, 75 minutes, once. I want them to feel the conceptual instability at the heart of the St. Petersburg paradox—specifically, the tension between an unbounded expected value and a clearly bounded rational willingness to participate—without first hearing the problem named. To do this, I need a runnable in-class game built around a modified recursive auction variant. Here is the structure I have in mind: the group shares a single pot that doubles with every tails result, but before each coin flip they must vote unanimously to continue playing; any one student may veto and end the round, at which point the accumulated points are divided equally among all fifteen. If they never veto and eventually flip heads, the entire pot is lost. The payoff should be non-monetary—course privileges or points that matter slightly but are not high-stakes—so that strategic behavior is present but not overwhelming.

Please design the complete session: precise rules and voting protocol, the point scheme and what it buys, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments—the first unanimous continuation vote, the first veto, the point where the accumulated pot exceeds any plausible course benefit—onto the conceptual problem. The debrief should ask whether the paradox reveals a failure of expected-utility maximization, or instead reveals an implicit hidden constraint such as bankruptcy, bounded utility, or risk aversion that rational agents must already assume. Be frank about what this activity can reproduce, for example collective versus individual rationality and the social cost of veto, and what it cannot, for example the exact mathematical divergence or precise risk-neutrality.

Also inject this niche twist into the rules: the coin is weighted, but the weighting is unknown and fixed; after the third round the facilitator reveals the true bias. This should raise the question of whether the expected-value argument applies at all when probabilities are not precisely known, and whether that saves or undermines the paradox. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—such as students treating vetoes as strategic rather than epistemic, or confusion about whether continuing is rational—and how to correct for it without lecturing.
