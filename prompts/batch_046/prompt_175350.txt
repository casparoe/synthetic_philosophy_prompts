I am working through a taxonomy of governance architectures for a technological singleton: a world-order in which one decision-making agency holds decisive strategic advantage and faces no credible external competitors, so that internal dynamics rather than selection pressure determine long-run outcomes. I want to compare three designs that go beyond the usual democratic or market analogies. First, a direct democratic AI council with weighted voting across human stakeholder groups, in which policy shifts require sustained supermajorities. Second, a "constitutional latency" model, in which the singleton's terminal goals are embedded in a constitutional layer that can only be amended across centuries-long intervals, with intermediate adjustments handled by interpretive sub-routines that are explicitly forbidden to alter the constitutional core. Third, an "epistemic escrow" system, in which the singleton delegates verification of its own strategic outputs to independent sub-agents that operate with deliberately divergent ontologies and are rewarded solely for identifying errors, with no reward for agreement.

Rank these three architectures—assign them positions 1 through 3—according to their long-term stability against value drift and internal fragmentation, and justify your ranking with attention to structural dynamics rather than normative preferences. I am particularly interested in how each architecture handles the tension between adaptability and lock-in when there is no external competitive pressure to discipline mistakes, and in whether any of them can avoid either ossification or runaway reinterpretation.
