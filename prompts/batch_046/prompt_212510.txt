I'm drafting a chapter on what I'm calling ephemeral normativity — basically the idea that rules like tipping, queueing, or not jaywalking create obligations that are genuinely binding but only while you're physically inside the context that sustains them. I want to avoid the usual 'is it moral or just etiquette?' framing, so I'm hoping for a taxonomy (decision tree style) that sorts positions by two things: first, whether a convention gets moral weight through explicit agreement / social contract or through interdependance and reliance, and second, whether a visitor who doesn't share the local background is still bound, or whether ignorance functions as a legitimate excuse.

The branches should split on concrete cases — like, does skipping a tip in a tipping culture count as a moral wrong or just a social misfire? Is jaywalking in Tokyo (where people wait at lights even with no cars) morally different from jaywalking in Berlin? And I need leaves that include named views where they exsist, plus 'unnamed' for combinations nobody defends — for instance, maybe the view that conventions never generate moral obligations but visitors should obey them purely for strategic reasons. Could you also flag any pairs of positions your tree can't actually seperate, like if a contract-based localist view and some practice-based virtue view collapse onto the same answers here? I want about six to eight leaves total, phrased so a grad student could navigate it without already knowing the philosophical labels. This is for a seminar I'm running on applied ethics, and my students keep conflating social norms with moral norms, so I really need the tree to make that confusion visible rather than hiding it.
