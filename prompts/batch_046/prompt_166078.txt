Using only the outside view—historical reference classes and base rates, with no inside-view causal modeling—rank the following five forecasting heuristics by the predictive weight you would assign them when estimating whether a decentralized, resource-dependent administrative federation will suffer systemic fragmentation within the next fifty years. Assign each a tier (S, A, B, C, or D) and justify its placement in exactly one sentence.

The reference classes are:

(1) The dissolution timelines of continental land empires with high ethnic heterogeneity and pre-modern long-distance coordination capacity (e.g., Habsburg, Qing, Ottoman).

(2) The organizational decay trajectories of vertically integrated monopolies that faced technological substitution and regulatory partition (e.g., the Bell System, Standard Oil’s refining network).

(3) The constitutional failure modes of federal experiments established after 1789 that lacked unified fiscal or military authority.

(4) The demographic and fiscal contraction curves of states whose public budgets depend primarily on a depleting extractive commodity.

(5) A proposed layer-redundancy decay framework, which treats bureaucratic hierarchies as depreciating capital stocks: once maintenance expenditure falls below a critical fraction of operating cost, failure cascades accelerate nonlinearly, producing collapse timelines much shorter than linear depreciation models would predict.

In constructing the ranking, apply two explicit criteria: structural homology (the degree of shared causal mechanism with the target system) and statistical power (the number of independent historical instances available to establish a base rate). For each tier, identify whether its placement is driven primarily by high homology, large-N reliability, or by a trade-off in which one dimension undermines the other—particularly noting where selection bias in the historical record distorts observed base rates because failed cases disappear from the sample. After the tier list, provide a single composite confidence score, expressed as a percentage, representing how much weight you place in the aggregated outside-view forecast relative to an uninformative prior.

Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. The evaluation should rest on conceptual coherence, the logic of reference-class selection, and explicit awareness of base-rate contamination rather than on appeal to specific scholarly authorities.
