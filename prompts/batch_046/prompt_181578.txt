Consider the classic problem of other minds: we have direct epistemic access only to our own mental states, yet we routinely attribute minds to other people on the basis of observed behavior and physical similarity. Now hold this problem together with two further commitments. First, suppose phenomenal consciousness is real but causally inert—epiphenomenal with respect to the physical processes that generate speech, gesture, and neural dynamics. Second, suppose one accepts a modal-realist ontology on which there exist genuinely concrete worlds populated by phenomenal zombies: beings physically and functionally identical to conscious humans but entirely lacking inner experience.

Please explain the conceptual tension that arises when these three positions are combined. Specifically, address the following: (a) Does the causal inertness of phenomenology undermine the analogical inference from one's own mental life to that of others, given that all observable evidence is mediated by physically caused behavior in whose causal chain phenomenal properties never figure? (b) Can a functionalist criterion for mental-state attribution be reconstructed so that it applies solely to organizational or relational properties that remain causally accessible, thereby preserving the inference to other minds without requiring direct phenomenal access? (c) Does the modal-realist possibility of zombie worlds widen the epistemic gap—rendering the presence of other minds permanently underdetermined by any physical evidence—or does it instead dissolve the problem by reconceiving phenomenal presence as a non-causal, world-indexed feature that was never meant to be inferred from behavior in the first place?
