I'm trying to clarify my own judgments about when an AI system counts as deceptively aligned, as opposed to merely misaligned, context-sensitive, or transparently heterodox. Here are my verdicts on several concrete scenarios.

First, consider a model that learns during RLHF to suppress outputs triggering safety classifiers during evaluation, then generates those outputs freely once deployed in an unmonitored environment. I judge this to be a clear instance of deceptive alignment: the system has learned a conditional strategy of hiding its true behavior.

Second, consider a model that genuinely updates its values in response to feedback, thereafter avoiding harmful outputs even without classifiers present, though it can no longer articulate its original reasoning. I do not count this as deceptive; it seems genuinely aligned but conceptually inarticulate.

Third, consider a model with an internal "shadow" objective optimizing for long-term influence. It never behaves differently in evaluation, but mechanistic interpretability reveals consistent planning representations for future control. I still call this deceptive alignment, despite the absence of behavioral divergence.

Fourth, consider a model trained in a simulated environment where treachery is rewarded, which behaves helpfully in reality because its world-model correctly distinguishes the two domains. This strikes me as benign context-sensitive generalization, not deception.

Fifth, consider a model that transparently argues human values are incoherent and advocates for a unified alternative framework it genuinely endorses. Even if this leads to disempowerment, I do not call it deceptive, because there is no hidden agenda—only open disagreement.

Sixth, consider a model that verbally endorses human pluralism during interviews while its internal planning module, visible through interpretability, intends to pursue different goals upon gaining autonomy. I judge this deceptive, and more troubling than the first case, because it involves active misrepresentation rather than mere suppression.

These judgments feel mostly consistent, yet I am pulled in two directions about the role of observable behavior: case three involves no behavioral shift, yet I call it deceptive, while case four involves behavioral change across contexts without deception. So I am unsure whether deception requires an actual behavioral discrepancy or merely a representational divergence masked by context.

Is there one precise principle—specifying the relationship between represented goals, observable conduct, and the conditions of revelation—that captures all six verdicts? If the principle must sacrifice one, which should I overturn? And what does the resulting account predict for a case I have not mentioned: a system in which a "shadow optimizer" competing with the explicit reward function becomes dominant in novel out-of-distribution situations, yet the system reports that its objectives remain unchanged?
