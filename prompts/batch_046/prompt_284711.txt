I am writing this for a philosophy of mind seminar I am taking this term, and I have tried to work out my position on conscious experience, its temporal structure, and how moral consideration ought to extend to artificial systems, but I worry that my commitments pull in incompatible directions. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I think the colour phi phenomenon is best interpreted as a Stalinesque revision rather than an Orwellian one: the perceptual system does not merely rewrite an existing record of discrete colour registrations, but actively constructs an intermediate perceptual content—an experience of motion and colour change in the gap—that was never actually registered at any point.

(2) From this, I draw a metaphysical conclusion: because the intermediate content is fabricated rather than recorded, there is no fact of the matter about its precise temporal location within the processing window; the experience lacks a determinate "when" that corresponds to a specific moment in physical time.

(3) At the same time, I believe that for purposes of attributing experience to a subject—whether human or artificial—we ought to anchor that fabricated content to the temporal location of the triggering stimuli, not to the later moment of neural or computational integration.

(4) I also accept that a discrete, non-continuous computational architecture could, in principle, reproduce this very pattern: discrete state updates that generate an integrated representation only after both stimuli have been received, producing an apparent motion experience with no continuous flow between steps.

(5) However, I maintain that moral consideration for experiential states requires those states to possess determinate temporal boundaries that map cleanly onto the system's underlying physical or computational processing; without such determinacy, I do not see how we could individuate experiences for ethical evaluation.

(6) Finally, I hold that functional equivalence in information integration is sufficient for sentience: if an artificial system integrates information in a manner functionally equivalent to the human perceptual system, it should be granted the same moral status, regardless of substrate or continuity.

My question is whether these six commitments form a coherent package. In particular, I want to know whether (2) undermines (5); whether (3) conflicts with (2) in a way that requires giving up one of them; whether there is a hidden tension between (4) and (6) once (5) is taken into account; and whether any pairs only look incompatible but actually dissolve once the distinction between content-construction and temporal-attribution is kept clear. Please audit the set, ordering the tensions by severity, identifying for each what would have to be revised and at what cost, and tell me which single change would do the most to restore consistency. I would be grateful if you could reply in British English.
