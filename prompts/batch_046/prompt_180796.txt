I'm trying to pin down what Descartes' cogito actually proves, and my gut reacts differently across tweaked versions of the argument. Here are my verdicts.

Standard case: I'm sitting alone doubting everything, and I notice I can't doubt I'm currently thinking. Verdict: this definately proves I exist, at least as a thinking thing right now.

Brain-in-vat case: my brain is in a vat and all my 'thoughts' are just neural patterns being stimulated by scientists. I still run the cogito reasoning inside the simulation. Verdict: it proves the brain (or whatever is running the simulation) exists, but not that I'm a physical human sitting in a room.

AI loop case: there's a language model running a self-check loop that outputs the token sequence 'I am thinking therefore I exist' with no consciousness or understanding behind it. Verdict: this proves nothing about existence; it's just syntax.

Swarm case: 'I' am actually a collective of 200 small minds networked together, and the cogito is performed by the collective. Verdict: it proves the collective exists as a thinking thing, but it doesn't prove there's one single unified self.

Possession case: a demon is doing all the thinking using my body as a host; I (the human soul) am actually unconscious the whole time. Verdict: the cogito proves the demon exists, but it doesn't prove I exist.

So my pattern seems to be: the cogito proves that *something* is thinking right now, and that thing exists, but it doesn't tell you what kind of thing it is or whether you're it. But then I'm stuck on the AI case — if syntax isn't enough, why is simulated brain-stuff enough? And I keep flip-flopping on whether the swarm case should feel different from the standard case.

Is there one principle that captures all five? Or do I have to drop one? Also, what should I say about a case I haven't listed: a person in a severe depersonalization episode who thinks 'I think' but feels zero ownership over the thought — does the cogito prove they exist, or the dissociated mental fragment?

Please be honest about which verdict is the outlier, and what the cleanest principle predicts for the depersonalization case.
