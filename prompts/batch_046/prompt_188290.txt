The Frege-Geach objection to moral expressivism—that sentences like “If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong” embed a moral judgment in logical contexts that seem to require the embedded clause to carry the same propositional content as its unembedded form—has generated responses from quasi-realist, norm-expressivist, and hybrid camps for decades, yet the exchange now feels ritualized: critics produce embedded constructions, defenders reply with semantic ascent, bifurcated-attitude semantics, or a special logic of commitment, and the core positions remain untouched. Is this stall substantive, verbal, or a case of two different questions—about compositional semantics versus about the dynamics of discursive commitment—running under a single label? Diagnose it by reference to what each side actually treats as decisive, not by surveying interpretations, and say whether a hidden empirical claim, a formal result about compositionality for non-cognitive states, a conceptual distinction, or a change of question could genuinely move it, or whether the impasse instead signals bedrock intuitions about what an explanation of logical behavior must provide.
