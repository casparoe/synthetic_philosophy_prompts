I'm preparing a presentation for a seminar on conceptual categories and found myself stuck on a problem that sits right between cognitive science and philosophy of art. We were discussing the old Letter Spirit idea—how a system might generate every stylistic variant of the letter "a" while still knowing each token is an "a"—and I realized I don't actually have a clear account of what such recognition, whether human or artificial, needs to preserve. The challenge is not mere tolerance for distortion: a blackletter "a," a neon-tube "a," an "a" formed by negative space in tree branches, and a severely compressed geometric "a" share almost no low-level visual features, yet we rarely confuse them with an "o" or a "d" unless the distortion genuinely crosses a categorical boundary. At the same time, differences in weight, serif, or slant feel like superficial font style, not a change in letter identity, which suggests there is a stricter structure being maintained beneath the surface variation. So I want the full specification of what a theory of letterform identity must actually deliver. Write the desiderata it must meet: the phenomena it has to explain, the distinctions it must not collapse—between letter identity and font style, between structural deformation and categorical misidentification, between token-level recognition and type-level membership—the constraints from vision science or design theory it cannot violate, and whether any subset of these demands turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable. For each of exactly five desiderata, write one or two sentences stating the requirement and the motivating case or consideration behind it; mark which are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect camp-specific preferences, such as whether topological continuity of the bowl is essential or merely one tradition's prejudice. Present the result as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
