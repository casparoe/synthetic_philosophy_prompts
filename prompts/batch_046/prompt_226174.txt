Having followed rationalist discussions of decision theory for some years, I keep returning to the absent-minded driver problem and feeling that the standard presentation misses a conceptual issue buried in its premises. The usual framing treats the driver as a single agent who, owing to imperfect memory, faces an evidential versus causal reasoning conflict at the second junction. But I want to push on a different angle: whether the problem only arises because we implicitly treat the driver as an enduring substance whose preferences must be consistent across time, rather than as a sequence of epistemic agents—temporal slices—bound together by structural continuity but lacking direct access to one another's mental states.

If we take the latter view seriously, the problem stops looking like an individual decision under uncertainty and starts looking like a coordination game between distinct nodes that share a utility function and a physical body. The standard resolutions—pre-commitment or evidential updating—then appear to smuggle in a substantialist assumption about personal identity. My question is whether this reconceptualisation genuinely dissolves the paradox, or whether it merely relocates the tension into the philosophy of collective rationality.

Please explain this conceptual issue, taking a committed position with clear reasons: does the multi-slice framing resolve the absent-minded driver problem by showing that causal decision theory applies differently across temporal parts, or does it collapse back into evidential reasoning once we require the slices to coordinate? In particular, argue for whether rationality must be predicated of enduring agents, or whether it can be coherently distributed across temporal slices without loss. Please provide your answer in British English, and do not merely catalogue positions—defend one.
