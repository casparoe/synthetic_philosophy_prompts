The debate over whether a perfect forgery is aesthetically inferior to an original artwork has persisted for decades without resolution: one camp maintains that causal history—being the very object the artist physically engaged with—confers a non-transferable value, while the other insists that all aesthetic value resides in perceptible structure and that knowledge of provenance adds only biographical interest. In discussions I have observed, the exchange quickly stalls on paired intuitions about museum visitors shown identical canvases with different labels, with neither side conceding that the other has missed a fact. I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is this disagreement verbal, turning on different uses of the term 'value'; substantive, turning on whether causal knowledge actually alters perceptual experience; or a case of two questions—one about art-market ontology, one about phenomenological encounter—being treated as one? To make the diagnosis concrete, consider a parallel case that may expose hidden structure: a musical performance reconstructed entirely from a composer's handwritten sketches and executed by musicians with no personal connection to the composer, versus an original recording by the composer herself. Does applying the forgery-debate framework here reveal that the dispute depends on a distinction between 'provenance-value' and 'encounter-value,' or does the temporal, performative dimension show that authenticity requires something other than either causal continuity or structural identity—perhaps intentional fidelity? Analyze the disagreement using what each side actually commits to about causal history and perceptual identity, avoid surveying interpretations, and tell me honestly whether any distinction, empirical result, or reframing could move the debate, or whether the question is ill-posed because 'original' denotes different relations across visual and performative media.
