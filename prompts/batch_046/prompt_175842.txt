I'm preparing a single ninety-minute session for first-year political-theory students on Raphael Lemkin's concept of genocide, treating it not as a synonym for mass killing but as a legal and moral category with a specific internal architecture. The students arrive with newspaper familiarity—images of camps, mass graves, ethnic violence—but no legal training and little patience for definitional hair-splitting. I want them to grasp why the international legal formulation turns on proof of intent to destroy a protected group "as such," and why that clause generates a persistent politics of naming: strategic incentives for states and international bodies to withhold the label even when the scale of destruction is enormous, precisely because the label triggers obligations they wish to avoid.

Plan the explanatory arc for me. What conceptual distinction has to be secure before I even utter Lemkin's term—perhaps the difference between killing individual members of a group and targeting the collective entity itself for destruction? Which concrete case should carry the entire session—one that unambiguously satisfies the legal criteria but also tempts students to misapply the category by expanding it to cover ethnically targeted warfare or punitive reprisals? I anticipate three predictable misconceptions: that genocide is essentially a quantitative threshold ("enough dead bodies"), that proving intent is merely an evidentiary hurdle rather than a structural feature that shapes diplomatic behavior, and that the category should naturally cover gradual cultural erasure or economic dispossession that destroys a group's conditions of life over generations without direct killing. For each, specify exactly where in the sequence it should be anticipated and defused—before the carrying example, during it, or after—and whether it requires a brief digression or can be handled with a single framing question.

I also want to introduce a specific boundary tension without resolving it: defenders of the narrow legal definition argue that expanding genocide to include distributed, structural, or "slow" forms of group destruction collapses it into general human rights abuse and strips it of its specific juridical force; critics reply that the intent requirement allows states to engineer group elimination through assimilation, displacement, or ecological destruction while maintaining plausible deniability. At what point should this dispute enter the sequence—after the students understand the legal architecture but before they have hardened into a moral posture? And what check—perhaps a brief written exercise comparing two hypothetical scenarios—would show whether they have grasped the dependency structure of the category rather than simply adopting a position?

Finally, state explicitly what I should refuse to cover and acknowledge that I am leaving it out: for instance, the full drafting history of the 1948 Convention's compromises over political groups, or comparative case law from international tribunals. The deliverable should be a teaching plan—the order of components, the carrying case with its required features and its deliberate limitations, the misconception map with placement, the understanding check, and the declared omissions—not the explanation itself.
