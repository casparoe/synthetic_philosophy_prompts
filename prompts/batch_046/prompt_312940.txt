Here are my verdicts on five cases about indexical reasoning—specifically about when the number or distribution of observers should update my beliefs, and when it should not. I want to know whether one principle (some form of the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, or a hybrid) can deliver all of them, or whether consistency requires overturning one.

Case 1: Observer-production theories. Two cosmological-evolutionary theories, O1 and O2, share every empirical prediction for telescopes and experiments. The only difference is that O1 predicts about 10^15 conscious observers will ever exist in our cosmic history, while O2 predicts about 10^30, distributed across the same physical volume with identical local conditions. Before considering my own existence, I assign them equal prior probability. Once I observe that I exist, I update in favor of O2 by a factor of roughly 10^15. The multiplicity of observers within the same causal and observational domain is evidentially relevant.

Case 2: The presumptuous cosmological update. I observe that I exist and that the cosmological constant permits observers. A philosopher then argues that I should update my belief about the total spatial extent of the universe—perhaps to infinity—because larger or infinite volumes contain more observers, and I am more likely to exist in such volumes. I reject this. My existence updates me about parameters that govern observer density within my reference class, but not about causally and observationally disconnected domains that I can never access.

Case 3: Doomsday reasoning and reference-class restriction. I learn that I am approximately the 110-billionth human born. Under the self-sampling assumption applied to the reference class "all humans who ever live," being born relatively early should make me lean toward demographic models predicting smaller total populations (say, 200 billion) over models predicting galaxy-scale colonization (10^20). I think this reasoning is valid. However, if I artificially restrict my reference class to "humans alive in the 21st century," the birth-rank argument collapses, and I think that restriction is ad hoc—valid reasoning requires the broad class.

Case 4: The causally isolated multiverse. Two multiverse theories, M1 and M2, are physically identical in every law and local history except that M2 contains 1,000 causally isolated universes with the same properties, while M1 contains one. I exist in one such universe. I do not update in favor of M2. Multiplying domains that are causally and observationally inaccessible to me does not change my evidential situation.

Case 5: The temporal-slice multiplicity. Two metaphysical theories of personal identity are phenomenologically identical. Theory U holds that I am one continuous person from birth to death. Theory S holds that I am composed of about 2.5 × 10^9 momentary "time-slices," each lasting roughly one-hundredth of a second, each with the same mental content, and each counts as a distinct observer. Both theories make identical predictions about memory, behavior, and physics. Despite Theory S predicting vastly more observers, I do not update in favor of it. The slices are not independent entries in my reference class; they are parts of a unified experiential stream, and treating them as separate sampling opportunities violates the conditions for legitimate indexical reasoning.

These verdicts seem to me mostly correct, but they sit in tension. Cases 1 and 5 both involve multiplying observers within a shared framework, yet I update in one and refuse in the other. Cases 2 and 4 both involve large-scale multiplicities, yet my rejection in Case 2 concerns cosmological scale while my rejection in Case 4 concerns causal isolation—are these the same reason?

Is there a single, precisely stated principle—perhaps a restricted self-indication assumption with a defined reference-class boundary, or a modified self-sampling rule—that accommodates all five? If so, state it, identify which clause of the principle is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for an unlisted case (for example, whether I should update if I learn that my mental state is instantiated in 10^6 identical computer simulations running on servers in the same building, all causally accessible but phenomenologically indistinguishable). If no single principle can save all five, tell me which verdict I should overturn and why. Be explicit about the trade-off: a more complex principle that saves every verdict, versus a simpler one that sacrifices one.
