Draft the joint statement for an adversarial collaberation between two camps fighting over Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the one-thought-too-many worry. Camp one — call them the Project-Integrity defenders — hold that integrity is violated whenever moral reasoning treats a ground project as fungible, and they take Williams to mean that even entertaining the explicit thought "this is my spouse" during rescue deliberation corrupts the relationship. Camp two — the Systematic-Morality defenders — hold that integrity is only violated by being forced to actually abandon a ground project, and that the one-thought-too-many line describes an emotional misfire rather than a structural conflict between morality and identity.

Write the document both could sign. Start with the statement itself — no intro, no "here is the document" framing, just the piece. First section: numbered agreed claims about ground projects, moral conflict, and what counts as corruption of identity, worded so neither camp has to wince. Second section: a disagreements table with rows for the precise formulations of each camp's view, each row phrased so its own side would accept it if they read their row. Then the cruxes — (i) whether gradual or partial erosion of a project counts as an integrity loss, (ii) whether there is a meaningful distinction between having a deliberative thought and being required to act on it, and (iii) a temporal-framing crux: is integrity evaluated at a single decision point or across a life-trajectory? For each crux, include the concrete case of the linguist who must choose between saving her endangered-language archive (which requires relocating and stopping instruction to her child, the only other fluent speaker) or staying while the archive burns. Both camps should agree this case bears on crux (iii), and record each camp's prediction about whether she suffers an integrity loss in option A, option B, or neither. Also include one other argument or test both sides accept as bearing on cruxes (i) and (ii).

Keep it in the weeds but don't ask for citations, bibliographies, or obscure named theories. Make the wording genuinely neutral — no camp should feel misrepresented. And lead with the document, not with commentary.
