Exam question: Explain Einstein's hole argument and how it threatens determinism in generally covariant gravitational theories. Then present the point-coincidence reply, and assess whether accepting that reply commits one to rejecting manifold substantivalism—that is, to treating spacetime points as lacking individual physical identity apart from the fields and coincidences defined on them.

Student response: The hole argument begins by selecting a region—the "hole"—in which all non-gravitational fields vanish. One fixes the metric and its first derivatives on a boundary surrounding this region, then applies a smooth diffeomorphism that equals the identity outside the hole but differs inside. Because Einstein's field equations are generally covariant, both the original metric and its pushed-forward image satisfy the same equations with identical boundary data. Hence the initial conditions do not uniquely determine the metric within the hole, and determinism appears to fail.

Einstein's point-coincidence reply observes that only the intersection points of material world-lines are empirically accessible. Since the diffeomorphism leaves every such coincidence unchanged, the two metric representations correspond to one and the same physical situation. Therefore we should identify them. This identification implies that spacetime points possess no intrinsic individuality; only the relational structure of fields and their point-coincidences is physically real. In this sense, the reply favors a relational ontology over manifold substantivalism.

Contemporary philosophers of physics have largely moved past this reasoning. They regard the point-coincidence argument as an outdated verificationist maneuver, and instead resolve the problem technically by treating active diffeomorphisms as gauge symmetries that act redundantly on the space of mathematical representations. On this modern view, one need not deny that spacetime points are real; one merely recognizes that the gauge group relates equivalent descriptions of the same state.

One further point is worth noting. The argument applies only in pure vacuum. Once matter—such as a perfect fluid or an electromagnetic field—is present inside the hole, the diffeomorphism is no longer free to act, because the matter distribution fixes the coordinate labeling. Thus, in realistic physical scenarios determinism is never genuinely threatened.
