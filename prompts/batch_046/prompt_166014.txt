I've been working through some puzzles in practical reasoning for an independent study, and in discussions with friends I keep running into a particular move that I don't have vocabulary for. The basic setup is familiar: an agent faces two options that are genuinely identical in every respect that matters to her—two job offers with the same salary, commute, and role, or two charitable donations of equal size to causes she values equally. Rather than falling into classic Buridan-style paralysis, she resolves the tie by inventing a distinction that she knows, even at the moment of invention, carries no real weight—say, that one office is on the left side of the lobby, or that one charity's logo is a shade of blue she marginally prefers. She then treats that manufactured feature as if it were a genuine reason and makes her choice accordingly. What strikes me is that this is not ordinary post-hoc rationalization, because the agent is fully transparent to herself about the arbitrariness; she never hides the manufacturing process from herself, yet she still allows the invented distinction to do decisive normative work.

A second instance I keep noticing involves group deliberation. Members reach a point where every proposed course of action has been shown to have identical expected outcomes. Rather than flipping a coin or delegating, they adopt an ad hoc procedural rule—"we always go with the option proposed by the senior member"—even when seniority has no prior claim to authority in this context, and they defend the resulting choice by appealing to the rule as though it had been part of the framework all along. Again, the rule is not an unconscious bias; it is introduced openly, acknowledged as a tie-breaker with no independent merit, and then immediately treated as binding. So the pattern seems to be the deliberate, self-aware construction of an arbitrary distinction followed by its immediate promotion to the status of a legitimate reason.

I'm wondering whether this configuration—deliberately constructing an arbitrary, self-transparent tie-breaker and then treating it as a decisive reason—has an established name in decision theory or the philosophy of action. I know it sits near Buridan's ass and ordinary rationalization, but it differs from both: unlike the ass, there is actual resolution, and unlike standard rationalization, the agent never loses sight of the arbitrariness. Is there a specific concept that captures this, perhaps related to preference construction, arbitrary choice, or the self-binding adoption of conventions? If nothing fits precisely, what would be the right way to distinguish it from unconscious bias, status quo preference, or simple randomization? I'd also like to understand where its edges lie: does it require real-time awareness of the invention, or can it apply when that awareness fades quickly? And how should we classify cases where the arbitrary rule is adopted collectively and then enforced, versus cases where a single agent privately manufactures a reason for herself?
