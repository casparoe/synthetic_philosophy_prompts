After a lengthy argument with a friend last night about whether chemistry genuinely emerges from physics, I have been trying to isolate a recurring argumentative pattern that I lack a precise term for, and I should like to know whether philosophy of science recognises it or whether I am merely describing my own exasperation.

The pattern appears in two familiar domains, but always with the same structure. First, in debates about consciousness and neuroscience: a critic asserts that mental properties possess some feature — subjective character, intentionality, or downward causal efficacy — that physical descriptions necessarily omit. Once neuroscientists present a mechanism that predicts and explains the occurrence of that mental property, the critic does not concede reduction. Instead, she distinguishes the mechanism as merely the “correlate” or “implementation” of the mental state, and insists that the genuinely emergent aspect was its “explanatory autonomy” or “causal power” at the mental level, which the mechanism has not touched. If pressed to say what would count as a true reduction, she defines it as whatever eliminates that autonomy entirely, so that every successful mechanistic account is retrospectively reclassified as having addressed only a derivative or implementational aspect, leaving the core emergence claim untouched.

Second, in the philosophy of chemistry: quantum mechanics now derives the energies, geometries, and reaction dynamics of many molecules with remarkable accuracy. Rather than accepting these derivations as reductions of chemical bonding, some philosophers argue that they provide only “computational” predictions and miss the “relational” or “structural” reality of the bond itself. When it is pointed out that the relational structure is fully determined by the quantum state, they reply that this determination represents only “upward determination in reverse” and that true chemical emergence consists precisely in whatever quantum mechanics cannot, even in principle, derive — a criterion that shifts whenever quantum methods improve.

In both cases, the move is not a standard auxiliary hypothesis, because it does not add a contingent condition to save a theory; it redefines the phenomenon under discussion so that any lower-level success is excluded by definition from counting against the emergence claim. It is not quite the motte-and-bailey, since there is not a defensible core paired with an indefensible extension, but rather a single claim whose content is silently substituted whenever it is threatened. Nor is it simple equivocation, because each revision sounds individually reasonable — of course an implementation is not identical to an explanation; of course computation is not ontology.

Does this configuration have an established name? How precisely does it differ from the familiar distinction between epistemic and ontological emergence, and from the position usually called non-reductive physicalism? If there is no specific term, please coin one with a rigorous definition, clear boundary conditions that separate it from legitimate theoretical refinement and from ordinary unfalsifiability, and an explanation of why the distinction matters for assessing whether emergence claims are substantive or permanently retreating.

Please begin with a clear bottom line — either the established term, or your proposed term together with its one-sentence definition. Then present everything in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, writing as in a letter or email. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; keep the discussion self-contained.
