I have just concluded a long and rather intense argument with a close friend over whether William James’s defense of belief without sufficient evidence genuinely rebuts W. K. Clifford’s evidentialist ethics, or whether James merely re-describes psychological compulsion as moral permission. I want to channel this tension into a rigorous assessment. Please construct a final-exam essay question for an upper-level philosophy seminar—final-year undergraduate or first-year graduate level—together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a detailed marking scheme. The question must be unanswerable by mere recitation of James’s triad of live, forced, and momentous options or of Clifford’s well-known shipowner parable. To force students into the conceptual weeds, embed the following specific scenario: a historian of institutional violence is asked to endorse a collective narrative about a past atrocity on the basis of survivor testimony that she cannot independently verify; she holds the belief not for herself alone but in what we might call doxastic escrow for a community that depends on her public affirmation. Ask students to determine whether Clifford’s universal prohibition against belief on insufficient evidence applies with equal force to escrowed belief, whether James’s appeal to our passional nature can justify the endorsement, and whether the belief-acceptance distinction—familiar from contemporary philosophy of mind—dissolves the tension or exposes a hidden voluntarism in James. The model answer must explicitly locate the point where Clifford’s individualist evidentialism fails to account for social epistemic dependence, identify the precise risk that James’s position collapses into wish-fulfillment when applied to testimony-mediated belief, and assess whether escrow changes the moral calculus. The marking scheme should distinguish an A-grade response, which makes all three moves and notes that James requires the option to be genuinely forced whereas escrow may render it merely socially compelled, from a B-grade response, which accurately summarizes both philosophers but treats escrow as an afterthought rather than a structural challenge. Time allowance: 75 minutes. Expected length: 900 to 1,100 words. Weight: 35 percent of course grade. Do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or further reading lists; the assessment should assume access only to the canonical texts and to standard distinctions in epistemology.
