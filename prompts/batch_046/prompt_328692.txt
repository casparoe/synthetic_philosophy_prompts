Before giving your verdict, take a moment to map out the conceptual landscape: distinguish Augustine's account of the purpose of speech (significatio and communicatio mentis), his taxonomy of lies arranged by severity, his distinction between false utterance and the concealment of truth by silence (taciturnitas), and his handling of biblical cases such as the Hebrew midwives. Two colleagues of mine—I'll call them Alex and Jordan—were arguing about Augustine's absolute prohibition on lying after a reading group on his ethics of language. I was present and did not take sides. Here is how their exchange went, reported as faithfully as I can.

Alex opened by insisting that Augustine's position is genuinely absolute and not merely a strong preference. For Augustine, language was instituted so that one mind might communicate with another; to speak falsely is therefore to abuse the very nature of the sign. This applies regardless of motive or consequence: even to save an innocent life from a murderer, one may not utter a falsehood, because lying corrupts the speaker's soul and we must never do evil so that good may result. Alex noted that Augustine permits silence—taciturnitas—when speaking would require falsehood, which shows the prohibition targets the utterance itself, not the failure to inform.

Jordan replied that this absolutism sits uneasily with Augustine's own taxonomy of lies, in which he ranks falsehoods by severity and treats the lie told to preserve bodily life as the least grave. If context truly does not matter, Jordan asked, why rank them at all rather than treating every instance as equally catastrophic? Jordan also argued that Augustine's framework creates an unstable triad: we are commanded to love our neighbor (including his body), permitted to conceal truth by silence, yet forbidden to speak falsely even to prevent murder. If silence allows the neighbor's death, then charity seems to demand speech; but if speech must be false, then charity demands we damage our own soul. Jordan suggested that Augustine resolves this tension only by smuggling in a distinction—something like mental reservation—where technically true but deliberately misleading utterances are permitted, even though Augustine never explicitly endorses such a device. For Jordan, this reveals that Augustine's real concern is not propositional falsehood per se but the speaker's intent to deceive; once intent is in view, the boundary between false speech and deceptive ambiguity collapses, making the formal prohibition look like an arbitrary line drawn for ritual purity rather than a principled linguistic ethics.

Alex came back that Jordan was importing later scholastic machinery that Augustine would reject. Augustine, Alex said, explicitly distinguishes false speech from ambiguous true speech: if I intend the listener to form a false belief, but do so without uttering a false sentence, I have still violated the spirit of the law, yet Augustine treats that as a separate moral failure, not as mendacium proper. The taxonomy, Alex insisted, is not a licensing scheme but a penitential guide—confessors need to know the gravity of the sin, not whether it is permissible. As for the biblical case of the Hebrew midwives, Augustine explains that they were rewarded for their fear of God and protection of the innocent, not for their false words; the lie was still a sin, however minor. Therefore, Alex concluded, there is no hidden escape hatch. The triad is not unstable: charity never requires sin, and if silence permits harm, the fault lies with the murderer, not with the speaker who refuses to become a liar.

Jordan's final move was to press the charge of incoherence from the other direction. If Augustine really believes that language's purpose is truthful communication, Jordan said, then he should care about the communicative outcome—whether deception actually occurs—not about the speaker's internal propositional hygiene. By fixating on the formal property of false utterance, Augustine makes the moral status of speech depend on a feature invisible to the listener and irrelevant to the harm done. Jordan argued that this reduces Augustine's view to a kind of moral fetishism: the speaker's soul is preserved at the cost of the neighbor's life, justified by an appeal to language's nature that functions more like a taboo than an argument. The fact that Augustine ranks the life-saving lie as least grave, Jordan added, proves he recognizes that consequences and intentions modulate moral weight; his refusal to let that modulation reach zero—i.e., to permit the lie—shows the taxonomy is doing contradictory work.
