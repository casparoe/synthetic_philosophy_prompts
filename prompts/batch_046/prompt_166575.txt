I am a senior philosophy major currently enrolled in a seminar on moral agency and free will. Next month I will deliver the toast at the retirement dinner for my advisor, Professor Elena Vasquez, who spent thirty-five years in our department defending a compatibilist, reason-responsive account of agency while consistently arguing that the libertarian variety of causal authorship is neither available nor necessary for the practices of praise and blame that actually matter. Roughly half the guests will be philosophers of action who will recognize these positions immediately; the other half are family members, university administrators, and former students from other disciplines who will not follow technical debates and should not be left behind.

Please write a five-minute toast—approximately 650 to 800 words—that I can deliver. The philosophical idea should do real structural work rather than serve as decoration: organize the remarks around three registers of agency I have been developing in my coursework. First, freedom as causal authorship, the libertarian variety that Professor Vasquez argued was metaphysically elusive and practically dispensable. Second, freedom as reason-responsiveness, the compatibilist variety she refined, taught, and defended across decades. Third, what I am calling pedagogical openness: the capacity of a teacher to remain genuinely revisable by her students, a form of agency that does not reduce to either causal origination or rational self-governance alone, but depends on a willingness to have one's own reasons reshaped through sustained intellectual encounter.

The toast should honor her specific intellectual commitments without misrepresenting them—she should be shown clearly rejecting the first register, refining the second, and, through the arc of her career, exemplifying the third. At the same time, the speech must land for the non-philosophical guests, translating technical distinctions into concrete anecdotes or plain language where possible, while remaining precise enough that the specialists in the room recognize the speaker understands the standard taxonomy and is deliberately complicating it with this third, somewhat niche category. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not quote specific texts verbatim; paraphrase well-known positions without quotation marks. The tone should be warm, precise, and appropriate for a mixed academic and personal occasion.
