I am preparing notes for an essay in a philosophy of science seminar that examines Erwin Schrödinger’s well-known advocacy of Advaita Vedanta—the doctrine that individual consciousness is ultimately identical with universal consciousness (Atman-Brahman)—and contends that this metaphysical commitment is not merely a biographical ornament but structurally integral to his resistance to the Copenhagen interpretation. Specifically, I argue that Schrödinger’s 1935 cat paradox was intended not only to expose the absurdity of macroscopic superposition, but to demonstrate that a physics requiring discrete, individuated observers to trigger irreversible collapses entails an ontological pluralism incompatible with Vedantic non-duality (advaita). I then examine his later writings—particularly Mind and Matter (1958) and What is Life? (1944)—where he describes the apparent multiplicity of minds as analogous to focal points produced by a single lens, an image drawn from the Upanishads, and I relate this to his epistemic reading of the wave function as a catalogue of knowledge rather than an ontic description of reality.

To extend the argument into less familiar territory, I propose comparing Schrödinger’s ‘one mind’ thesis to contemporary adverbialist theories of consciousness, which reject the existence of numerically distinct subjects and treat awareness as a single process that assumes different modal qualities. I am uncertain whether this anachronistic mapping genuinely illuminates Schrödinger’s physics or merely imports modern conceptual machinery into historical material. I also worry that my framing neglects the influence of Schopenhauer, whose notion of a singular, blind will is often cited as the more proximate philosophical source for Schrödinger’s monism, and I wonder whether the Vedantic interpretation overstates the direct textual evidence in the 1935 paper itself.

Could you comment on the coherence and historical defensibility of this framing? In particular, I would value your assessment of whether the comparison to adverbialism is philosophically productive, whether the distinction between Schrödinger’s epistemic and the rejected ontic interpretations of the wave function is drawn accurately, and where the essay’s central claim—that Vedantic monism shaped the technical structure of his objection to Copenhagen—might be strengthened or qualified.
