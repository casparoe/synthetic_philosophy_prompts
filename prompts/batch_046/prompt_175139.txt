I am auditing a course that crosses philosophy of action with behavioral economics, and I keep returning to the way weakness of will—akrasia—is frequently modeled not as a failure of reason but as hyperbolic discounting, where an agent's present self systematically undervalues rewards that belong to her future self in a pattern that violates exponential consistency. The framework treats the self almost like a committee of temporal agents negotiating broken contracts, and during a recent discussion with friends someone joked that this logic practically demands a pharmaceutical intervention: if present bias is a cognitive malfunction, why not correct it? That conversation made me want to see the idea pushed to its deadpan extreme.

Write a satirical, deadpan direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical advertisement—approximately 600 words, formatted with standard sections such as Indications, Mechanism of Action, Clinical Trial Data, Side Effects and Warnings, and a paired Patient and Physician Testimonial—for a fictional medication with a plausible clinical name, marketed to treat chronic hyperbolic discounting and restore exponential rationality to intertemporal choice. The satire should arise from taking the actual literature seriously: present the present-biased self as a malfunctioning module, describe the drug's mechanism as enforcing an all-things-considered judgment over local affective pulls, and include side effects that map onto genuine philosophical objections—such as a dangerous flattening of urgency toward genuinely immediate threats, or a felt estrangement from future selves once all temporal distances are weighted identically, without naming specific theorists. Include at least one invented quote from a satisfied patient who realizes she has lost the ability to make spontaneous promises she knows she will break, and one from a concerned clinician who asks whether the treatment cures weakness of will or merely collapses the distinction between rational preference revision and irrational capitulation. The voice must remain deadpan corporate-medical throughout, never winking, so that a specialist recognizes the exaggeration as fair but a casual reader might briefly wonder whether this is a real advertisement for the medicalization of agency.
