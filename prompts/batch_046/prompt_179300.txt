I work in software engineering with no formal philosophy background, and I am trying to understand the standard neuroscience challenge to free will that comes from Libet’s experiments on the readiness potential. In the discussions I encounter, the brain is treated like a distributed computing system: unconscious neural processes initiate a movement before conscious awareness arrives, and conscious will is analogized to a CI/CD monitoring dashboard that receives a late webhook notifying it that a deployment—the motor action—has already begun. The conscious “veto” is then compared to a rollback signal or circuit-breaker that can halt the rollout, but only after the build process has started. This analogy is being used around me to argue that conscious agency is largely epiphenomenal, reduced to a narrow cancellation function in an otherwise automated pipeline.

Could you audit this analogy systematically? First, give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the backend daemon, the webhook notification, the approval gate, the rollback mechanism, and the overall system architecture? Next, identify precisely where the mapping breaks—especially whether treating neural causation as analogous to deterministic software execution imports false assumptions about linearity, modularity, reversibility, or a homuncular observer that the neuroscience does not support. Please separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the conclusion (that free will is essentially a veto switch) from those that are merely cosmetic. Then say whether the epiphenomenalist conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table, or whether the computing metaphor was carrying the argument all along. Finally, if this analogy collapses, is there a better one—drawn from engineering or elsewhere—that captures the temporal relation between the readiness potential and conscious control without smuggling in the wrong ontology about causation and agency?
