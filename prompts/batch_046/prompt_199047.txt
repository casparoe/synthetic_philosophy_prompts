I recently received clinical genetic results indicating that I carry a recessive allele present in only about three hundred living individuals, all traceable to a single eighteenth-century founder. As I have tried to integrate this discovery into my broader beliefs about population size and observer selection, I have become absorbed in the debate between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, and in particular in the so-called presumptuous philosopher objection. My difficulty is this: if self-sampling directs me to treat my existence as a random draw from the reference class of all observers like me, then my actual membership in such an extremely small cohort seems to provide strong Bayesian evidence against any world-model in which the cohort really is that small, and instead seems to push me toward self-indication, which rewards hypotheses with larger populations in which my specific profile is less anomalous. But the literature presents the presumptuous philosopher not as a welcome statistical correction but as a reductio of self-indication—almost as though the error lies in favoring worlds with more observers, rather than in my own failure to condition correctly on rarity. Have I therefore misapplied the reference-class logic by including my phenotypic rarity inside the sampling frame rather than treating it as a conditioning event? And is the presumptuous philosopher objection genuinely a charge of metaphysical arrogance—claiming our world is special—or is it specifically an indictment of using one's own existence as evidential leverage for cosmological scale? I want to know whether my genetic rarity gives me any legitimate reason to prefer self-indication over self-sampling in this personal case, or whether I have simply confused indexical conditioning with ordinary statistical inference.
