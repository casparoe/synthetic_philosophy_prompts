hey so im in ap gov and weve been arguing for like three weeks about judicial review. like, should judges who werent elected be able to strike down laws that congress and the president agreed on? my dad calls it the counter-majoritarian problem and says its obviously undemocratic, but my teacher says thats a feature not a bug - courts exist specifically to stop majorities from being tyrants. the weird part is that this exact same argument keeps recycling with different examples. roe v wade gets overturned and suddenly everyone is having the same conversation they had in the 1950s about brown v board. it feels like a loop.

i think the loop happens because both sides share the same word 'democracy' but mean totally different things by it. one side means 'the will of the current majority expressed through elections,' the other means 'a system where even unpopular groups have rights that cant be voted away.' so maybe the disagreement is verbal? but then again, there might be a hidden empirical question about whether judicial review actually protects vulnerable groups in practice, or whether it mostly protects property rights and corporate interests. if we had perfect data on what courts actually do, would that break the loop?

heres my actual question: is this debate stalled because its a clash of values that cant be resolved, or is it a conceptual confusion about democracy, or is there a real factual disagreement about institutional effects? and if its the first one, why do people keep acting like one more court case or election will settle it? also, i keep noticing that in countries with very active courts people complain about judicial overreach, and in countries where courts never interfere people complain about legislative tyranny. is that just coincidence, or does it mean the debate is actually about something deeper - like whether human beings are just wired to always see the other institution as the threat? i dont want a list of books, just a diagnosis of whats really going on and whether anything - data, a distinction, a new example - could actually move it forward. and be honest if you think its just going in circles forever.
