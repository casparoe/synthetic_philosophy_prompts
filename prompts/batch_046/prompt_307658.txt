I've been working through Berkeley's master argument—that to conceive of an object existing unperceived is to conceive of it being conceived, hence perceived—and I think I've identified the precise representational problem Berkeley is actually targeting. He isn't merely observing that we happen to perceive our ideas; he's claiming that representing an object as completely lacking perceptual properties requires representing a "pure negative," an entity constituted solely by the absence of all qualitative content. This seems structurally analogous to trying to represent a boundary with no enclosed space: the representation collapses into contradiction because the absence is only intelligible against a background of presence.

This framing makes me think about recent predictive-processing accounts of perceptual absence—the idea that the brain doesn't merely fail to register a missing stimulus, but actively generates a precise prediction error for its non-occurrence, thereby giving absence a kind of positive representational form. If that's correct, then modern cognitive science appears to have supplied exactly what Berkeley claimed was impossible: a coherent mental representation of an unperceived state of affairs that doesn't collapse into self-contradiction.

So my question is: does the predictive-processing model of absence actually defeat Berkeley's master argument by providing the "positive form" that makes representing the unperceived coherent? Or does the argument still apply, on the grounds that even a structured prediction error is itself an idea present to a mind, so that representing an object as "predicted absent" still requires that object to be present in the representational content of some perceiver?
