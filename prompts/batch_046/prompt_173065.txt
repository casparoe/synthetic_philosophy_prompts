Write a joke that operates inside the logic of Plutarchan moral biography rather than treating it as antiquarian curiosity. The setup should present a modern writer attempting a Parallel Lives pairing of two contemporary public figures whose lives intersect only in one trivial domestic habit—for instance, both alphabetize their spice racks—while their moral, political, and historical contexts are otherwise incommensurable. The punchline should turn on the writer defending this pairing with the classic distinction that biography reveals character through small, private actions rather than through public events, then deriving from that shared alphabetization an absurd, grandiose moral lesson about leadership, human nature, or civic virtue. To earn its place in a serious discussion of hagiography and moral learning, the joke must show that the writer is not ignorant of the method's original purpose—comparative moral education through paired exemplars—but is instead pushing its hagiographic logic of extraction to a reductio ad absurdum. The humor should come specifically from the collision between rigorous historiographic awareness and the relentless moral hermeneutics of biography, not from a generic "ancient writers were irrational" framing.
