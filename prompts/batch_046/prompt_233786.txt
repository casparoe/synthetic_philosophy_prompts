I am giving the opening remarks—about ten minutes—at the interdisciplinary workshop "Boundaries of the Category: Law, Logic, and Language," co-hosted by the philosophy and law schools. The audience is split: half are philosophers of logic and language who will recognize Gongsun Long's White Horse dialogue and the School of Names; the other half are legal practitioners and scholars with no background in classical Chinese philosophy. I want the dialogue to do real structural work in the talk, not serve as decorative cultural reference. Specifically, I want the speech to frame the workshop's central problem—how legal categories attempt to separate essential membership conditions from accidental attributes—through the dialogue's logical form: the distinction between form (the shape or kind) and color (the perceptual property), and the argument that combining them produces something that is no longer identical to the original kind. I want you to adopt a particular interpretive stance that is somewhat non-standard: treat the dialogue not as a metaphysical paradox about universals, but as an institutional problem about registry membership, where horse-ness is a status conferred by an authoritative list and whiteness is an external marker that does not alter registry entry. This framing should genuinely constrain what the speech can claim—for example, if the dialogue suggests that any added property fractures identity, the speech should not comfort the lawyers with the idea that statutory categories are stable; if it suggests that properties are separable only in language, the speech should reflect on how legal drafting uses language to freeze fluid distinctions. The speech must be accessible to the lawyers without condescending, and precise enough for the philosophers that it does not caricature the logical structure. After the speech, please append a brief meta-note stating how confident you are in this institutional reading of the dialogue and identifying the single point—whether historical, interpretive, or logical—where you are least sure.
