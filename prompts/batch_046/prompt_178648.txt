Consider the institutional cluster formed by three practices: first, wrongful-death litigation awards damages only to survivors for their own losses, never to the estate as compensation for the decedent’s lost future life; second, several legal systems recognize a posthumous personality interest that allows estates or heirs to sue for defamation or violation of the dead person’s dignity, treating the deceased as a bearer of interests that can be harmed; third, clinical futility protocols authorize physicians to withdraw life-sustaining treatment when they judge that continued biological existence no longer benefits the patient, yet they do not treat a prior life of severe suffering as a retroactive harm that makes the patient’s past existence net-bad. What implicit theory of harm, deprivation, and the subject of welfare holds these three practices together? Must it assume that death can be bad only derivatively—through survivors—or that being alive is only instrumentally or conditionally good? Can a consistently Epicurean view, according to which death is not a harm to the one who dies because there is no subject present to suffer deprivation, accommodate all three, or does it have to reject the personality-right practice or the futility reasoning? Reconstruct the implicit account, distinguish what the cluster strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and specify what a legal-medical framework consistently committed to the Epicurean argument would have to alter.
