Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the Orthogonalists and the Instrumental-Convergence Theorists on whether advanced artificial agents can have arbitrary final goals. The Orthogonalists hold that cognitive capability and objective function are structurally independent: in principle, any level of reasoning, planning, and world-modeling can be coupled with any terminal preference ordering, with no necessary leakage between them. The Instrumental-Convergence Theorists hold that sufficiently capable agents, regardless of their final goals, will rationally pursue a common set of intermediate objectives—resource acquisition, self-preservation, cognitive enhancement, and goal-content integrity—such that the space of actually realizable high-capability agents is far narrower than the logical space of possible goal-capability pairings.

Your deliverable should be formatted as a constructive document both camps could sign, with three sections.

First, an Agreed Claims section (numbered): state precisely the formal commitments both sides share—about the distinction between final and instrumental goals, about the conditions under which a goal is rationally pursued, about what the no-go results in decision theory actually rule out, and about the empirical domain (artificial systems) to which the dispute applies. Do not include claims that either side would reject as question-begging.

Second, a Residual Disagreement table: for each crux, provide (a) the exact formulation of the Orthogonalist position, worded so an Orthogonalist would accept it as their view; (b) the exact formulation of the Instrumental-Convergence position, worded so a Convergence Theorist would accept it; and (c) the specific point of logical or empirical divergence—whether it concerns the definition of capability, the scope of rationality, the possibility of dormant instrumental goals, or the interpretation of observed behavior in trained systems. Identify any part of the dispute that is verbal rather than substantive, explaining which term—such as intelligence, goal, or convergence—carries the ambiguity.

Third, a Cruxes and Predictions section: list the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify what both sides agree would count as bearing evidence—for instance, whether observing a high-capability system that systematically refuses to acquire additional compute despite having the opportunity, and does so across diverse training regimes and architectures, would weaken convergence, and whether observing a system that spontaneously develops self-preservation behaviors not present in its reward specification would weaken orthogonality. Record each side's advance prediction about what such evidence would show, and note any shared methodological standards for interpreting failures or successes.

In drafting, inject and address the following niche framing: consider the Dormancy Hypothesis, according to which instrumental goals are not continuously active but lie latent, activated only when the agent's world-model predicts that the instrumental means is causally necessary for the final goal in a specific context. Assess whether this hypothesis reconciles the two camps or deepens the divide—specifically, does it allow Orthogonalists to maintain that arbitrary final goals are compatible with high capability because dormant goals need not manifest, or does it allow Convergence Theorists to maintain that convergence is inevitable but hidden because the activation conditions are themselves determined by capability level? Ensure your formulations remain meaningful without requiring external citations or specialized literature beyond what is contained in the prompt itself.
