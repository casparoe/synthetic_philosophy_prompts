I am designing a 75-minute workshop for about ten graduate students in philosophy of art and value, and I need a structured simulation that makes them feel—before they are told—why the value of an original artwork cannot be fully captured either by its perceptible properties or by its causal history taken in isolation. The setting should be a closed-market game with small stakes: each student receives a hidden role card—collector, forger, or authenticator—and a budget of points that determine a minor privilege such as selecting the next seminar's discussion question. The game proceeds in three rounds: in the first, collectors bid on artworks represented only by identical high-resolution images and brief descriptions, with no provenance information; in the second, provenance cards are revealed showing that some works are originals, some are perfect material forgeries with fabricated histories, and one is an authorized studio replica executed by assistants under the artist's direct supervision but without the artist's own hand touching the material, a case that breaks the simple original-versus-fake binary; in the third, collectors must decide whether to keep, return, or destroy their acquisitions under a new rule that any object lacking direct indexical connection to the artist's body loses half its point value, while objects with that connection gain a bonus regardless of perceptual identity. The rules and payoff scheme should be explicit enough to run without technology beyond printed cards, and the incentives should be arranged so that participants experience a genuine conflict between aesthetic judgment and historical knowledge—specifically, that they are penalized for holding an object they initially valued purely for its appearance once its causal origin is known.
