As a novelist constructing a multi-generational narrative about a fishing cooperative that slowly collapses into overextraction despite every member's sincere personal opposition to depletion, I have been thinking about Moloch, multipolar traps, and races to the bottom not merely as economic mechanisms but as architectures of moral narrative. To sharpen my conceptual framework, I am drawing a distinction—one I do not know has an established name—between what I call exit-cost asymmetry, in which leaving the destructive equilibrium is individually ruinous but collectively inexpensive, and gradient invisibility, in which each incremental downward step is too small for any actor to perceive the trajectory, even though the cumulative slope is catastrophic. Given these two dynamics operating together within a multipolar trap, what are the most important implications for how a writer should represent moral agency, collective blame, and the possibility of redemption? In particular, how should one understand the relationship between localized benevolence—each character earnestly trying to do right within a narrow sphere of influence—and the global equilibrium that renders those local virtues structurally futile? Please describe the most important implications, writing in formal American English, and please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or scholarly citations; I seek conceptual analysis rather than attribution.
