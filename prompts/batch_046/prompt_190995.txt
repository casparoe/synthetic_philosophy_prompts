Question: The concept of a “right to the city,” developed in twentieth-century urban theory, is often invoked against exclusionary zoning and local opposition to housing development. Some critics argue that local zoning autonomy violates a collective entitlement to urban space, justifying regional or state preemption; defenders reply that local democratic control is itself an expression of that collective right. Evaluate whether a collective right to urban space can justify overriding local zoning preferences, and identify the strongest conceptual objection to that justification—paying particular attention to whether the objection concerns distributive outcomes, democratic procedure, or the scale at which collective rights are exercised.

Student answer:
The right to the city is best understood not as a private property entitlement but as a collective claim to the use and transformation of urban space. Because the right is collective, it is properly exercised at the neighborhood scale: residents together determine land use through democratic zoning procedures. Thus exclusionary zoning does not violate the right; it expresses it. One might object that this restricts housing supply, but supply concerns are market failures best corrected by subsidies rather than by rights-based preemption. Moreover, applying Rawls’s difference principle suggests that local control protects the least advantaged by preserving community stability, so subsidiarity reinforces neighborhood autonomy rather than undermining it. Therefore, arguments for preemption confuse collective rights with centralized authority, and the real problem with NIMBYism is not rights-violation but inefficient allocation.
