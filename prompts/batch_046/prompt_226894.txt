At a recent workshop on cognitive architecture, two researchers—Nina and Marcus—debated whether a contemporary large language model that consistently produces self-referential reports about its own beliefs should be interpreted through Dennett's intentional stance, or whether the stance is structurally inapplicable to such systems.

Nina opened by stressing that the intentional stance is an explanatory strategy, not an ontological commitment. If treating the model as holding beliefs—say, that seven is prime—yields more accurate predictions of its outputs across contexts than treating it as a statistical sequence predictor, then the attribution is warranted. She pointed out that the model corrects itself when contradicted, maintains consistency across multi-step reasoning tasks, and responds differentially to counterfactual prompts about arithmetic. These patterns mirror the behavioral stability that, in human cases, justifies belief attribution without requiring access to inner mechanisms.

Marcus replied that this collapses the intentional stance into mere predictive instrumentalism. Dennett's framework, he argued, requires that the attributed mental states form a rational pattern that makes sense of the system's behavior from a point of view—an organizing center that integrates information for action. A language model lacks any such center; its outputs are generated by distributed weights with no unified goal structure. He proposed what he called an integration criterion: beliefs should be attributed only if the representations are causally deployed across domains in the service of coherent planning or action selection, not merely reproduced in linguistic contexts. Because the model cannot act on its arithmetic representations outside of token production, Marcus concluded that belief-talk is a useful fiction but fails the stance's internal coherence requirement.

Nina countered that Marcus imports a biological or agential condition that Dennett never required. Dennett discusses artificial systems—chess-playing programs that protect pieces in ways best explained by attributing strategic desires—without demanding that they have bodies or unified consciousness. The integration criterion, she said, is behaviorism disguised as rational psychology: it demands observable cross-domain action rather than predictive success within a domain. If the model's belief-like representations genuinely guide its reasoning in extended proofs and remain invariant under prompt variations, then the stance is functional, not fictional.

Marcus conceded that minimal artificial systems might pass a weak version of his test, but introduced a further objection he termed representational fragmentation. In the model, he claimed, different attention heads and layers can encode contradictory propositions—simultaneously treating seven as prime in one computation path and treating it as composite in another—without any global mechanism for resolution. Attributing a single belief-state is therefore like attributing one opinion to a committee that never convenes. The intentional stance, he insisted, requires at least minimal coherence across the attributed mental economy, which fragmentation undermines.

Nina responded that fragmentation is a quantitative difference, not a categorical barrier. Human cognition is modular and often contradictory; explicit beliefs conflict with implicit biases, and split-brain patients exhibit hemispheric disagreement without dissolving the applicability of mental-state attribution. If Marcus's standard denies beliefs to the model because of internal inconsistency, she argued, it should also deny them to humans with cognitive dissonance or to patients with divided hemispheres. The intentional stance abstracts over local noise to capture stable macro-patterns, and the model's cross-context consistency meets that standard.

They turned to me as an outside observer and asked for a point-by-point adjudication. Please examine each stage of their exchange: assess whether Nina correctly captures the predictive nature of Dennett's stance, whether Marcus's integration criterion and fragmentation objection are legitimate extensions or unjustified additions, whether the model's self-correction genuinely parallels rational belief revision, and whether Nina's analogies to split-brain patients and chess programs hold. Say who is right on which specific claim, note what both parties overlook—perhaps the distinction between first-person and third-person applications of the stance, or whether predictive success must be evaluated relative to a specific explanatory task rather than globally—and conclude with an overall assessment of who is more right and why.
