I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been reading on my own about consciousness and ethics. Recently I encountered Erwin Schrödinger's view—drawn from Vedanta—that there is literally only one consciousness in the universe, and that what we call separate minds are more like facets of a single jewel than independent subjects. I tried to work out what this would mean for morality, and I constructed the following argument, which now strikes me as absurd in its conclusion, though each step looks plausible on its own.

(1) If consciousness is genuinely singular, then every experience of harm, regardless of which body it occurs in, is experienced by that same single subject.
(2) Therefore, harming another person is not harming a different being; it is harming the same being that I call "myself," only through a different facet.
(3) The ordinary moral prohibition against harming others is thus a special case of the prohibition against self-harm.
(4) Self-harm is permissible when it serves a greater overall good for the self—such as undergoing surgery to save one's life, or enduring pain for long-term health.
(5) By analogy, harming one facet is permissible whenever it serves the greater overall good of the single conscious subject.
(6) This means that if killing one person would preserve resources or prevent suffering for many others, the single self is simply reallocating its own resources, and the act is morally permissible, perhaps even required.
(7) But this implies that I should be willing to kill an innocent neighbor if it marginally improves my own survival, since "we" are the same self and I am merely optimizing within one organism.
(8) I find that conclusion intolerable.
(9) To avoid it, I must reject one of the earlier steps. Rejecting (1) abandons Schrödinger's core metaphysical claim. Rejecting (2) seems to require that metaphysical unity does not carry over to moral unity, but then I do not see why the unity of consciousness should matter ethically at all. Rejecting (5) requires treating self-harm as always wrong regardless of benefit, which conflicts with our acceptance of necessary surgery and risk.
(10) I would most like to keep (1) and (8). Where is the actual slip—between (2) and (3), or between (4) and (5)—and what does the correct rejection cost me?

I feel like something slipped between the metaphysical claim and the moral conclusion, perhaps involving what it means for harm to be "the same," but I cannot pinpoint the exact ambiguity. Which premise should I give up, and why does it only look plausible because of a hidden slide?
