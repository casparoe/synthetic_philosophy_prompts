I am teaching a graduate seminar on early analytic philosophy and the good life, and in our discussion of Frank Ramsey's work I have become convinced that his scattered reflections—particularly the pragmatist theory of partial belief he develops in his writing on probability, together with his brief but suggestive remarks about ethical judgement—contain the seeds of a distinctive position on the meaning of life that standard histories systematically overlook in favour of logical-positivist or existentialist narratives. I should like you to write an essay of approximately 1,500 words that takes a committed, definite stand on the following controversy: does Ramsey's subjectivist account of belief, combined with his apparent view that ethical judgements express preferences structured only by rational consistency, entail that the question of life's meaning is not about objective purpose but rather about the coherence and intensity with which an agent integrates their highest-order preferences into a stable life-plan? Or does this reconstruction collapse into a form of instrumental rationality that cannot account for the normative weight we ordinarily attach to questions of meaning?

In developing your answer, you should weigh the interpretation that treats Ramsey as a proto-existentialist—one for whom meaning is constructed through the act of committing to uncertain but deeply valued propositions—against the rival reading that his pragmatism about belief drains the concept of meaning of any non-instrumental content. Do not merely survey these options; defend a clear verdict with reasons. I am especially interested in whether Ramsey's framework can accommodate what we might call tragic meaning: cases in which an agent's highest-order preferences are irreconcilable, yet the intensity of belief in each remains undiminished. You should also address the misconception—common among students who first encounter Ramsey through his technical contributions—that his philosophy of belief is purely descriptive and therefore silent on normative questions about how one ought to live. Please avoid any bibliography, reading list, or invented page references; rely solely on the conceptual structure of the arguments as they are generally known. Conclude with an explicit judgement on whether Ramsey's view, properly reconstructed, offers a viable account of life's meaning or whether it ultimately reduces that question to a matter of psychological coherence.
