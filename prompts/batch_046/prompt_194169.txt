The question I posed was this: experimental psychologists have shown that minor situational shifts—being in a hurry, standing near fresh bread—can radically change whether people help a stranger or tell the truth. Virtue ethics traditionally asks us to cultivate stable, global character traits such as courage or integrity. If there is no globally honest person, only someone honest-in-particular-conditions, does that mean virtue ethics rests on a fiction about human psychology? Can the theory survive by revising its account of character, or must it abandon the very idea that we can evaluate people as having good or bad character?

My partner wrote the following:

"I think virtue ethics can survive, but only if it abandons global traits and accepts that virtues are locally bound to specific contexts. The situationist experiments show that behavior is driven by external conditions rather than by any fixed inner soul. Aristotle was wrong to treat virtue like a craft that, once mastered, remains permanently stable in every setting; he assumed that a courageous soldier would automatically be courageous in an office dispute. But in reality someone can be honest at work yet deceptive with family. That fragmentation does not destroy virtue ethics—it just makes it realistic. Once we see that virtues are tied to particular relationships and settings, virtue ethics naturally moves closer to care ethics, which also rejects universal rules and focuses on particular contexts. So the situationist critique does not kill virtue ethics; it forces the theory to drop its outdated global psychology and become a more relational, context-sensitive view of character."
