The debate over intergenerational national guilt—structured since Jaspers by the distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—appears stalled: individualists deny that guilt survives the death of perpetrators without personal agency, while collectivists insist that structural complicity and institutional continuity transmit responsibility across generations. Diagnose this impasse: is it substantive disagreement about the ontology of collective agency, a verbal dispute over whether “guilt” can apply non-metaphorically to groups, or a cross-disciplinary confusion in which moral philosophers ask about blameworthiness while political theorists ask about institutional repair obligations? Focus especially on whether the deadlock conceals an unexamined assumption about symbolic inheritance—whether a constitutional or memorial mechanism can “carry” guilt without attributing culpability to present citizens. If the disagreement is verbal or disciplinary, specify the conceptual distinction that would dissolve rather than rename it; if substantive, identify what kind of normative, historical, or institutional finding could actually move one side.
