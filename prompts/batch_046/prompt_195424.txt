I'm the senior compliance officer for a university civic forum that runs binding deliberation on campus policy—health mandates, endowment ethics, speech regulations, and budget priorities. Our charter divides submissions into Track A, an open background culture where students and faculty may invoke any comprehensive doctrine, and Track B, the binding deliberation, where only arguments meeting a public-reason standard may ground a vote. I process about twenty files a month, have fifteen minutes per submission, must sign off without requiring philosophical expertise from my staff, and need a one-page recommendation for each: Track A only, Track B eligible, or Track B with a translation rider. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions with clear stop rules, explicit flags for unavoidable judgment calls, and no dependence on outside citation checks.

Ground the procedure in the actual architecture of public-reason theory: the distinction between a reason being publicly accessible and being publicly acceptable; the proviso that religious or metaphysical reasons may remain in Track A but must be paired with a sufficient public reason to enter Track B; and the reciprocity condition that citizens who reject the underlying doctrine should still be able to endorse the policy for reasons they recognize as their own. To operationalize this, design a three-gate audit specific to this forum: the Accessibility Gate (can the normative claim be stated without invoking the doctrine's authoritative source?), the Reciprocity Gate (would a reasonable citizen who rejects the doctrine still possess an independent political reason to accept the outcome?), and the Residual Burden Gate (if the doctrine is fully bracketed, does the argument retain its original normative force, or does it collapse?). For each gate, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a "no" or "rider required," and where the decision must be handed to a human because the issue is one of values rather than classification.

The procedure must handle hard cases: ambiguous translations where a theological concept seems to map onto a secular value but may change its meaning; arguments that combine public and non-public reasons in ways that are inseparable; and proposals where the doctrine provides unique motivational urgency that no translated reason can replicate. Apply it to three submissions: (1) extending dining services through Ramadan nights, justified both by Islamic obligations of communal care and by equal accommodation for religious practice; (2) denying student-health funding to a group that provides abortion referrals, justified solely by the claim that human life bears the imago Dei from conception, with no accompanying secular argument offered; and (3) committing the campus to carbon-neutral operations by 2035, justified partly by Christian stewardship of creation and partly by intergenerational justice, where translating "stewardship" into "sustainability" is contested and may either preserve or drain the argument's urgency. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation—Track A only—that you, as the officer, might personally find unwelcome but must endorse for consistency. Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: which disputes it leaves unsettled, where it is likely to misfire, and why it is not a substitute for democratic judgment. The deliverable should be self-contained; do not append bibliographies, reading lists, or source citations.
