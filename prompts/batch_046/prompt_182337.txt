I'm a high school student working on an independent philosophy paper, and I've become stuck on a dispute that seems to have gone nowhere for generations: the debate between endurantists, who hold that a persisting object is wholly present at every moment of its existence, and perdurantists, who hold that it persists by having distinct temporal parts spread across time like spatial parts spread across space. The familiar arguments are all well worn—the problem of temporary intrinsics, the rotating homogeneous disc, cases of fission and fusion, and the charge that one view either multiplies entities needlessly or collapses change into contradiction—yet in every discussion I read, the two sides restate these same moves without any visible convergence. I want to understand why this particular disagreement has stalled so completely.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily about ontology but about hidden standards of explanation that neither side makes explicit. Endurantists appear to treat it as a constraint that an adequate account of change must preserve exactly one complete object at each time without invoking a "four-dimensional" ontology, whereas perdurantists appear to treat spatiotemporal analogy as a mandatory explanatory framework, so that rejecting temporal parts violates a principle of theoretical uniformity. But I also wonder whether there is a deeper, rarely discussed divergence: perhaps the sides disagree about whether diachronic identity requires a primitive "binding" relation across time, analogous to spatial contiguity, or whether such a relation can be constructed entirely from instantaneous stage-relations, and they have never agreed on whether that binding relation needs an "internally structured" temporal form—like a line composed of points—rather than being an unanalyzed primitive. If that hidden structural question is doing the real work, then they may be answering different questions about what persistence must explain, even while they use the same word.

So I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal about the term "persistence," or a clash of bedrock intuitions about naturalness and ontological parsimony? Do the sides differ about which thought experiments count as decisive data, about what a successful explanation of change must deliver, or about whether the question itself is well posed? Please engage with what each side actually says in these debates—about temporary properties, about analogies between space and time, and about the costs of multiplying entities—rather than offering a survey of the literature. And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a new conceptual distinction, a formal model of temporal parts, a clarification of the spatiotemporal analogy, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why. If you think the question is ill-posed, say so and explain what misconception generates the illusion of a single debate.
