I am drafting a feature for an international magazine on science and ideas, examining whether unwritten social conventions generate genuine moral obligations for visitors. While reporting recently in a city where pedestrians observe a precise, unspoken choreography at crossings — specifically, holding back until every other person present has begun to move, so that no one is visibly first — I deliberately stepped ahead to observe reactions, assuming the norm was purely strategic coordination. Instead, strangers treated the breach with unmistakable moral anger, as though I had violated a duty of respect rather than merely disrupted an efficient pattern. My editor now wants me to use this incident to ask whether an outsider who recognises a local convention as arbitrary nevertheless owes compliance, or whether the obligation dissolves once the strategic rationale is transparent. Can you advise me on how to structure this argument for a general audience? I need to distinguish, without hand-waving, between obligations that are real but conventional, obligations that are illusory, and the grey area in between — particularly whether my wrong was a failure of respect, a failure of practical reasoning, or neither. Please reply in British English, and avoid citing specific works or offering a reading list; I am writing for readers who will not follow academic references.
