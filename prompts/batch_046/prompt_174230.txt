In my philosophy of mind independent study, I have been following the long-running disagreement over whether cognition can penetrate perception—whether beliefs, expectations, or conceptual training can alter the content of visual experience itself, or whether they only influence post-perceptual judgment about an unchanged sensory given. Proponents of penetration often point to expert pattern recognition and emotional face perception, arguing that sustained conceptual learning can restructure early visual processing; opponents, drawing on modular architectures, treat those same cases as reinterpretations of a fixed percept and insist that one-off beliefs never reach the sensory level. Despite decades of exchange using similar experimental paradigms—ambiguous figures, color-adaptation studies, and neurological dissociations—neither camp has moved substantially. My working hypothesis is that part of the stall is verbal and structural: one side may be describing diachronic restructuring of perceptual architecture through long-term expertise, while the other addresses synchronic influence within a single perceptual episode, and both are grouped under the single label "cognitive penetration." At the same time, I wonder whether a hidden empirical disagreement about which neural pathways count as genuinely sensory is doing the real work, or whether the dispute is ultimately ill-posed because "perception" and "cognition" do not carve nature at the joints in the way the debate assumes. Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is—conceptual, empirical, verbal, or a case of two distinct questions hidden under one heading—supported by what each side actually says about experience, judgment, and neural processing? Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that separates diachronic restructuring from synchronic influence, an empirical result about early visual pathways, or an argument that the framing should be abandoned? I want an analysis of why the argument has frozen, not a survey of the literature.
