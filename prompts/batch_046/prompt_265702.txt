I am a doctoral candidate in the history and philosophy of science, and I am drafting a narrative illustration for a chapter on historiographical ontology—specifically, the question of whether “the Middle Ages,” “the Renaissance,” and “the Scientific Revolution” denote real temporal structures or are retrospective administrative inventions. I need a short philosophical fiction piece, roughly 900–1,100 words, that dramatizes the performative dimension of periodization: the idea that these categories do not merely describe past events but actively reorganize the material and institutional conditions under which events become visible as belonging to one epoch or another. The story should be set in a concrete, somewhat niche institutional environment—for example, a state conservation archive where a senior conservator is tasked with re-cataloguing manuscripts and scientific instruments according to a new “continuist” mandate that collapses the Renaissance and Scientific Revolution into a single administrative block. The narrative should show the character discovering that the old period boundaries are not just conceptual but are physically embedded in preservation chemistry, storage protocols, access permissions, and funding classifications, so that the artifacts themselves seem to resist the new taxonomy not because they “naturally” belong to one age, but because decades of bureaucratic practice have literally altered their material conditions of existence. The philosophical point should emerge through the character’s practical, sensory engagement with the objects—handling paper, reading faded labels, negotiating with curators—rather than through explicit exposition. The story should take seriously the strongest version of the “invention” thesis—that periodization is a bureaucratic technology with material effects—while also giving honest weight to the opposing intuition that there are genuine discontinuities in intellectual practice that any taxonomy must accommodate. Please avoid didactic dialogue, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific historians; the story should stand entirely on its own conceptual architecture.
