Please adopt the role of a senior philosopher of science and institutional decision-theorist. Before constructing the deliverable, take a moment to brainstorm the distinctions that must survive translation from formal epistemology into an administrative checklist: specifically, the difference between observing a definite event and revising one’s confidence in an evidential partition, and the constraint that prior conditional probabilities within each cell of that partition must remain fixed regardless of how surprising the new evidential probabilities appear.

You advise a university research-integrity board that convenes fortnightly to determine whether ambiguous audit reports should trigger formal institutional responses — suspension of recruitment, mandatory re-analysis, or referral to an external regulator. The board rarely receives reports that specify a definite event; instead, they shift the board’s credences over a partition of possible evidential states (for example, “probable protocol deviation of unspecified type”). The board has commissioned a reusable decision procedure, grounded specifically in Jeffrey conditionalization rather than in standard Bayesian conditioning, because standard conditioning presupposes the observation of a definite outcome and the board almost never possesses one.

Your procedure must satisfy the following:

- Structure it as an ordered sequence of questions or tests that a non-specialist board member can run in under thirty minutes, with explicit criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage.
- Identify the precise points where the procedure halts with a determination of “no institutional action”, the points where it mandates escalation, and the points where the question is irreducibly one of values rather than belief revision, requiring a recorded human vote.
- Include a small, concrete set of diagnostic conditions — analogous to test sentences — that board members can apply to determine whether a given report constitutes an evidential-partition update rather than a direct observation.
- Apply the procedure to three cases: (i) an internal audit that assigns 60 per cent credence to “some form of data irregularity” without identifying whether it involves fabrication, mislabelling, or statistical error; (ii) a peer reviewer who reports ambiguous graphical anomalies that raise the probability of “selective reporting” to 0.5 without confirming its presence; and (iii) a whistle-blower allegation that is credible but whose evidential partition overlaps with hypotheses the board had previously assigned near-zero prior probability.
- Ensure that at least one of these applications yields an answer the board is likely to find unwelcome — for instance, a mandate to take no formal action despite serious ethical concern — and explain exactly why Jeffrey’s rule produces that result.
- Mark clearly where the procedure breaks down or depends on a prior judgement call disguised as a mechanical step, especially regarding the choice of the evidential partition and the assignment of probabilities over its cells.
- Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: for example, if the board’s priors over hypotheses within a partition cell were themselves formed by a process now suspected of being corrupted.

Write the procedure in polished, formal British English. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote any external text verbatim. To prevent this from becoming a generic summary, inject one niche conceptual constraint of your own design: require the board to observe a strict “no-lookahead” discipline — they must not revise their prior conditional probabilities within any partition cell after seeing the new evidential probabilities, even if doing so seems intuitively reasonable — and build that constraint explicitly into step three of your checklist.
