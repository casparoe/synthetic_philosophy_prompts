Analyze the West German Fischer controversy of the 1960s and 1970s—specifically Fritz Fischer's claim that Imperial Germany's leadership deliberately exploited the July Crisis of 1914 to launch a long-planned war of expansion—through the lens of collective moral responsibility, treating the dispute not merely as a historiographical quarrel but as a philosophical test case for whether a nation-state can bear responsibility for an act of war in a genuinely non-metaphorical sense.

Proceed in three parts.

First, distinguish two frameworks that the debate implicitly presupposes: an aggregative model, on which national responsibility reduces to the summed culpability of individual office-holders and citizens; and a corporate or institutional model, on which the state possesses continuous decision structures, strategic intentions, and an institutional identity that can bear responsibility non-reductively. Map Fischer's specific historical arguments—particularly his evidence that German leadership welcomed rather than resisted escalation, and that pre-war planning revealed expansionist intentions—onto each framework. Under the aggregative view, whose individual guilt is sufficient or necessary for national blame? Under the corporate view, what features of the Kaiserreich—such as the relationship between the General Staff, the Foreign Office, and the Chancellery—would need to constitute a unified agent capable of collective intention?

Second, identify precisely where the mapping fractures. Fischer's critics often conceded individual culpability yet rejected attributing war guilt to Germany as a collective subject; others pointed to bureaucratic fragmentation and democratic deficit to deny that the Kaiserreich could form corporate intentions. Choose one specific feature of the 1914 decision structure—such as the absence of a unified cabinet vote for war, the autonomous strategic planning of the military leadership, or the disjunction between diplomatic signaling and mobilization schedules—and show whether it undermines Fischer's attribution, undermines the corporate model's applicability, or reveals a silent spot in both.

Third, draw out what the controversy reveals about the conditions for meaningful national responsibility. Does the eventual shift in West German public memory—away from attributing blame to the nation as a whole and toward structural or systemic explanations—indicate that the aggregative model was abandoned because its evidential demands were too severe, or only that collective responsibility was reconceived rather than rejected? And does the case suggest that holding a nation responsible requires something like shared democratic authorship, or is strategic coherence across institutional roles sufficient?

Your analysis should be disciplined: specify who counts as the agent, what counts as the action, and what counts as the evidence. Where either philosophical framework offers no clear verdict, say so explicitly rather than forcing it to speak.
