Construct a Death-in-Damascus-style thought experiment engineered to separate logical fatalism from theological fatalism by manipulating only the agent's epistemic route to the prophecy, while holding the prophecy's content and the outcome fixed. Feature two structurally parallel agents, each warned that they will be in location L at time T: one derives this conclusion by logical deduction from premises she herself knows to be true and unalterable, the other receives the identical warning as testimony from a fallible but highly credible human informant. Specify that both attempt to avoid L by an ordinary means—e.g., refusing to board a specific train—and that both succeed in reaching a different location, only to suffer an unrelated fatal accident there. Then demonstrate: (i) why a logical fatalist must treat the first agent's flight as rationally futile in a different way than the second's, despite identical outcomes; (ii) why a theological fatalist—who ties the threat to freedom only to infallible or causally efficacious foreknowledge—must rate both attempts identically; (iii) which structural feature (the epistemic source of the belief, not its content or truth-value) drives the divergence; and (iv) whether removing that feature collapses the case into a standard fatalism paradox or eliminates the distinction altogether. Constraints: no omniscient beings, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics; the prophecy concerns an everyday decision; keep the narrative under one paragraph; and do not ask for bibliographic references or reading lists.
