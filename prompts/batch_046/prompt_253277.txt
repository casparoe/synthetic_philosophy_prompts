I defend a narrowly indexical reading of Descartes's cogito: the argument establishes only that an occurrent act of thinking is occurring and that this act carries a non-optional first-person indexical reference—this thinking—that cannot fail to refer in the way a definite description might. On this view, sum does not ascribe existence to a substantial subject but registers the grammatical reflex of the act's self-monitoring structure; ego is not a name for a soul but an indexical anchor fixed by the present occurrence. Consequently, the cogito proves nothing about persistence, simplicity, or immateriality—those commitments enter only later, through premises about clear and distinct ideas and the real distinction introduced in subsequent Meditations. What would have to be true for this indexical reading to fail? Which formal properties of self-reference—drawn perhaps from the logic of indexicals, from phenomenological accounts of inner time-consciousness, or from contemporary debates on the transparency of self-knowledge—would show that an act of thinking cannot occur without already presupposing a diachronically unified or substantial bearer? And if one reads Meditation II without quarantining it from the metaphysical architecture of Meditations III and VI, do Descartes's own claims about the clarity of the idea of a thinking thing genuinely entangle the core argument in ontological commitments it cannot avoid? I want an honest assessment of exposure: has this reading insulated itself by selective separation, or does it pay a hidden price—perhaps in rendering the cogito philosophically trivial, or in being unable to account for the specific modal certainty Descartes claims? What evidence, argument, or formal result would genuinely threaten the thin interpretation rather than merely being awkward for it?
