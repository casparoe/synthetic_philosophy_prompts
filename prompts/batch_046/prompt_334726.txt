I've been comparing how different regimes treat sex work institutionally, and there's a tension I can't place philosophically. In Sweden you can sell but not buy, and if you apply for state housing you usually have to enter an exit programme — committing to stop working to recieve support. In Germany and the Netherlands legalisation means registration, mandatory health checks, and brothel licensing, treating the work as labour but the body as a site of public risk. Meanwhile New Zealand decriminalised it: workers can refuse clients contractually, and operators need a certificate, though the goverment can revoke that for offences that have nothing to do with the workplace.

What philosophical view of agency, harm, and state paternalism does this trio presuppose? The Nordic model treats the seller's consent as structurally compromised, so welfare is conditional on leaving. The legalised regimes treat consent as valid but the body as dangerous, hence medical oversight. The NZ model treats the worker as a rational contractor, yet still lets the state vet managers for moral suitability in ways it wouldn't for a café owner. Is there one coherent theory — maybe harm reduction plus public order — that licenses all three? Or are they pulling in opposite directions: one denying autonomy, one affirming it but regulating the body, one affirming contract rights but retaining moral gatekeeping?

If we replaced the underlying view with a strict capability approach or a pure consent-based framework, which parts of these designs would collapse? And does the fact that none of these systems fully criminalise the seller, despite very different rationales, suggest there's a shared hidden commitment — or just political compromise?
