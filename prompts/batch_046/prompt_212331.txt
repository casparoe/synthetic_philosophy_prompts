Under the framework, patients with severe cortical damage are placed in immersive virtual environments generated directly from their residual neural signals. Any contractual or medical decisions made inside that virtual space are treated as fully legally binding. At the same time, family members may authorize a system suspension if they judge that the patient's subjective experience has become merely simulated rather than genuinely present. Meanwhile, all data from these pods must be archived as phenomenal logs, treating first-person experience as a readable interface layer rather than an irreducible inner reality.

What philosophical commitments about consciousness and personal identity does this practice presuppose? Specifically, does it implicitly adopt the view that consciousness is a kind of user illusion—an interface model generated for predictive control rather than a direct window onto reality? Is the framework internally coherent, or does it simultaneously treat the virtual agent's choices as authoritative while allowing outsiders to dismiss those same choices as unreal? If we replaced its implicit view with a straightforward realist account of consciousness—one that denies experience is just a functional dashboard—what specific provisions of the regulation would have to change?

Please take a definite position: argue either that the framework is philosophically consistent under an illusionist reading, or that it collapses into contradiction and requires revision. Give your reasons point by point rather than laying out both sides and leaving the judgment open. Also include a brief note on where your analysis takes liberties with the idea of consciousness as an interface, or where it relies on a contested reading of what user illusion actually entails.
