A familiar way of presenting Wittgenstein's notion of language-games is to liken language to chess: there are explicit rules governing legal moves, a shared objective, and participation requires knowing those rules in advance. This analogy is often used to support two further claims: first, that meaning is nothing more than rule-governed use within the game; second, that the concept 'game' itself exhibits family resemblance rather than a common essence, since chess, football, and patience share no single defining feature. I want to examine how far this picture holds if we adjust the source domain. Instead of chess with fixed rules, consider cooperative games in which the rules are partly negotiated during play and in which success conditions can shift without anyone violating an explicit prior constraint — something closer to an improvised collaborative construction than to a board game with a manual.

Can you set out the correspondence precisely: what in language maps onto pre-established rules, what maps onto negotiated norms, and what maps onto the absence of a shared essence? Where does the modified analogy break down, particularly with respect to the normative dimension — the fact that in language there are still correct and incorrect uses, even if no necessary and sufficient conditions define a concept? Which features of cooperative, open-ended play does the analogy quietly import that language might lack, and which features of linguistic normativity does it suppress? Finally, does the conclusion that meaning is use survive once we recognise that 'use' in chess is tightly circumscribed by a prior rule-set, whereas in family-resemblance concepts the constraints appear to arise only from overlapping similarities rather than from an explicit constitution?
