In debates over whether norms like dueling or slavery collapse through rational argument, material interest, or shifts in honor—Appiah's honor-based account versus more rationalist or economic explanations—defenders of the honor view often analogize honor to a reputation-backed currency: norms are contracts denominated in status, arguments are attempts to alter exchange rates by persuasion alone, and moral revolutions are bank runs triggered when the underlying backing collapses. Critics reply that this smuggles in an instrumental ontology: honor is neither fungible nor centrally cleared, and treating moral commitment as portfolio management distorts the phenomenon. Audit the analogy: lay out the explicit correspondence table (honor, norm, argument, interest, revolution, and the "backing" mechanism); separate the disanalogies that undermine the intended conclusion—that rational argument is impotent in moral change—from those that are merely decorative; identify which features of currency (quantifiability, rapid liquidity, central issuance) the analogy quietly imports and the target lacks; say whether the conclusion survives once those imports are exposed; and propose a better analogy—perhaps drawn from architecture, immunology, or distributed consensus—that captures the Appiah-Williams dynamic, especially Williams's concern that reflection can dissolve thick ethical knowledge, without licensing the same distortions.
