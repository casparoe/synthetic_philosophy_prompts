Consider the institutional practices that govern how much citizens hide from the state: Germany's data-protection regime, rooted in a constitutional right to informational self-determination; the American framework of governmental transparency, public-records access, and normative openness; and China's social-credit infrastructure, which collapses behavioral data into state-visible scores. Each practice encodes a different answer to what a citizen is epistemically—whether an opaque subject, a transparent participant, or a continuously readable profile.

Reconstruct the philosophical commitments that each framework strictly presupposes about autonomy, the state's entitlement to know, and whether personal information has an ontology independent of its use. Say whether each practice is internally consistent, whether its parts depend on the same underlying theory or pull in different directions, and whether any of these commitments are genuinely necessary rather than merely convenient for the policy. Then take a definite position: which of the three implicit philosophies is most defensible as the foundation for democratic information relations, which commitments in the others must be abandoned, and what conceptual or institutional evidence over the coming years would overturn your judgment? Give me a committed, dependency-aware defense, not a comparative survey.
