I am drafting a proposal for a one-semester independent study in moral philosophy—approximately four thousand words, to be completed over four months—because a recent personal crisis has made the abstract debate suddenly unavoidable. For the past year I have served as the primary caregiver for a parent with advancing dementia while continuing clinical practice, and several months ago I faced a decision in which placing them in residential care and continuing to care for them at home each seemed to violate a distinct, non-derogable obligation: one tied to professional and institutional duties of care, the other to a filial commitment that is not reducible to beneficence. What struck me was not merely that the choice was painful, but that neither option felt like a resolvable conflict in which one obligation outweighed the other; instead, both seemed to leave a normative residue that was not simple regret or guilt, but something closer to an unfulfilled duty that persisted even after the decision was made.

My half-formed idea is that the longstanding debate over whether genuine moral dilemmas exist has stalled partly because both sides treat the phenomenon as a conflict between values, principles, or outcomes. I want to ask a more specific question: whether there exists a class of dilemmas—let us call them structural dilemmas—that arise not from competing values but from the internal logic of practical identities themselves. Being a clinician and being a child are not merely roles that happen to pull in different directions; they may constitute practical identities whose internal norms contain mutually exclusive directives, such that satisfying the logic of one necessarily violates the logic of the other, and this incompatibility survives rational resolution not because of ignorance or incommensurability of value, but because of a fracture in the coherence of the agent’s practical self. Precisely stated: can practical identities generate genuinely incompatible obligations that remain after all relevant moral reasons have been fully specified and weighted, and does recognizing them require abandoning the assumption of a unitary practical reason?

The proposal needs the following elements. First, a sharp formulation of the research question that distinguishes structural dilemmas from both epistemic dilemmas (where conflict stems from ignorance about facts or priorities) and standard value-conflict dilemmas (where resolution is achieved by ranking or balancing). Second, an account of why existing positions leave this open: the denial camp typically argues that moral consistency requires that all obligations be jointly satisfiable in principle, treating apparent remainders as emotional artifacts, while the defense camp often treats moral residue as evidence of genuine conflict but analyzes it within a framework of competing values rather than fractured identity-structures. Neither camp, I will argue, has adequately tested whether the conflict can be isolated at the level of role-constitutive logic rather than at the level of value comparison. Third, the method: I will construct three tightly controlled hypothetical cases—one clinical, one familial, one civic—that are designed to isolate identity-based structural conflict from other sources of moral tension, then apply the standard resolution strategies (specification of principles, proportional balancing, temporal or spatial compartmentalization, and role-abandonment) to see whether any actually dissolves the conflict or merely displaces it onto a different normative register. Alongside this, I will analyze whether the post-decisional residue in these cases has a distinct normative structure—functioning not as retrospective emotion but as continued recognition of an unfulfilled obligation—without appealing to empirical psychology beyond what philosophical analysis of practical reason can license.

Fourth, the possible conclusions, which I take to be three: either structural dilemmas reduce to value conflicts once roles are properly analyzed, and the appearance of uniqueness comes from conflating institutional obligations with personal values; or they are genuine but do not threaten practical rationality, instead indicating that practical identity can be non-unitary without being irrational; or they are genuine and require a revision to our model of practical reason, specifically a move away from the assumption that a single agent must be able to integrate all obligations into one coherent deliberative framework. Fifth, failure conditions: the project fails if I cannot construct cases in which the conflict clearly survives all standard resolution strategies, or if the analysis of moral residue collapses into ordinary regret without independent normative significance. Sixth, scope and timeline: I will restrict case analysis to three domains, avoid a comprehensive historical survey of the dilemma literature, and explicitly bracket citation work for a later stage, focusing instead on whether the conceptual framing holds. Finally, an honest risk assessment: the project could become too large if analyzing practical identity requires a full theory of personal identity over time, or if the institutional logic of clinical and familial roles demands an empirical sociology that exceeds philosophical method; I will guard against this by treating roles as normatively structured practices rather than as sociological entities, and by limiting claims about identity to their practical-rational dimension.

Please write this as a polished, formal proposal document. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation suggestions—I will supply those myself. Frame the conceptual distinctions freshly rather than through any existing named taxonomy, since the purpose is to test whether the framing stands independently.
