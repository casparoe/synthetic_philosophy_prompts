After a long argument last night with a friend who insisted that Sartre’s analysis of bad faith is essentially an antiquated precursor to modern research on motivated reasoning and cognitive bias, I tried to outline an essay that resists that reduction. The central claim is that bad faith is not a doxastic failure—an empirical error about one’s own determinism—but an ontological project of the for-itself. I want to focus on the café waiter who plays at being a waiter: he does not merely hold a false belief that he is thing-like, but enacts a false synthesis of facticity and transcendence that attempts to collapse his freedom into an institutional role. The tension I see is that Sartre describes this attitude as involving a kind of sincere faith in one’s own determinism, yet if radical freedom is pre-reflective and inescapable, it seems structurally impossible for consciousness to genuinely take itself as fully determined without simultaneously betraying that freedom in the very act of belief. So I want to reconstruct bad faith not as self-deception in the psychological sense, but as a practical, performative contradiction: one performs determinism while the structure of consciousness necessarily undermines it. I also want to ask whether this interpretation inadvertently makes bad faith unavoidable—since the contradiction is built into self-consciousness rather than culpably chosen—thereby weakening Sartre’s moral condemnation of it. Could you comment on these notes? Specifically, I would value your view on whether the doxastic-error versus performative-contradiction distinction is philosophically defensible in Sartre’s framework, whether I have mischaracterized his use of faith in the bad-faith attitude, and whether there is a more precise way to articulate the relation between facticity and transcendence in social roles without collapsing into the determinism Sartre rejects.
