I have been thinking about how groups reach decisions when they must agree both on the reasons for a choice and on the choice itself, and I have come across an idea—perhaps in a discussion of collective reasoning—that there is a formal difficulty called the discursive dilemma. As I understand it, a group can be made up entirely of individuals who each hold a perfectly consistent view, yet when they vote separately on the premises that lead to a conclusion, the collective results on those premises can combine through logic to imply a conclusion that the group, if it voted directly on that conclusion, might reject. I have tried to imagine a concrete case: a small panel judging whether someone is responsible for an outcome, where each member considers intent, awareness of risk, and direct causation, and where majority votes on each of these three points, taken together, point to responsibility, even though a direct vote on responsibility itself might come out differently.

This led me to think about Condorcet and his jury theorem, which I understand only in rough form: if each member of a group is slightly more likely to be correct than incorrect, then as the group grows larger, the probability that the majority reaches the right answer approaches certainty. That sounds like an optimistic view of collective wisdom. But the discursive dilemma seems to cut the other way, suggesting that organizing collective reasoning through separate judgments on parts can fracture coherence even when no individual is irrational.

What I am trying to understand is what Condorcet himself—given his confidence in probabilistic reasoning, his writings on democratic deliberation, and his assumption that citizens can reason together—would have made of this tension had he lived to see it formalized. Specifically, I wonder whether he would have regarded the discursive dilemma as a genuine threat to collective wisdom, or merely as a puzzle about formal aggregation that real discussion and transparency solve. I also want to ask about a further twist that occurred to me: if people in a deliberative body know in advance that their separate judgments on premises will be fed into a logical rule to determine the collective conclusion, might they be tempted to misstate their true beliefs about a premise in order to manipulate that final outcome? Would Condorcet have anticipated such strategic distortion, and if so, would he have concluded that groups should avoid voting on premises altogether and vote only on final outcomes? Or would he have believed that open reasoning and public accountability make such manipulation impossible?

I have no formal training in logic, mathematics, or political theory, so I am asking from genuine curiosity rather than expertise. I do not want a survey of modern scholars, a list of readings, or citations. I want a reasoned reconstruction of Condorcet's likely stance—how his own commitments about collective reason and democratic judgment would have led him to respond to both the coherence problem and the possibility of strategic premise-voting—written entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a long thoughtful letter.
