I am preparing a conceptual analysis for a course on comparative moral philosophy, and I find myself genuinely puzzled by what happens if one treats Ubuntu not merely as an ethical orientation but as an ontological claim about personhood—specifically, that to be a person is to stand in relations of reciprocal moral recognition within a living community. Please describe the most important implications of adopting this relational conception, rather than a liberal capacity-based one, for three rather specific cases.

First, consider a solitary, self-sustaining conscious being—whether a human astronaut on a multi-generational voyage with no interlocutors, or an artificial agent operating in permanent isolation—who retains full rationality, self-awareness, and emotional depth: does the absence of actual reciprocal relations diminish its personhood, and must the relationalist invoke counterfactual or modal relations to avoid an implausible conclusion? Second, examine restorative justice frameworks that conceptualise harm not as a violation of an individual’s rights but as a rupture in the relational fabric connecting victim, offender, and broader community: what does this imply for the moral permissibility of punitive isolation, and for the obligations of third parties who were not direct participants in the wrong? Third, analyse the case of a being that possesses all standard internal capacities yet is permanently embedded in a coercive, non-reciprocal structure—such as a synthetic worker denied any mutual recognition or a prisoner held in indefinite solitary confinement—asking whether personhood itself can be structurally suppressed even when cognitive and affective capacities remain fully intact. For each domain, identify the implication that is most counter-intuitive from an individualist perspective, and explain why a defender of the relational view should nevertheless embrace it rather than retreat to a hybrid account.
