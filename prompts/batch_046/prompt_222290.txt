I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I'm trying to debug an argument about Pascal's wager that I reconstructed from an old forum thread. It seems to show that rational belief must contain explicit contradictions, which feels wrong, but I can't locate the error. Here is the chain:

(1) Pascal's reasoning can be formalized as a decision rule: if a proposition B carries prior p > 0 and yields utility U = +∞ conditional on belief in B, then any competing belief with finite expected utility is dominated, and rationality requires adopting B.

(2) The rule applies to any B satisfying the condition, not only to one particular theology.

(3) Define a family of reward modules R_1 through R_n. Each R_k promises infinite reward if R_k is believed and assigns -∞ if any R_j (j ≠ k) is believed. Each R_k is internally consistent, and assign each a small prior epsilon_k > 0 with Σ epsilon_k < 1.

(4) By (1), rationality requires adopting belief in R_1, R_2, ..., R_n simultaneously.

(5) But belief is subject to a closure constraint: if you believe R_k, and R_k entails the falsity of R_j, then you must believe R_j is false. Holding all R_k therefore violates logical closure.

(6) So applied consistently, Pascal's rule demands a doxastic state that is explicitly contradictory.

(7) That result is unacceptable: rationality cannot require holding contradictory beliefs.

(8) The argument therefore forces a choice: reject (1) and abandon the wager form; reject (5) and allow belief to be fragmented like isolated database tables with no cross-table consistency; or reject (3) by assigning prior 0 to all but one R_k without independent justification.

I want to preserve (1) if I can, because the core intuition about infinite expected utility seems hard to dismiss. I'd rather pay the price at (5) or (8) than surrender the wager entirely, but I'm unsure what either rejection costs in terms of epistemic coherence. Which step should go? Is there a hidden equivocation between (2) and (4)—perhaps between treating belief adoption as a voluntary action and treating it as a continuous credence assignment? Please diagnose precisely: identify the weakest premise or transition, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives—especially what allowing fragmented belief costs, and whether that rescue actually saves Pascal or just breaks rationality elsewhere.
