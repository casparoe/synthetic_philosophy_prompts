I've been following the long-running disagreement between institutional designers who advocate legislative sortition coupled with strict term limits and those who defend career-based representative democracy, particularly as it has played out in governance-reform discussions within rationalist and effective-altruist circles. The sortitionist position holds that random selection of citizens for limited terms eliminates the perverse incentives of reelection campaigns and severs the long-term relational ties that enable regulatory capture, while the career-democracy position maintains that effective legislation depends on tacit institutional knowledge—something analogous to cognitive apprenticeship—that requires years of sustained engagement with policy detail, bureaucratic procedure, and historical precedent, and that frequent rotation or random assignment produces legislators who are structurally dependent on permanent staff and therefore more vulnerable, not less, to hidden influence. These two camps often talk past each other: one treats corruption primarily as a function of tenure, the other treats incompetence primarily as a function of inexperience, with neither side clearly conceding whether expertise is genuinely non-transferable or whether capture is genuinely reducible by rotation.

Please write the joint statement that a committed sortitionist and a committed defender of career politics could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims about the empirical prevalence of regulatory capture, the observable competence differences between novice and experienced legislators, the compensatory role of staff and institutional archives under high turnover, and the outcomes observed in jurisdictions that have experimented with citizen assemblies or strict term limits. Follow this with a disagreements table in which each camp's position is formulated precisely in wording its own members would accept: the sortitionist view that the epistemic costs of rotation are overstated and can be managed through structured staff support and transparent archives, versus the career-politics view that the anti-corruption benefits of rotation are illusory because influence simply shifts to permanent bureaucrats and well-resourced staff. Then identify the cruxes—specifically, whether legislative expertise is genuinely tacit and time-dependent or largely codifiable and transferable, and whether observed correlations between tenure and corruption survive controls for selection effects and jurisdiction-level confounders. For each crux, propose a concrete institutional comparison or empirical pattern both sides agree would be diagnostic—such as comparing policy-error or amendment-quality metrics in term-limited versus non-term-limited state legislatures, or examining whether outputs from randomly selected citizen bodies show systematically different patterns of industry-aligned provisions than those from long-tenured parliamentary committees—along with each side's explicit prediction of what that comparison will show. The document should be constructive, not adjudicative: neither side should concede its core view, but both should endorse the common ground and sign the exact residual disagreement. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the reasoning should stand on its own.
