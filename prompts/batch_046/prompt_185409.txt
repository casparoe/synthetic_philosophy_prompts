Einstein's hole argument — that general covariance allows one to slide the metric and matter fields within a region with fixed boundary conditions, producing mathematically distinct but empirically identical solutions — has been replayed in philosophy of physics since its modern revival: one camp holds that this genuinely threatens determinism for any view that grants spacetime points independent identity, while the other camp holds that the threat dissolves once we recognize that diffeomorphism-related models represent a single physical situation, leaving determinism intact. Despite decades of exchange, the literature feels stalled; the same examples, the same distinction between active and passive diffeomorphisms, and the same charge of question-begging recur without movement. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not really about determinism at all, but about a hidden disagreement over whether spacetime points carry intrinsic identity conditions or are purely structurally individuated — a metaphysical divide that neither side argues for but both presuppose — and that the two camps are therefore answering different questions under one heading: one formal, about representational redundancy in the mathematics; one ontological, about what it takes for a point to be the same point across models.
