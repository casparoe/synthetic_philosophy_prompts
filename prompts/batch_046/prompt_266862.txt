I've been trying to work through a tension in Nietzsche's physiological turn, particularly his writings on diet, digestion, and climate as conditions of thought. He seems to claim that philosophical positions are not free-standing arguments but symptoms of bodily health — that a philosopher's diet, metabolism, and physical environment shape what he finds convincing. He apparently criticized the heavy German diet and praised lighter Italian-style eating, and he linked his own migraines and digestive troubles to the shape of his philosophy, even suggesting that questions of nutrition lie closer to the root of existence than the highest metaphysical problems. A friend formalized this into an argument that feels like it collapses into self-refutation:

(1) For Nietzsche, every philosophy is essentially a symptom of the philosopher's underlying physiological state; concepts express the health or sickness of the body that produces them, including conditions shaped by diet and digestion.

(2) If philosophical claims are symptoms of bodily states, then assessing a philosophy requires assessing the health of the body behind it; an unhealthy body produces an unreliable philosophy, regardless of the arguments offered.

(3) Therefore, to reject any philosophical position, it is sufficient to show that its author suffered from poor health or followed a damaging dietary regimen.

(4) Nietzsche himself endured chronic migraines, progressive blindness, and severe digestive disorders, and he experimented with strict dietary rules — avoiding alcohol and heavy foods, favoring light meals associated with Italian cuisine, and treating nourishment as prior even to questions of eternal recurrence.

(5) By the criterion in (2) and (3), Nietzsche's own philosophy is a product of sickness and should be dismissed.

(6) Moreover, since every philosopher has some bodily limitation, applying this criterion generally dissolves philosophical reasoning into medical diagnosis and makes rational assessment impossible.

(7) So Nietzsche's physiological reduction either undermines itself or proves too much.

My friend insists the escape is to weaken (2): bodily conditions influence philosophy without determining its epistemic status. But I wonder whether that weakening is genuinely available to Nietzsche, given how strongly he ranks thinkers by the vitality they express and how explicitly he treats philosophical systems as confessions of organic need. Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Can I preserve the idea that diet and digestion genuinely shape thinking — that philosophy grows out of the stomach as much as out of reason — without turning philosophical evaluation into gastroenterology?
