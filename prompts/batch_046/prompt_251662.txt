I am a verification engineer for distributed physics simulations, and I must repeatedly decide whether two tracked entities in a sparse virtual environment are one object duplicated by error or genuinely two distinct objects. Write a reusable decision procedure grounded in Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and in the relational counterexample Max Black raised with two symmetric spheres in an otherwise empty universe. Structure it as an ordered sequence of tests—intrinsic properties, spatial-relational properties, temporal persistence, and relational properties to other entities—specifying for each what counts as passing or failing, where it returns a definitive verdict of "same entity" or "two entities," and where it halts and requires human judgment because the distinction turns on a metaphysical commitment rather than an observable fact. The procedure should be runnable without external reference databases, should note which steps are judgment calls disguised as checks, and should flag the conditions under which it will misfire. Apply it to three cases: (1) two spheres with identical internal states separated by two meters in an otherwise empty space; (2) two spheres with identical internal states initialized at different times but now occupying the exact same coordinates; and (3) two spheres that differ only in that one rotates about an axis with no external frame of reference. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will dislike—most likely forcing me to treat observationally identical tracks as distinct entities—and state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle.
