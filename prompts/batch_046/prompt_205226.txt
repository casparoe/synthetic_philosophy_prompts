Exam question:
Consider the epistemological problems of bootstrapping and so-called “easy knowledge.” The bootstrapping difficulty arises when an agent uses the outputs of a cognitive faculty to justify that very faculty’s reliability, apparently without independent support. A prominent variant is the problem of easy knowledge: if a subject knows an ordinary empirical proposition (for instance, that she presently has hands), and she also knows the relevant entailment (that having hands implies she is not a handless brain in a vat being deceived by a simulator), then by a closure principle for knowledge she can come to know she is not a brain in a vat—despite having done nothing that specifically addresses the skeptical scenario. Some philosophers treat this result as a reductio of closure; others treat it as showing that anti-skeptical knowledge is more accessible than it seems, but only if we avoid vicious circularity in justifying our faculties.

Now consider the “amortized warrant” reply recently introduced in the literature. On this view, warrant for a belief-forming process is not a token-level property that must be independently certified for each judgment; rather, it is a distributed, empirically accumulated resource. The reliability of a mechanism is tracked over time through cross-modal checks, predictive success, and intervention—channels that do not depend on assuming the truth of any particular perceptual deliverance. Once this distributed warrant is in place, particular closure-based inferences from ordinary knowledge to anti-skeptical conclusions are legitimate: they draw on an already-established base rather than generating warrant ex nihilo.

Write an essay addressing three tasks: (a) Explain why the bootstrapping objection is especially pressing in the easy-knowledge case involving skeptical hypotheses and closure. (b) Evaluate whether the amortized-warrant reply genuinely dissolves the circularity, or whether it merely relocates it—pay particular attention to whether cross-modal verification can be genuinely independent when a global skeptical hypothesis (e.g., universal simulation) threatens all sensory modalities simultaneously. (c) Reach and defend a reasoned verdict: is the problem solved, benign but unsolved, or symptomatic of a deeper defect in reliabilist or closure-based epistemology?

Student’s answer:
The bootstrapping problem occurs when you try to prove your eyes work by using your eyes. In the easy-knowledge case, this gets worse because of closure. If I know I have hands, and I know that hands mean no brain-in-a-vat, then closure says I know I’m not a brain in a vat. But I never actually checked for the vat; I just used my hands-knowledge, which comes from the same eyes I’m trying to justify. Stewart Cohen argued in his work on easy knowledge that this shows closure is false—if we keep closure, we let people bootstrap themselves into anti-skeptical knowledge for free.

Jonathan Vogel disagrees, but he also rejects closure, saying that knowledge doesn’t always transmit across entailments. Actually, Vogel’s real point is that you can’t use a source to vouch for itself, which is the bootstrapping problem in a different form. So both Cohen and Vogel end up denying closure to stop the bootstrapping.

The amortized-warrant model tries to save closure by saying warrant is like a bank account. You build it up over time by checking vision against touch and sound, so you don’t need to check every single belief. Because touch and hearing use different brain pathways, they are completely independent of vision. That means the circularity is broken: vision’s reliability is confirmed by non-visual sources, and then closure can safely take me from hands to no-vat.

But there is still a problem. The model says warrant is distributed, so I don’t need token-level justification. That solves both the faculty-level bootstrapping and the token-level easy knowledge in one move. Once the account is funded, any withdrawal—like my hands-belief—carries full warrant automatically.

So I think the amortized reply mostly works. The cross-modal checks are independent enough, and distributing warrant avoids circularity at both levels. The remaining worry is just practical: we don’t actually do all that checking. But philosophically, easy knowledge is solved and closure can stay. The deeper flaw, if there is one, lies not in reliabilism but in expecting knowledge to be earned one belief at a time.
