Two colleagues in a philosophy-of-logic reading group have been arguing about whether the Löbian obstacle can be circumvented by replacing a binary modal self-trust framework with a graded, Bayesian architecture. I was present as a bystander, do not favor either side, and want a point-by-point adjudication of the actual arguments made.

The first colleague—call her A—argued that the classic obstacle depends essentially on a discrete modal operator enforcing exact equality in fixed-point conditions. If an agent instead assigns real-valued credences and applies a self-trust rule formulated as an expectation constraint—namely, that its present credence in p should align with its expectation of the credence its future reasoning will assign—then the derivation that forces acceptance of arbitrary propositions collapses. Approximate equality in a continuous space, she said, breaks the discrete iteration driving the explosion, so a bounded probabilistic agent can coherently trust its future reasoning without inconsistency.

The second colleague—B—replied that this merely disguises the obstacle. Inside the expectation operator, he said, lies the same modal structure: if the agent expects its future self to reach credence above some threshold in p, and its update rule treats that expectation as evidential, then iteration produces a probabilistic analog of Löb’s derivation. The agent is forced to push current credence toward 1 for any proposition it expects itself to eventually endorse, including contradictions arising from reasoning errors. Grading belief, he insisted, transforms an all-or-nothing explosion into gradual high-confidence drift toward contradiction without eliminating the self-reference loop.

A responded that B conflates conditioning on a future belief-state with treating that state as an oracle. She proposed a hierarchical model in which the agent regards its future reasoning as a noisy instrument, updating via a structure closer to Jeffrey conditioning on observed outputs with explicit error parameters, rather than identifying present credence with the content of its future belief-state. Because the agent maintains structural separation between its present epistemic state and the data generated by future reasoning, there is no direct self-reference loop of the kind Löb’s theorem requires. The obstacle is avoided, she claimed, not by weakening logic but by restructuring the epistemic architecture.

B countered that this restructuring merely relocates the problem. Any hierarchical model still requires a parameter—call it a trust coefficient—that determines how heavily the present agent weights outputs from its future self. If that coefficient is itself subject to reflective adjustment—if the agent updates it based on expectations about what coefficient its future self will use—then the same modal loop re-emerges at the meta-level. If the coefficient is fixed exogenously, then the agent is not genuinely self-trusting; it relies on an external anchor breaking reflective closure. So, B concluded, either the Löbian obstacle returns at the parameter level, or the agent abandons full reflective self-trust altogether.

Please go through this exchange step by step and adjudicate who is right about each specific claim, what errors or omissions each party makes, and whether a stable middle position exists that resolves the tension without either giving up reflective self-trust or accepting the explosion. In particular, address whether A’s hierarchical-noise model genuinely breaks the modal loop or merely hides it, and whether B’s trust-coefficient argument succeeds in showing that any reflective closure must eventually regenerate the obstacle.
