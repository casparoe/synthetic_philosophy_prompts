I am preparing a podcast interview for a general audience, and my guest is a philosopher who argues that heuristic reasoning—whether analogical arguments in ethics or abductive judgments in science—depends on a hidden presumption of independence between the relevant variables, and that we can only validate or reject a heuristic by formalizing that presumption. I have no academic background, but I have been turning this idea over for months on my own. Write me a sequence of interview questions that goes well beyond asking him to restate the view. The sequence should include: first, a question that asks how he distinguishes a legitimate independence assumption from a hidden correlation we have not yet discovered, followed by a reply that anticipates his likely answer about predictive tracking and then asks what he makes of cases where two competing heuristics, each with its own independence assumption, both succeed in different domains; second, a question about whether the formal apparatus he proposes—probabilistic representations or structural models—is itself justified only through further heuristic reasoning, with a follow-up that presses him if he denies circularity by asking whether that denial already assumes the very independence he seeks to ground; and third, a closing question about the practical implication for someone like me, who relies on intuition without technical training: does formalizing independence make gut judgment more trustworthy, or does it show that untrained intuition is always vulnerable to hidden dependencies? For this last one, include a follow-up keyed to his most probable response, that intuition can be calibrated by context without full formalization, asking how that calibration avoids importing an unexamined independence claim. I want the questions arranged so that by the end he must either defend a circular justification or admit that some heuristics lie permanently outside his framework.
