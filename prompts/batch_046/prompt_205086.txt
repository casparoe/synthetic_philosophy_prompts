I am drafting a proposal for a six-month interdisciplinary workshop grant on the meta-problem of consciousness—specifically, on why phenomenal consciousness appears to pose a distinctive explanatory difficulty. The standard literature, whether deflationary, illusionist, or accommodationist, treats the meta-problem as a uniform phenomenon: explaining why intelligent agents generate hard-problem judgments about consciousness in general. I want to argue that this framing overlooks a structurally distinct component I will call the distribution problem. Our explanatory-gap intuitions are not uniformly distributed: they are intense for ourselves, variable across non-human animals, weak for artificial systems, and absent for simple reflex arcs. Any adequate meta-theory must therefore explain not only why the gap seems present, but why it seems present in precisely these gradations, without presupposing an independent first-order solution to the hard problem. The precise research question is this: can the graded distribution of hard-problem intuitions be explained by models of resource-bounded agents that generate explanatory expectations from structural similarity and causal transparency rather than from phenomenal presence? And if uniform-distribution meta-accounts fail to predict these gradations, does that show that the meta-problem and the distribution problem must be solved together rather than separately? The project will proceed in three stages. First, I will taxonomize existing meta-accounts—cognitive-closure views, phenomenal-concept strategies, and deflationary error theories—according to whether they entail uniform or graded intuitive outputs. Second, I will construct minimal formal models of agents with bounded representational resources that assign explanatory difficulty based on accessibility of internal causal structure, testing whether these models reproduce the observed gradation patterns without invoking phenomenal properties. Third, I will assess whether the results dissolve the hard problem, relocate it to a theory of cognitive architecture, or leave a genuine residual that requires first-order consciousness science. A successful deliverable will be a framework allowing consciousness researchers to predict where explanatory-gap intuitions will arise without committing to a specific ontology of consciousness. The project fails if the graded patterns turn out to be fully derivable from general psychological biases—such as anthropomorphism or agent-detection heuristics—with no specific connection to consciousness, in which case the distribution problem collapses into general cognitive psychology and the framing is redundant. The scope is realistic for six months: months one and two for taxonomy, months three and four for model construction, and months five and six for comparative assessment and a workshop white paper. I also need an honest risk assessment: the framing may prove too ambitious if constructing the minimal models requires substantive empirical commitments about neural or computational architecture that lie outside philosophy proper, or if the distinction between the meta-problem and the distribution problem turns out to be unstable under analysis. Please write the proposal with a sharply stated question, an explanation of why current positions leave the distribution problem open, a month-by-month method, the possible conclusions and what considerations would decide among them, a description of the deliverable, and a frank evaluation of what could make the project fail or exceed the grant period. Do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation; I will supply references myself.
