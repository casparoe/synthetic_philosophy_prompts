I convene a 120-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of physics with eight participants. Design a structured, rules-based simulation—using only printed grids, named cards, tokens, and a transparent overlay—that makes the group experience the hole argument from the inside before anyone names it. The activity should have three phases. In Phase 1, participants assign proper names to specific grid intersections and build a relational map of events (spatial distances, causal links, and assigned field values) tied to those named points; they should feel that they are tracking genuine individuals and should be encouraged to make predictions about where named point Alpha will lie after an anticipated shift. In Phase 2, introduce an unannounced transformation rule: the facilitator slides the transparent overlay relative to the named cards, copies all relational and field data exactly onto the new overlay positions, and leaves the original cards unmoved. Participants must then place token bets on whether Alpha now coincides with coordinate position (3,4) or (5,2) relative to the fixed sheet. In Phase 3, reveal that every physical measurement and relational fact remains identical across the two configurations, yet every bet is unresolvable because nothing in the data distinguishes one assignment from the other; the participants should discover this failure through their own conflicting wagers, not through explanation. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact token payoff structure, rules that prevent the facilitator from speaking during Phase 2, and instructions for handling the predictable confusion when participants insist that the cards “must have moved.” Then include a debrief that maps Phase 1 onto substantivalist commitments, Phase 2 onto active diffeomorphisms, and Phase 3 onto the metric underdetermination known as the hole; state clearly which feature—the formal mathematical statement of the hole, or the distinction between bare manifold points and physical events—the simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally. Finally, add a brief fourth-round deliberation prompt that asks the group whether an analogous “hole” arises if particles are treated as unnamed nodes in a purely relational quantum-graph ontology, so that the problem is shown to travel beyond general relativity. Write your response in British English, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid relying on obscure technical literature that could not be reconstructed without search.
