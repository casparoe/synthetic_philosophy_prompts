I am a journalist who writes long-form features at the intersection of social science and public policy, and I need a reusable editorial procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit stopping rules—that translates the philosophical tension between methodological individualism and methodological holism into a practical framing decision I can run in about fifteen minutes before filing. The phenomenon I cover—whether a drop in violent crime, a pilot study on cognitive-behavioral recidivism, or a regional obesity epidemic—can almost always be narrated through individual-level choices, traits, and behaviors, or through collective-level structures, institutions, and emergent dynamics; I want the procedure to tell me which framing the explanatory logic actually supports, where it demands that I stop and choose one, and where it must hand the decision back to me because the remaining dispute is normative rather than epistemic.

The procedure should contain, in sequence: (1) a set of diagnostic questions about aggregation—whether the observed pattern is a simple sum of independent individual actions or requires reference to relational properties, feedback loops, or downward causation—together with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (2) explicit stopping conditions that mandate an individual-level framing, mandate a holistic framing, or declare the case genuinely underdetermined; (3) a brief conceptual test, drawn from the philosophy of social science without requiring mastery of its literature, that distinguishes compositional explanation (individual parts arranged differently) from emergent explanation (system-level properties that constrain the parts), with clear markers for when I am masking one behind the other; (4) identification of hard cases where the procedure yields an answer I will not like—specifically, where the data and logic push toward a framing that conflicts with the human-interest angle, the available sources, or the ethical posture of the story; and (5) clearly flagged judgment points that no procedure can eliminate, especially where attributing causation to individuals risks moralizing structural harm, or where attributing causation to structures risks erasing agency that is genuinely present.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three hypothetical feature subjects: a forty-person pilot study finding that a narrowly defined cognitive-behavioral intervention reduces recidivism, proposed as the basis for a statewide policy feature; a rapid, documented decline in a city’s opioid-related mortality that coincides with both a harm-reduction policy change and an unmeasured shift in the local drug supply; and a proposed feature framing a documented regional rise in obesity primarily through individual dietary choice and willpower, despite extensive epidemiological evidence linking the rise to food-environment restructuring and supply-chain dynamics. For each, show the procedure’s output, note where it hands the decision to me on value grounds rather than explanatory grounds, and state precisely what the procedure cannot do—particularly where it might misfire by forcing a false dichotomy, by treating statistical correlation at the aggregate level as proof of holistic causation, or by allowing me to invoke “emergence” as a rhetorical escape from specifying the actual micro-macro mechanism.

The deliverable should be a single reusable workflow, honest about its misfires and its limits, and it should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I need it to function with my current expertise and under real deadline constraints, without requiring me to search for sources.
