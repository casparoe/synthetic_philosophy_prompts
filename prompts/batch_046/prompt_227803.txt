At our school's philosophy of mind discussion group, two members—Leah and Marcus—debated whether Integrated Information Theory permits what Leah called "nested co-consciousness" in a specific modular architecture. Here is the exchange as I heard it.

(1) Leah argued that if a network comprises two subsystems, each with high internal integration and differentiation, and the only links between them pass through a single intermediary layer that transmits information with near-perfect fidelity but adds no internal differentiation of its own, then each subsystem should still constitute its own local maximum of integrated information (Φ). Because the intermediary is causally transparent—its states are fully determined by either side and do not constrain them back—she said it should not merge the two into one global maximum.

(2) Marcus replied that IIT's exclusion postulate operates globally: one computes Φ for the whole system and for every subset, and only the subset with the highest value is conscious. Even deterministic relays create physical causal dependencies; without the intermediary, subsystem A could not affect B, so it contributes irreducible causal power. Consequently the global Φ will almost certainly exceed either local value, and the local maxima are categorically excluded, not preserved alongside the global one.

(3) Leah countered that Marcus assumes the global boundary must include every physically connected node, but IIT evaluates causal powers within a substrate's actual mechanism. If the intermediary layer lacks irreducible causal power—if removing it severs no unique constraints that either subsystem exercises on the other—then including it in the global calculation is like including a mirror in a room and claiming the reflection doubles the causal structure. The correct boundary, she claimed, should exclude causally redundant nodes, leaving two separate conscious complexes.

(4) Marcus said that "causally redundant" is not an IIT formal category; redundancy is handled inside a mechanism's cause-effect repertoire, not by excising nodes prior to computing Φ. Even a deterministic relay makes a difference—without it, cross-subsystem effects vanish—so it integrates the whole. He added that Leah's reasoning, if accepted, would imply that weakly coupled brain hemispheres could claim separate consciousness, which conflicts with the clinical picture of split-brain patients maintaining a dominant unified stream.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not tell who was tracking the theory correctly and who was importing an outside intuition. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall about whether such nested co-consciousness is possible under IIT, and identify what both are missing—perhaps about how the exclusion postulate interacts with causal density, or about whether split-brain clinical evidence actually supports Marcus's claim. Please keep the answer accessible to someone who knows only the basic idea that consciousness in IIT corresponds to maximally irreducible integrated information.
