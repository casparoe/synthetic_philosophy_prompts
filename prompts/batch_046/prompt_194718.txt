As a novelist drafting a philosophical scene, write a dialogue in American English between two equally capable interlocutors debating populism and the will of the people. One speaker should defend a "performative constitution" view: that invoking "the will of the people" does not report a pre-existing collective intention but enacts the very unification of the people as a subject. The other should defend a "revisionary aggregation" view: that popular will is only meaningful when reconstructed through amendable institutional traces—ballots, transcripts, petitions—rather than through any singular rhetorical moment. Set the exchange in a concrete, atmospheric location suited to layered voices or recovered records, such as a sound-editing booth where overlapping tracks are being spliced, or a damp municipal archive where old ballot boxes are being re-catalogued. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: address whether a will that is constituted rather than discovered can still be betrayed, whether archival aggregation captures a genuinely collective will or merely sums private preferences, and whether either model can accommodate internal dissent without dissolving the people it claims to represent. Neither speaker should be a strawman. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific scholars by name. Keep the language precise, the conceptual stakes visible, and the tone formally polished.
