I'm designing a graduate seminar rubric for evaluating philosophical accounts of state punishment, and I want the criteria made explicit before students turn to comparing familiar doctrines. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of why the state may intentionally inflict suffering on offenders must deliver. For each requirement, specify the concrete case or conceptual tension that motivates it—such as the categorical wrongness of punishing the innocent, the intuition that severity should track culpability rather than optimal deterrence alone, the need to distinguish punishment from mere coercion, taxation, or quarantine, and the demand that punishment expresses communal condemnation rather than only producing future benefits. Be sure to include a desideratum addressing whether the theory can accommodate non-individualized or vicarious penal burdens—e.g., corporate criminal liability or collective sanctions—without collapsing them into purely instrumental trade-offs, and flag at least one pair of desiderata you take to be genuinely in tension, especially where expressive or retributive demands conflict with institutional feasibility or with Kantian constraints on using persons merely as means. Then, briefly, score pure retributivism, pure consequentialism (centered on deterrence and rehabilitation), and a hybrid expressive-retributive view against your list, noting precisely where each must compromise. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
