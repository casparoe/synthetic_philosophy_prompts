As a novelist composing a narrative about competing witnesses, I keep returning to the parable of the blind men and the elephant—each grasps a tusk, an ear, or a flank and declares the beast a spear, a fan, or a wall. In philosophical discussions of Jain anekāntavāda, this image is regularly invoked to defend the doctrine of manifoldness: reality is irreducibly complex, every naya or standpoint captures only one facet, and dogmatic one-sidedness is the error of mistaking a part for the whole. I want to audit this analogy with precision, because my characters must inhabit partial perspectives without collapsing into simple relativism.

Set out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the elephant, the blind men, the tactile encounter, the absence of sight, and—crucially—the sighted observer or king who, in many versions of the tale, adjudicates that each speaker is partly correct? Then trace the fractures. Does the analogy presuppose a single, unified elephant existing prior to and independent of the partial grasps, and if so, does that commit anekāntavāda to a hidden essentialism that its own doctrine of non-absolutism rejects? Does the presence of a sighted adjudicator smuggle in a transcendent, God-like viewpoint that Jain epistemology, with its reliance on syādvāda—the conditional mode of predication—cannot accommodate? How does the analogy treat the sevenfold conditional structure known as saptabhaṅgī—where an object may be said to be, not to be, both, neither, and so on in qualified form—when each blind man asserts only one unconditional predicate?

Next, examine what values the analogy quietly imports about parts and wholes: does it privilege the integrity of the complete elephant over the legitimacy of each tactile claim, and does that map onto a hierarchical ordering of naya? Does epistemic humility belong to the individual witness or only to the collective assembly? Finally, tell me whether the conclusion usually licensed—that all perspectives are equally valid fragments of one truth—survives these disanalogies, or whether the repaired analogy actually supports syādvāda: not that every view is correct, but that every unconditional assertion is necessarily incomplete. If the image breaks irreparably, propose a different analogical structure—perhaps drawn from narrative form itself—that could carry the philosophical burden without importing the hidden ontology of the elephant.
