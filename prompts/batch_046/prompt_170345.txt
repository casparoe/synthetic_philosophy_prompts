In a study-group discussion on the paradox of phenomenal judgment, two participants argued over whether epiphenomenalism undermines itself. The first claimed that if conscious states are causally inert, then the judgment that one is conscious is not caused by the conscious state itself, rendering it unreliable—including the judgment that epiphenomenalism is true. The second replied that direct acquaintance justifies the belief without requiring causal efficacy, so the argument confuses causation with justification. The first then sharpened the point: even with acquaintance, the brain state that actually produces the judgment is a neural correlate, not the experience, so the judgment concerns a state that plays no causal role in generating it, which threatens to reduce acquaintance to representational fiction. The second countered that acquaintance is not representational and needs no causal contribution from its object to be authoritative. They asked me to adjudicate the dispute. For each step—causal inertness, the acquaintance reply, and the fiction objection—say which party is right and where, if anywhere, both are missing something, particularly regarding whether cognitive phenomenology raises different issues than sensory phenomenology, or whether self-knowledge necessarily requires causal efficacy at all.
