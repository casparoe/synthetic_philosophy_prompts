In philosophical discussions of Darwin's evolutionary account of morality — particularly his argument that the moral sense arises from social instincts and sympathy graded continuously with animal behavior — there is a persistent stall between interpreters who treat him as undermining moral objectivity, since an evolved sentiment is merely an adaptive fiction, and those who treat him as naturalizing normative progress without deflating it. Both camps agree on the biological texts and have not moved in decades. Is this disagreement mainly about what evolutionary explanations can justify, a verbal confusion over whether natural origin entails non-objectivity, or an unarguable clash of bedrock intuitions about whether any normative claim can survive being fully explained by evolutionary history? Given that diagnosis, what kind of move — a conceptual distinction, a historical clarification about Darwin's religious agnosticism, or a formal argument — could actually shift it?
