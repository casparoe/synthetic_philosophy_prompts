Consider the following verdicts delivered by Professor K, a philosopher of aesthetics, on whether and when aesthetic testimony can justify belief about a work:

1. A lay acquaintance describes an unseen painting as profoundly moving. Professor K rules that this does not justify belief in its beauty; non-experts cannot transmit aesthetic value.
2. A respected art historian describes the same unseen painting as profoundly moving. She rules that one may justifiably adopt the belief, though direct encounter remains necessary for full appreciation.
3. You view the painting and find it moving; the historian later calls it kitsch. She rules that you should maintain your judgment—direct aesthetic experience takes precedence over expert contradiction on matters of pure taste.
4. You view the painting and feel nothing; the historian explains its negative-space composition and asserts that this formal feature makes it beautiful. She rules that you should revise your judgment and accept the claim; expert analysis of structural properties corrects an untrained response.
5. You read a poem in a language you do not understand; a poet-translator explains its intricate metrical structure. She rules that belief in its formal beauty is justified.
6. You attend a live concert; afterward, a musicologist who was not present describes the emotional trajectory of the performance based solely on the score. She rules that this claim should be rejected—testimony about the lived emotional arc of a specific performance requires shared presence.

Now consider a new case. You experience an immersive installation that combines visual projection, temperature modulation, and scent diffusion. You have normal sight and thermal sensation, but congenital anosmia, so you have no olfactory access whatsoever. The perfumer who designed the scent—possessing deep technical knowledge but no institutional art-world authority—describes the scent profile as the dominant emotional driver of the piece and urges you to judge the work beautiful primarily because of it. The museum's chief curator, an acknowledged authority who experienced the full installation, asserts that the scent is merely ambient and that the aesthetic achievement lies entirely in the visual-thermal interplay. You personally find the visual-thermal elements striking, but naturally register nothing about the scent.

Predict Professor K's verdict: should you accept the perfumer's claim about the scent's dominance, defer to the curator's overall framing, maintain your own visual-thermal judgment, or adopt some combination? Identify the competing rules—perhaps an access-and-competence principle versus an institutional-authority principle, or others—that equally accommodate verdicts 1 through 6. State what each rule predicts about this case, defend the prediction you think she would actually make, and specify one further hypothetical case that would cleanly distinguish the rules you have identified.

Write your answer in American English. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
