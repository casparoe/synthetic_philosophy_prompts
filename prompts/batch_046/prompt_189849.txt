I've been chewing on the old question of why promises actually bind, and the usual consequentialist-vs-deontologist framing feels stale to me. I think there's something to the idea that a promise isn't just a speech act that creates an obligation — it's more like a credible commitment device that restructures your own decision-space across time, especially when there's social recognition that makes backing out structurally costly for your future self. But I'm not sure how far that gets me.

Here are some verdicts I have, and they're not all clean:

- If I tell a friend I'll help them move Saturday and then I don't, I'm clearly bound — I owe them more than an apology, there's a real debt there.
- If I privately resolve to write 500 words every morning and tell no one, that feels like a goal, not a promise. If I fail, I'm disappointed but I haven't wronged anyone, including my past self. So that doesn't bind.
- But if I post on my blog that I'm donating 10% of income to GiveWell-style causes for a year, that binds even though the "victim" is diffuse. The public structure changes what my future self can do without recieveing massive social cost.
- I promised my dying grandfather I'd keep the family farm running. Its now bleeding money and my kids need the capital. I still think I'm bound — not because he can be disappointed, but because breaking it collapses a kind of legacy-structure I definately adopted.
- My boss threatened to fire me unless I promised to cover weekends for a month. I said yes. That doesn't bind — it was never a real restructuring, just coercion wearing promise-clothing.
- Here's the pair that bugs me: I promised my partner I'd always be honest, but now total honesty would destroy the relationship for no compensating good. I feel released — the commitment was meant to stabilize the relationship, not blow it up. But I also promised my father on his deathbed not to sell the failing business, and even though selling saves my family, I think I'm still bound there. Both involve changed circumstances and harm to living people, but my verdicts split.

Is there a single principle behind all this? Maybe "promises bind when they function as freely adopted, socially mediated commitment devices that restructure intertemporal coordination"? But that seems to miss the grandfather case and maybe over-explain the partner case. Or maybe I have to drop one verdict.

What principle, if any, captures these? And what does it say about weird edge cases — like promises made to an AI assistant that I then tell my friends about, or promises where the other party has completely forgotten? I want something precise, not just "it depends."

Also, do I have to drop either the partner-release verdict or the father-binding verdict? They feel equally real but they point opposite directions.
