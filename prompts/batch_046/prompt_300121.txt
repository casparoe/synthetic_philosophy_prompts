I have just finished a long and exhausting argument with a friend about Erwin Schrödinger's later metaphysical writings, specifically his adoption of Advaita Vedanta and his claim that the multiplicity of conscious minds is an illusion produced by a single, universal consciousness. My friend argues that this is not philosophy but poetic mysticism, and that it fails entirely to account for the phenomenological privacy and specificity of individual experience—why, for instance, my headache is not yours, or why memory and mental deficit seem tightly correlated with particular brains. I tried to defend Schrödinger by distinguishing the empirical ego, which is indeed localized and private, from the transcendental consciousness that underlies it, but my friend countered that this distinction merely relocates the problem rather than solving it, leaving us with a kind of universal solipsism that cannot explain intersubjective disagreement or the boundaries of awareness.

Write an essay that addresses the following controversy: does Schrödinger's Vedantic monism provide a coherent philosophical resolution to the problem of other minds, or does it inevitably collapse into an untenable solipsism that cannot accommodate the empirical reality of separate, embodied perspectives?

In your essay, weigh the strongest objections—particularly the charge that a unitary consciousness cannot explain the boundaries of individual awareness, the correlation between brain damage and mental deficit, and the first-person accessibility of experience—against the resources available within Schrödinger's own framework, including his insistence that physical multiplicity is an appearance rather than an ultimate reality and his analogies comparing individual minds to divisions of a single underlying source. You should also examine the tension between this metaphysical view and his earlier scientific work on biological organization and negentropy, in which organisms appear as distinct, self-maintaining systems with clear physical boundaries. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: either that Schrödinger's view succeeds in dissolving the problem of other minds at an acceptable philosophical cost, or that the cost—whether solipsism, the denial of empirical science, or an irreducible ambiguity about personal identity—is too high.
