I've noticed a recurring structure in practical reasoning that I can't map cleanly onto the standard taxonomy, and I'm looking for the precise carving—whether there's an established label or whether the edges have to be drawn fresh. Consider two concrete cases.

First, a person enters clinical medicine with no prior desire to heal others and little tolerance for long training. Over fifteen years the work genuinely reshapes her motivational psychology: she comes to experience patient welfare as an internal source of motivation that feels entirely her own. When she explains why she made the original choice, she points to this now-deep commitment as if it were the reason she had all along, even though it did not exist before she entered the field.

Second, someone adopts an austere monastic regimen with no prior inclination toward asceticism. The practice gradually cultivates a stable, authentic preference for simplicity that he experiences as genuinely internal. He then defends the initial adoption by citing that cultivated preference, treating it as a reason that validates the first step rather than as a product of it.

In both cases, a commitment appears to generate the very internal reasons that are then used to justify it. These reasons are not mere post-hoc rationalizations, because the agents genuinely hold them; but they are not pre-existing internal reasons, and they do not seem like fully external reasons that could motivate anyone regardless of psychology. I want to know whether this specific configuration—call it retroactive internalization—has an established name in the philosophy of practical reason.

If it does: how does it relate to the internal/external reasons distinction, and what separates it from adaptive preference formation, mere rationalization, or transformative experience? If it does not: please coin a precise term, define it with necessary and sufficient conditions, and explain how it differs from neighboring phenomena such as Stockholm syndrome and value acquisition through socialization.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

2. The disanalogies (approximately 200 words): for each concept identified in section 1, specify exactly where the mapping breaks down with respect to the two instances.

3. Taxonomy and proposed term (approximately 200 words): state clearly whether an exact term exists. If not, propose a name and a formal definition that includes boundary conditions, plus one case that clearly falls inside and one that clearly falls outside.

4. Philosophical stakes (approximately 150 words): explain whether this pattern poses a distinctive problem for internalist theories of reasons, or whether it can be accommodated within an internalist framework without collapsing into externalism.

The point is not a bare label; I want the carving—what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
