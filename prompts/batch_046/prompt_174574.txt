You serve as the senior editorial-standards officer for a mid-size investigative magazine that publishes roughly twenty-five submissions per week, mixing reported news, expert analysis, and opinion. Your job is not to censor controversial content but to prevent the magazine from putting its institutional authority behind claims that exceed the epistemic position of the author. You need a reusable decision procedure—grounded in the philosophical debate over norms of assertion, specifically whether an assertion requires knowledge, mere belief, truth, or some intermediate epistemic status—that a non-philosopher editor can run in about ten minutes per sentence-level claim. The procedure should determine whether a claim may stand as a flat assertion, must be downgraded to a qualified report or expert attribution, or must be rejected for revision.

Structure the procedure as an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions. For each step, specify: the exact question to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed; the stopping point that yields a mandatory “no” (rejection or required revision); and the point where the procedure hands the decision to human judgment because the issue is institutional values rather than epistemic fact. You must preserve, and not collapse into simpler language, the distinction between an author’s internal epistemic entitlement and the audience’s licensed belief—treat this “authority–entitlement gap” as the central failure mode the procedure is designed to detect. The procedure should apply regardless of section (news, analysis, opinion), though the weight given to author expertise may vary by context; flag exactly where that contextual weighting enters and who must sign off on it.

Then run the procedure on three manuscript excerpts:

(i) A reported piece states, “The agency director knew the data had been altered before signing the memo,” sourced only to two anonymous staff members who did not witness the signing;

(ii) An opinion essay states, “It is undeniable that current carbon-offset markets violate intergenerational justice,” written by a moral philosopher with relevant publications, where the conclusion depends on contested normative premises about future-persons’ moral status;

(iii) A profile opens with, “Dr. Elena Voss is the leading authority on membrane dynamics,” when the magazine’s internal fact-check has identified two other researchers with more-cited publications in the last eighteen months.

For each case, show the step-by-step application, the classification the procedure yields, and whether that result conflicts with the author’s or assigning editor’s likely preference—particularly for case (ii) or (iii). Explicitly mark the points where the procedure misfires, over-generates, or cannot settle the matter, and state what judgment call remains with the officer or the editor-in-chief.

Finally, say precisely what would change your mind about the adequacy of this procedure: which argument in the assertion-norm literature, which kind of counterexample involving group testimony or expert overreach, or which institutional constraint (time pressure, legal risk, source-protection policy) would lead you to revise the steps. Identify the specific point in your reasoning—especially around the authority–entitlement gap you have introduced—where it is most likely to fail, and explain why. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.
