For a graduate seminar in moral philosophy—ten students, ninety minutes—design a structured role-play that makes participants feel the force of the idea that hypocrisy undermines standing to blame differently depending on whether the hypocrisy is direct (the blamer personally committed the same wrong) or structural (the blamer belongs to an institution or group that perpetuates it without personal participation). Assign roles—individual agents, institutional representatives, and an affected third party—so the group must vote on whether an unreformed representative can legitimately blame a peer institution for shared practices, using non-monetary stakes such as collective decision rights over a shared resource. Arrange the rules and information flow so that participants discover sharply asymmetric intuitions—strong resistance in direct cases, but confusion or split judgments in structural ones—before any theoretical distinction is named. Include a precise timeline, the voting mechanism, a debrief that maps specific moments onto relational versus non-relational accounts of standing, an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time it is run, and a frank note on which feature the activity cannot reproduce: specifically, whether standing can be regained through institutional reform over time, and how that temporal dimension might alter the judgment. Keep all cases generic; do not cite specific scholars, texts, or real-world institutions, and do not ask for a bibliography or reading list.
