I'm currently drafting a novel in which a physicist protagonist becomes convinced that rotational motion might be fully explicable without any external reference frame—only through internal relational dynamics among bodies and fields. This premise led me into the long-standing dispute over Newton's bucket experiment and the broader clash between spacetime substantivalism and relationism. What strikes me is the curious stasis of the debate: Newton's appeal to the concavity of rotating water, Mach's counter that inertia derives from relations to distant masses, and the modern synthesis through general relativity all appear to address one another without ever converging. Despite centuries of engagement by philosophers and physicists working from the same formal apparatus, the disagreement has not dissolved but rather fragmented into sub-debates about diffeomorphism invariance, the ontological status of spacetime points, and whether rotational dynamics in nearly empty universes require boundary conditions at infinity.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical—no hidden experiment waits to be performed—but conceptual, specifically about what counts as a satisfactory explanation of inertial effects. One side seems to demand an ontological substrate, a container or manifold with independent existence capable of grounding absolute motion; the other treats any such substrate as redundant, insisting that relational structure among bodies or fields is explanatorily sufficient. Yet I am uncertain whether this diagnosis captures the true fault line, or whether a disguised formal disagreement is doing the hidden work—perhaps about the conditions under which the differential equations describing rotation are well-posed in the absence of distant matter, or about whether "spacetime" and "matter" can be separated in the ontology of general relativity without losing predictive power.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure rather than a verdict between the positions: is it verbal, arising from competing uses of the word "space"; is it a substantive metaphysical dispute about what exists; is it a clash over explanatory standards, with each side requiring a different kind of ground for physical law; or is it a hidden technical question about gauge redundancy and the formal representation of rotational dynamics? Please engage concretely with what substantivalists and relationists actually claim about the bucket case and about Mach's principle, and identify what—if anything—could actually move the debate forward: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about the redundancy of absolute structures, a reframing of the original question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected because the frameworks are empirically equivalent by construction. Be honest about where judgment must substitute for proof, and avoid treating the dispute as a simple survey of interpretations.
