I'm developing a regulatory framework for identifying systemic neighborhood disinvestment, a legal status that triggers mandatory municipal intervention. The boundary seems irreducibly vague: one vacant lot does not disinvest a district, yet there is no precise point at which accumulated vacancy suddenly transforms the classification. After studying the sorites paradox, I have concluded that any binary statutory threshold—say, 35 percent property abandonment—must be metaphysically arbitrary in precisely the way the paradox exposes. To avoid this, I propose abandoning binary classification altogether in favor of a continuous disinvestment index, scaled from 0 to 1, where legal obligations scale smoothly with the score. A colleague objects that this merely relocates the problem: selecting a subsidy rate for a score of 0.47 versus 0.48 still requires drawing a boundary, and the same tolerance arguments apply to the index itself. I think this objection misses the structural point. If every legal consequence is a smooth function of the continuous score—so that there are no discrete jumps in obligation—then there is no single cutoff to attack with the sorites reasoning. Does this continuous-modeling strategy genuinely dissolve the paradox for policy design, or does vagueness re-emerge at the level of the measurement inputs that determine the score? And if the latter, does that mean any administrable standard must eventually accept an arbitrary binary line, or can a fully graded legal regime avoid commitment to sharp boundaries altogether?
