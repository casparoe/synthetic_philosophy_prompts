I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a close friend about whether a perfect forgery that is never actually mistaken for the original can still damage the original's value, or whether forgery is purely a relational crime of deception rather than an ontological threat to the object itself. Tomorrow morning I am delivering the opening remarks for the workshop "Material Truth: Conservation in an Age of Replication," hosted jointly by a museum conservation lab and a university philosophy department; the room will be split between practicing conservators and art historians who think in terms of craquelure and substrate, and philosophers of art who will catch any sloppy move on ontology or value. Please write these remarks as a formal, polished spoken address of about five to seven minutes, in which the philosophical problem does real structural work—shaping what I can and cannot claim about the purpose of conservation—rather than serving as decorative framing.

Take the role of a senior philosopher of art with practical familiarity with museum ethics, think carefully through the logic before drafting, and begin with brief brainstorming: first map the familiar opposition between the causal-historical view of authenticity and the perceptual-experience view, then center the speech on a niche idea I want to test, which we might call "prospective vulnerability"—the claim that part of an original's value lies in its genuine susceptibility to being displaced, doubted, or logically threatened by a sufficiently perfect copy, a vulnerability that copies themselves cannot possess. The address should ask whether conservators therefore have a duty not only to preserve physical matter but to preserve the very conditions under which an original can be genuinely contested, without ever reducing that claim to mysticism. It needs to land respectfully for the material experts in the room and for the philosophers, and should feel like the generous resolution of the argument I just had—not a victory lap. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; paraphrase any well-known thinkers freely without quotation marks, and avoid directing the audience toward specific texts. The tone should be reflective, intellectually serious, and appropriate for an occasion that is celebratory but not frivolous.
