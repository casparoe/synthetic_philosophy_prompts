I've been working through Pascal's Pensées on my own and trying to build a coherent view about justice and force, and I'd like an audit of whether these commitments actually hang together. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Pascal is right that justice without force is powerless—if a rule can't be enforced, calling it "just" is empty.
(2) I also think there's no hidden natural justice behind our laws; what people call justice is basically custom plus the power to make people obey. I do distinguish two kinds of acceptance—going along because you think the rule is right, and going along just because it's the established rule—but both are just social facts, not windows onto some deeper moral reality.
(3) From that, I conclude that citizens should obey established laws even when they feel personally wrong, since there's no external moral court to appeal to.
(4) At the same time, I think a regime that seizes power by pure terror and gets neither kind of acceptance—people don't think it's right and don't treat it as the rule—has lost all legitimacy, even if its written laws are word-for-word copies of ones I personally consider fair.
(5) I see voting mainly as a mechanism for distributing force among competing groups, not for discovering moral truth; still, I feel I have a strict duty to vote for the least harmful candidate, because abstaining just lets worse forces win by default.
(6) I reject Pascal's religious appeals to divine justice, since that would mean there really is a true justice behind human convention, which contradicts (2).

Please audit this package: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the real tensions by severity with the cheapest fix for each. Also say whether one single revision would clean up the most problems and what that revision costs.
