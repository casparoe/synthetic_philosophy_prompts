I'm prepping a ninety-minute session for a small reading group of technically-minded people—mostly rationalist-blog regulars who are comfortable with expected-value reasoning and AI-alignment framing but have never taken a philosophy course—on why hedonism faces a structural problem that isn't solved by saying "pleasure is good." Specifically, I want to teach the heterogeneity problem: if pleasures are as different as the satisfaction of completing a formal verification proof and the bodily relief of a perfectly calibrated temperature bath, what single property makes them all instances of pleasure and therefore all good? I need a pedagogical plan, not the lecture itself: the dependency-ordered sequence in which ideas must be introduced, the one carrying example that forces the problem into view, the predictable misconceptions and exactly where in the arc each should be intercepted, the check that shows they've grasped the conceptual difficulty rather than just memorized Mill's quality distinction, and what to explicitly leave out.

For the carrying example, construct a paired case in which intensity and duration are held constant—say, a mathematician's aesthetic recognition of an elegant symmetry versus a swimmer's proprioceptive ease in warm water—so that no shared sensory modality, cognitive content, or emotional tone remains, and specify what properties the example must have and must lack (it must not invoke pain-avoidance, must not allow a "higher vs. lower" classification to do the work, and must be describable without reference to any external goal achieved). Then map the misconception sequence: first, the "unit-of-account" error in which listeners assume pleasure can be measured like energy in joules and that heterogeneity is just a scaling problem; second, the "Mill escape hatch" in which they think qualitative distinctions solve the problem by adding dimensions rather than supplying a unifying substrate; third, the "revealed-preference collapse" in which they retreat to "people choose both, so they share value" and thereby abandon hedonism for preference-utilitarianism. For each, indicate the precise point in the explanatory arc—before the example, during its analysis, or after the quality-distinction is introduced—where the misconception should be headed off, and say how.

Finally, specify a check for understanding that requires them to generate a novel pair of pleasures and argue whether a hedonist can treat them as commensurable without smuggling in an independent value judgment, and list what must be excluded from this session—specifically, any neuroscience of reward pathways, the full Benthamite calculus with its seven dimensions, and the historical narrative of British empiricism—stating these omissions explicitly. The audience should leave seeing that hedonism needs a "common currency" analogous to intertheoretic reduction in philosophy of science, but without having been given any bibliography, citation, or further reading list, since this is meant to stand alone for someone working through the idea without web access.
