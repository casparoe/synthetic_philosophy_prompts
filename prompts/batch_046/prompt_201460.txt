My Kierkegaard seminar professor's rulings on hypothetical cases of the teleological suspension of the ethical have been consistent but ambiguous in their underlying logic. She has ruled as follows: in the Abraham and Isaac narrative, there is a legitimate suspension of the ethical for the sake of a higher telos; in Agamemnon's sacrifice of Iphigenia for the fleet, there is only tragic heroism within the universal, not a true suspension; in Jephthah's vow leading to the loss of his daughter, there is similarly tragic heroism within the universal rather than Abrahamic suspension; in a modern physician's refusal to administer life-saving care to a child because she reports hearing a direct, contradictory divine command, there is no legitimate suspension but rather subjective delusion or fanaticism; and in a soldier's refusal to carry out an unlawful order followed by willing acceptance of court-martial, there is conscientious ethical opposition within the universal, not a suspension.

Next week's case is the following: a climate scientist receives no articulated revelation, reports no expectation of reward, restoration, or miraculous outcome, and gives no rational account that could be shared within universal ethics. She nonetheless abandons her career, family obligations, and the universal ethical duty of scientific transparency, suppressing critical data and living in obscurity, on the basis of a silent, unverifiable conviction that the eternal relates to her absolutely in this particular act, such that the universal norms of truth and care are genuinely transcended rather than merely violated.

Predict her verdict on this case. Identify at least two interpretive principles that could explain her prior track record—such as a principle requiring an explicit, particular divine command that contradicts ethics; a principle requiring the double movement of infinite resignation followed by the expectation of recovery through faith; or a principle requiring only that the individual stand in an absolute, unmediated relation to the absolute regardless of command-content or expected outcome. Say what each principle predicts for the scientist. Give your best-guess verdict with reasons for preferring that principle over the others based on coherence with her past rulings. Finally, specify what further hypothetical case—distinguishing between expectation of recovery and pure absolute relation, or between explicit command and silent conviction—would settle which principle she is actually applying.
