In many doctoral programs within a particular humanities field, there exist exhaustive formatting and citation rules—specifying everything from margin width to the ordering of footnotes—that are presented as non-negotiable institutional requirements. Yet senior scholars routinely advise their most promising advisees to ignore these conventions when they interfere with argumentative flow, and committees rarely reject a dissertation for formal violations if the intellectual contribution is perceived as exceptional, whereas identical violations in weaker work are cited as grounds for revision. What philosophical conception of social norms does this pattern presuppose? Does it require viewing rules as primarily coordination devices that must be suspended for high-value cases, or can it be explained by a tight-culture model in which procedural enforcement functions mainly to filter out those who lack insider sponsorship? And is the practice internally consistent with the field's public commitment to loose, exploratory intellectual norms, or does it implicitly rely on a distinction between procedural and substantive norms that collapses when we ask why one group is permitted to break rules that bind another?
