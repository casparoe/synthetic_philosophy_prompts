You serve on a hospital neuroethics review board that evaluates decision-making capacity for patients with progressive cognitive impairment. Increasingly, patients rely on tightly coupled external systems — customized digital notebooks, predictive AI wearables, or dedicated human partners — that they use not only for storage but for active reasoning, preference formation, and practical decision-making. The board needs a reusable clinical-legal procedure, grounded in the philosophical thesis that cognitive processes can extend beyond the brain into environmental structures and artifacts, to determine whether a given external scaffold should be counted as part of the patient's cognitive baseline for informed-consent and capacity assessments. Design a step-by-step decision procedure that a non-philosopher clinician can complete in approximately thirty to forty minutes per case. It should include an ordered set of practical checks, clear criteria for what counts as sufficient evidence at each stage, explicit stop-points where the procedure yields a determinate answer, and clearly marked judgment-nodes where the question is normative rather than factual and therefore requires board-level human sign-off. The procedure must also flag the conditions under which it is likely to misfire or yield a result that conflicts with clinical intuition. Apply it to three cases: (i) a patient who maintains a static, handwritten decision journal updated weekly with no feedback loop; (ii) a patient whose real-time predictive wearable completes, corrects, and anticipates her practical reasoning in a continuous dynamic loop, where rigorous application of the procedure classifies the device as part of her cognitive system — an outcome the clinical team will likely resist because it complicates withdrawal of consent; and (iii) a long-term partner who serves as the patient's exclusive reasoning and memory interlocutor, where coupling is high but the boundary between two minds is ethically loaded. For each case, present the step-by-step evaluation, indicate where the procedure halts and hands authority to a human judge, and note any ambiguity the procedure cannot resolve. Conclude by stating two explicit limitations: one category of prosthesis or coupling that the procedure is structurally unable to classify, and one value-laden question about personal identity or autonomy that the procedure deliberately excludes from its scope.
