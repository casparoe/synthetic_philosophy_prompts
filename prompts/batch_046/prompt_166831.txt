I am writing from a political philosophy seminar. Our student housing cooperative currently contracts a private security firm for nighttime patrols, but after repeated failures by municipal police to respond promptly to incidents here, some residents want to dissolve the contract entirely, redirect those funds to community mental-health outreach, and rely instead on an unarmed neighborhood patrol that has no state coordination. Others propose keeping the private firm but requiring explicit board authorization before any intervention, arguing that this preserves democratic oversight without abandoning protection. I find myself torn between thinking that only the state should legitimately wield coercive force in a given territory, and thinking that persistent state failure dissolves that claim and permits organized community enforcement—provided it is more accountable than the police department. Is there a coherent middle position that treats state monopoly as a normative ideal yet allows private or community coercion as a permissible temporary substitute? Or does accepting any non-state authority to use force necessarily commit one to abolition or at least deep structural reform? In answering, please distinguish what is well established in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed normative claim—such as whether territorial monopoly is a moral requirement or merely a descriptive feature of modern states—as if it were the consensus.
