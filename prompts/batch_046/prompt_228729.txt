The charge that historians write “Whig history”—narrating the past as an inevitable progression toward present institutions and values—was famously articulated by Butterfield in 1931, yet every subsequent generation of historians insists the error has been exorcised, only for philosophers of history to observe that retrospective selection for coherence seems structurally unavoidable. After nearly a century of this cycle, the disagreement shows no sign of resolution. Is the impasse verbal: do “Whig” and “progress” mean different things to the practicing historian and to the philosopher of narrative? Is it substantive, with one side denying that explanation can avoid selecting events for their contribution to an outcome that was not intended by the agents involved, and the other insisting that such selection is a normative distortion rather than a logical feature of explanation? Or are they answering different questions—one about the ethics of representation, one about the formal conditions of narrative coherence—under a single heading? Diagnose the disagreement using the actual commitments of each camp, and specify what kind of result—a formal analysis of retrospective selection, an empirical study of explanatory practice in historical texts, or a conceptual distinction between development and teleology—would count as genuine progress rather than another repetition of the same exchange.
