After a long argument last night with a friend about whether an advance directive binds a later self that no longer shares its values, I want to test whether her position is genuinely consistent or whether it conceals two different principles. Here are her judgments across five cases, as I understand them. First, a woman with advanced Alzheimer’s smiles at caregivers, enjoys music, and no longer recognizes her family; her competent, earlier directive refuses artificial nutrition and hydration in severe dementia, and my friend says we should honor it and withdraw. Second, a man in a permanent vegetative state has a directive refusing mechanical ventilation, and against his family’s wishes she says honor it and withdraw. Third, a woman who was secular undergoes a years-long, stable religious conversion; she now holds that earthly life is sacred, demands aggressive life-extending treatment that her old directive refused, and retains full reasoning capacity—my friend says do not honor the old directive and provide what she now requests. Fourth, a man in temporary, severe infection-induced delirium begs for resuscitation contrary to his directive; she says honor the directive and do not resuscitate. Fifth, an adult with congenital cognitive disabilities that have always precluded autonomy never executed a directive herself, but her parents wrote one refusing antibiotics for serious infections; my friend says that document carries no authority and we should treat.

The case we did not resolve is this: a patient suffered a focal brain injury that eliminated emotional affect and social attachment while leaving logical reasoning fully intact. Over two years she has developed and consistently maintained a rational philosophical position that emotional bonds are illusory and that only logical consistency has value. She now requests life-extending treatment for a terminal condition. Her pre-injury directive, composed when she deeply valued emotional connection and relationships, refused such treatment should she ever lose those emotional capacities. She retains complete reasoning ability, understands the consequences, and has never wavered.

Predict what my friend will say. Identify the two rules that both generate her five verdicts—one turning on present decision-making capacity, the other on whether a value change is pathological or authentically transformative—and state what each predicts here. Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog? Do her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, and if so, what principle is she implicitly adopting with each choice? Finally, describe the single further hypothetical case that would most sharply force her to choose between these two rules. I am less interested in a bibliography than in the internal logic of her position.
