Design a decision-tree taxonomy of responses to Jackson’s knowledge argument from Mary’s room, built so that each internal node is a concrete judgment a student could make without knowing the position-label it leads to. Root node: Mary, who knows every physical fact about color vision, is exposed to red for the first time via direct cortical stimulation that reproduces the exact neural signature of red-experience but involves no external object—does she thereby learn a genuinely new fact, acquire only a new ability, or undergo no cognitive change at all? First branch: deny any new factual content versus grant it. For the denial branch, separate representationalist dissolution (the experience reveals only structural relations already in her physical knowledge) from the ability hypothesis (she gains a knowing-how, not a knowing-that). For the grant-it branch, ask whether the gap is epistemic (physical descriptions are complete but not a priori derivable) or ontological (there exist non-physical phenomenal properties). Within the epistemic branch, distinguish modal-rationalist physicalism (the derivation fails for rational, not metaphysical, reasons) from a strict derivability thesis (complete physical knowledge should in principle entail all truths). Within the ontological branch, ask whether phenomenal concepts are transparent (directly revealing properties) or opaque (mediated by recognition capacities), mapping these onto the corresponding concept-strategy and dualist leaves. The named leaves must include: Jackson-style anti-physicalism, Lewis/Nemirow ability hypothesis, representationalist dissolution, phenomenal-concept strategy with transparency, phenomenal-concept strategy with opacity, and modal-rationalist physicalism. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate—specifically, whether the ability hypothesis and a dispositionalist knowing-how account collapse into one branch, and whether modal-rationalist physicalism can be separated from the strict derivability thesis without an extra diagnostic case. Identify one logically possible combination of answers that no prominent view occupies, and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep every node phrased as a case-judgment or claim-assessment, not as “Do you accept view X?”
