I hold a qualified version of the language of thought hypothesis: Mentalese operates not only as the format of propositional attitudes, but as a combinatorial substrate specifically for analogical mapping, in which relational frames are tokened with variable-binding slots that are filled during mapping operations. On this view, failures of analogical reasoning under high working-memory load arise not from general executive depletion, but from a format-specific limitation in tokening capacity for these Mentalese slots; linguistic reasoning, which relies on parsed surface syntax rather than on substrate tokening, should therefore exhibit different load-sensitivity profiles. What would count against this position? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, specifying which empirical dissociations (between analogical and linguistic load effects), which representational objections (from connectionist or embodied-cognition frameworks), and which formal considerations (about compositionality, systematicity, or the need for syntactic structure) would genuinely undermine it. Which of these would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which could be dismissed by refining the view—for instance, by restricting the substrate claim to non-linguistic domains or by attributing counterexamples to implementation-level failure rather than to the representational format? Finally, assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a methodological liability for a claim about mental architecture.
