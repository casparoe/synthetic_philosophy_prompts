I was discussing Buddhist metaphysics with a colleague in philosophy of religion, and we found ourselves caught in what looks like an unresolved tension at the heart of the classical account of anatta. The doctrine, as articulated in the Abhidharma traditions and adopted across Theravada and Mahayana, denies any permanent, unitary self—an atman—analyzing the person instead into the five skandhas, which are impermanent, dependently originated, and lacking intrinsic essence. Yet these same traditions maintain a rigorous law of karma and rebirth across successive lives, which seems to presuppose some principle of continuity linking the agent of a past action to the being who later receives its fruit; without such a link, moral responsibility appears to collapse into a kind of inter-lifetime arbitrariness, as if one person could be punished for another's deeds.

I recently encountered the Pudgalavada school, which proposed a 'pudgala'—a person that is neither identical to nor different from the aggregates—apparently functioning as just this kind of conventional bearer that avoids both eternalism and annihilationism. If that solution genuinely preserves moral continuity without reifying a metaphysically robust soul, I do not understand why the mainstream traditions rejected it as heretical rather than embracing it. Is the standard reply that mechanisms like the Theravada bhavanga-citta or the Yogacara alaya-vijnana already supply the necessary continuity without constituting a self? And if these processes genuinely carry karmic traces and mental dispositions across lives, do they not effectively serve as the hidden substrate—the very quasi-substantial bearer—that orthodox Buddhists accuse the Pudgalavadins of illegitimately smuggling back into the doctrine?
