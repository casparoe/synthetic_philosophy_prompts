I am designing a single 75-minute seminar for advanced philosophy undergraduates on the intersection of the WEIRD psychology thesis and moral objectivity. Rather than summarizing Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan, I want the session to turn on a precise conceptual problem: whether systematic cross-cultural divergence in folk moral judgments undermines the argument from disagreement against moral realism, or merely shows that moral cognition is causally shaped without bearing on epistemic justification. Plan the explanatory arc. First, specify the order in which the components must be introduced and why—particularly how the descriptive claim about WEIRD outlier status must be secured before the normative implication is even raised, and where the distinction between causal origin and epistemic warrant enters. Second, identify the one concrete comparative scenario that should carry the discussion: a specific moral judgment (for instance, about distributive fairness or harm attribution) in which a WEIRD population and a small-scale non-WEIRD population diverge, yet both judgments appear internally coherent from within their respective normative frameworks. Explain what this scenario must include and what it must avoid (e.g., it should not reduce one side to ignorance or irrationality). Third, map the misconceptions: I anticipate students will conflate descriptive psychological variation with normative moral relativism, misread the WEIRD thesis as a claim that Western moral beliefs are false rather than unrepresentative, and treat moral objectivity as requiring universal intuitive agreement. For each, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, propose one check question for the end of the session that would reveal whether students have understood the origin/justification distinction rather than merely feeling unsettled by cultural difference. Finally, state explicitly which part of the standard metaethical literature—for example, debates between moral realists and anti-realists about the epistemology of disagreement—should be deliberately omitted at this level, with a brief justification.
