I'm writing a long-form feature for a science and ideas magazine on why philosophers of logic have failed to converge on a single theory of truth, despite the Liar paradox being formulated with perfect formal clarity for over two millennia. The same contradiction—generated by self-referential sentences that deny their own truth—coexists with radically incompatible responses: deflationary dismissal, hierarchical restrictions on language levels, paraconsistent tolerance of true contradictions, and revisionist cycles that allow truth-values to fluctuate. My working thesis is that this is not a hidden empirical disagreement about how natural language behaves, nor a purely technical dispute resolvable by another formal result; rather, the stalemate is normative at root. The camps appear to diverge, beneath their technical apparatus, over whether a formal language ought to retain unrestricted expressive power—including the ability to predicate truth of its own sentences—or whether logical coherence should take precedence over that kind of expressive freedom. Before I commit to this framing, I need an honest diagnosis of the disagreement's structure: do the sides actually differ about bedrock intuitions regarding the purpose of logical formalization, about standards of evidence for semantic theories, or about which cases should count as data? And given that diagnosis, is there any conceptual distinction—perhaps between semantic ascent and object-language consistency, or a novel constraint on self-reference within restricted formal languages—that could genuinely move the debate forward, or should we treat this as an impasse that no theorem or distinction should be expected to dissolve?
