I am a graduate student in the philosophy of science and artificial intelligence track, currently serving as the student representative on my university's AI Ethics and Research Oversight Committee, and we are deadlocked over a proposal I need to resolve. In our last session, we reviewed an unusual audit protocol submitted by an interdisciplinary cognitive-science lab that wants to examine the open-weight language models hosted on our cluster through what they call indirect latent-knowledge elicitation. Rather than relying on conventional red-teaming or direct factual queries, the researchers plan to construct nested counterfactual scenarios—fully synthetic histories of fictional scientific disciplines with internally consistent but imaginary laws—and then prompt the model to identify logical inconsistencies or predictive anomalies within those fabricated worlds. Their working hypothesis is that if the model has internalized unstated real-world knowledge, such as hazardous chemical interactions or subtle sociopolitical dynamics, its patterns of error, correction, and confidence calibration under these synthetic frames will diverge in statistically detectable ways from pure statistical confabulation, thereby revealing hidden representations without ever asking directly about the sensitive topics. Half the committee sees this as a legitimate transparency technique; the other half contends that adversarial framing of this kind risks destabilizing model behavior, generating spurious audit artifacts, or functioning as an indirect extraction mechanism for training data that should remain inaccessible, and they want an outright prohibition on any elicitation protocol that does not use direct, interpretable prompts. Draft a formal decision memo for the committee recommending whether to approve the protocol, reject it, or approve it with binding conditions. The memo should evaluate the epistemic legitimacy of inferring internal representations from counterfactual error divergence, treat the strongest objection about destabilization and false positives with genuine intellectual honesty rather than dismissal, and propose concrete, implementable safeguards—such as pre-registration of synthetic scenario templates, mandatory human-in-the-loop verification of divergence claims, explicit termination criteria for probing sessions, and a prohibition on iterative refinement of prompts based on intermediate outputs—rather than offering only general ethical guidance. Please write in American English, with a clear, actionable bottom line that the committee could actually vote on.
