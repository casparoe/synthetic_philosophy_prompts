I've been working through my own view on whether dreams are literally phenomenal experiences occurring during sleep, as opposed to retrospective narratives we construct on waking—the old Malcolm denial against Dennett's cassette-style acceptance. Rather than reasoning from axioms, I'm going by reflective equilibrium on concrete cases.

My verdicts:

1. A standard REM dream with spatial navigation and emotional valence—feeling lost in a familiar house: this is a genuine experience.
2. A hypnagogic jerk with a split-second falling image but no sustained phenomenology: not a full experience; closer to a sensory glitch.
3. A lucid dream in which I deliberately test whether I can read stable text and observe the failure: still experiential, just with an unusual meta-cognitive layer.
4. A sleepwalking episode involving complex goal-directed behavior—unlocking doors, putting on shoes—but with zero recall and no reportable imagery upon forced awakening: not an experience; behavior without phenomenology.
5. A false awakening with detailed tactile and proprioceptive phenomenology—"brushing teeth" in my bathroom—while my body is motionless in bed: this is an experience; the stimuli are absent but the phenomenology is present.
6. A narrative reconstructed post-hoc from decoded fMRI patterns, where the reconstructed story matches nothing I reported feeling during sleep and I have no "feeling of having experienced" it: there was no experience at the time; the dream is manufactured retrospectively.
7. Anesthesia awareness with explicit recall of voices and pressure, documented during a flat EEG epoch: an experience, though severely degraded.

The pair that keeps breaking my intuition is cases 3 and 4. Both involve complex cognitive processing and behavior in the absence of ordinary waking consciousness, yet I want to call the lucid dream experiential and the sleepwalking episode non-experiential. Meanwhile, cases 2 and 5 feel importantly different despite both lacking external sensory input—case 2 lacks phenomenology, case 5 has it in abundance.

The case I keep going back and forth on involves targeted memory reactivation during REM: an auditory cue (a specific melody) is played repeatedly without arousal, and upon waking the subject produces a coherent narrative involving that melody. EEG and behavioral markers indicate continuous deep REM with no arousal; crucially, she reports no "feeling of having lived through" anything—only a cold, reconstructed story that feels like recalling a film she watched rather than an episode she inhabited. Is this an experience?

I'm looking for one principle, or a small family of precisely competing principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. For each candidate, state the clause that is motivated by a specific case above, give its verdict on the TMR case, on a lucid dream induced by real-time EEG neurofeedback that the subject describes as "watching from outside," and on a sleep-paralysis hallucination with full visual and emotional content but complete bodily immobility. Be explicit about which of my verdicts each principle must reject, and whether that rejection is principled or ad hoc. Then say which candidate best balances simplicity and fidelity.

Please present the candidates clearly.
