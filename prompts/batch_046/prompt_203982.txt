After a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend about whether the Judy Benjamin problem genuinely undermines standard Bayesian conditioning—specifically whether receiving an intelligence report that updates one specifically on the conditional "if the enemy occupies Red territory, then they are using a night-vision protocol" should restructure belief in the same way as jointly learning both propositions—I want to design a classroom experience that makes the structural asymmetry visceral rather than merely notational. For a 90-minute session with approximately twelve advanced philosophy or formal-epistemology students, design a structured betting simulation using only paper, chips, and a pooled snack budget with small real stakes. The mechanics should center on three interlocking contracts that participants must price and hold: Contract R on enemy occupation of Red territory; Contract N on deployment of night-vision equipment; and Contract C on the indicative conditional linking the two. Assign roles—a market maker, two partisan analysts with opposing priors, and a coherence auditor tasked with enforcing a preliminary coherence constraint across all three prices. In the first phase (20 minutes), the group negotiates opening prices that satisfy standard probabilistic coherence. In the second phase, the facilitator delivers an ambiguous "signal intelligence" card that updates belief specifically about the conditional without confirming either disjunct, and all participants must lock revised settlement prices. In the third phase, contracts are resolved according to the updated prices, with payouts calculated so that any collapse of Contract C into the conjunction of R and N produces a visible arbitrage or loss for at least one role. Before any formal terminology is introduced, the settlement rules themselves should push participants toward either violating coherence or intuitively "freezing" one contract. Include a precise timeline, explicit locking and revelation rules for bets, and the exact payout structure. The debrief should map each mechanical crisis—the divergence between conditioning on the conditional versus conditioning on its components, the intuitive resistance to updating R independently, and the failure of a naive reflection principle applied to Contract C—onto the corresponding feature of Jeffrey-style updating. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong: several students will attempt to resolve the tension by treating Contract C as reducible to the joint distribution of R and N, and you should have a prepared response that uses their own settlement numbers rather than a lecture to show why the collapse fails. Finally, be frank about which formal feature the physical game cannot reproduce—specifically the continuous measure over an infinite partition required for full Jeffrey conditioning—and describe exactly how you will supply that conceptually once the chips are put away. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the point is that participants should leave seeing that conditioning on conditionals introduces a structural asymmetry that standard joint-distribution updating cannot absorb, regardless of which literature they have read.
