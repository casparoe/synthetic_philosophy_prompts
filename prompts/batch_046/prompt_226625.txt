I am drafting a feature for a science-and-ideas publication on the philosophy of historiography, and I keep returning to a conceptual tension I have been calling teleological compression: the tendency to narrate the history of a scientific domain—say, the transition from classical mechanics to quantum theory—not as a branching tree of genuinely incommensurable research programs, but as a linear stripping-away of error that makes the present framework appear inevitable. Can you provide an intuitive, gut-level explanation of why this specific form of Whig history is conceptually distinct from ordinary hindsight bias? I am particularly interested in understanding the mechanism by which treating past theoretical disagreements as mere noise around an eventually revealed true signal corrupts not only our image of the past, but our present-day methodological standards—specifically, our criteria for recognizing a contemporary alternative framework as a legitimate parallel architecture rather than as a preliminary mistake. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need a conceptual clarification that will hold up for readers who cannot access external archives.
