The economic and migration fallout is so different that conception-timing and partner-choice shift across continents—call this 'intergenerational substituion.' Explain why, under B, it's hard to say the 2150 population is harmed, and outline Parfit's person-affecting vs impersonal distinction—especially why a purely person-affecting view struggles when the affected group is totally replaced.

Student answer:
The core issue is genetic lottery. Policy B changes who meets whom and when people reproduce, so the specific individuals in 2150 wouldn't exist under A. That's the classic nonidentity setup from Parfit's Reasons and Persons. If those people have lives worth living, they can't claim harm—the alternative for them is non-existence, not a better life.

Parfit resolves this via the person-affecting/impersonal split. A person-affecting principle only condemns actions harming specific, identifiable people. Since the 2150 cohort under B isn't worse off compared to never existing, pure person-affecting intuition can't object. To fix it, Parfit endorses an impersonal average-utilitarian principle: maximize average wellbeing across generations regardless of who exists. That lets us prefer A even though it produces different people.

There's also the repugnant conclusion in play—if total utility is all that matters, a huge population with barely-okay lives outranks a smaller happy one. That's why climate ethics needs the average principle, not just person-affecting reasoning.
