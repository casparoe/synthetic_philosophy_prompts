I'm giving the toast at my mentor's retirement dinner. She's an evolutionary biologist who always said Darwin and Wallace hitting on natural selection wasn't luck but convergence -- like camera eyes in octopuses and vertebrates evolving seperately. But she also got furious when people used that to say Darwin was basically replaceable. The room's split: half biolgists who know the simultaneous discovery literature cold, half family who just want to cheer. I need about five minutes that asks whether discoveries are inevitable without either saying her life's work was redundant or making her sound like a lone genius. I want to use this tension between path-dependent tech -- QWERTY gets locked in -- and supposedly path-independent science, where the truth is just floating there waiting. I think the analogy breaks because the specific theoretical object -- the math, the experiments, the framing -- gets locked in even if the bare idea was going to arrive anyway. Wallace's version wasn't just Darwin's with a different name; it was a different beast, and that shaped what came next. So the discoverer matters not because they "found" something hidden, but because the form they gave it determined the field's trajectory. Can you write the toast so both halves of the room get it? Keep it casual -- I might mispronounce convergeance in front of her nephews.
