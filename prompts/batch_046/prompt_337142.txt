I'm preparing a presentation on institutional discourse for a philosophy of language seminar, and I have constructed an argument that appears to drive Grice's distinction between saying and implicating into an unacceptable dilemma. I want to know precisely which step is unsound, because I am reluctant to abandon either Grice's psychological account of speaker meaning or the reality of interpretive practices in institutional texts. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Grice's account treats implicature as arising when a speaker says something that, taken literally, violates a conversational maxim, but can be presumed cooperative and therefore intends the audience to recover an unstated proposition precisely by recognizing that communicative intention. (2) Now consider a multi-author legislative statute or corporate contract that has been revised by committees, lawyers, and stakeholders over months; there is no single psychological agent who holds all the communicative intentions associated with the final text, and the remaining intentions are often distributed, conflicting, or reconstructed only after drafting. (3) Nevertheless, courts and interpreters routinely derive unstated commitments—filling gaps, resolving ambiguities, or inferring obligations—by treating the text as if it were governed by a cooperative purpose, without attributing these derived contents to any particular individual's mental state. (4) If implicature can be successfully generated and recovered in the complete absence of a unified speaker intention that the audience recognizes, then Grice's psychological condition is not necessary for implicature. (5) But if speaker intention is not necessary, then the boundary between what is said, fixed by conventional linguistic meaning, and what is implicated, fixed by speaker meaning, dissolves: both become products of institutional conventions and interpretive norms rather than of individual psychology. (6) Consequently, either Grice's theory applies only to face-to-face dyadic conversation and is silent about, or false for, institutional language, or it must concede that implicature is not essentially about speaker meaning at all. (7) Both disjuncts seem to gut the distinctive core of Grice's framework, yet the premises about distributed authorship and legal interpretation strike me as accurate. Which premise should I reject, and what does surrendering it cost? If I must preserve Grice's central insight, am I forced to deny that institutional texts genuinely carry implicatures, or is there a more subtle diagnosis—perhaps an equivocation between recognizing an intention and attributing it to a specific mind—that saves the theory without abandoning the phenomena?
