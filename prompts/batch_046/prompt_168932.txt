Two people in my study group got stuck in an arguement about Montaigne's essay on cannibals. (1) One said it's basically proto-relativist: he praises the cannibals' honour and says European torture is worse, so moral norms must be culture-bound. (2) The other said that's too quick — he might just be attacking European hypocrasy, not denying universal standards exist. Pointing out cruelty while being cruel doesn't make cruelty okay everywhere; it just means you've lost standing to judge. (3) The first came back: but he also calls cannibalism barbarous even while defending it, and claims they live closer to nature. That sounds like a universal yardstick, not local scepticism. (4) The second replied that the closer-to-nature bit is ironic given Montaigne's usual doubt about knowing what's natural; she's smuggling in a Stoic framework he actually mocks elsewhere. (5) Then she noted that the cannibals brought to court seemed genuinely baffled by European inequality, which suggests they can recognise injustice across cultures — so Montaigne allows cross-cultural moral judgment, which clashes with pure relativism but also with the idea that they live in pure natural innocence.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each from clearly wrong to clearly right, briefly justify, and say who's right about what and what's missing from both sides. Write in British English.
