I am developing a framework—tentatively called recursive environmental agency—for treating pathogens, climate regimes, and volcanic events as genuine historical actors rather than passive background conditions, without collapsing into environmental determinism. Before I present this in my dissertation workshop, I need the full specification of what such a theory must achieve to count as adequate for historical explanation.

Set out the desiderata: the concrete cases it must cover (for instance, the 1783 Laki fissure eruption's interaction with European grain markets and mortality patterns; the Black Death's dependence on Mongol trade networks while simultaneously reshaping them; Holocene climate shifts that preceded agricultural adoption but were later modified by it); the distinctions it must preserve (causal contribution versus agentive power; deterministic forcing versus contingent human response; evolutionary interest versus epidemiological effect; single-event perturbation versus systemic regime change); and the results from epidemiology, paleoclimatology, and volcanology it cannot contradict.

For each requirement, state the motivating case and whether it is non-negotiable or specific to a camp (e.g., intentionalists who demand mental states, relationalists who accept only counterfactual dependence, or determinists who deny autonomous human response altogether). Crucially, examine whether the demand for robust non-human agency and the demand for irreducible human autonomy are jointly satisfiable given recursive causation—where societies alter the very pathogen or climate systems that subsequently act back upon them—or whether some subset of these requirements is necessarily unsatisfiable. Emphasize the criteria and their internal conflicts over any scoring of existing theories, since my immediate aim is to determine whether the framework is conceptually coherent before I apply it to archival cases.
