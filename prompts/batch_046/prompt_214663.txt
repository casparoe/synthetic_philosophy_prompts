I'm currently in a philosophy of mind course, and we have been examining epiphenomenalism—the thesis that phenomenal consciousness is a real feature of the world but exerts no causal influence on physical events, including brain states and behavior. My instructor mentioned the paradox of phenomenal judgment, which I take to be the challenge that if consciousness is causally inert, then our reports and beliefs about our own experiences must be produced entirely by unconscious neural mechanisms, apparently leaving us with no reliable access to the very states we claim to be describing. I understand the standard reply involves some version of causal overdetermination or a non-causal epistemic relation, but I want to press a different concern.

It seems to me that the paradox runs deeper than mere unreliability. If phenomenal properties genuinely have no causal powers whatsoever, then there is never any causal interaction between those properties and the cognitive processes that form our concepts. Concept acquisition, however, typically requires that the concept's referent play some role—whether direct or indirect—in shaping the mental representation. Under strict epiphenomenalism, consciousness never enters the causal chain that produces the concept of consciousness; the concept is built entirely from physical stimuli and neural computation that have nothing to do with phenomenal character. So the view doesn't just make our judgments about consciousness suspicious; it appears to make the very possession of the concept impossible in principle, since we could never have acquired a representation of something with which we have zero causal contact. Given this, shouldn't we regard epiphenomenalism as conceptually incoherent rather than merely epistemically awkward? And why do philosophers treat it as a live metaphysical position if it undermines the conditions for thinking about its own subject matter?
