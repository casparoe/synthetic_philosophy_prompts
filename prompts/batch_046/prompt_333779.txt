I am grading a philosophy of science and language exam for an upper-level undergraduate course. The exam included the following question, followed by a student's response. I need an evaluation: please assign a point score out of 20 and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify it. Do not signal your own agreement or disagreement with the student's conclusions in general terms; instead, identify precisely where the reasoning is sound, where a view is misattributed, where a step fails to follow, and whether any distinctions are conflated.

Exam question:
Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" attacks both the analytic/synthetic distinction and reductionist verificationism. Focusing on the first dogma: (a) Reconstruct Quine's argument that attempts to define "analytic" via synonymy are circular, with particular attention to why interchangeability salva veritate in purely extensional contexts fails to ground the distinction and why appealing to intensional contexts does not rescue it without circularity. (b) Explain Quine's holistic image of belief revision—the "web of belief"—and assess whether this holism eliminates the analytic/synthetic boundary altogether or merely transforms it into a pragmatic gradient of centrality and peripherality. Be specific about the status of logical and mathematical statements in this picture, using the contrast between revising a logical law and revising an observational report. (c) Briefly state how Quine relates the first dogma to the second (reductionist verificationism), and indicate whether his argument against reductionism depends on having already demolished the analytic/synthetic distinction.

Student answer:
(a) Quine shows that analyticity is usually explained as truth-by-meaning, and meaning is then explained through synonymy. Synonymy is in turn defined by interchangeability salva veritate: two expressions are synonymous if substituting one for the other in any statement never changes its truth value. The problem is that in purely extensional contexts, terms that merely happen to apply to the same objects—like "creature with a heart" and "creature with kidneys"—are interchangeable salva veritate even though they are not synonymous. To exclude these accidental co-extensive pairs, one must restrict interchangeability to intensional contexts, but identifying which contexts are genuinely intensional already requires knowing which pairs are truly synonymous. So the definition of analyticity presupposes the very notion it was meant to explain.

(b) Quine's web-of-belief model treats our commitments as an interconnected network that encounters experience only at the periphery. If an observation conflicts with the system, we can preserve any belief—including logical or mathematical ones—by making sufficient adjustments elsewhere in the web. This demonstrates that no statement enjoys absolute immunity from revision, which undermines the idea of a fixed, unrevisable analytic class. Nevertheless, Quine admits that logical beliefs sit near the center and observational reports near the edges, so in practice we protect logic and revise observation. That means Quine has not really destroyed the analytic/synthetic distinction; he has only relabeled it. What he calls "centrality" is precisely what Carnap called "analyticity"—a conventionally protected class of statements. So Quine inadvertently reinstates the boundary he attacked.

Furthermore, because logical truths are confirmed only indirectly—through the overall empirical success of the theories that embed them—they actually function as high-level synthetic generalizations about the world. Their acceptance depends on experience, mediated through many inferential connections, just like ordinary empirical claims. Thus Quine's holism converts logical truths into synthetic ones.

(c) The two dogmas are largely independent lines of attack. Quine's critique of reductionism relies on the Duhem-Quine thesis: individual statements do not face experience in isolation but only as parts of larger theoretical bodies. This undermines the verificationist idea that each synthetic statement carries its own unique empirical content. This argument stands on its own logical feet; it does not require that the analytic/synthetic distinction be false. Even if there were a sharp analytic/synthetic line, the Duhem-Quine problem would still defeat the claim that every synthetic statement has discrete verification conditions.
