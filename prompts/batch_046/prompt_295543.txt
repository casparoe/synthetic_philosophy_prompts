You are a professional moral philosopher with expertise in normative ethics, metaphysics of personal identity, and the logic of agent-relative reasons. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm: list the main families of objection to asymmetry arguments in population ethics, recall where debates over the procreation asymmetry typically stall, and identify which criticisms target the metaphysics of potential persons versus those that target the normative structure of initiating moral relationships.

Here is the position I hold. The procreation asymmetry — that we have serious moral reason not to bring a suffering person into existence, but no corresponding reason to bring a flourishing person into existence — should not be explained by appeal to impersonal axiological value, by the non-identity problem, or by any claim that merely possible people possess shadowy present interests. Instead, I defend a framing on which the asymmetry is an artifact of the temporal indexing of agent-relative obligations. Specifically: (i) an obligation not to harm applies once a being exists to be harmed, but the act of creation is precisely the point at which that being comes to exist, so harm-avoidance constrains the decision to initiate existence; (ii) by contrast, there is no analog for benefit, because a non-existent being cannot be owed a benefit, and initiating existence is not the fulfillment of a pre-existing claim but the creation of the very subject to whom a claim might be owed; (iii) therefore the asymmetry survives even in a fully person-affecting framework that rejects stronger antinatalist conclusions and denies that future people have present moral standing. I claim this explains the intuitive pull of the asymmetry without requiring that possible people have interests that can be weighed in advance.
