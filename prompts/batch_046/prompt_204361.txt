As a retired physicist who has lately turned to philosophy of mind, I am preparing notes for a small seminar on the conceivability argument against physicalism. Rather than rehearse the standard zombie hypothetical, I want to read Ridley Scott's Blade Runner as an implicit stress-test for the argument's hidden assumptions. In particular, I want to treat Roy Batty's death-scene monologue not merely as pathos but as a first-person phenomenological report: if a physically identical zombie twin could deliver those same lines with no inner experience, does the scene still function as an acquaintance-based rebuttal embedded in the narrative itself? At the same time, Deckard's ambiguous replicant status—implanted memories, no biological birth—raises the question of whether the absence of an organic origin undermines the intuition that consciousness requires a living substrate, or whether that intuition is already question-begging. I also want to press the tension between the Voight-Kampff test, which measures pupillary reflex rather than phenomenological presence, and the anti-behaviorist thrust of Chalmers's argument: the film seems to presuppose that consciousness can be detected from the outside, precisely the assumption the zombie thought experiment denies. Could you provide a critical reading that tracks which scenes genuinely illuminate this philosophical tension—especially between first-person authority and third-person detection—and which scenes merely romanticize consciousness without advancing the argument? I need no bibliography or source list; I need an honest map of where the film illuminates the debate and where its narrative logic resists the philosophical framing.
