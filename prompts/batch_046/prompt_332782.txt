As a novelist drafting a constitutional debate for a character who is a former tax lawyer turned dissident theologian, I have constructed an argument about secular fiscal neutrality that now strikes me as overreaching, yet I cannot locate the precise fault line. Here is the chain, numbered as in my notes. (1) A genuinely secular state may deploy its revenue apparatus on behalf of a private association only when that association holds public-law corporation status and membership is formally voluntary with a low-cost exit mechanism. (2) In Germany, churches collect Kirchensteuer through state tax offices precisely because they are Körperschaften des öffentlichen Rechts, and citizens can exit by filing a Kirchenaustritt with a civil registry office—an exit far more concrete than leaving citizenship. (3) By contrast, secular philosophical societies, artistic collectives, and moral NGOs operate as private Vereine; the state never collects their dues. (4) If secular neutrality requires that like cases be treated alike, then either the church-tax mechanism must be extended to every voluntary association with a public-benefit claim, or it must be withdrawn from churches alone. (5) Universalizing state collection to all worldviews would make the Finanzamt a billing agent for every comprehensive doctrine, which is administratively impossible and conceptually incoherent: it collapses the distinction between civil society and the state. (6) Therefore Kirchensteuer is illegitimate, and by parallel reasoning any fiscal privilege—such as the tax exemptions in England and the United States that depend on being classified as religious rather than merely charitable—must also fall, leaving religious bodies with fewer rights than secular nonprofits. (7) But this conclusion seems to prove too much: it does not merely level the playing field but actively penalizes institutions that happen to organize themselves theologically, which is not what secularism demands. I am inclined to keep (1) and (4), because I want neutrality to be a genuine constraint, but I am unsure whether the error lies in (3)—perhaps churches are not like secular NGOs in a relevant constitutional sense—or in an implicit slide between fiscal privilege and state coercion somewhere between (4) and (6). Which step should I reject? Explain why that step initially seemed sound, then lay out what giving it up costs for someone who wants secularism to be neither hostile to religion nor secretly establishmentarian.
