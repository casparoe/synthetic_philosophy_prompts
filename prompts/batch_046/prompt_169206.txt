Following an exhausting but illuminating argument with a close friend about whether intellectual stubbornness in the face of disagreement is ever rational, I want to examine a real case with some precision. Analyse the well-known 1975 Royaumont debate between Noam Chomsky and Jean Piaget on language acquisition and cognitive development as a study in peer disagreement. Both were undisputed authorities with access to the same empirical literature; both left the debate entirely unmoved. Using the conciliationist and steadfast positions in the epistemology of disagreement, map precisely who counts as the epistemic peer, what the shared evidence comprised, and what each view prescribes they ought to have done with their respective credences in innate universal grammar versus constructivist development. Then address the further complication that their disagreement was partly meta-level: they disputed not only the correct answer but what would count as an adequate scientific explanation in this domain. Does conciliationism require them to reduce confidence in their own methodological frameworks, or does steadfastness become more defensible when peers disagree about the very standards of evaluation? Finally, say what this episode reveals about the theory—whether it constitutes an unanticipated anomaly, and whether the distinction between first-order and framework-level disagreement needs to be built explicitly into the conciliationist calculus.
