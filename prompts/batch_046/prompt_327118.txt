My university’s history department requires every course on twentieth-century authoritarian regimes to include a biography-centered module on the primary leader and grades essays partly on whether students identify a “decisive individual choice” as the narrative turning point; at the same time, accreditation rules mandate coverage of structural preconditions—economic collapse, institutional decay, ideological diffusion—but explicitly prohibit awarding credit for explanations that treat those structures as sufficient without the leader’s agency. What theory of historical causation does this two-tier pedagogical system presuppose: a strong Great Man view, a structural view with an agentive exception clause, or some interactionist compromise? If we replaced the implicit theory with a fully structural account—one on which a Nazi Germany of comparable form and timing would have emerged even without Hitler—what exactly in the syllabus, rubric, and accreditation language would have to change? In your response, say how confident you are in each part of the reconstruction and single out the point about which you are least certain.
