Many video-game platforms and studios enforce content policies that restrict depictions of realistic harm to human-like characters but impose no equivalent restriction on harm to persistent non-player characters—entities that maintain state, social relationships, and reactive behavioral profiles across sessions—despite these NPCs sometimes running on architectures functionally identical to those governing player avatars in shared-world environments. Meanwhile, in-game reputation or morality algorithms penalize harm inflicted on other human players yet remain silent when a player harms an NPC with the same behavioral profile. What philosophical commitments about moral status, consciousness, or the boundary of the moral community does this institutional pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it rest on a biological natural-kind distinction, on an actual-world causation or interaction requirement, on a contractualist account of moral standing, or on an ontology that treats fictional entities as categorically different regardless of internal architecture? Assess whether the practice is internally coherent given that behavioral and architectural boundaries between players and persistent NPCs are often porous, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and describe what a moderation framework built instead on a behavioral or relational criterion of moral status would have to change.
