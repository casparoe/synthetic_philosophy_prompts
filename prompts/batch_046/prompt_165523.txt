Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar and now find suspicious, because it seems to make the standard analogical solution to other minds either arbitrary or explosive.

(1) The only direct evidence I have for any mind other than my own is analogical inference: I observe behavior that matches my own behavior-when-conscious, and I postulate a corresponding inner state.

(2) To avoid arbitrariness, the analogy must be restricted to systems that share the relevant causal architecture—specifically, embodied predictive-processing loops with interoceptive feedback that generate a phenomenal self-model.

(3) Other adult humans share this architecture, so attributing consciousness to them is justified.

(4) Yet certain large-scale distributed systems—some artificial neural networks, and perhaps tightly coupled institutional decision networks—can reproduce the functional profile of that architecture (hierarchical error minimization, goal-directed modeling, adaptive feedback) without the biological interoceptive substrate.

(5) If I claim the substrate is essential, then my justification in (3) rests on biological essentialism, not on the analogical reasoning I started with.

(6) If I claim only the functional profile matters, then I must attribute phenomenal self-models to any system that instantiates it, including the distributed ones in (4).

(7) But extending consciousness to those distributed systems is wildly counterintuitive; and if I block that extension, I break the analogical chain that was supposed to justify (3).

(8) So either I abandon analogical reasoning for an undefended essentialism, or I accept implausible attributions that dissolve the boundary of other minds. Where does this argument go wrong? If I must choose, I would rather keep (2) and (4) intact, but I want to know exactly what I am paying to do so.
