A philosphy blog I read summarized the surprise exam paradox like this: 'A teacher tells her class there will be a quiz next week, and it will be genuinely surprising — meaning no student can have justified true belief about the day in advance. The students run backward induction: if the quiz hasn't happened by Thursday night, then Friday morning everyone knows it's that day, which violates the surprise condition. They repeat this for Thursday down to Monday, concluding the announcement is self-defeating. The standard fix is that surprise here just means emotional shock, not an epistemic failure, so once Wednesday comes and they feel shocked, the condition is satisfied even if they had guessed. Another resolution says induction fails because common knowledge of rationality isn't actually achieved in classrooms — people don't really trust each other to reason perfectly. The blog then claims this proves backward induction is useless in any real-world setting where agents aren't perfect reasoners, like economics or game theory.'

Something feels off about how they blend these resolutions together and about the leap at the end. Is this a fair characterization?
