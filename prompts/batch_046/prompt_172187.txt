I am a sixth-form student preparing an extended essay for my A-level Philosophy course, and I have recently encountered Samuel Scheffler's argument — developed in his writing on mortality and value — that the prospect of human extinction is troubling not merely because of the deaths it entails, but because it undermines the meaning of present projects by removing what he terms the collective afterlife: the expectation that future generations will continue to exist and carry forward the activities and values we hold dear. I should like to apply this theoretical lens to the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962, which I understand as a real-world episode in which the immediate survival of millions was genuinely at stake, yet in which both superpowers eventually found a path away from direct confrontation. Could you analyse the crisis through Scheffler's framework, paying particular attention to whether Kennedy and Khrushchev, or their advisers, appear to have been motivated by something resembling an agent-neutral commitment to the continuation of humanity as such — rather than solely by strategic self-interest or the avoidance of immediate casualties? I am especially interested in how the secret back-channel negotiations between Robert Kennedy and Anatoly Dobrynin, together with the unpublicised withdrawal of American Jupiter missiles from Turkey, might be interpreted as expressions of a shared, implicit recognition that preserving a future for human civilisation mattered independently of the particular payoff structures of the nuclear game. At the same time, I should welcome an honest assessment of where Scheffler's conjecture fails to illuminate the episode: for instance, does the pronounced asymmetry of risk between a direct threat to Soviet strategic interests in Cuba and a symbolic challenge to American credibility in Turkey suggest that any collective-afterlife reasoning was always subordinate to conventional geopolitical calculation? Finally, please indicate whether this case ultimately strengthens Scheffler's claim that our moral reasons include a distinctive concern for humanity's future, or whether it demonstrates that such concern collapses into ordinary prudential reasoning about present survival.
