I'm preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy-of-science doctoral students who are fluent in reductionist frameworks but have never encountered Braudel's concept of the longue durée. I want them to understand the specific distinction between structures that move at geological or climatic speed, intermediate social and economic rhythms, and the rapid surface of events and individual decisions—not Braudel's biography or the Annales school as such. Plan the pedagogical sequence for me: do not explain the concept itself. First, lay out the dependency order—what distinction must be secured before the next layer can be introduced, and why the sequence collapses if reversed. Second, propose one carrying example that is not Braudel's Mediterranean study; specify exactly what features it must possess (it should display the constraining force of a slow structure, preserve visible space for human agency, and avoid rapid political turnover that would blur the temporal layers) and what it must lack. Third, identify the misconceptions this particular audience will predictably form—I anticipate they will read long duration as geographical determinism and confuse it with mere historical background—and state precisely at which point in the dependency order each misconception should be intercepted. Fourth, design one closing diagnostic question that reveals whether a student has genuinely grasped the layered-temporal framework or has only adopted the vocabulary. Finally, state explicitly which part of Braudel's tripartite scheme should be left out at this level and why including it would undermine the session's aim.
