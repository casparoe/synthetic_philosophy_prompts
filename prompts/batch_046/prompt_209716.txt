I'm writing an essay for my philosophy of science seminar on the metaphysics of natural law, and I've become stuck on a tension that doesn't seem to be addressed in the standard textbook framing of the debate. Most discussions present Humean regularity theory—laws as descriptions of the mosaic of local particular facts—against anti-Humean governance accounts, on which laws are relations of natural necessitation that constrain or produce what happens. I want to argue that this binary misses a distinctive pressure coming from contemporary physics: symmetry principles in quantum field theory appear to carry modal robustness (they survive counterfactual perturbations of particular events) yet they do not seem to govern specific outcomes in the anti-Humean sense, since they restrict the space of possible dynamics without determining individual measurements or trajectories.

My proposed thesis is that a Humean can treat these symmetries as high-level structural descriptions of the mosaic—patterns invariant across large regions of actual and nearby possible worlds—without committing to governing relations. But I worry that once the Humean admits these modal patterns are genuinely explanatory rather than merely accidental, they have smuggled in something functionally equivalent to natural necessity, just relocated at the level of global structure rather than local necessitation. I would particularly value comments on whether this objection succeeds, and whether there is a principled way for the Humean to distinguish "structural description" from "disguised governance" without appealing to epistemic access or simplicity considerations that feel circular in this context.

Could you comment on the coherence of this line of argument, especially on whether I am mischaracterizing either the Humean supervenience base (the idea that all modal facts supervene on local matters of particular fact) or the anti-Humean treatment of symmetry constraints? I am also interested in whether my framing introduces a false trilemma—perhaps there are resources within the existing debate, such as distinctions between contingent and necessary laws within necessitarianism, or the Humean acceptance of counterfactual-supporting regularities, that dissolve the tension without resolving it cleanly in either direction. Any suggestions for sharpening the conceptual boundary between describing invariant structure and governing by relation would be especially helpful, as would notes on whether quantum symmetries genuinely present a novel challenge or merely recapitulate familiar problems about universal generalizations with uninstantiated instances.
