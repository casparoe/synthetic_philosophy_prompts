I have been trying to apply Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public—his claim that modern publicity creates an abstract, quantitative "public" that dissolves individual responsibility and replaces passionate commitment with leveling comparison—to the information dynamics of the early COVID-19 pandemic, specifically the period from January through March 2020. Rather than asking for a summary of his writings or a reading list, I want a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. First, map the episode onto the theory: in this crisis, what functions as "the press"—is it legacy media, social media platforms, algorithmic recommendation systems, or some hybrid? Who or what constitutes "the public" that Kierkegaard warned about? Did the rapid circulation of both authoritative epidemiological guidance and contradictory misinformation exhibit the kind of leveling he described, where quantitative volume replaces qualitative commitment?

Second, identify precisely where the framework breaks down or needs structural revision. For instance, individual scientists and public-health officials often retained highly specific, personally accountable positions despite being embedded in these abstract channels; does this contradict Kierkegaard's prediction that the public necessarily swallows the individual? Furthermore, algorithmic amplification introduces a form of selective leveling—certain voices are elevated by engagement metrics rather than flattened uniformly—that Kierkegaard, writing about print culture, never anticipated. Does this represent an anomaly for his view, or can the concept of leveling be extended to cover algorithmic hierarchy without losing its critical force? Please be explicit about which actors count as the players, what options they faced, and where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Conclude with whether the episode confirms that modern publicity still produces the irresponsible abstraction he feared, or whether the persistence of individual expertise within the noise suggests his diagnosis needs a substantive amendment—particularly regarding how digital mediation alters the relationship between the single individual and the anonymous crowd.
