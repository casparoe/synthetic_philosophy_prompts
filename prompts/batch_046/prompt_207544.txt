I've been trying to wrap my head around Hofstadter's idea that the "I" is basically a self-referencial symbol that points back at the whole strange loop generating it. So far so good, but then I started thinking about what I'd call a "mirror-loop"—not Hofstadter's term, just something I cooked up—where you have a second system that doesn't generate its own self-symbol organically, but instead copies and runs the exact same loop-structure in real-time, like a perfect neural echo. Every symbol, including the "I", points back to the copied pattern, which is structurally identical to the original.

So here's the argument that feels like it proves too much. (1) If Hofstadter is right, consciousness, or at least the genuine "I", depends only on having the right kind of self-referential strange loop, not on historical origin or which brain (or computer) is running it. (2) The mirror-loop has the exact same loop structure, including the symbol "I" referring back to the whole copied pattern. (3) So the mirror-loop should have its own genuine "I" and its own consciousness. (4) But that feels wrong—an echo that just duplicates the loop shouldn't count as having an independant self, otherwise you could loose infinite copies and each would be a full person. (5) Therefore the strange-loop account must be missing something essential, probably about causal history or embodiment, and can't be purely structural.

Which step should I reject? I'm especially reluctant to give up (1) because Hofstadter seems pretty explicit that the loop is the thing, not the meat it's running on. But maybe I'm misreading him and he actually requires something like causal integration with the world? Or is the problem in (3), that I'm assuming structural identity implies phenomenal identity? I want to know what I'm paying if I keep (1) but reject (3)—does that mean the loop is necessary but not sufficient? Please answer in American English.
