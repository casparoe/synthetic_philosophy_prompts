I've been serving on our town's redevelopment committee, and we're deadlocked over a proposal that I keep turning over in my mind, partly because it feels like it's about something larger than zoning rules. About forty years ago, a single warehouse on the old mill side of the river received an accidental live-work variance—not because anyone planned an arts district, but because a council member owed a favor to a local weaver who needed unusually high ceilings and open floor plates for her looms. That one space attracted other textile makers, then a dye supplier, then a café, and eventually the entire mill quarter became the arts district that people now treat as the defining heart of our town. Meanwhile, the warehouse cluster across the river had nearly identical cheap brick buildings and river access, yet it stayed ordinary industrial space and eventually declined.

Now a developer wants the same kind of variance for one warehouse across the river, hoping to seed a second cluster. Half the committee argues that the original district was inevitable: cheap space, river proximity, and a town with artists meant something like it would have emerged within a few years no matter what, so granting a new exception is just theater. The other half insists that the district followed a specific, unrepeatable chain—starting with that weaver's particular needs and her network—so that without replicating that exact first link, we shouldn't expect the same result. Both sides seem convinced they're right, but neither explains how to tell whether we're dealing with a history that got stuck on one track or with a story we're telling ourselves after the fact.

What I need is not a vote, but a way to think clearly enough to advise the committee. How should I distinguish between the parts of the district's growth that became self-reinforcing once they started—so that later steps felt unavoidable—and the parts that genuinely hung on that first, arbitrary decision? Is there a coherent principle for knowing when a historical outcome is so locked in that it wouldn't have arrived through any other nearby path, versus when we're exaggerating the power of a single small choice? And if some trajectories do lock in, does that mean we should try to copy the original conditions precisely, or does the locking itself suggest that once a path is established, the initial conditions matter far less than we think?
