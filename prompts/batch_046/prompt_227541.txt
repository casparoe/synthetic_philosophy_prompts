I'm the tech lead for a software platform my team has built and refined over eight years. A recent analysis shows that a full rebuild on modern infrastructure would reduce operating costs by about thirty percent and eliminate several serious security vulnerabilities. The difficulty is that our legacy system contains thousands of bespoke configurations—each the product of years of direct negotiation with clients and incremental fixes that taught us which behaviors actually work in our domain. Several senior engineers argue that we should continue investing in the existing stack, because abandoning it would mean discarding all of that accumulated practical judgment and breaking implicit commitments to clients who depend on these precise behaviors. The rest of the team insists that whatever effort went into the old system is now irrelevant: if the rebuild is objectively superior, we should migrate immediately and not let history dictate the choice. I feel genuinely torn. Is there ever a rational basis for preserving a system mainly because of the work, relationships, and institutional knowledge already embedded in it, or does honoring that past always represent a failure of reasoning? Please distinguish what is philosophically settled about how prior investments ought to guide present choices from what remains genuinely contested among people who study this—especially whether commitments and accumulated practical knowledge embedded in this way can carry legitimate normative weight that goes beyond pure forward-looking efficiency—and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
