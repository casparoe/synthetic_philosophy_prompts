I am preparing a draft essay for an advanced seminar in the philosophy of social science, and I would be grateful for your critical comments and suggestions for revision. The working title is “Institutional Omission and the Boundaries of Methodological Individualism.”

The central claim is that standard defenses of methodological individualism—whether in the Weberian or Popperian traditions, or in more recent rational-choice reconstructions—presuppose an ontology of social causation in which every causal contribution can be decomposed into the actions and omissions of identifiable individual agents. Against this, I propose that we must recognize a distinct category of what I call institutional omission: a causal factor consisting in the absence of a coordinating rule or mechanism that was never assigned to any particular agent’s jurisdiction, and therefore cannot be reconstructed as an aggregate of individual failures to act. I distinguish this sharply from distributed omission, in which many agents each omit a small contribution that collectively produces an outcome.

I apply the distinction to systemic risk accumulation in decentralized markets: no single trader omits to prevent a crisis, yet the absence of a macroprudential framework is causally implicated in the outcome. I then ask whether recognizing institutional omission commits us to methodological holism, or whether an individualist can respond by treating the missing framework as an unintended consequence of individual preferences for minimal regulation. I am especially interested in whether my distinction survives the holist’s objection that all institutional absences are ultimately traceable to patterns of individual behavior, and whether I have adequately addressed the individualist’s counterfactual strategy—namely, that if each agent had acted differently, the framework might have emerged.

Please comment on the coherence and originality of the argument, the fairness with which I treat both methodological individualism and its holist critics, and any conceptual confusions or gaps in reasoning you detect. I would also welcome suggestions for sharpening the boundary between institutional omission and distributed omission, and for clarifying whether the example I have chosen genuinely illustrates the former rather than the latter. Please provide your response in American English.
