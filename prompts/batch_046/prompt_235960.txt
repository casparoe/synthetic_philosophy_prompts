Okay so I've been dealing with long-range planning at work (infrastructure forcasting, don't ask) and I started reading about old population theories for fun, and there's this pattern I keep seeing that I don't have a word for.

You have a theory built on some deep mechansim that says eventually X happens — like food supply can't keep up with people forever, or we'll hit an energy ceiling. Then in the near term, say fifty or a hundred years, X doesn't happen. In fact the opposite happens. But instead of ditching the core idea, defenders say the clock was just wrong, or there was some hidden buffer that bought time. Then centuries later, if you see even a small sign of X, they claim the theory was 'right but early.' The failed intermediate checks get treated as 'before the mechanism kicked in' rather than real counterevidence. I see this with Malthus, with those 1970s Limits to Growth reports, and now with some AI timelines people talk about.

Is there an established concept for this exact rescue move — stretching a long-run prediction's timeline and writing off near-term failures as 'not yet'? I know about ad hoc hypotheses and maybe Lakatos's protective belt, but those feel like they're about adding new rules to the theory, not just saying 'wait longer.' If there's no exact name, how do you draw the boundary between this and ordinary falsification-avoidance? And here's the part that got me thinking: since the people living during the 'delay' are different from the ones who'd exist if X had hit on schedule, does that change whether anyone can actually complain about the delay? I feel like there's a non-identity thing lurking here but I'm not sure how it connects. Does this configuration have a name of its own?
