As I am developing the philosophical architecture for my next novel, I have devised a character—Dr. Althea Reed, a moral philosopher—whose published verdicts on gradated moral status form a consistent yet ambiguous pattern. For the sake of narrative coherence, I wish to understand the implicit logic she is running, and precisely where it might fracture under pressure.

Her stated positions are these: a conscious adult human possesses maximal moral status, and a sleeping human retains it by continuity; a chimpanzee possesses near-maximal status; a domestic cow possesses substantial status; an octopus possesses moderate-to-high status despite evolutionary distance; a honeybee colony, considered as a collective entity, possesses minimal but non-zero moral weight; an individual worker bee possesses negligible status, approaching zero without reaching absolute nullity; the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans possesses absolute zero status; a domestic thermostat possesses absolute zero status; a contemporary large language model, however behaviourally fluent, possesses absolute zero status; and a Venus flytrap possesses absolute zero status.

I now wish to apply her framework to three cases central to my plot: a chicken (Gallus gallus domesticus) raised in conventional conditions; a slime mould (Physarum polycephalum) navigating a labyrinth through distributed cellular signalling without any neural tissue; and a lab-grown human cortical organoid with active nociceptive pathways but no brainstem, body, or behavioural output.

Please answer in British English, with formal precision. First, predict Dr. Reed’s verdict on each of the three new cases, with reasoning. Second, identify the principle she appears to be running—whether unified sentience, biological homeostatic integration, functional agency, or something else—and explain how it accommodates her earlier rulings. Third, set out at least one alternative principle that accommodates all her earlier verdicts equally well, stating explicitly what each principle predicts about the slime mould and the organoid in particular. Fourth, give your best guess as to which principle she actually holds, and defend that preference. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—one that does not depend on obscure empirical findings—that would decisively distinguish between the two principles you have outlined.
