I'm a senior in an independent philosophy-of-mind seminar, and I've been working through Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self is not a substance but a "strange loop"—a self-referential pattern of causal interactions that represents itself. I've tried to extend this idea into a specific view about distributed identity, but before I present it I need a consistency check. Here is my package. First, I hold that the "I" is just that pattern, and that if it were sustained across two physically separated brains via a continuous, bidirectional neural interface—where each brain contributes unique processing but neither contains the full loop on its own—there would genuinely be one distributed self, not two cooperating minds. Second, I think consciousness requires a single, unified phenomenal field: at any moment there is one "what it's like," not a pair of parallel streams. Third, I take personal identity over time to be psychological continuity—the preservation of that self-referential pattern—not bodily continuity. Fourth, I think that if the interface were severed and one brain retained only a partial, degraded loop, that brain would still be the same "I," merely damaged (like surviving a lobotomy), whereas if both brains retained full, independent copies of the loop, that would be branching, and neither could claim to be the original. Fifth, I maintain that this distributed "I" could survive the destruction of one brain provided the other retained the complete loop, though it could not survive the destruction of both.

My concern is that these commitments may not hang together, but I cannot see exactly where the fault lines are. I worry there is tension between my claim that the loop is genuinely distributed—so that neither brain houses it alone—and my claim that one brain could retain the "complete" loop after separation, which sounds as if the pattern was localized there all along. I also wonder whether my insistence on a unified phenomenal field is compatible with two brains that still have separate sensory apparatuses, and whether my rejection of bodily continuity is undermined by my treating the physical location of the "complete" loop as determinative of survival. Please audit this specific set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and rank the tensions by severity. For each real tension, indicate the least costly revision and what I would have to give up. I am not asking for a general lecture on personal identity or on Hofstadter's books; I want a targeted consistency check on these five commitments, with attention to whether I am smuggling in bodily continuity or substrate-dependence through the back door.
