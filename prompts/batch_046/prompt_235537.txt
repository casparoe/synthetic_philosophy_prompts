Please grade the following student response to an exam question on pain asymbolia and the badness of pain. The exam asked students to analyze whether the dissociation observed in pain asymbolia—where subjects report nociceptive sensations without unpleasantness or avoidance behavior—undermines the claim that pain is intrinsically bad in virtue of its phenomenal character, and to address whether the absence of aversive motivation entails an absence of harm to well-being.

Student answer:
Pain asymbolia demonstrates that nociception and suffering are not identical. Patients experience the sensory properties—location, intensity, and quality—without the characteristic aversion, which shows that the badness of pain is not built into its sensory feel but is added by an independent affective system. Consequently, the phenomenon undermines any view that identifies pain's qualitative character with its negative hedonic tone; since the sensory profile can exist without the negative tone, the two must be separable. Furthermore, because these patients do not suffer or seek relief, there is no harm to their well-being, which confirms that pain is only instrumentally bad—as a warning signal—rather than intrinsically bad. The only remaining problem is explaining why normal subjects conflate the two dimensions, but that is a psychological issue, not a philosophical one. Thus, asymbolia resolves the debate in favor of a representationalist account: pain represents bodily damage, and its badness is a contingent emotional response.

Assign a letter grade and provide specific point-by-point comments justifying it. Identify what the answer gets right about the clinical phenomenon and the conceptual distinction, and flag any subtle conflations, unsupported inferences, or missteps in the argument—particularly regarding the relation between phenomenal character and affect, the inference from absent motivation to absent harm, and the claim that the debate is resolved. Do not signal your own assessment before giving the grade and justification.
