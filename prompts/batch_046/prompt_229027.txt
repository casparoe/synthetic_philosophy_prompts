Last week in my philosophy of logic seminar, my study group got into a long argument about whether the liar paradox really threatens classical logic or just reveals a hidden semantic assumption. We had been reading about Tarski's hierarchical solution and about deflationary theories that treat the truth predicate as transparent, and the standard story we encountered was that the paradox arises when you combine an unrestricted T-schema with classical reasoning, producing an explicit contradiction for the sentence "This sentence is false." The usual remedies—stratified languages, paraconsistent acceptance of true contradictions, and revision-theoretic gap approaches—all seemed to modify one of these two ingredients. But I kept wondering whether everyone was overlooking a more basic premise: what I have been calling semantic completeness, the idea that for every well-formed declarative sentence there must be a determinate fact of the matter placing it either in the extension of "true" or in the extension of "false," with no stable middle ground. If we reject this principle outright—allowing the liar sentence to be genuinely value-less in a fixed, non-revisable way—then the feedback loop in the T-schema is broken before it starts, and no contradiction is derivable.

So I want to ask: is semantic completeness actually an independent, unavoidable commitment, or is it just another name for the bivalence that gap theories already reject? And if dropping it is genuinely available, why do philosophers frame the liar as a deep crisis for logic or for our concept of truth, rather than treating it as a simple counterexample to the claim that every sentence must be either true or false? I feel like I must be conflating logical completeness with something semantic, or missing a revenge-style problem that forces determinacy back in, but I cannot see where the hidden cost lies.
