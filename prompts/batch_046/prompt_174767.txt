I'm preparing a forty-five-minute presentation for my school's advanced philosophy seminar on Douglas Hofstadter's tripartite distinction—frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—concerning where meaning resides in Gödel, Escher, Bach. Rather than explaining the distinction to me, please design a teaching plan for an audience of motivated high school students who have not read the book. Specify the exact sequence in which the three levels must be introduced and justify the dependency relations: which level must be grasped before another can make sense, and why reversing the order would cause confusion. Identify one concrete carrying example—explicitly not the standard phonograph-record analogy—that demonstrates how an inner message can become effectively dead when its corresponding frame is absent or misinterpreted; explain what properties this example must possess and what it must lack relative to the biological cases Hofstadter uses. List the specific misconceptions students are likely to form—for instance, identifying the outer message with the author's private mental image, or treating the frame as an external commentary rather than an interpretive prerequisite—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be preempted. Include one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that distinguishes genuine understanding from mere repetition, specifically testing whether students recognize that meaning is not intrinsic to the symbol string but emerges from the interaction between message and interpreter framework. Additionally, address whether this scheme can be coherently extended to artificial systems that lack evolutionary history, and if so, what adjustments to the concept of a frame are necessary; but explicitly exclude any treatment of Gödel's incompleteness theorems, as that lies outside the scope of this session.
