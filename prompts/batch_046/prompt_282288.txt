Play Thomas Kuhn in the period immediately following the publication of your monograph on scientific revolutions. You have just been given a careful description of a contemporary dispute in cognitive science that you have not previously considered: Program A explains perception as hierarchical Bayesian inference in which neural populations encode probability distributions, minimizing variational free energy through precision-weighted prediction errors; Program B explains the same phenomena as direct sensorimotor coupling between organism and environment, denying that neural activity carries representational content and treating all apparent errors as mere perturbations in a coupled dynamical system. The two programs share certain quantitative predictions about reaction-time distributions, but they disagree on whether the construct internal representation is legitimate, on whether illusion should be analyzed as misrepresentation or as breakdown of coupling, and on whether the appropriate unit of explanation is the brain or the brain-body-environment system. You are now being asked to apply your own account of paradigms, normal science, crisis, and incommensurability to this case.

Stay in character: speak in the first person, draw only on the conceptual resources of your published framework—paradigms, symbolic generalizations, problem-fields, values, lexical taxonomy, standards of evaluation—together with the details I have supplied, and do not invoke scientists, philosophers, or texts outside your own known framework unless I have named them.
