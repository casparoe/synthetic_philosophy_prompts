I'm preparing a long-form essay for a science and ideas publication on the enduring puzzles of formal epistemology, and I find myself returning to Fitch's paradox of knowability. In the standard formulation, if one accepts that every truth is in principle knowable, together with the seemingly innocuous principle that knowing a conjunction requires knowing each conjunct, one appears forced toward the radical conclusion that every truth is actually known—an outcome that conflicts sharply with our ordinary understanding of epistemic limitation. Most discussions in the literature focus on which premise to reject, but I want to press further into a somewhat less traveled corner: suppose we treat the paradox not merely as a tension between modal principles and epistemic modesty, but as a stress test for how we individuate epistemic states across possible worlds.

For my piece, I have identified five distinct strategies for dissolving or accommodating the paradox, and I would like your evaluative judgment on their relative philosophical merit, specifically in terms of how well they preserve both intuitive epistemic humility and the coherence of modal logic. The strategies are as follows: first, an outright rejection of the universal knowability principle, treating it as an overly optimistic relic of verificationist thinking; second, a domain restriction that applies the principle only to observationally accessible propositions; third, a revisionary modal semantics in which knowability is indexed not to actual agents but to an idealized epistemic community with unbounded but non-omniscient cognitive resources; fourth, a bold acceptance of the paradoxical conclusion coupled with a redefinition of unknown as not yet actualized in this particular world-branch; and fifth, a synthetic position—one I have been developing in conversation with colleagues—that treats unknowability itself as a second-order modal status rather than a first-order property of propositions, thereby blocking the derivation without abandoning either the knowability principle or standard conjunction closure.

Please rank these five strategies from 1 (most philosophically defensible) to 5 (least defensible), and provide a brief justification for each placement that addresses specifically whether the strategy avoids ad hoc stipulation and whether it respects our pre-theoretic intuition that there genuinely are truths no actual or ideal agent will ever grasp. If you wish, you may also assign each strategy a score between 0 and 10 on a scale of preserves epistemic humility without collapsing into skepticism, but the ranking itself is what I need most for the structure of my argument.
