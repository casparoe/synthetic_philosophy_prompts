I advise the acquisitions committee of a mid-size university art museum, and we need a reusable curatorial procedure for deciding whether to acquire or exhibit aesthetically distinguished works whose creators have documented serious moral failings. The committee meets monthly, has no staff ethicist, must reach a written decision within thirty minutes per case, and must be able to defend that decision to a public that conflates biography with endorsement. Please translate the philosophical debate between autonomism and moralism into an operational checklist: ordered questions with sufficiency conditions, clear stop points (approve, approve with framing conditions, defer, or reject), and explicit flags where the decision is irreducibly a value judgment rather than a factual determination.

To keep the procedure from collapsing into a generic “separate the art from the artist” formula, build it around three distinct contamination vectors: (1) internal moral error in the work’s representational or expressive content; (2) conditions-of-production harm, meaning direct exploitation, coercion, or violence enacted during the creation of the specific object; and (3) post-hoc biographical taint, meaning the risk that audiences will misread exhibition as institutional celebration of the artist’s moral failure, especially when institutional framing cannot reliably neutralize that attribution. Include a “framing audit” step that asks whether our specific gallery context—labeling, adjacency, programming, and revenue flow—can present the work without amplifying complicity.

Apply the full procedure to three cases:
- Case A: A formally innovative documentary film by a director later convicted of violent assault against a collaborator; the film depicts that collaborator’s trauma with critical distance and ethical seriousness, and licensing revenue would flow to the director’s estate.
- Case B: A non-representational abstract sculpture produced with state resources that relied on prison labor; the artist was an active propagandist for the regime that administered the labor, though the object carries no political signification.
- Case C: A gallery installation adapting a widely praised novel whose author publicly defended the moral permissibility of the harm narrated in the text; the installation reproduces the narrative imagery without critical reframing.

For each case, run the procedure in order and state the outcome. One of these outcomes must recommend acquisition or exhibition despite serious moral contamination—i.e., the answer the committee will find unwelcome. Then specify precisely what the procedure cannot settle, where it is likely to misfire, and which philosophical distinctions it deliberately collapses in order to be usable by non-specialists.

Write in American English, in a formal register appropriate to a faculty memorandum. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
