I hold a modified version of the afterlife conjecture: human extinction is bad not chiefly because of the deaths it entails or the loss of future welfare, but because it terminates what I will call the Arc—the multi-generational, collective rational project of extending the conditions for value-realization across deep time. On this view, present human lives are meaningfully embedded in this Arc as contributing nodes, and extinction represents not merely a welfare deficit but a specific failure mode of extended agency: the truncation of an intentional structure that requires biological-human continuity to maintain its normative identity. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Which empirical discoveries—about the actual coherence of long-term human planning, about the replaceability of human agency by artificial systems, or about the psychological mechanisms of meaning-formation—would undermine it? Which conceptual commitments, such as collective intentionality, a non-aggregative theory of diachronic agency, or the irreplaceability of biological continuity, are load-bearing, and what arguments could show they collapse? Would the view be defeated if it turned out that human projects are not genuinely directed at deep-time outcomes but are merely short-term preferences aggregated by cultural narrative? How should the position respond to the possibility that post-human successors could continue the Arc, and does that possibility rescue it or destroy its distinctive claim? Finally, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether the Arc Hypothesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a vice for a claim of this kind.
