I am working through a passage in the closing books of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (around Book X), where he distinguishes the life of practical virtue from the life of theoretical contemplation, arguing that the latter is the highest human activity because it is self-sufficient, continuous, and resembles the activity of the gods. Crucially, he adds that not every human being is suited to this life: some are better fitted by nature and circumstance for the active life of civic virtue, and flourishing therefore takes different forms depending on one's capacities and constitution. I want to understand how this bears on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism—especially his claim that moral theory can ask for one thought too many, as when a rescuer pauses to weigh his spouse's life against a stranger's before acting, and that such demands violate the agent's integrity because they treat constitutive commitments as fungible. Does Aristotle's functionalist picture—where the value of a personal life-plan derives from its fit with a universal human nature—offer an ancient anticipation of Williams's defense of ground projects? Or does it actually conflict with Williams, since Aristotle makes even the most personal commitments answerable to an objective account of human flourishing, whereas Williams treats them as non-derivable sources of normative authority? Please help me understand the passage's reasoning about the variability of the good life, and whether that reasoning strengthens, weakens, or transforms the modern objection that utilitarianism fails to take the separateness and particularity of persons seriously.
