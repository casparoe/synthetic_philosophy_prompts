As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of language, I want to apply Donald Davidson’s radical interpretation to an actual historical dispute rather than to a philosopher’s fiction. You are a professional philosopher of language with deep expertise in Davidson; please think carefully, avoid superficial answers, and begin with a brief brainstorm of the three core commitments of radical interpretation—charity, triangulation, and the rejection of conceptual-scheme incommensurability—stated in your own words.

Consider the real episode of the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi. The salient facts are these: representatives of the British Crown and numerous Māori chiefs signed two texts, one in English and one in Māori (te Tiriti o Waitangi). The English text states that the chiefs cede sovereignty fully to the Crown. The Māori text employs kawanatanga (governance) and guarantees the chiefs tino rangatiratanga (full chieftainship or sovereignty over their lands and affairs). Both parties apparently consented, yet the texts diverge sharply on the distribution of authority, and for nearly two centuries the disagreement has been framed either as deliberate deception or as good-faith mistranslation.

Analyze this episode through Davidson’s lens, running the analysis in both directions.

First, apply the theory to the case. Identify the interpreters and interpreted: who plays the role of speaker, who of radical interpreter, and what counts as the shared perceptual environment? Apply the principle of charity: to interpret the Māori chiefs as rational agents with mostly true beliefs, what must we attribute to them regarding sovereignty and land? Does charity compel the conclusion that they could not have intended to cede full sovereignty in the British sense, or can a shared concept be reconstructed despite the linguistic divergence? Examine triangulation: Davidson requires shared causal interaction with a common world. In this colonial setting, where the world in question is contested territory and power is radically asymmetrical, can triangulation be established between Crown and chiefs, or does the asymmetry break the conditions for interpretation? Address indeterminacy: Davidson allows that translation can be indeterminate. Is there a determinate fact of the matter about what kawanatanga meant in 1840, and if interpretation is genuinely indeterminate, how should a historian or court resolve the dispute?

Second, identify precisely what the model explains and what it misses. Where does Davidson illuminate the impossibility of incommensurable conceptual schemes separating British and Māori understandings? Where is the theory silent—particularly regarding institutional power, coercive enforcement of one textual reading, and the fact that one party subsequently controlled the archives and legal apparatus?

Third, test the theory with a non-standard extension. Davidson applies charity and triangulation primarily to individual speakers. Suppose we treat the British Crown and the collective iwi as corporate institutional agents capable of collective belief and meaning. Does radical interpretation scale to such entities? If charity must be applied to institutions rather than to individual persons, does the principle change, and does this reveal a boundary condition for Davidson’s framework?

Conclude with an honest, disciplined assessment: is the Treaty episode a confirmation of radical interpretation, an anomaly that exposes its limits, or a case the theory never anticipated? State your verdict clearly, and flag precisely where you are extending or speculating beyond Davidson’s explicit commitments.
