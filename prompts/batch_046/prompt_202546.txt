I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been studying philosophy of science, and I have been asked to help design a reusable review protocol for a practical decision-making body. I serve on the evaluation panel of a small regional foundation that funds public-facing scientific explanations—museum exhibit texts, policy briefs, and educational podcast scripts—where each proposal must defend a single central explanatory claim. The panelists are working scientists and communicators, not philosophers; each proposal receives roughly thirty minutes of review, and a panelist must personally sign off on a recommendation to fund or reject. The board wants a procedure that addresses the epistemic regress problem: every justification for a claim seems to require a further justification, leading to either circularity, an arbitrary stopping point, or an infinite chain. The procedure should translate the three standard philosophical responses—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—into operational rules that these reviewers can apply without philosophical training.

Please construct the procedure as follows. First, specify the role, constraints, and stakes in precise terms: the reviewers' scientific literacy but philosophical naivety, the thirty-minute limit, the public-accountability context, and the requirement that the reviewer take personal responsibility for the sign-off. Second, convert the three philosophical positions into a sequential, ordered checklist: define the exact question to ask at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer for that framework, and the specific point at which that framework permits the reviewer to stop asking how do you know that. For foundationalism, identify what would count as a basic belief or direct observational stopping point in this scientific-communication context; for coherentism, specify the breadth and consistency conditions the surrounding belief web must satisfy; for infinitism, specify the conditions under which an unending but non-circular chain is acceptable and when it forces deferral. Third, embed explicit decision nodes: states where the procedure outputs fund, reject, or defer to expert judgment, and clearly flag the nodes where the issue is not epistemic structure but a value judgment—such as whether it is permissible for a public explanation to rely on an unverified instrument calibration or to omit known alternative hypotheses. Fourth, apply the completed procedure to three worked proposals: (a) a brief arguing that a commercially available microplastic filter reduces water toxicity, supported only by a single small study and a manufacturer datasheet; (b) a brief claiming that a local wetland restoration improves regional water quality, justified entirely by an interlocking network of ecological simulation models with no direct pre-intervention baseline measurement; and (c) a brief asserting that a newly proposed Bayesian statistical correction eliminates systematic bias in historical climate reconstructions, where fully understanding the justification requires mathematical expertise that the reviewers do not possess, forcing a choice between accepting an expert testimony chain and rejecting the claim for opacity.

For each case, show what the procedure outputs when run under a foundationalist stopping rule, a coherentist stopping rule, and an infinitist stopping rule. Identify the case where all three frameworks converge on the same recommendation and the case where they diverge so sharply that the procedure must declare an irreconcilable conflict and hand the decision to the reviewer's personal judgment—including at least one case where the output is one I, as a funding board member, will find unwelcome. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which failure modes of justification it will miss, which philosophical disputes it cannot adjudicate, and under what conditions a reviewer must abandon the checklist and issue a bare judgment call. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for a scientist addressing interdisciplinary colleagues, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; paraphrase any named theoretical concepts in your own words rather than quoting them.
