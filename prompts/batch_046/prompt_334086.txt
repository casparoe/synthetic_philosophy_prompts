I hold the view that Imperial Germany bears primary moral—not merely causal—responsibility for the First World War in the strong Fischer sense: that its leadership pursued a calculated risk-policy of escalation in July 1914, and that attributing blame to the nation as a collective is conceptually coherent rather than a category mistake. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by decisiveness: not just the standard diplomatic counter-narratives (Serbian initiative, alliance-system rigidity, defensive readings of the Schlieffen Plan), but the deeper conceptual defeaters—about whether collective agency requires a shared intentionality that nations lack, whether responsibility dissipates across bureaucratic layers and non-complicit civilian populations, and whether the distinction between causing and being culpable for a war collapses once distributed over millions. Which of these objections would defenders of the Fischer thesis concede as genuinely counting against them, and which would they dismiss as misframing the nature of state action? Does the view risk anything empirical—does it predict specific patterns in the archival record of decision-making that rival interpretations do not—or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? And if it has been insulated by ever-narrower specifications of what “deliberate risk” requires, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind?
