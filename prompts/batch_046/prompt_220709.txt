I recently served on a nonpartisan municipal redistricting commission and watched party operatives treat every procedural disagreement as a loyalty test rather than a matter of justice, an experience that made Simone Weil's political writings feel urgently personal rather than merely historical. I have become convinced by her argument—developed in her essay calling for the abolition of all political parties—that parties do not simply organize preferences but actively manufacture collective passions and suppress the individual's capacity for careful attention to particular truths. I want to present at an upcoming democratic-theory seminar a thesis that extends this abolitionist claim into our present environment: modern partisan identity, I will argue, now operates as a form of costly signaling in the precise sense of the handicap principle, where members display loyalty through cognitively expensive and increasingly extreme ideological performances, transforming parties from organizational vehicles into epistemic hazards that destroy the conditions for genuine deliberation. From this I intend to argue that democratic institutions should abandon party lists in favor of sortition-based assemblies that treat partisan affiliation as an obstacle to judgment rather than a mechanism of representation.

The audience will be composed primarily of deliberative democrats and institutional political scientists who accept that parties need reform but consider abolition naive and dangerous; they care deeply about democratic accountability, preference aggregation, and practical feasibility, and they will be joined by one historian of twentieth-century French thought who knows Weil's mystical and religious framework intimately. They will likely object that I am smuggling her theological concepts—her ideas of gravity and attention—into secular institutional design without adequate justification, that I mistake American-style polarization for a universal feature of party politics, and that I underestimate how organizational necessity constrains any realistic alternative. They will want concrete answers about how accountability survives without parties, whether costly signaling is an appropriate analytical lens for collective political behavior, and where exactly my institutional proposal stops being philosophy and starts being fantasy.
