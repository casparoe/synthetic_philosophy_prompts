Question: Philosophers often distinguish an excuse (the act was wrong, but blame is reduced) from a justification (the act wasn't wrong at all). Apply that distinction to the 'man of his time' defense for three figures: Thomas Jefferson's ownership of enslaved people, Winston Churchill's imperial policies and racial hierarchy beliefs, and Christopher Columbus's violent extraction and enslavement of Taíno populations. In particular, discuss whether lacking modern moral concepts in one's era works as an excuse, a justification, or neither, and whether the defense applies differently when the agent had access to live counter-arguments (like Jefferson with abolitionist writing in Virginia) versus when a concept like indigenous sovereignty was absent from European legal frameworks altogether.

Student answer:
The distinction matters a lot. An excuse admits harm but lowers blame; a justification denies harm was wrong. For Jefferson it's mostly an excuse, but a weak one—Quaker and abolitionist texts were circulating in Virginia, so he had epistemic access to counter-views and ignored them. That doesn't justify slavery, but it does mean he can't fully hide behind his era.

Churchill is more complicated. He operated inside a democratic state and a scientific-racial framework that was widely shared, so he gets some excusing cover for imperial violence and famine. Actually, though, if you weigh the railways and legal codes he brought against the repression, you could say his empire-building was on balance justified by outcomes. So he's less blameworthy than Jefferson, partly because he didn't personally profit and partly because the net result was positive.

Columbus is the extreme case. In 1492 European law had no category for indigenous sovereignty, so Columbus literally couldn't have formed the intent to violate rights that didn't exist in his conceptual scheme. Without the concept, there's no moral wrong to excuse—it's justification by default, like blaming a hurricane. So calling his actions genocide is conceptually confused, and renaming monuments is unfair because blame requires a shared moral vocabulary that wasn't available then.
