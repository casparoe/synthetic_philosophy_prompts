I am currently taking an undergraduate course in philosophy of language, and I have encountered a disagreement with my flatmate that I cannot resolve with the tools we have covered so far. We both use the term "solidarity" with what feels like an identical mental picture: a form of mutual commitment that binds people together in shared purpose. Yet I was raised in a community where the word is anchored almost exclusively to labour-organising and collective bargaining, while my flatmate was raised in one where it denotes primarily cross-border charitable obligation. In our seminar, we analysed a case in which a workers' cooperative refuses to outsource production to cheaper overseas labour. I argued that the cooperative acts in solidarity by preserving internal loyalty; she argued that true solidarity requires donating the surplus to the overseas workers they refused to employ. Our tutor suggested that we are not merely disagreeing about a case, but that the word itself may carry different contents for each of us because of the social and linguistic environments in which we were formed. My flatmate now claims that what I mean by "solidarity" in this essay is determined not by my internal understanding but by the conventions of the academic community I am writing for. I find this deeply counter-intuitive: if my mental concept is clear to me, how can my environment override it? Could you help me see whether her position is philosophically defensible, and whether there is a coherent way to hold that meaning is partly fixed outside the head? Please respond in British English.
