I am a novelist currently drafting a novel about a forensic archivist named Elara who reconstructs destroyed state records. In the course of her work she uncovers evidence that she herself set the fire that destroyed her childhood home, a fire she has always sincerely remembered as accidental lightning. I have been discussing with a friend who studies philosophy of mind whether Elara’s condition counts as genuine self-deception, and I found myself dissatisfied with the usual explanations that treat it as one part of her mind lying to another. I want to build the character’s psychology on something more structurally interesting.

Please argue that self-deception is not only possible but necessarily involves a form of epistemic compartmentalization: rather than a single unified subject simultaneously believing and disbelieving the same proposition, the mind distributes contradictory representations across distinct functional roles—much as I, in writing fiction, maintain contradictory notes about a character’s motivations without resolving them into one coherent belief. Defend the view that belief itself should be understood not as a binary mental state but as a graded, context-dependent disposition, and show how this dissolves the classic paradox of self-deception. I need the argument to be rigorous enough that I could use its architecture to justify Elara’s sincere protestations of innocence—she genuinely lacks access in one functional mode to what she knows in another—without reducing her to mere hypocrisy or theatrical bad faith. I am not asking for a bibliography, citations, or references to specific theorists; I want the reasoning developed from the ground up, in a way that illuminates both the philosophical problem and the particular psychology of my character.
