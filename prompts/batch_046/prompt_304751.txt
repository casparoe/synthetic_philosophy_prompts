2. during sleep, prefrontel monitoring is mostly offline, so there's no functional "i" present to do that identifying. so if dreams occur during sleep, they violate the constraint — they're subject-less experiences.

3. dennett avoids this with his cassette idea: the brain writes the narative during sleep but the actual experiencing only happens when it's played back upon waking.

4. but then the "experience" is really just a present-tense seeming-memory, experienced now, with no independent check on whether it tracks anything that actually happened in sleep.

5. here's the niche twist — call it the retroactive binding problem: if we allow that an experience can be fully constituted after the fact, with its content fixed by a narrative bound to the waking self, then we have to ask what stops us from applying the same logic to waking perception.

6. waking perception is also reconstructed with delays, predictive filling-in, etc., so by dennett's lights it's also a post-hoc narrative loaded into consciousness.

7. if both dream-narratives and waking perceptions are just post-hoc constructions, then dennett can't distinguish them: either both are veridical experiences of reality, or both are confabulations with no world-contact.

8. so if you want to save dreams as experiences without falling into full-blown skepticism about waking life, you have to reject the constraint in step 2 — but then you lose the reason to think sleep-state mental events are different from unconscious brain processes.

9. therefore either malcolm is right and dreams aren't experiences, or we have to give up first-person accessibility for all consciousness.
