Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on whether culture—specifically value-rational orientations—can operate as an independent causal factor in historical explanation, taking Max Weber’s thesis on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism as the focal case but extending the analysis to the general problem of explaining social outcomes with values.

Camp A (the value-causalist) holds that certain cultural value-orientations—such as the ascetic, inner-worldly rationalization Weber describes—possess explanatory autonomy. They maintain that actors can orient conduct to the subjective meaning of a value itself, initiating or redirecting economic and institutional change in ways not reducible to material conditions, legal structures, or selective retention, and that this orientation functions as a primitive causal node in the explanatory narrative.

Camp B (the institutional-mediationist) holds that all apparent causal work performed by values is fully mediated by organizational incentives, resource distributions, and institutional scaffolding. They maintain that values are best treated as post-hoc rationalizations, selection-criteria, or descriptive classifications of behavior already determined by other mechanisms, and that explanatory adequacy requires every causal pathway involving values to be redescribable in non-intentional, mechanistically transparent terms.

The document should be structured as follows:

1. Agreed claims. State at least four propositions both camps can sign regarding: (a) the formal distinction between value-rational and instrumentally rational action; (b) the empirical correlation Weber identified between certain ascetic Protestant denominations and patterns of early capitalist accumulation; (c) the inadequacy of purely materialist explanations that ignore interpretive frames entirely; and (d) the conditions under which a cultural factor counts as genuinely causal rather than epiphenomenal.

2. Precise disagreements. Phrase each camp’s core claim in wording that the opposing camp would accept as an accurate representation of its own view. Include the disagreement over whether world-rejection functions as a causal mechanism or as a descriptive classification of behavior already determined by other mechanisms, and the disagreement over whether explanatory adequacy requires that value-orientations be eliminable in principle from the causal narrative.

3. Cruxes. Identify at least three sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. Suggested cruxes include: (i) whether two societies with identical resource endowments and institutional frameworks but divergent interpretations of frugality can exhibit systematically different accumulation patterns; (ii) whether predictive models that include value-orientations as variables outperform structurally identical models that substitute institutional-incentive proxies; and (iii) whether the concept of explanatory autonomy for values can be rendered compatible with a mechanistic account of social causation without collapsing into dualism.

4. Test case and predictions. Present a concrete comparative scenario—two monastic economies in the twelfth century with identical land tenure, technology, labor availability, and market access, but one interpreting ascetic discipline as a means of spiritual merit and the other as a technique of communal survival. Specify what evidence both camps agree would be relevant (for example, differential patterns of capital reinvestment, labor discipline, or inter-monastic trade), and record in advance what each camp predicts about the outcome and about whether the interpretive difference does any irreducible explanatory work that institutional variables cannot capture.

Conclude by assessing whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—particularly regarding whether causation by values is defined so narrowly that Camp B’s mechanistic redescriptions automatically exclude it by fiat, or so broadly that Camp A’s claims become untestable. The tone should be formal, constructive, and free of rhetorical appeals to authority. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
