At a seminar on synthetic biology and metaphysics, two participants—Mira and Leo—disagreed about whether a de novo engineered minimal cell, call it Cell-9, counts as alive. Cell-9 was built from scratch: its genome employs a synthetic backbone chemistry absent from natural organisms, it maintains homeostatic membranes, metabolizes a provided nutrient medium, and replicates with mutation rates within bacterial ranges, yet it possesses no evolutionary ancestry, was explicitly designed by researchers, and its replication fidelity is tuned so high that adaptive variation is structurally suppressed.

Mira opened by saying Cell-9 is clearly alive. It satisfies the core functional markers—boundary maintenance, metabolism, and reproduction—without needing an evolutionary pedigree. She added that requiring descent with modification excludes viruses, which are borderline but generally treated as biological entities, and that insisting on an evolutionary origin confuses how something came to be with what it is.

Leo replied that this misses the point of the evolutionary criterion. Viruses share evolutionary histories with hosts; Cell-9 shares none with anything. He argued that reproduction without open-ended evolutionary potential is just mechanized copying—like a precision manufacturing system reproducing its own components—so the functional markers are insufficient. Life, he said, is a historical kind defined by participation in an evolutionary lineage, not a checklist of capacities.

Mira countered that Leo’s view makes the first living cell impossible to classify, since it too lacked evolutionary ancestors. She said that if evolutionary history is necessary, then either we deny life to the origin case—which is absurd—or we concede that the criterion applies only derivatively, which undermines its use here. She also noted that Cell-9 does exhibit mutation, so evolutionary potential is present, just constrained; a constrained lineage is still a lineage.

Leo answered that the first cell emerged through chemical evolution, not design, so its membership in the historical kind was secured by the process that produced it. Design, by contrast, places Cell-9 in the category of artifacts. He granted that mutation occurs, but said it is noise tolerated by the machinery, not variation upon which selection can act, because the fidelity tuning suppresses heritable phenotypic differences. Without selectable variation, there is no evolutionary process, only replication error.

Mira then argued that Leo is smuggling in essentialism about origins. A synthetic heart transplanted into a patient is still a heart; its artificial provenance does not change its functional kind. By parallel reasoning, Cell-9’s designed origin should not override its biological functioning. She concluded that Leo’s position collapses into the claim that life requires a particular causal history, which is a genetic fallacy applied to ontology.

Leo replied that the heart analogy fails precisely because hearts do not reproduce. Reproduction with heredity is what makes evolutionary history relevant. He also said that Mira’s functionalism is too permissive: a flame maintains boundaries and metabolizes fuel, yet no one calls it alive. To avoid over-inclusion, functionalists must either add hidden conditions—which often turn out to be evolutionary ones—or accept that life is a graded cluster concept with no sharp boundary, in which case calling Cell-9 “alive” is just a conventional labeling decision rather than a discovery.

They turned to me and asked for an adjudication. Can you work through their exchange point by point, indicating where each is correct, where they talk past each other, and whether either is missing a relevant distinction—such as between necessary and sufficient conditions, or between life as a property and life-form as a kind? Then say who is more right overall, and whether there is a coherent middle position that does not merely split the difference by stipulation.
