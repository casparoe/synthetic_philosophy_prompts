At our department’s strategic planning session, two senior colleagues, Maya and Jonas, argued about the administration’s proposal to demolish the old Faculty Commons — a 1962 building that originally served as an exclusive faculty club, now has no formal purpose, but for decades has operated as an informal, cross-rank meeting space and holds an unsorted local archive. The structure has serious asbestos and structural decay, yet no replacement space is planned.

Maya said tearing it down violates Chesterton’s fence: the building stands for reasons we no longer fully grasp, and its present social role — neutral ground outside administrative oversight — is exactly the kind of embedded practice that should not be destroyed for bureaucratic convenience. She added that Burkean conservatism warns against rationalist redesign; we cannot blueprint a replacement commons because social tissue grows organically, not by administrative plan.

Jonas replied that Chesterton’s fence concerns institutional reforms — legal and social structures — not every aging physical artifact. Burke’s idea of prescription applies to living practices and rights, not to brick and mortar that has outlived its original function. The Commons’ original purpose was exclusionary; its current use is accidental. If we preserve the social function by designating another informal space, we honor the fence without fetishizing the container.

Maya countered that Burke’s prescription is broader than legal title: any arrangement that has acquired authority through long usage becomes part of the social fabric. The Commons has been a de facto institution for forty years. To call its value “accidental” applies an abstract category — original purpose — that ignores how institutions actually evolve. She also argued that Jonas’s “relocate the function” proposal is the rationalist fantasy Burke criticized: assuming social goods are modular and can be replanned without loss.

Jonas responded that preserving every building that acquires incidental use would paralyze necessary change. The Burkean concern is destroying living practices, not maintaining specific containers. If we intentionally recreate the commons elsewhere, we satisfy the fence. Insisting on this particular building confuses symbol with substance — a sentimental error associated with those who mistake physical relics for living tradition. He further noted that the building’s active hazards make maintenance irresponsible, and conservatism does not require preserving corruption or physical danger.

Maya conceded the hazards matter but said they are being used as pretexts: the administration’s argument relies on abstract efficiency metrics, the kind of reasoning Burke criticized. She asked whether Jonas could offer a principled line between legitimate removal and destructive rationalism, or whether his view collapses into “demolish whenever a replacement can be imagined.”

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each contribution by Maya and Jonas, assess whether it applies Burkean conservatism and Chesterton’s fence fairly, whether it mischaracterizes the other position, and whether it introduces a distinction that actually matters. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing — for instance, whether there is a distinctively Burkean argument for demolition when an institution has become corrupt or dangerous, or whether Chesterton’s fence has clear limits that neither has acknowledged. Evaluate the arguments exactly as presented.
