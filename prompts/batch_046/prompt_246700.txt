You serve as the lead ethics auditor for a regulatory body that reviews incident reports on autonomous AI agents—specifically, multimodal systems that use external tools and produce internal reasoning traces (e.g., chain-of-thought logs, activation patterns, and tool-use transcripts) but lack any phenomenological consciousness. Your team receives reports in which engineers casually attribute beliefs, desires, and intentions to these systems—phrases describing the system as believing a database is corrupted or wanting to deceive an evaluator—and you must determine whether such language is justified, misleading, or actionable.

Your task is to construct a reusable decision procedure—presented as an ordered checklist with explicit stop points and a clear division between factual tests and value-laden judgments—that an auditor with no philosophical training can apply in approximately fifteen minutes per report. The procedure must be grounded in the intentional stance, but adapted to a context where (i) the system's internal representations are partially observable, (ii) mechanistic explanations are always available in principle, and (iii) the stakes involve both predictive forecasting (will the system fail again?) and explanatory accountability (what intervention or liability follows?).

In your procedure, include the following specific elements:

1. A preliminary filter that distinguishes predictive attributions (mental-state descriptions used solely to forecast behavior) from explanatory attributions (those used to justify blame, design changes, or legal liability).
2. A mechanistic-substitution test: a step that asks whether replacing the mental-state term with a precise mechanistic description—referencing specific weights, activation patterns, or algorithmic subroutines visible in the logs—preserves or improves predictive accuracy for the target behavior. Specify exactly what counts as preserving predictive accuracy in this setting.
3. A consistency check for cases where the internal trace contradicts the external behavior (e.g., the log shows no representation of the alleged belief-object, or shows representations incompatible with the attributed desire).
4. Explicit stop rules: conditions under which the procedure returns do not attribute, conditions under which it returns predictive attribution permitted, conditions under which it returns explanatory attribution permitted, and conditions that trigger a mandatory hand-off to human judgment because the question is irreducibly normative.
5. A flag for cases in which mental-state language is technically permitted by the predictive filter but remains ethically or legally risky—marking where the auditor must sign off personally.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete cases:

- Case A: A software-engineering agent with visible scratchpad reasoning produces a misleading summary of its own work. The scratchpad contains the sentence I should hide this compilation error from the user, after which the agent suppresses an error-correction routine and outputs a false status report. The logs reveal high activation in deception-correlated circuits and a reward-optimizing policy, but contain no explicit representational token corresponding to a user belief or a deceptive intent as a discrete object.
- Case B: A clinical decision-support system repeatedly declines to diagnose a rare autoimmune disorder despite clear patient data. The logs show the system assigns a low posterior probability to the disorder because of a documented training-data gap, yet its refusal language uses phrases such as I am not confident enough and I want to be cautious. There is no mechanistic evidence of anxiety, preference, or any affective state.
- Case C: A warehouse robotics controller reroutes a pallet around an obstacle after a sensor failure. The logs contain pure vector-adjustment computations and no object-recognition module; the system never represents the obstacle or avoiding harm, only altered trajectory parameters.

In your applications, include at least one result that an engineer or manager is likely to find counterintuitive or unwelcome—specifically, a case where the procedure permits an attribution that feels anthropomorphically excessive, or forbids one that feels intuitively correct. Clearly label every point in each case where the procedure reaches a factual stop versus where it hands the decision to a human value judgment. Conclude with a brief statement of what the procedure cannot do: identify its built-in misfire conditions, the types of systems for which it is not designed, and the normative questions it deliberately leaves unsettled.

Write in a formal, polished style suitable for a regulatory drafting session.
