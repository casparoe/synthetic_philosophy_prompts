I recently encountered a summary of Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator that struck me as partly right but possibly distorted. It says that moral judgment begins with sympathy—an imaginative projection into another's situation—and that because our sympathy is naturally partial, we correct it by appealing to an impartial spectator. According to this summary, Smith treats the spectator as essentially an internalized social audience: we picture a specific observer whose anticipated approval or blame shapes our conduct, so moral development is largely social conditioning. The summary further claims that because the spectator has direct access only to outward behavior, Smith ultimately grounds judgments of propriety in observable actions and consequences rather than in motives, making his view closer to conventionalist rule-following than to an intuitionist ethics of virtue. How accurate is this characterization? Please assess it claim by claim, distinguishing what is fair from what is subtly wrong or overstated, and in particular clarify whether Smith's spectator is really a specific internalized observer and whether he subordinates motive to conduct in the way described. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
