As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been reflecting on the norms that govern when it is acceptable to assert something in a technical or professional setting, and I find my intuitions do not line up neatly with any single rule I have encountered. I should like to present several concrete verdicts and ask whether a coherent principle lies behind them, or whether I must revise one or two. First, if a senior engineer states during a stand-up that a database migration will complete without data loss, yet she has not run the full regression suite and is relying chiefly on confidence, I feel she has spoken improperly: she does not know the outcome, and high confidence alone does not license the assertion. Second, if the same engineer says instead that she believes the migration will succeed, based on partial testing, and she later turns out to be wrong, that strikes me as entirely proper; being mistaken does not make her assertion blameworthy. Third, when a colleague tells a client that an API will support a requested feature by the third quarter, knowing that the roadmap is speculative and that she personally doubts it, I think she has done something wrong regardless of whether the feature eventually ships; the impropriety seems tied to her mental state rather than to the eventual truth. Fourth, if a team lead asserts that a particular bug lies in the caching layer after examining logs, and the root cause is later found in the message queue, I do not regard him as having spoken improperly, provided he genuinely held that belief on reasonable evidence; being wrong here feels quite different from guessing or misrepresenting. Fifth, there is a case that continues to trouble me: an engineer writes in official documentation that the system employs AES-256 encryption because she has read the specification and trusts the implementer, though she has never personally audited the source code. If the specification is correct, her assertion seems acceptable; but if the implementer has secretly deployed AES-128, I feel she is not blameworthy in the same way as the colleague who misled the client, yet she also did not strictly know the fact for herself. Is there a single norm of assertion—perhaps governed by knowledge, by truth, or by justified belief—that can accommodate all five judgments? Or does consistency require me to abandon one? I should also welcome your principle’s verdict on a further case I have not listed: an engineer asserts that a deployed neural-network classifier is “fair” because she has verified statistical parity on the training data, but she has not examined the deployment environment where demographic shifts occur. Does this assertion violate the norm, and if so, on what precise grounds?
