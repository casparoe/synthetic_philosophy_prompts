I'm running a workshop on philosophy of AI for about 12 grad students, 75 min. I want a game or structured activity that makes them feel the frame problem in their bones—not just understand the concept but actually experience the pressure of not knowing which background conditions to preserve when an action happens.

Here's the niche framing: instead of robots, they're "archival curators" maintaining a shared institutional record (a fictional museum archive). They take turns performing an "action" (moving an artifact, updating access rules, or firing a security guard) and then must update a shared whiteboard of "facts that remain true." But they only get 3 update slots per action, and there's a hidden relevence deck that determines which background conditions actually matter for the next round—like the fact that firing the guard changes humidity control access because he was the only one with the climate-room key. If they miss a critical preservation, the archive "corrupts" and they lose collective points. If they try to preserve everything, they blow past the slot limit and also lose points for "computational overload."

Design the full rules, roles (maybe split into curators and auditors), materials (paper, cards, whitebord), payoff/penalty scheme with no real money, timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps the failure modes onto the formal distinction between the frame problem and the qualification problem. Be frank about what feature—probably the relevance problem—this setup cant reproduce and you'll need to supply verbally.
