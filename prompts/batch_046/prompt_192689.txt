I cover science and ideas, and at a recent symposium on synthetic taxonomy and engineered ecosystems a panelist kept invoking the refrain that species are individuals, not classes, using it to dismiss any attempt to classify a chimeric, gene-edited organism alongside its wild-type progenitors. The slogan seemed to do heavy lifting—settling ontological, methodological, and even legal questions in one breath—yet I am unsure which precise claim was actually being advanced. Could you lay out the distinct theses hiding inside that one-liner, treating them as a menu rather than a single doctrine: for example, the spatiotemporal-continuity thesis about lineage individuation versus class membership; the methodological thesis about how biologists ought to delimit reproductive communities when horizontal gene transfer breaks tree-like descent; the epistemic thesis that species terms refer to particular entities rather than to sets with essential properties; and any normative thesis about whether conservation or biosafety law should count species as discrete, non-divisible units? For each reading, indicate whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or false, and specify which of those readings actually supports dismissing taxonomic questions about chimeric or horizontally-transferring organisms. I want the disambiguation sharp enough to take back to the session, not a lecture anchored to one interpretation.
