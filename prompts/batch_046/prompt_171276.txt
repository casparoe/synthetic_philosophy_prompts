I am drafting an essay that has become urgently personal after a recent rupture in a long-standing professional collaboration. A colleague with whom I shared years of mutual trust failed to honor a commitment that carried serious consequences for me; when I expressed resentment, they responded not with apology or moral justification, but with a detached account of severe dissociation and cognitive overload that, in their own description, left them unable to recognize the commitment's binding force at the time. This experience has led me to examine Strawson's well-known essay on freedom and resentment, and I would welcome your comments on the argument I am developing.

In the standard reading, moral agents either occupy a participant stance—engaging in resentment, gratitude, and forgiveness—or they are viewed through an objective stance, as objects of treatment, management, or clinical explanation. My experience, however, suggests a structural possibility that this binary obscures: what I call asymmetric reactive suspension. I remain fully within the participant stance, experiencing genuine resentment that presupposes my colleague as a responsible interlocutor, while my colleague, without having deliberately chosen withdrawal, now occupies an objective stance toward themselves. They do not seek forgiveness; they seek clinical understanding. This creates a mismatch in which one party's reactive attitude is directed toward a self that no longer presents itself as a moral agent.

My essay proposes that this asymmetry reveals a neglected temporal and relational dimension in Strawsonian responsibility. Reactive attitudes are not merely present or absent; they can become desynchronized across the parties to a relationship. I suggest that moral responsibility should be analyzed not only as an internal condition for the appropriateness of reactive attitudes, but also as a form of intersubjective synchronization. When one party's reactive capacities are involuntarily suspended—through trauma, mental illness, or extreme stress—the continued resentment of the other party may become inappropriate, not because determinism undermines all responsibility, but because the relational conditions for shared moral agency have broken down.

I tentatively argue for a triadic taxonomy in place of the binary: a full participant stance; a symmetric objective stance, in which withdrawal is deliberate and mutual; and asymmetric suspension, in which one party remains reactive while the other is objectively self-alienated. The essay concludes that recognizing this third category has implications not only for interpersonal morality but also for institutional practices—such as workplace accountability or the assessment of legal excuse—where we must determine whether an agent's failure stems from a suspended stance rather than a simple absence of moral capacity.

I would be particularly grateful for your comments on three matters. First, does this interpretation distort Strawson's original framework, or can it be defended as a legitimate conceptual extension? Second, is the notion of relational synchronization philosophically coherent, or does it risk collapsing into a merely psychological observation about mismatched expectations? Third, how might I sharpen the argument so that it addresses institutional and legal contexts without sacrificing the precision of its conceptual distinctions?

Please also feel free to suggest any other objections, structural improvements, or points of clarification that I may have overlooked.
