You are advising a community mental-health clinic where intake psychologists conduct eight to ten fifty-minute assessments weekly. They must document an evidential rationale for every risk-related determination, and a supervisor must sign off whenever a clinician discounts or overrides a patient’s first-person report. The staff have no philosophical training, but they constantly face one practical question: how much weight should they assign to a patient’s introspective report—of current anxiety, absence of suicidal ideation, emotional numbness, or the qualitative feel of a perceptual disturbance—when behavioral observation, file history, or (rarely) collateral input conflict with it?

Turn the philosophy of introspection into an ordered, reusable decision procedure they can run in session. Ground it in the distinction between direct access to occurrent phenomenal states and reconstructed or dispositional self-knowledge; in the conditions that generate confabulation, incentive distortion, and failures of emotional granularity; and in the idea that first-person reports can function in three epistemic modes—direct registration, reconstructed narrative, or social performance—each carrying different evidential weight. The deliverable should specify: the sequence of questions; what answers are sufficient at each step; the exact points where the procedure outputs “weight fully,” “weight with corroboration,” “discount,” or “escalate to supervisor”; the hard cases it must handle; and the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be flagged for human decision with documentation.

Fix the practical constraints: fifty-minute session, high stakes for risk assessment, required documentation, mandatory supervisor sign-off for any negative-risk report that is discounted.

Apply the procedure to three intake scenarios:
(i) A patient offers a precise qualitative description of a panic episode (“cold pressure rising behind the sternum”) with no behavioral contradiction.
(ii) A patient recently hospitalized for self-injury denies all current suicidal ideation; affect is restricted, sleep and appetite reports are internally inconsistent with the denial, and no collateral is available.
(iii) A patient from a community with limited emotion vocabulary states, following bereavement, that “my heart is heavy and dark”; it is ambiguous whether this is a somatic anxiety report, a grief metaphor, or a culturally specific idiom with no direct clinical equivalent.

One case should produce an answer the clinician will not welcome—likely a mandatory escalation or documentation burden that removes autonomy.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—e.g., resolve cultural translation without external consultation, or substitute for judgments about therapeutic alliance.

Separate, in your answer, what is broadly settled in the philosophy of mind (for instance, that occurrent phenomenal reports have a different epistemic status than long-term dispositional self-attributions) from what is genuinely contested (such as the extent of unconscious emotional access, whether transparency ever fully obtains, or how severely social framing undermines first-person authority). Do not present a disputed position as the consensus view.
