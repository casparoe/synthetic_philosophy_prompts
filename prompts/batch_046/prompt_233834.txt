At a small city's historical society, two board members—Nina and Tom—are disputing whether to accept and fully preserve the donated papers of Miriam Voss, a recently deceased planner whose affordable-housing reforms remain in force but whose private journals, audio recordings, and an interactive decision-model trained on her administrative choices document sustained emotional abuse of junior colleagues.

Nina argues three points. First, rejecting or redacting any portion repeats the historical erasure of harm; the victims' suffering deserves archival witness, not concealment. Second, the community needs an integrated shared memory—progress and cruelty together—because separating them produces a false moral narrative that serves the powerful. Third, if curators are permitted to decide which parts of a life are "too abusive" to preserve, the institution becomes an arbiter of moral cleanliness rather than history, and the line between preservation and sanitization collapses.

Tom responds with three counter-points. First, full preservation—especially maintaining the interactive model and private recordings—institutionalizes Voss's abuse by embedding it in a public resource that future users must navigate as part of her legitimate legacy. Second, the community can remember her structural achievements through the buildings and policy records alone; preserving her private cruelty is commemorative, not necessary for historical understanding, and strategic forgetting of abusive details can be a form of moral repair for the living. Third, Nina's position confuses remembering with perpetual exposure: a universal duty to preserve every trace binds every future generation to live inside the archive of past cruelty.

Nina replies that Tom's distinction between "structural records" and "private cruelty" assumes a clean separation that does not exist—her policies and her abuse operated through the same administrative mechanisms—so redacting one necessarily distorts the other. Tom answers that this proves too much: if every mechanism of harm must be archived in full, then no institution could ever limit the memorial footprint of any wrongdoer, which means the living are permanently subordinated to the dead.

Then state who is more right overall, and identify the specific philosophical distinction—between caring for particular victims' memories and imposing a universal duty to remember, and between archival preservation and public commemoration—that both speakers invoke but neither applies consistently. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; reason from the structure of the arguments themselves.
