Since democratic legitimacy requires both, preference aggregation for constitutional AI is conceptually impossible without a hidden dictator.

Participant B replied that this overextends the theorem. In practice, alignment values are not drawn from an unrestricted universal domain; they are constrained by logical coherence conditions (for instance, a principle cannot simultaneously require full interpretability and maximal capability gain). Once the domain is restricted to coherent subsets, non-manipulable rules can exist—median-voter outcomes over single-peaked dimensions being one example.

Participant A countered that those coherence conditions are themselves designed by the institution running the aggregation. Restricting the domain to what is “logically coherent” is merely letting the mechanism designer act as a hidden dictator, selecting which preferences are admissible. The theorem then applies at the meta-level: any rule for selecting coherence conditions is itself manipulable.

Participant B answered that this meta-level objection confuses object-level aggregation with constitutional design. The coherence constraints are not preferences but feasibility conditions, analogous to budget constraints in economic voting. Applying Gibbard–Satterthwaite to feasibility sets is a category error.

Participant A then said that, even granting that distinction, real-world stakeholder preferences over alignment values are demonstrably multi-dimensional and cyclically ordered—surveys show intransitive majority preferences among safety, autonomy, and performance. Without universal domain, one must either restrict preferences artificially or accept manipulation. So the theorem still bites.

Participant B concluded that the presence of cyclical majorities only shows direct democracy is unstable, not that deliberative mechanisms with structured option spaces are impossible. The theorem proves impossibility within a specific formal framework; it does not prove impossibility for any institutionally embedded process.
