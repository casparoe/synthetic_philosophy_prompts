Two colleagues in my interdisciplinary seminar—Jordan and Maya—have reached an impasse over whether the memetics framework genuinely extends Darwinian evolutionary theory into culture, or whether the analogy dissolves upon closer mechanical inspection. Jordan defended a strict replicator view by pointing to the rapid variation and selective retention of a four-panel image macro: the textual overlay mutates in seemingly random ways, audience engagement functions as differential selection pressure, and the variants that generate the most remixes proliferate, all without any central designer directing the specific mutations. Maya conceded the surface resemblance but advanced three deeper objections. First, she argued that memetic transmission is essentially Lamarckian—creators deliberately revise a meme in anticipation of audience response before transmitting it—whereas genetic evolution operates without foresight. Jordan replied that horizontal gene transfer and stress-induced mutation in bacteria show blindness is not universal even in biology, and that cultural foresight is merely accelerated selection rather than a different kind of process. Second, Maya claimed that cultural replicators lack discrete unit boundaries: a "meme" blends with others in ways that do not preserve identity, more like mixing pigments than copying a gene. Jordan countered that gene interaction and regulatory networks produce similarly blended phenotypes, so blending alone does not break the replicator framework. Third, Maya introduced what she called "memetic niche construction": unlike genes adapting to pre-existing environments, successful memes actively restructure the cognitive environment—shaping attention economies, altering platform algorithms, and generating new social norms—such that the selection environment is itself a product of prior memetic success. She argued this creates a circularity absent from genetic evolution, since the replicator and selector co-construct each other without an independent external filter. Jordan responded that niche construction is well-documented in biology—beavers, earthworms, and microbiomes—so circularity is not unique to culture, and that algorithms are simply externalized selection mechanisms analogous to climate or predation.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. Assess the strength of Jordan's initial replicator claim; then rate each of Maya's three objections and Jordan's corresponding replies, noting specifically where either party relies on a false analogy or overlooks a mechanistic distinction that actually matters. Finally, state who is more right overall about whether memetics constitutes a genuinely Darwinian theory of culture, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether there exists any independent criterion of memetic fitness that does not already presuppose the theoretical framework being defended.
