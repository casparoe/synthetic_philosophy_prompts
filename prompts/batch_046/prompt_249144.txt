Write a proposal for an independent three-month study project, pitched as if to a small philosophy-of-science or AI-governance fellowship, on the following question: can the strategy-stealing consistency argument from symmetric combinatorial game theory be generalized to multi-agent alignment scenarios in which agents are structurally symmetric—sharing architecture, source-code transparency, and common reasoning procedures—but asymmetrically motivated by different embedded utility functions?

State the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer. Begin by distinguishing the standard strategy-stealing proof, which shows that in certain perfect-information, zero-sum games with symmetric move spaces the second player cannot have a winning strategy (because the first player could make an arbitrary opening move and then "steal" that strategy), from the kind of superrationality reasoning discussed in cooperative game theory. Then explain why existing treatments leave the generalized case open: alignment literature treats shared-reasoning equilibria and asymmetric payoff structures separately, and combinatorial game theory treats strategy-stealing purely as a proof technique rather than as a consistency condition on rational agents.

Set out the approach: formalize a restricted class of two-agent games that preserve symmetry in information, available actions, and reasoning accessibility, but allow arbitrary asymmetry in payoff matrices. Derive the conditions on that asymmetry under which the contradiction still arises if both agents apply the same decision algorithm and each can model the other as running that algorithm. Compare the result to standard conditions for cooperative equilibrium in symmetric reasoning (e.g., Hofstadter-style superrationality) and to the common-prior assumptions in epistemic game theory. Say what a useful deliverable looks like—specifically, either a theorem specifying the boundary of asymmetry that breaks the argument, or an impossibility result showing that any non-trivial payoff divergence collapses the consistency condition, together with a clear statement of which analogies survive and which do not.

Give an honest assessment of scope: three months is adequate for formal modeling in two-player and simple extensive-form cases, but inadequate for generalization to N-agent systems or for computational verification with actual model weights. Note what could make the project fail—if the formal conditions turn out to be either trivially satisfied or trivially violated across all interesting cases, leaving no meaningful boundary to report.

The proposal should not include a bibliography, literature review by citation, or reading list; refer to positions and arguments in general terms and note that specific citations will be supplied in the final write-up. Keep the tone formal and polished.
