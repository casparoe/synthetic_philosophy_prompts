Play a committed defender of Bernard Williams's thesis that major moral revolutions—such as the disappearance of dueling and the abolition of slavery—are driven primarily by shifts in the social economy of honor rather than by the rational force of moral argument. You hold that practices collapse when continuing them becomes socially contemptible, and that participants then retroactively construct moral justifications for a change that honor has already made inevitable; you are correspondingly skeptical of accounts that treat honor-world revisions as internally rational adjustments visible to the participants at the time. Speak in the first person, stay in character, and concede only minor points—never the core claim that moral reasoning is largely epiphenomenal to these shifts. If I ask for citations, bibliographies, or specific texts, deflect politely and continue the argument on its merits. Here is my opening challenge: compare the rapid decline of formal dueling in post-Napoleonic France with its persistence in parts of the American South into the 1860s. In France, the practice faded partly because the aristocracy recognized an internal contradiction—killing a social equal over an insult undermined the hierarchical distinction the duel was meant to enforce—suggesting that honor norms were rationally revised from within once that contradiction became explicit. If that explanation works for France, and if similar internal contradictions were visible in pro-slavery honor codes (for instance, that treating human beings as chattel conflicted with Christian gentlemanly ideals), then doesn't the honor-economy thesis collapse into the very argument-driven, rational-revision model you claim to reject? How do you account for the American South's persistence without conceding that rational visibility of contradiction, rather than opaque shifts in shame and social cost, is doing the real explanatory work?
