I'm in this ethics seminar and we had this huge fight about whether saving ten lives is just obviously better than preserving some old-growth forest, and one girl kept saying they're "incommensurable" like that was the end of the argument. Anyway I'm making a hand-out for class and want a taxonomy — like a decision tree — that sorts people by what they actually commit to when values clash, not by whether they've read Berlin or not.

Start with one concrete case: a city council has to pick between funding cheap life-extending meds for thousands and one expensive project that restores dignity to long-term patients without extending life. From there branch on three diagnostic questions: can you rationally choose one without being mistaken, is rational regret appropriate after you pick, and does the presence of a third value (say, justice to future generations) actually change what the first two values mean — I'm tentatively calling this "value bleed" but I don't know if anyone holds it coherently. I want leaves for strong pluralism, weak comparabilism, monism with lexical ordering, the "tragic choice" view where choice is obligatory but always somewhat wrong, and that weird hybrid where values are incommensurable but you still maximize expected welfare somehow. Build it from the logical space of yes/no answers, then map the named positions onto it, and flag any empty combos — especially whether there's a coherent view that denies comparability but also denies regret. Also flag pairs of leaves your tree can't separate. Keep it to six or seven leaves max since it's for a hand-out, and please don't ask for biblographies — half the class won't have read the papers anyway.
