In her 1691 letter commonly known as the Respuesta a Sor Filotea, Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz argues that women have a right to study secular disciplines because all knowledge ultimately serves the understanding of Scripture. She asserts that God distributed reason without regard to sex, yet she also insists that learning must be accompanied by humility, since greater knowledge brings greater moral accountability. At the same time, she observes that women are confined to ignorance not by divine decree but by custom, and she suggests that denying them letters is as harmful as denying them the means to understand salvation.

Please help me understand a specific tension in this reasoning. Sor Juana grounds women's right to learn in an egalitarian, natural-capacity claim: reason belongs to every soul. But she also frames the permissible scope of study through an instrumental lens—knowledge is valuable chiefly insofar as it serves spiritual ends and is governed by moral caution. Does this mean her defense is actually conditional, allowing authorities to restrict women's education whenever they judge it spiritually unnecessary or dangerous? Or does she invoke a separate principle—perhaps an epistemic one—that makes the pursuit of understanding itself a form of proper worship, so that external restriction violates the correct use of a God-given faculty?

More precisely, is there a consistent distinction in her argument between knowledge that perfects the soul and knowledge that corrupts it, or is she describing a single continuum of inquiry that must be regulated by internal virtue rather than by gendered limits? And if she relies on the latter, how does she prevent her own reasoning from being co-opted by the very authorities she is addressing?
