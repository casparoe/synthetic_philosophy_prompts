Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration would produce between two camps on Nozick's entitlement theory and the Wilt Chamberlain argument: Camp A holds that justice in holdings is purely historical—just acquisition, voluntary transfer, and rectification of discrete injustices—and that any end-state pattern is incompatible with liberty once voluntary exchange is permitted; Camp B holds that individual transfers are formally valid but that structurally cumulative distributions can regenerate obligations of democratic equality or relational fairness, particularly when initial conditions involve collective investment in human capital.

The collaboration should address both the canonical Chamberlain case (an initially just distribution D1 is transformed into an unpatterned D2 through voluntary payments to watch Wilt Chamberlain play) and a modified recursive variant: Chamberlain's athletic skill was initially cultivated through a universal, tax-funded training program; he then enters voluntary contracts with spectators, accumulates substantial wealth, purchases the arena outright, and institutes tiered admission pricing that effectively excludes the lowest-income cohort that originally subsidized his development.

Use this structure: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims—about the logical form of entitlement theory, the conditions under which a patterned principle is defeated by voluntary exchange, the distinction between token-level consent and the structural conditions that shape meaningful consent, and the limited scope of the rectification clause; (2) a disagreements table with two columns, "Camp A (Historical Entitlement)" and "Camp B (Structural Pattern)," in which each entry is phrased in wording that camp would accept for its own position, covering whether public subsidy in skill-formation creates a lingering co-ownership claim, whether private ownership of the arena permits exclusion that violates relational equality, and whether emergent patterns can trigger rectification obligations even absent discrete unjust acquisitions; (3) the cruxes—the specific sub-questions (for example, whether counterfactual stability of choice under asymmetric information undermines the voluntariness of tiered-admission contracts, and whether a principle permitting recursive accumulation must also permit the privatization of collectively funded infrastructure) such that resolving them would move at least one side; (4) for each crux, record what each camp predicts or requires, and identify whether both sides agree on any shared conceptual test, hypothetical variation, or consistency check that would bear on it; (5) if any portion of the dispute reduces to a verbal disagreement about the meaning of "voluntary," "just holding," or "pattern," specify exactly which portion and explain why.

The statement should be formal, precise, and constructed so that neither camp is straw-manned: Camp A should not be reduced to "anything voluntary is just regardless of origin," and Camp B should not be reduced to "all unequal outcomes are unjust regardless of process." The deliverable is the text both parties could sign, with the residual disagreement rendered exact and the path to resolution made explicit.
