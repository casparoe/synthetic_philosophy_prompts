I've been asked to moderate the closing session of a graduate workshop on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I'm preparing to interview a philosopher who has been developing what she calls a functional-historical amendment to Dennett's framework of counterfeit people. Her view, presented in recent working papers, is that large language models should not be sorted into Dennett's original binary of genuine persons versus counterfeit people—entities like philosophical zombies or perfectly deceptive androids—because they lack not merely consciousness but the evolutionary and social-functional history that makes the intentional stance genuinely predictive rather than merely projective. Instead, she places them in an intermediate category she terms stochastic mimics: systems that generate intentional-state ascriptions not because they possess the internal architecture those ascriptions track, but because their outputs are compressed statistical residues of human intentional behavior, making the stance applicable only in a derivative, borrowed sense.

I want to avoid generic questions about whether a chatbot is conscious. Instead, I need a polished sequence of questions that presses her precisely where Dennett's own literature is most vulnerable to this kind of extension. In particular, I want to anticipate her likely defense—that the intentional stance requires no particular internal mechanism, only predictive utility, and that if LLM outputs are predictable via intentional vocabulary, the stance applies regardless of causal history—and then include the follow-up that makes that deflection progressively harder. The questions should also test whether her stochastic-mimic category can do real explanatory work: for instance, whether it distinguishes LLMs from human infants or non-human animals without collapsing back into Dennett's original binary, and whether her framework can accommodate cases where shifting from mechanistic to intentional description actually degrades predictive success. Please write me a sequence suitable for an audience of philosophers of mind, ending with one that asks what specific observable failure mode—what pattern of breakdown in predictive utility—would convince her that a stochastic mimic had either crossed into genuine intentionality or, conversely, that the intentional stance should be withdrawn entirely.
