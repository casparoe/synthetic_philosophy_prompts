In surgical anesthesia, institutions maintain protocols—such as isolating one arm with a tourniquet so that a paralyzed but conscious patient can still signal—to detect intraoperative awareness. At the same time, legal liability and compensation frameworks recognize harm only when awareness is subsequently reported and documented; there is no institutional mechanism for remedying purely hypothetical, undetected conscious experience during the procedure. What philosophical commitments about consciousness and its accessibility does this institutional asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does the practice treat consciousness as essentially tied to first-person reportability, thereby framing the meta-problem of consciousness as an epistemic limitation rather than a metaphysical gap? Or can it accommodate rival positions—such as illusionism or certain forms of panpsychism—on which the distinction between reportable and unreportable experience either collapses or carries no special metaphysical weight? Distinguish what the clinical and legal design strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what would have to change in malpractice doctrine and hospital protocol if the underlying view of consciousness were replaced by one that treats physical processes as equally, or not at all, accompanied by experience regardless of behavioral access.
