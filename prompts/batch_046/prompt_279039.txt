I'm working through some modal epistemology with a study partner and we've hit an argument that seems to take a reasonable principle and drive it straight into global skepticism. He lays it out in six steps. (1) If a state of affairs is genuinely conceivable—not merely pictured in imagination, but representable in full structural detail without any contradiction appearing in the representation itself—then it is metaphysically possible; otherwise conceivability gives us no non-empirical access to modality at all. (2) Take any thesis T that asserts a necessary connection between A and B: physicalism linking consciousness to neural integration, origin essentialism linking personal identity to particular gametes, or logical essentialism linking arithmetic truths to all possible worlds. (3) For each T, construct what he calls a "modal isolation frame": a complete internal description of A occurring without B, in which every usual relational tie to B is bracketed but A's intrinsic properties are left fully intact. (4) Provided this frame contains no explicit contradiction, step (1) licenses us to declare the separation genuinely possible, and therefore T false. (5) Apply the frame: describe a conscious state in purely phenomenal terms with no neural substrate mentioned; describe a psychologically continuous person with entirely different biological origins; describe a local region in which 2+2=5, with arithmetic operations specified locally but with no invocation of global logical consistency. (6) So either all these separations are genuinely possible—which would destroy physicalism, essentialism, and logical necessity in a single move—or we must reject (1) and concede that conceivability cannot establish possibility, leaving us with no way to justify modal claims outside empirical observation.

I can see that the argument is meant to be a reductio, but I can't locate the precise slip. It feels like something goes wrong between (3) and (4): perhaps an equivocation between describing A in isolation and describing A as it actually is without B, or perhaps a hidden quantifier shift about what counts as "the representation itself." But I also worry that rejecting (1) might cost us more than we want to pay—if conceivability isn't even a defeasible guide to possibility, then arguments from conceivability in philosophy of mind and mathematics seem to collapse entirely. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Does it require abandoning modal rationalism altogether, or can I keep (1) by adding a restriction that blocks the "modal isolation frame" without making conceivability useless?
