I serve on a local reconciliation board in a country emerging from decades of authoritarian rule, where a transitional mechanism allows conditional amnesty in exchange for full disclosure about past abuses. We are weighing a request from a former civil-service archivist who, for fifteen years, maintained the classified registry that tracked dissidents and enabled their disappearance. He never arrested, interrogated, or harmed anyone physically; his role was purely documentary—indexing names, cross-referencing locations, and ensuring that records remained accessible to security forces but hidden from families. He now offers to reveal the precise location of a mass grave containing victims he catalogued, along with the identities of several officials who ordered the removals, but he insists that this disclosure must be contingent on guaranteed criminal immunity and on a sealed record that prevents his public identification. Some members of the board argue that because his harm was mediated entirely through paperwork and information systems, granting amnesty is proportionate: he provides irreplaceable knowledge that can restore dignity to the dead and close cases for survivors, and his direct moral culpability is attenuated by the bureaucratic distance between his actions and the violence they facilitated. Others contend that treating his knowledge as a negotiable asset commodifies justice, that amnesty for anyone who maintained the informational infrastructure of repression collapses the distinction between perpetrator and bystander, and that allowing him anonymity rewards the very secrecy that sustained the regime. I am trying to decide whether to support the deal.

What complicates my thinking is a distinction I recently encountered between culpability for direct physical harm and liability for what might be called informational complicity—the idea that in highly bureaucratized violence, the moral weight of an act is not diminished by physical distance but is instead concentrated in the control of knowledge that makes harm systematic and irreversible. If that framing is sound, then his administrative role might actually carry a distinctive form of responsibility rather than a diminished one, because without his registry the disappearances would have been disorganized, traceable, and less total. At the same time, if truth is the only currency we have to offer families, refusing the exchange might preserve abstract justice at the cost of concrete knowledge about the dead.

Should I vote to approve the conditional amnesty?
