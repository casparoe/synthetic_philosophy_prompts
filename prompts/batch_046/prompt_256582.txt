I've been drafting some notes on institutional design for a discussion group, and I'd appreciate comments from anyone who's thought seriously about mechanism design or democratic theory. The context is the usual comparison between American-style career politics—with its revolving doors and fundraising imperatives—and European parliamentary systems with stronger party discipline and longer tenure, but I'm trying to avoid the standard "term limits good versus bad" framing that dominates most rationalist-adjacent discussions of governance.

Instead, I'm separating two dimensions that usually get collapsed: the selection mechanism (election versus sortition) and the temporal structure (career horizon versus enforced rotation). My core claim is that term limits without sortition don't eliminate careerism; they displace it into what I call the proxy-career effect, where elected officials become temporary figureheads while staffers, lobbyists, and post-legislative advisory roles absorb the long-term incentives. Meanwhile, pure sortition—random selection for legislative service—eliminates electoral careerism but introduces a different dependency: because randomly selected citizens lack accumulated institutional knowledge, they become epistemically captured by permanent bureaucracies and recurring expert witnesses. In other words, rotation without lot produces amateur legislators with professional intermediaries, while lot without rotation risks creating a different class of permanent experts who effectively govern.

I'm particularly interested in whether this distinction survives contact with public choice theory. Is the proxy-career effect just a re-description of the principal-agent problem we already know about, or does the random-selection element change the incentive structure in a way that actually matters? Also, I'm unsure whether my framing of "epistemic capture" in sortition bodies is accurate or whether I'm overstating the information asymmetry—after all, Athenian-style bodies used rotation precisely to prevent concentration of expertise. If you see subtle errors in the reasoning, especially any conflation between selection-pool effects and temporal-horizon effects, I'd value specific corrections rather than general impressions. Does this seem like a genuinely distinct angle, or am I just restating familiar concerns about legislative expertise under a new vocabulary?
