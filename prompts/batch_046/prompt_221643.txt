As a journalist covering science, technology, and governance, I am preparing the opening argument for a seminar on state power and digital architecture. My thesis — which I term the correlation-asymmetry position — is that citizens ought to calibrate the information they withhold from the state not according to that state's present legal commitments or declared intentions, but according to its structural capacity for retrospective, cross-domain data fusion. I examine three architectures: Germany, where rigorous legal opacity rooted in informational self-determination coexists with technically porous infrastructures; the United States, where an openness tradition treats transparency as a corrective despite high-capacity private-sector correlation; and the People's Republic of China, where retrospective fusion is explicit, centralised, and embedded within social-credit administration. The audience will be fifteen comparative-law and political-theory scholars, mostly European, including two East Asian governance specialists who reject cultural essentialism and analyse social credit as an administrative system rather than a civilisational one; a German constitutional lawyer committed to the Grundgesetz conception of informational self-determination; and an American civil-liberties technologist who holds that robust encryption and legal transparency make concealment redundant everywhere. I require a Q&A prep sheet, not a full defence. Please list the objections I will actually face, in the order they are likely to arise, phrased precisely as each interlocutor would put them. For each, indicate what I should concede, what I must hold, the weakest point in my reply, and a concise response fitting a ten-minute exchange. Finally, identify the objection I am most hoping nobody raises, with a brief strategy for handling it if it does.
