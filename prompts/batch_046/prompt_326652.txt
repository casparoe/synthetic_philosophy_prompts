Please read the following short essay and offer critical comments on its argument, its historical accuracy, and whether it succeeds in showing that Smith’s anti-imperialism is internally connected to his broader economic theory rather than being an incidental political opinion. Write entirely in continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one might write a letter or an email. Please use British English.

Adam Smith is usually read as a critic of mercantilism and, by extension, of the colonial system that mercantilism sustained. Yet this reading treats his attack on empire as a side-effect of his preference for free markets. I want to suggest instead that Smith’s view of colonies is structurally integral to his theory of political economy, because he treats imperial possession as a kind of institutional moral hazard: the British state bears the fiscal and military costs of territorial control, while a narrow mercantile class captures the monopoly rents that the empire secures. In The Wealth of Nations he argues that colonies have not enriched Britain proportionally to the expense of defending them, but he goes further by implying that the very existence of a colonial administration creates a feedback loop in which domestic producers lobby for imperial protection in order to maintain artificial scarcity at home. On this reading, the colony is not merely an unprofitable possession but a mechanism for exporting regulatory capture: the visible hand of imperial bureaucracy, far from correcting market failures, becomes the instrument through which private interests externalise the costs of monopoly onto the public treasury. If this is right, then Smith’s scepticism about the sufficiency of the invisible hand in the presence of monopoly takes on a geopolitical dimension—he is not just warning about domestic cartels, but about a transnational structure in which empire and monopoly are co-constitutive. The essay needs sharpening: does Smith actually describe this feedback loop, or am I importing a modern concept of regulatory capture into his eighteenth-century framework? And does his comparison of British colonies to the Greek colonies of antiquity support or undermine the claim that he sees empire as essentially a fiscal drain rather than a source of strategic value?
