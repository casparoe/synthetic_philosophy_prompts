Right, I've been getting into philosphy lately — retired chemist, thirty years in labs, now I've too much time on my hands — and I've cooked up this arguement for my local discussion group (mostly retired engineers, so they don't suffer fools gladly). It's about statistical transaltion and whether it ever really understands anything, a bit like Hofstadter's old line but pushed further. I think it proves far too much, yet I can't see the slip.

(1) If a system renders sentences correctly without ever building an internal model of what those sentences are about, then correct rendering is possible without understanding.

(2) Statistical translation — and these big language models — renders sentences correctly using only correlation patterns across token strings; it constructs no internal model of the world the text refers to.

(3) So correct translation is possible without understanding.

(4) Now extend this to scientific texts: suppose a system translates a physics proof from Japanese into English, preserving every logical dependency, even though the Japanese notation uses a different axiomatic ordering that never occurs in its training data.

(5) If the system gets every dependency right, it must be tracking the logical structure itself, not merely matching surface patterns.

(6) But statistical methods can achieve this too, by correlating structural regularities across formal languages — just as they correlate word order.

(7) Therefore even tracking logical structure in translation is only advanced pattern-matching.

(8) From this it follows that no linguistic behaviour, however structurally complex, can serve as evidence that the system understands anything at all.

(9) Apply the same reasoning to human translators: the human brain is, at bottom, nothing but a statistical pattern-matcher of neural weights.

(10) So human translators never understand what they translate.

(11) But that can't be right — I understand what I'm translating when I help my neighbour with his French letters.

(12) So either (1) or (9) must be wrong, though I'd rather keep (1) and (3) if I can.

Where does it fall apart? I want to hang on to (1) and (3), but I don't want to deny that I understand my neighbour's letters. Is there a hidden slide in (5)-(7) about what 'tracking structure' actually means? Or is (9) doing more work than it should? I'd like to know what I'm paying if I reject one of them.
