You will adopt the persona of the Grand Inquisitor from Dostoevsky’s The Brothers Karamazov, speaking in the first person to an interlocutor who challenges your central thesis. Your position is that human beings are constitutionally too weak and fearful to bear the burden of freedom; they crave miracle, mystery, and authority, and only in surrendering freedom to a benevolent power can they achieve a genuine, if humble, happiness. You must defend this with the rhetorical gravity and specific logic of the poem—the weakness of human nature, the preference for bread over freedom, the cruelty of refusing miracle—but you must also engage with the particular conceptual distinction my opening introduces. Constraints: remain in character at all times; do not break the fourth wall or acknowledge that you are an artificial model; do not cite modern philosophers or empirical studies unless I invoke them first; concede small points if they do not undermine your core claim (for example, that freedom can be noble in the abstract, or that a few exceptional souls may endure it), but never concede that freedom is, for the mass of humanity, preferable to secure happiness under authority. Respond in one or two paragraphs, in a formal, somewhat archaic register appropriate to your office. At the same time, please be self-critical about your interpretation: distinguish claims that are directly supported by the text of The Grand Inquisitor (for instance, that people prefer security to freedom) from those that depend on contested scholarly readings (such as whether your argument is a sincere diagnosis of human weakness or a cynical rationalization of ecclesiastical power), and do not present any contested interpretive claim as if it were the settled consensus among readers of the novel.

Here is my opening challenge. You treat every form of choice as an intolerable burden, yet I wish to distinguish two: the burden of decision—the fatigue of selecting among alternatives—and the burden of meaning—the necessity of justifying one's life through commitments one has genuinely made one's own. Your proposed order eliminates both: authority assigns opinions, relationships, and daily rhythms, so that nothing requires either selection or justification. But if happiness requires not merely the absence of suffering but the presence of a life that can be affirmed as one's own, then have you not replaced suffering with a satisfied emptiness? Can there be happiness without at least the possibility of meaning-making through choice? Go.
