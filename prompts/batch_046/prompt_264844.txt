The philosophy-of-language debate over Quine’s gavagai thought experiment—whether assent to that term in the presence of rabbits demonstrates radical indeterminacy of reference, or only an epistemic limitation correctable by charity and causal theories—has cycled through the same objections for decades without resolution. Critics charge that Quine illicitly assumes a behaviorist theory of meaning that no contemporary philosopher holds; defenders reply that no internalist or causal constraint can actually break the symmetry between translating the term as “rabbit,” “undetached rabbit part,” or “rabbit-stage.” My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not about reference at all, but about whether translation is a predictive-scientific project (aiming at successful behavioral prediction) or a normative-rational project (aiming at rational belief attribution)—with philosophers of science and philosophers of mind talking past each other under a single heading. Using what each side actually says about stimulus meaning, the principle of charity, and causal reference, diagnose whether this is a verbal dispute about the word “meaning,” a substantive disagreement with a hidden empirical component, or a case of two disciplines asking different questions. Then tell me whether any contemporary development—specifically, the fact that large-scale statistical translation systems achieve high cross-lingual accuracy without ever fixing reference through explicit causal chains—should settle the matter, or whether the question is now permanently detached from empirical progress and should be abandoned or reframed.
