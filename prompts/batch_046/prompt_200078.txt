I've been going back and forth with a friend about Sen's liberal paradox for weeks and we can't tell if we're actually disagreeing or just talking past each other. You know the standard setup: there's two people, a prude and a lewd, and a controversial book they might read. Sen shows that if you grant each one sovereignty over their own personal choice domain, plus require Pareto efficiency and unrestricted domain, the aggreation rules crash into a contradiction. My friend thinks this is basically a reductio of treating rights as absolute within welfare economics—she says it proves liberal values have to be softened or bounded if we want coherent social outcomes. I think that's the wrong takeaway, that Sen is actually showing social choice theory is a bad frame for rights in the first place, not that rights are internally inconsistent. But then she points out that Sen himself uses preference orderings to formalize the rights, so if the framework is wrong why build the paradox inside it? At this point I wonder if the whole debate has stalled for decades because half the literature is solving a formal consistency problem for aggregation rules while the other half is doing normative political philosophy under the same label "liberal paradox."

What I want is a diagnosis, not a winner. Is this disagreement verbal—are we using "liberal" or "right" differently—or substantive about whether rights should ever be weighd against efficiency? Or is it that one side cares about which formal conditions fail and the other cares about institutional design, and they're answering different questions? Also, I've been wondering about a weird variant: what if sovereignty isn't binary but graded, so you only get veto power over a domain if your preference intensity crosses some threshold? Would that dissolve the paradox or just relocate the disagreement to where we set the cutoff? If the debate is genuinely ill-posed, tell me why; if it's hard but real, tell me what kind of result or distinction would actually move it foward.
