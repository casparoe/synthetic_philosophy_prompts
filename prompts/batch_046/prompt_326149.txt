I chair my university's strategic investment board. We must decide whether to approve a proposed $14 million, five-year interdisciplinary research center advanced by a senior faculty coalition. Their argument is an elaborate inside-view case: specific institutional partnerships, a curated talent pipeline, bespoke evaluation metrics, and a causal narrative they maintain distinguishes this initiative from prior attempts. Our institutional research office offers an outside-view reference-class analysis: nine of the last eleven comparable interdisciplinary centers launched since 2008 either missed core milestones or required emergency capital infusions, with no ex-ante feature that reliably predicted the successes. The proponents reply that the reference class is heterogeneous and that forcing this proposal into it commits a category error; the skeptics reply that invoking uniqueness without specifying a pre-registered, falsifiable discriminating feature is precisely the epistemic overconfidence the outside view corrects, and that institutional modesty requires deferring to the statistical record absent such a feature. Draft the board's formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with binding conditions—stating the actionable bottom line and reasoning that gives full weight to the strongest consideration on the losing side. If recommending conditional approval, specify exactly what conditions would make reliance on the inside view compatible with epistemic modesty rather than its abandonment.
