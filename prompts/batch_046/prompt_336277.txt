In rationalist-adjacent discussions about cognitive outsourcing and AI alignment, there's a recurring analogy used to defend heavy reliance on language models: using an LLM for synthesis and argument-checking is like navigating an unfamiliar city with a high-quality map. The map doesn't erase your innate sense of direction; it extends your range, and if you never possessed a mental model of the terrain, the deficiency wasn't caused by the instrument. People lean on this to argue that fears of epistemic erosion — sometimes called understanding atrophy — are largely misplaced, because dependence is framed as amplification rather than replacement, provided you retain an independent capacity to verify outputs against reality.

I'm skeptical that the analogy holds under closer inspection, largely because it quietly imports a picture of a stable, pre-existing navigator whose evaluation criteria remain untouched by the tool. Can you audit the correspondence explicitly? Give me the table: what plays the role of the navigator, the terrain, the map's projection, the legend, the verification landmarks, and the act of putting the map away. Then identify the fractures, especially the one that rarely gets mentioned: a physical map has a fixed projection and doesn't adapt to how you've used previous maps, whereas an LLM's outputs are shaped by interaction history, and the linguistic landmarks you use to check coherence are themselves partly artifacts of prior model exposure. Does the benign conclusion — that AI dependence is just amplification without erosion — survive once that feedback loop is visible, or was the analogy carrying the normative weight all along? If the map comparison collapses here, is there a better structural analogy — perhaps apprenticeship with a master who slowly reshapes your taste, or a prosthetic limb that rewires the nervous system — that can accommodate both the genuine cognitive extension and the real risk of atrophy without presupposing an untouched core self?
