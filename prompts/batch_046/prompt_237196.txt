The virtue ethics vs situationist critique thing has been going in circles since like the 90s and honestly it feels like nobody's moved. You have the virtue people saying moral character is real—courage, honesty, etc. are stable dispositions that explain why someone does the right thing across contexts. Then you have the situationists (Doris, Harman, etc.) throwing experiments at them—Milgram, the Good Samaritan study, all that—showing that tiny situational tweaks (a dime in the phone booth, being in a hurry) predict behavior way better than any personality score. The virtue crowd usually bites back by saying virtues are rare, or that they're "local" not global, or that situationists miss the role of moral perception—how you see the situation depends on your character. The situationists reply that's just moving the goalposts, redefining traits until they're unfalsifiable. And then we just loop.

My working theory is that this isn't really about empirical psychology at all. I think the virtue ethicists are doing normative theory—saying what a good person should be—while the situationists are doing descriptive psychology, and they're truely talking past each other under the word "character." But maybe I'm wrong and there's actually a hidden disagreement about what counts as an explanation of action, or about whether traits need to be cross-situationally consistent to be real.

Can you diagnose what's actually going on? Is it verbal—are they using "trait" and "disposition" in totally different ways? Is it substantive—do they disagree about facts, or about what evidence should count? Are they answering different questions—normative evaluation vs. behavioral prediction—under one heading? Or is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, like whether "local virtues" can be measured or trained?

Also, there's this idea I keep circling back to: both sides seem to assume that if virtue ethics is true, then personality psychology has to back it up with global, stable traits that show up in inventories. But what if virtue is more like a skill or a narrative construct—something that doesn't need to appear in a personality test to be real? Does that idea break the deadlock, or is it just another way of moving the goalposts?

Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side actually says, and be honest: is there anything—some distinction, some experiment, some shift in the question—that could actually make progress here? Or should we just accept that the debate is ill-posed and stop expecting resolution?
