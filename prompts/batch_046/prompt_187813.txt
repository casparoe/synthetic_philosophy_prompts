For a feature I am writing on unconditional commitment and the limits of ethical reasoning, I want to close with an original poem that treats Kierkegaard’s Abraham not as a familiar parable of obedience but as a live philosophical problem. Write a sestina in blank verse—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—spoken in Abraham’s voice during the descent from Mount Moriah, not the ascent. The six end-words, in the order they close lines in the first stanza, must be: universal, knife, silent, faith, father, mountain. Follow the standard sestina rotation precisely, so that the displacement and eventual reconvergence of these terms structurally mirrors the teleological suspension of the ethical: the universal is moved from its governing position but not annihilated, and in the envoi all six words must converge in one compressed image of Abraham re-entering the finite order he never actually abandoned. The speaker should carry the secret that distinguishes the knight of faith from the knight of infinite resignation—he receives Isaac back not as compensation but as the impossible return of the finite after its absolute surrender—without resolving that secret into either a defense of divine command theory or a clinical narrative of trauma. Keep the silence structurally ambiguous: it should read as inwardness rather than madness, and a specialist should recognize the movement of the pseudonymous writings on the absolute relation to the absolute, yet the poem should stand on its own as verse without exposition. Do not sermonize about modern politics or intervention ethics; let the form do the work.
