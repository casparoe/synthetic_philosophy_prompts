Having spent thirty years in experimental condensed-matter physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science and epistemology, I am struck by how often discussions of peer disagreement rely on stylized examples—philosophers in armchairs, or abstract betting agents—rather than on the kinds of interpretive disputes that actually arise in a working laboratory. I would like you to construct a thought experiment engineered to precise specifications, using concepts that are familiar but arranged in an unfamiliar configuration.

Design a single-paragraph, realistic scenario set in a research workplace—say, a materials-characterization group—rather than in a trolley-problem or science-fiction context. Two investigators of comparable general intelligence, training, and intellectual honesty examine the same noisy dataset (for instance, temperature-dependent resistivity measurements on a novel compound) and reach opposing conclusions about whether the data reveal a particular electronic phase transition. The disagreement is empirical and carries practical stakes (e.g., whether to commit resources to a larger synthesis run), not moral or aesthetic.

Engineer the case so that conciliationism about peer disagreement requires both investigators to revise their confidence levels substantially toward the middle, treating each other as genuine epistemic peers. At the same time, steadfastness should permit at least one investigator—specifically the one whose belief was formed through a mechanism you will identify—to retain her original degree of belief without epistemic fault. The divergence must not come from one party being fatigued, financially conflicted, or using malfunctioning equipment; it must emerge from a structural feature of the belief-formation process itself. To avoid recycling standard templates, inject a somewhat novel asymmetry: for example, one researcher calibrated her analysis pipeline directly against a primary reference standard in her own lab, while the other applied an identically documented algorithm but calibrated it only against a published summary table derived from that same standard, with both unaware of a subtle rounding convention that shifts the threshold.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning: state what conciliationism says each party should do and why; then state what steadfastness says, identifying which investigator may stand firm and on what grounds. Next, isolate the precise structural feature that allows steadfastness to peel away from conciliationism—what must be present for the divergence to occur. Then explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into a symmetric peer-disagreement scenario in which both theories converge on the same recommendation? Finally, present the strongest reply a committed conciliationist could make to the steadfastness-friendly interpretation of that feature, and assess whether that reply succeeds or begs the question.

Constraints: no demons, perfect predictors, exotic physics, or lottery-style reasoning; stakes and cognitive demands should be ones actual scientists face; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; and do not quote verbatim from any philosophical text—paraphrase as needed.
