I'm preparing a seminar on value pluralism and the structure of rational choice, and I want to use the novel Sophie's Choice as a test case for Ruth Chang's account of hard choices rather than the more familiar tragic-choice framing. The narrative centres on Sophie's decision at Auschwitz—which child to send to the gas chamber—and treats it as the defining trauma that eventually drives her to suicide. Rather than asking whether she made the 'right' choice, I want to examine whether the values at stake—the life of each child, her survival as a mother, her religious and moral identity—are genuinely incommensurable in Chang's sense, meaning there is no covering value that renders them comparable, or whether the horror lies precisely in the fact that they are comparable and she must bear the rational weight of that comparison.

How does the novel's treatment of Sophie's subsequent guilt, her inability to narrate the choice without collapsing it into either pure agency or pure victimhood, and the framing of her eventual death illuminate or resist the philosophical distinction between hard choices (where reasons run out) and tragic choices (where all options violate something of value)? Specifically, I'd like a reading that treats the scene not as an illustration of philosophical theory but as a challenge to it: does the novel suggest that the very attempt to classify the choice as 'hard' or 'tragic' is a form of moral evasion, and what does that imply for whether a culture can construct institutions that acknowledge incommensurability without abandoning the victims to it?
