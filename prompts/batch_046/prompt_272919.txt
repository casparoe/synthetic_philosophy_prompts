I am delivering the opening remarks—roughly four minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled Value Beyond Use: Art, Friendship, and the Structure of Intrinsic Good. The room is split between philosophers of value, several of whom specialize in G. E. Moore, and artists, museum curators, and clinical psychologists who have never engaged with his writings. I need a speech that frames why the workshop pairs aesthetic experience with friendship without becoming a lecture, and I want the philosophical idea to do real structural work: it should determine what I can and cannot say.

The spine should be Moore’s claim that both friendship and the appreciation of beauty are intrinsic goods, together with his further, often overlooked suggestion that when they occur together they form an organic unity—a whole whose value exceeds the sum of its parts. This should block two common reductions: I must not treat art as a mere instrument for social bonding, nor friendship as merely a setting for shared taste. I also want to inject a somewhat niche, contested twist: that Moore’s framework actually undermines contemporary cultural designs that strictly separate contemplative viewing spaces from communal interaction spaces, because such separation, on this reading, actively diminishes the value of both goods. I should acknowledge briefly that this application extends Moore’s thesis beyond its original scope and depends on a disputed interpretation.

Please write the address in polished, formal American English—warm enough for a welcome, precise enough that the philosophers recognize the framing, and accessible to the non-specialists. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. After the spoken text, please include a brief separate note stating: (1) where the framing takes liberties with Moore’s actual arguments or applies them beyond their original context; (2) which parts rest on a contested reading of organic unity; and (3) what specific argument or evidence would lead me to abandon the central framing—for example, if Moore’s unity claim turned out to be purely additive rather than qualitatively transformative, or if his account of friendship were shown to exclude shared aesthetic experience altogether.
