I was chatting with my friend who just took an intro to eastern philosphy course, and she summed up Confucian role ethics for me in a way that sounds smart but I'm not sure I buy it all. She said your moral self isn't some inner core like in Western philosophy, it's totally defined by your social roles—father, son, ruler, subject, and so on. Confucius apparently laid out five fixed relationship scripts, and each one is strictly one-directional: the elder commands, the junior obeys, end of story. Because ethics is basically just learning to play your assigned part, Confucians don't believe in universal moral rules that apply to everyone regardless of role—there's no abstract "human dignity," only the specific obligations that come with being in that script. She also said modern interpreters use this to show that Western liberalism is incoherent because it pretends people can have moral obligations without first being embedded in these relational roles.

My instinct is that she's right about roles being central instead of some abstract individual, but the "strictly one-directional" part and the claim that there's zero universal morality feel off—like, isn't there something called ren that applies to everyone, and don't the roles have some kind of reciprocity? Is her summary fair? Can you break down what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what's just wrong?
