I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, yet I have been trying to understand the design logic behind transitional justice institutions—specifically mechanisms such as South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Argentina's Full Stop and Due Obedience amnesty laws, and Rwanda's Gacaca courts—under which perpetrators of serious human-rights violations are granted legal amnesty or reduced punishment in exchange for full public disclosure of their actions.

In distributed systems engineering, we sometimes implement vulnerability-disclosure protocols: a party reveals a critical system flaw, and in exchange the system owner grants immunity from liability rather than pursuing punitive sanctions, on the theory that transparency restores integrity and prevents future failure. Transitional justice mechanisms appear structurally analogous—disclosure is traded for immunity—yet in ordinary criminal law we do not treat confession as a currency that cancels punitive debt. This tension prompts me to ask what implicit philosophical framework makes the trade coherent.

What theory or theories of moral responsibility, justice, and forgiveness does this disclosure-for-immunity practice actually presuppose? Must it assume that the primary harm of atrocity is epistemic—that a society suffers principally from not knowing what occurred—so that revealing the truth functions as a form of restitution capable of offsetting punitive obligations? Or does the institution depend on a consequentialist calculus in which social stability and the prevention of renewed violence outweigh individual desert? Could a deontological or rights-based framework accommodate the practice, perhaps by treating amnesty as a collective act of forgiveness that the state may grant even when victims do not? Reconstruct the implicit theory, but distinguish rigorously what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to align with it—for instance, is restorative justice conceptually necessary, or would any framework that weights systemic transparency over retributive punishment license the same design?

Moreover, the three cases vary in structure: South Africa required individualized applications and public testimony; Argentina initially granted blanket amnesty with minimal disclosure obligations; Rwanda employed community courts that blended accountability with reconciliation. Do these variants presuppose consistent philosophical commitments regarding personal culpability, collective guilt, and the moral standing of victims, or do they demonstrate that the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival conceptions of justice? If the underlying view were replaced—for example, by a position holding that moral debt is strictly non-transferable and cannot be canceled by speech acts—what specific institutional features of these commissions would necessarily have to change?
