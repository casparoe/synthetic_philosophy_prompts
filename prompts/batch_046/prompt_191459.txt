There's this weird assymetry in how museums and textbooks frame figures like Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus. Their big achievements—writing democratic theory, winning wars, exploring—get treated as timeless or even transcendent, but when you hit the slavery, colonial violence, or racism, the framing suddenly shifts to this man-of-his-time excuse like moral knowledge was unavailable back then. Meanwhile abolitionists or indigenous critics from those exact same eras get filed away as footnotes, not as proof that people could have known better. What theory of moral responsability and moral knowledge does that institutional framing actually presuppose? Is it a coherence view—people can't be blamed for what they couldn't have conceived—or is it really just a power-preserving excuse? And is the practice even philosophically consistant, since if moral knowledge was genuinely blocked back then, wouldn't that also undermine the authority of the democratic ideas we're praising? I want to know what has to change in the framing if we drop that presupposition.
