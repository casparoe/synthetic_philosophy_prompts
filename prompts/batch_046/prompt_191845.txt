Recently, in an online discussion about whether it is acceptable to hold a belief in a local supernatural event based solely on personal intuition, someone invoked W. K. Clifford's strict principle: "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence." I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been working through the idea on my own, and I am struck by how many different things that sentence might actually be saying. Could you lay out for me a menu of the precise theses it could express, ranging from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, please specify what counts as "evidence," what makes it "sufficient," whether "believing" covers only voluntary commitments or also involuntary perceptual judgments forced upon us by direct experience, and whether "anyone" applies uniformly even to people who lack the education, time, or physical safety to investigate further. Then say, for each reading, whether it is defensible, whether it is false, and — most importantly — whether that particular interpretation actually supports using the line to dismiss my interlocutor's belief, which was formed from immediate personal experience rather than from a deliberate choice for emotional comfort. I am also trying to formulate a distinction of my own: does the principle apply with equal force to beliefs we adopt to soothe ourselves and to beliefs that seem pressed upon us by the world itself? Please answer in British English; I write in American English by habit, but I want British spelling and phrasing throughout.
