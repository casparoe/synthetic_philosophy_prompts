I was chatting with this philosopher of science for a feature I'm writing on how Gödel gets dragged into every field, and she has this super consistent but kinda subtle track record. She'll tell you Gödel doesn't show physics can never be finished, since physical theories aren't formal axiom systems with recursive axioms in the right sense. She rejects the Lucas-style claim that human minds out-compute machines, cause minds aren't provably consistent formal systems either. But she also insists Gödel genuinely bites when you're talking about formal verification systems for neural-net architectures — there really are true properties those verification frameworks can't prove about the networks. At the same time she's adamant it doesn't license some fuzzy "there are always truths beyond human reach" idea; she calls that a catagory error mixing up syntax and semantics.

So here's the case I'm stuck on: there's an argument in theoretical linguistics that Universal Grammar, if you formalize it with enough expressive power to generate all grammatical sentences, is Gödel-incomplete — meaning there'd be grammatical sentences that are true but unprovable inside the grammar itself. The author jumps from that to saying native speakers must be using non-algorithmic mechanisms to access those sentences. What's her verdict? I'm trying to map whether she's running "Gödel applies only inside pure mathematics" or "it applies to formal descriptions of domains but never to the domains or minds themselves," or maybe some other principle about representational closure. Which precedent is the closest analog — the physics case, the Lucas mind case, or the neural-net verification one? And if her past rulings genuinely underdetermine it, what principle is she committing to with each possible answer? Also, what new case would actually split those rules apart?
