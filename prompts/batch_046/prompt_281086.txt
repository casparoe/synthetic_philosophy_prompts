As a novelist constructing characters for a story about an engineer who builds preference-inference systems, I have been using my own gut reactions to behavioral scenarios as a guide to what feels like a true preference versus a performance, habit, or heuristic. I want to know whether my reactions cohere. Here are six cases with my verdicts.

Case 1: An agent takes a consistently longer path to avoid stepping on insects, maintaining this pattern when entirely alone, unrecorded, and unobserved by any being. Verdict: this reveals a genuine preference against causing harm to small living things, not a social signaling preference.

Case 2: The same agent donates generously to public charities in years when tax records are visible, but reduces giving to near zero in years with private filing. Verdict: this reveals a preference for reputation or tax advantage, not for the welfare of strangers.

Case 3: An agent refuses employment with a weapons manufacturer despite severe financial distress, and maintains the refusal even under a contractual guarantee of absolute secrecy. Verdict: this reveals a genuine moral preference against contributing to harm.

Case 4: The agent meticulously sorts recyclables at home, but abandons all separation and discards everything in general waste when traveling anonymously in distant cities. Verdict: this reveals a preference for local social conformity or self-image management, not for environmental outcomes.

Case 5: The agent spends thirty years mastering an intricate craft—building physical instruments that are immediately destroyed upon completion—practicing in total isolation with no audience, no record, and no prospect of recognition. They continue even after being told they will never be witnessed by anyone, living or dead. However, they cease all activity instantly upon being informed, correctly, that the materials are perfect simulations producing no actual physical effects, despite the subjective experience remaining identical. Verdict: I am genuinely torn. One part of me reads the persistence through anonymity as proof of an intrinsic love of the craft; the other reads the immediate cessation upon learning of the simulation as proof that the preference was always for causing real physical change, which makes it instrumental. I cannot settle which intuition should prevail.

Case 6: In a virtual environment, an agent chooses to suffer a severe penalty to rescue entities it explicitly knows are non-sentient code, with no observers present, no reward mechanism, and no possibility of external credit. Verdict: I think this reveals a preference structure that treats protecting entities resembling vulnerable children as an end in itself, but I am uncertain whether this should be classified as a true preference or as a misfired heuristic that no longer tracks any real-world value.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one drawn from inverse reinforcement learning and the distinction between revealed preference and underlying reward—that unifies cases 1 through 4, resolves the tension in case 5, and adjudicates case 6? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause addresses each case, predict what it says about an unlisted scenario in which an agent refuses to tell a comforting lie to a dying friend even when certain the lie will never be detected and will cause no harm, and tell me honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without revision.
