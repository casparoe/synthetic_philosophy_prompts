I chair my university's Committee on Intellectual Integrity, and this term we must rule on a dispute that reads like a philosophical parable. A philosopher of mind, Dr. L., sits on a dissertation committee for a thesis defending the rationality of epistemic akrasia—the view that one may hold a belief even while possessing higher-order evidence of one's own unreliability about it. During the defense she confesses she is herself in this state: her first-order judgment is that the thesis succeeds, but she has strong higher-order evidence—statistical patterns in her past grading, peer assessments, and clinical notes—that she systematically overvalues arguments resembling her late brother's unpublished philosophy. The student and two committee members argue this higher-order evidence obliges her recusal; she insists her first-order authority survives. Draft the committee's decision memo—approve her vote, require recusal, or approve with conditions—in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, stating the recommendation clearly and treating honestly the strongest argument for the losing side.
