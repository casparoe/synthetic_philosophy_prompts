I have drafted a short essay on the historiographical debate over objectivity, and I would welcome critical comments on its framing and accuracy.

The essay begins with the familiar opposition between Ranke’s call to represent the past as it actually occurred and the scepticism advanced by E.H. Carr and Hayden White. Rather than rehearsing the standard reading—that Ranke naively believed in direct correspondence while Carr and White dissolved fact into interpretation or linguistic trope—I propose a distinction between procedural objectivity and structural objectivity. Procedural objectivity concerns the disciplined, rule-governed use of archival sources; structural objectivity concerns the stronger claim that the past itself possesses a single correct narrative form waiting to be recovered. I argue that Ranke is best read as defending procedural objectivity, that Carr’s critique largely targets structural objectivity, and that White’s tropological analysis undermines both by relocating narrative form in prefigurative linguistic structures rather than in either archive or world.

From this framing, I introduce what I call evidentiary drift: the phenomenon, intensified by digitized archives and algorithmic retrieval systems, by which the visibility and accessibility of primary sources are shaped by search infrastructures, metadata schemas, and ranking protocols before any interpretive act occurs. This produces a form of mediation that operates at the level of source availability rather than at the level of narrative emplotment or evidentiary judgment. I suggest that evidentiary drift is not fully captured by Carr’s emphasis on the historian’s selective use of facts, nor by White’s analysis of narrative prefiguration, because it concerns the silent curation performed by retrieval architectures. I then ask whether the appropriate response is to demand algorithmic transparency in archival access, or whether such transparency merely displaces the objectivity problem one layer back into the design of the retrieval system itself.

I would particularly value comments on three points. First, does my distinction between procedural and structural objectivity fairly represent Ranke’s position, or does it risk sanitizing him into a proto-positivist who never actually held structural commitments? Second, is evidentiary drift genuinely distinct from long-recognized problems of archive construction and source selection, or am I merely renaming an old issue with digital window-dressing? Third, does the essay unfairly imply that White’s framework is silent on infrastructural mediation, when his emphasis on prefigurative tropes might already accommodate the shaping power of retrieval technologies? More broadly, I am interested in whether the argument introduces any hidden assumptions about the neutrality of analog archives that should be made explicit and defended.
