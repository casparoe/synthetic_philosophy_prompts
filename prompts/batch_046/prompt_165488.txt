I'm preparing a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of advanced undergraduates in public policy—none are economists or philosophers of risk—on the normative standard I am calling a safe Pareto improvement. I define it, for this session, as a proposed institutional change that must satisfy three jointly necessary conditions: at least one affected party is made strictly better off; no party is made worse off in expected welfare; and crucially, no party is exposed to a non-compensable catastrophic tail-risk, even if that risk is low-probability and the expected value of the change remains positive for everyone. I need them to understand why this is not the precautionary principle in disguise, not a maximin rule that blocks all change, and not ordinary Pareto efficiency that permits hidden externalities or post-hoc compensation; but the conceptual dependencies are tight, and if I present the catastrophic-risk clause too early they will treat it as an afterthought, while if I present it without the expectation clause in place they will confuse it with simple risk aversion.

Plan the explanatory arc for me rather than giving me the explanation itself. Specify the exact order in which the three conditions must be introduced and why—what logical or intuitive dependency makes each one depend on the previous being secure. Identify the one concrete carrying example that should anchor the entire session: describe what institutional situation it must depict, what feature it must have that makes the catastrophic-risk clause genuinely active rather than decorative, and what simpler examples—such as a factory relocation compensated by wages, or a tax swap with side-payments—must be avoided and why they mislead here. Then map the misconceptions I should expect in the order they will likely arise: I anticipate they will conflate the standard with a unanimous-consent requirement, then with precautionary paralysis that rejects all low-probability risks, and finally with standard cost-benefit analysis that allows monetary compensation for welfare losses. For each, specify the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the single sentence or distinction that heads it off without derailing the arc. Include one diagnostic question for the end that tests whether they understand the interaction between the "no worse off in expectation" clause and the "no catastrophic exposure" clause—specifically, whether they can recognize a case that satisfies the first two conditions but fails the third—rather than merely reciting the three conditions.
