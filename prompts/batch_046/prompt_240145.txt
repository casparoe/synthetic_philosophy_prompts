I'm trying to work out my own reflective equilibrium on intoxication—when it is permissible, valuable, or corrupt—using Platonic instincts as a guide but testing them against concrete judgments rather than texts. Here are my gut verdicts on cases, with one pair I keep flipping.

Cases and verdicts:
1. A small philosophical circle shares diluted wine through a long evening, letting inhibition drop so they can stress-test arguments about justice; conversation stays precise, and they stop well before anyone slurs. Verdict: this is not merely allowed but genuinely good—the wine serves the logos.
2. A brilliant but unstable young man, Alcibiades-like, gets drunk at a symposium and confesses hidden political ambitions and erotic shame; his sober friends use the revelation to correct him. Verdict: the drunken disclosure is valuable, even though the state is risky.
3. A democratic assembly votes to declare war during a public drinking festival, with citizens deciding in aggressive, camaraderie-fueled groups. Verdict: clearly corrupt—deliberation is destroyed.
4. A physician gives strong wine to a dying soldier to ease his final hours, knowing it will cloud his reason. Verdict: permissible, perhaps compassionate—the purpose is relief, not decision or revelation.
5. A general allows his troops heavy drinking the night before battle specifically to deaden fear of death; the battle is won, but with unnecessary cruelty. Verdict: wrong—using intoxication to suppress moral judgment and fear is a misuse of the soul's hierarchy.
6. A poet insists she writes true verse only in blackout drunkenness, producing work she cannot remember or defend sober. Verdict: this feels wrong despite the art's quality—something about reason being alienated from its own product.
7. A lawgiver institutes supervised state symposia for citizens over thirty, with magistrates present, precisely so hidden character traits emerge and can be corrected by the community. Verdict: wise—not indulgence but a diagnostic instrument governed by law.
8. A teacher gets drunk with students to "level hierarchy" and, disinhibited, reveals contempt for one student. Verdict: bad—the vulnerability created is exploited rather than healed.

The tension: case 1 and case 6 both involve intoxication producing something truthful (sharper philosophical testing; true verse), yet I endorse the first and reject the second. Meanwhile, case 2 and case 8 both involve hidden truths emerging through drink, but one is therapeutic and the other harmful.

My tentative principle: intoxication is permissible only when it operates within a framework where reason retains ultimate authority—either directing the session (case 1, 7), being served by it (case 4), or being restored through the revelation (case 2)—and impermissible when it either replaces reason in a decision (case 3, 5) or severs reason from ownership of the result (case 6, 8).
