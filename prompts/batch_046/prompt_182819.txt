I am a retired physicist who, since leaving active research, have turned to philosophy of science and formal epistemology. One issue that arises repeatedly in discussions of persistent scientific disagreement—over quantum interpretations, for instance—is the failure of the common prior assumption in actual research communities. Rather than treat this as a mere curiosity, I want to understand which conceptual questions about it are genuinely upstream and worth sustained philosophical attention.

If a small group—perhaps four philosophers working for three years—were to focus on this, I would ask them to prioritize among at least three distinguishable lines of work: first, whether prior divergence in science mainly reflects informational asymmetry, normative disagreement about what counts as adequate explanation, or structural incommensurability between theoretical frameworks; second, whether the standard Bayesian account of agreement—under conditions of common knowledge—adequately describes long-running scientific disputes, or whether it systematically mischaracterizes them; third, what follows for the design of scientific institutions if we treat persistent disagreement not as a rational failure but as a predictable feature of distributed inquiry.

But I do not want a survey; I want a defended agenda. Which of these lines should command the bulk of effort, and in what order? For each, please explain why it is tractable for philosophers rather than formal economists or cognitive scientists, how resolving it would affect adjacent debates, and what conceptual clarification or empirical development over the next few years would change its ranking. Please also distinguish sharply between what is settled—such as the formal conditions under which rational agents with identical priors must converge—and what remains genuinely contested among those who study the topic, and avoid presenting any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
