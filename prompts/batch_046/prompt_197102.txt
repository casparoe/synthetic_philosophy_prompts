In discussions of wild-insect welfare, proponents of the scale argument observe that if even a small fraction of the enormous global insect population—perhaps on the order of 10^19 individuals alive at once—possesses even minimal sentience, the aggregate moral stakes become astronomical; opponents reply that without taxon-specific evidence for integrative consciousness or nociception, multiplying a near-zero prior by a vast number still yields negligible expected moral weight, and that moral consideration should require a binary threshold of established status rather than probabilistic weighting. Despite decades of new neuroethological findings about mushroom-body architecture and nociceptive pathways, neither camp has moved. Is this impasse genuinely empirical—awaiting a decisive anatomical or behavioral discovery—or is it conceptual? In particular, do the sides disagree about whether sentience is graded or binary, about whether radical uncertainty licenses expected-value reasoning in ethics, or about what counts as adequate evidence for attributing consciousness across distant phyla? Or are they answering different questions—one about aggregate risk management, the other about individual moral standing—under a single heading? Diagnose the structure of the stall using what each side actually says about probability and moral consideration, and specify what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result about decision-making under ignorance, an empirical finding that shifts priors, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the framing, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned.
