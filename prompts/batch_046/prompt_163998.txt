Please describe the most important implications of Gilbert Ryle’s distinction between knowing how and knowing that for the evaluation of expertise in domains—such as clinical diagnosis, judicial reasoning, and musical interpretation—where highly competent practitioners routinely render correct judgments yet cannot articulate the explicit rules or propositional grounds that govern those judgments. In particular, address whether the distinction genuinely supports the stronger claim that such expertise is in principle resistant to formalization and explicit pedagogy, or whether it reveals only practical obstacles that leave the project of codifying expert knowledge fundamentally intact. Further, consider what the distinction implies about the legitimacy of interrogative evaluation: if an expert cannot state that p is the reason for judgment J, does that failure undermine the epistemic standing of J, or does knowing how operate with autonomous criteria of success that do not depend on corresponding knowing-that? Conclude by indicating which of these implications, if any, survive once we distinguish procedural mastery from mere habituation or intuition.
