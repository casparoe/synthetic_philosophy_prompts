You are advising the director of a small interdisciplinary research program—five scholars with backgrounds in formal epistemology, decision theory, and institutional design, funded for four years—whose mandate is strictly conceptual: to clarify what Aumann's agreement theorem and its direct conceptual descendants actually imply for rational disagreement, common knowledge, and institutional coordination, without running experiments or collecting data. The group may tackle foundational questions (for example, whether the common-prior assumption can be justified without circularity; how severely logical-omniscience failures undermine the result; whether agreement is best understood as an epistemic obligation, a consistency constraint, or a coordination norm) or applied conceptual work (for example, how agreement-theoretic reasoning should inform the design of peer-review protocols or federated learning architectures in which agents share raw observations but process them through structurally opaque or formally incompatible representational frameworks).

Your task is not to survey the literature but to propose a prioritized research agenda: which three to four open questions or lines of work deserve the bulk of this group's effort, in what order, and which should be explicitly deprioritized? For each prioritized item, defend its placement using criteria of upstream dependency (how many other questions depend on resolving it first), tractability for this specific mix of philosophers and formal theorists rather than for empirical scientists, and the theoretical or practical payoff if the confusion is dissolved. Specify also what kind of result—say, a formal model that relaxes an unrealistic assumption, a normative distinction that separates consistency from convergence, or a conceptual argument about institutional design—would cause you to reorder the agenda after two years. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation of specific papers; assume the director is familiar with the canonical framework. In particular, address whether the highest-value work lies in repairing the theorem's unrealistic premises, in drawing out normative implications for real-world disagreement among bounded agents, or in extending its logic to settings of shared data but incompatible processing frameworks, and explain what makes one of these directions more urgent than the others for this particular group.
