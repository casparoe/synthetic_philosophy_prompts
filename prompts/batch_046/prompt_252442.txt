I have recently been working through Dennett’s four-tier taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—to understand adaptive behavior in artificial systems, and I keep encountering an analogy that maps them onto generations of AI: hard-coded expert systems are Darwinian; reinforcement-learning agents are Skinnerian; planning algorithms that simulate outcomes internally are Popperian; and large language models, because they depend on culturally transmitted symbolic scaffolding, are Gregorian. The analogy is being used to argue that aligning AI should shift away from reward-engineering toward a kind of cultural socialization, on the grounds that Gregorian minds are shaped more by language and tradition than by direct reinforcement. Before I rely on this framing, I want to audit the mapping precisely: what corresponds to the genotype, the phenotype, the selection environment, the internal model, and the cultural toolkit in each pairing? Where does the correspondence break down—particularly regarding whether a Popperian planner’s internal “death” of failed hypotheses has any meaningful analog in Monte-Carlo tree search, and whether an LLM’s statistical pattern-matching genuinely parallels the Gregorian acquisition of conceptual tools? And once these disanalogies are on the table, does the normative conclusion—that alignment requires socialization rather than reward shaping—survive, or was the analogy carrying the argument all along?
