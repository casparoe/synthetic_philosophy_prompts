I was chatting with a colleauge after a panel on existantialism and we got stuck on this thing about Sarte. Everyone always brings up the waiter in bad faith — you know, the guy who over-identifies with his role like he *is* nothing but a waiter — but I keep wondering if that example actually holds up once you drag in his later stuff from the Critique of Dialectical Reason. In Being and Nothingness he says consciousness is pure nothingness, always transcending its facticity, and bad faith is basically a personal lie we tell ourselves to flee our radical freedom. But then in the Critique he describes seriality and the practico-inert, where people are locked into passive, interchangeable roles by material conditions and institutional inertia, not by personal choice. So can you explain the conceptual tension between Sartre's early ontology — where freedom is absolute and bad faith is an individual moral failure — and his later idea that structures can produce a collective, structural bad faith that isn't just a bunch of individuals lying to themselves? I want a committed take: does seriality actually undermine the claim that freedom is always absolute, or does it just shift bad faith from a psychological mistake to a socio-material trap? And if it's the latter, was the waiter example always too individualistic, missing how institutions can enforce false identity regardless of what the waiter personally believes? Don't just lay out both sides — pick one and defend it, but be honest about where you're stretching Sartre's texts. I'm writing this up for a science-and-ideas column so I need the gut-level mechanics, not a bibliography.
