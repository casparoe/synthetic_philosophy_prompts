I'm designing a diagnostic handout for a graduate workshop on self-locating belief and statistical reasoning, and I need a decision tree—not a list of proper names—that sorts positions on the reference class problem by the commitments that actually drive disagreement. The tree should begin with a concrete scenario a student can hold in mind: a reasoner knows she is one of exactly 1,000 people who share an observable profile, but she also knows that an unobserved causal mechanism divides that population into subgroups with radically different frequencies for the trait she wants to predict. From there, the first branch should ask whether observable similarity alone is sufficient to define her reference class, or whether knowledge of that hidden causal structure is required in advance to justify splitting the class. The second branch should ask whether her indexical perspective—the bare fact that she is asking the question now, or that she occupies this particular spatiotemporal position—updates the probabilities within a fixed partition, or whether it functions as a non-evidential selection filter that leaves the probabilities unchanged but determines which ones apply to her. The third branch should ask whether there is an objectively correct partition independent of her epistemic state, perhaps grounded in natural kinds or in the world's causal architecture, or whether the appropriate class is always relative to the information available to the agent.

For each combination of answers, the tree should locate the corresponding position—such as an unrestricted frequentist view, a hierarchical empirical-Bayesian view, a pure indexical-selection view, or a causal-objectivist view—and should also mark any unoccupied quadrant, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap stems from incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or mere historical neglect. In particular, flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, especially views that agree on all three branches but differ only in rhetorical framing or in how they describe the metaphysical status of the causal partition. Every node should be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which philosophical label it leads to. Do not include bibliographies, assigned readings, or requests for citations; the deliverable should be a text-rendered flowchart whose purpose is to let a reasoner place herself in logical space rather than identify herself by a theory's name.
