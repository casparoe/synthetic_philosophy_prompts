As a philosophy student currently taking a course on philosophy of language and applied ethics, I am working through a practical briefing exercise. I want you to act as an expert in philosophical logic and institutional design—someone who can translate formal debates about vagueness and the sorites paradox into runnable protocols for non-specialists. Please think carefully before answering, and begin with brief brainstorming: which features of sorites-style accumulation pose the greatest risk for regulatory thresholds, and which broad theoretical responses—sharp-boundary accounts, degree-theoretic frameworks, or contextualist adjustments—best survive translation into a form an officer can sign?

Then construct a reusable decision procedure, presented as an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes, for a municipal heritage-preservation officer. The officer must determine whether a proposed bundle of incremental modifications to a protected historic district—each change individually within permitted tolerance—collectively crosses the statutory threshold of "material alteration" that triggers full commission review. The procedure must be grounded in the logic of vagueness and tolerance principles, yet fully operable by a practitioner without philosophical training in under thirty minutes per proposal.

The procedure should specify, in strict sequence: the questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer at each node; the precise stopping point where the result is a definite "no further review required"; and the precise hand-off point where the remaining issue is one of values or convention rather than fact, requiring an explicit signed judgment by the officer or commission. It must distinguish horizontal accumulation (small changes dispersed across many features) from vertical accumulation (repeated deep changes within a single feature), and it must account for interaction effects in which two permitted changes combine to yield a non-permitted visual or structural outcome. Incorporate an invented "cumulative deviation index" that weights changes not only by individual magnitude but by directional coherence—whether modifications pull the district’s character in a unified direction or in conflicting directions—and explain how this index relates to the philosophical problem of tolerance principles without resolving the underlying metaphysics.

Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire, particularly where the boundary is genuinely indeterminate and any sharp cutoff is conventional. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (A) replacing five individual non-original windows with energy-efficient replicas of slightly different proportions, each within single-window tolerance; (B) adding three small rooftop HVAC units and repainting two facades in marginally lighter shades, producing horizontal accumulation with potential interaction effects on skyline uniformity; and (C) a vertical accumulation case involving successive reductions in cornice height across adjacent buildings that, taken together, alter the street-wall rhythm, even though each reduction falls within the individual-building variance—this should be the case where the procedure returns a clear "material alteration" finding that the applicant will strongly contest.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: for example, whether it can adjudicate metaphysical debates about whether there is a "real" boundary, and whether it eliminates all need for human judgment rather than relocating it. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote philosophers verbatim; refer to theoretical positions only in general terms. Maintain a formal, polished tone throughout.
