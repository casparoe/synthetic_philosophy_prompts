I run a philosophy-of-mind reading group of eight graduate students for 90 minutes. Design a structured role-play that makes the group feel the conceptual pressure behind "quining" qualia — the argument that intrinsic phenomenal properties are not merely epistemically hidden but incoherent, since any property that can be attributed to a subject or play an explanatory role must be relational or functional, while a genuinely non-relational property could not belong to anyone.

Divide participants into four "phenomenal auditors" and four "case presenters." Give the auditors a worksheet with four criteria that any "qualia" must satisfy: (1) intrinsic (not defined by relations), (2) non-functional, (3) directly known by the subject, and (4) causally efficacious (able to explain behavior or reports). Give the presenters four scenarios: a blindsight patient who behaves adaptively without reportable experience, a perfect functional duplicate with no "inner light," a subject who reports inverted color experience with no behavioral difference, and an artificial system that passes every report-based test. The presenters argue for classification; the auditors vote and justify.

Build in a hidden logical tension, revealed only after 30 minutes: if a property is intrinsic and non-functional, it cannot be relational to a subject and thus fails direct knowledge; but if it is relational, it is not intrinsic. The auditors will find themselves applying the criteria inconsistently, with opposite votes on the same case.

Then run a 15-minute forced-choice round: each auditor must either drop one criterion (and admit they are no longer describing "qualia") or keep all four (and concede that no mental state satisfies them, rendering the concept empty).

Debrief (20 minutes): map the classification chaos onto the intrinsic/relational contradiction, the hidden-rule exposure onto the emptiness of the concept, and the forced choice onto the cost of preserving it. Predict the failure mode: participants will initially treat the conflict as epistemic — assuming further science could resolve which criterion applies — and instruct the facilitator to press them in the forced-choice round by asking whether any empirical discovery could reconcile all four conditions. Be explicit about the activity's limit: it cannot reproduce the first-person phenomenological pull of "what it's like," so the facilitator must supply that in the final minutes, asking the group to hold the felt reality of experience alongside the conceptual box they have just shown to be empty. Do not name specific authors or cite texts; the idea should emerge from the rules.
