I've been teaching this unit on environmental determinism and now I'm stuck on an argument a student threw at me. It seems to show that if germs or volcanoes are "historical actors" in any serious sense, then human moral responsibilty evaporates. Here's the chain: (1) To call X a historical actor is to say X's contributions aren't fully reducible to background conditions — there's some minimal agential profile. (2) Germs, climate shifts, and volcanic winters have shaped wars, revolutions, and economies in ways that can't be reduced to human choices (think the Black Death restructuring feudal labor stuctures, or Tambora's harvest failures feeding into 19th-century unrest). (3) So they meet the condition for being historical actors. (4) But if non-human entities are co-actors in every major event, then no human outcome is ever solely attributable to human agents. (5) Moral responsibility requires that the agent be the sufficient source of the action. (6) So if volcanoes are always co-actors, humans are never solely responsible, hence never morally responsible. (7) That's absurd — we clearly blame people. (8) Therefore we must reject (1) or (3), which means environmental determinism is conceptually incoherent, not just empirically false. I feel like there's a slide between causal contribution and agential co-authorship somewhere, but I can't pin it. Which step do I reject, and what's the cost? I'd like to keep (2) if possible since the empirical cases feel solid.
