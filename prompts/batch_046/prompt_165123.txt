Essay: "Referential Collapse and the Epistemic Event Horizon"

Consider two ontological posits, Φ and Ψ, which are structurally identical across every domain of observation and intervention accessible to any epistemic agent. By hypothesis, the information that might distinguish them lies beyond an "epistemic event horizon" — not a physical boundary, but a principled limit on what any possible observer could ever register, even in principle. The realist insists that Φ and Ψ may nevertheless be distinct kinds, because reality need not align with our epistemic reach. Against this, I propose a principle of referential collapse: if no identification condition can ever be satisfied — not because of contingent ignorance, but because the distinguishing features are permanently inaccessible — then Φ and Ψ do not constitute two separate ontological categories. They are not identical in the strong sense of being one and the same entity, but they collapse into a single reference frame for the purposes of ontology. To maintain them as distinct is to multiply kinds beyond what any structural account of identification can justify. One might object that inaccessible differences could still play a causal or explanatory role hidden from view. Yet if that causal role is itself undetectable in every accessible consequence, it becomes metaphysically idle. The burden, I suggest, lies with the realist to show why an undetectable distinction should be preserved in our ontology rather than treated as a redundant duplication of reference.
