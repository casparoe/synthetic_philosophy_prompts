I am preparing a syllabus for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of history, and I want to open the unit on historical knowledge with Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon. The film presents four mutually incompatible eyewitness accounts of a single violent event in a forest—the assault on a woman and the death of her samurai husband—without ever confirming which testimony, if any, corresponds to what occurred. I would like you to read the film through the lens of the debate between Leopold von Ranke's ideal of describing the past as it actually was, captured in his formulation rendered in German as wie es eigentlich gewesen, and the sceptical or constructivist challenges raised by E.H. Carr and especially Hayden White, for whom historical narratives are not discovered but imposed through prior tropological choices.

Specifically, I want an interpretation that avoids the banal conclusion that Rashomon merely demonstrates that history is subjective. Instead, examine whether the four testimonies correspond structurally to Hayden White's four modes of emplotment—romance, tragedy, comedy, and satire—and whether this mapping suggests that historical disagreement is not simply an epistemic failure but an inherent feature of narrative form itself. How does the woodcutter's final, apparently redemptive account complicate the pattern? Does it provide a Rankean escape route back toward a recoverable fact, or does it collapse into yet another narrative frame with its own rhetorical demands? Furthermore, consider whether Kurosawa's refusal to resolve the contradiction dramatizes something that Carr's attack on objective fact and White's metahistory overlook: for instance, the possibility that the event itself is ontologically underdetermined rather than merely epistemically opaque.

Please be precise about which scenes or narrative devices support your reading—the bandit's self-aggrandizement, the wife's shifting emotional registers, the medium's possession, the commoner's cynical framing—and be honest about where the film resists reduction to philosophical illustration. Where does the emotional or moral weight of a particular testimony push back against treating it as an example of tropology? Finally, say what this cinematic case contributes to the philosophical debate about objectivity that a purely textual argument about archives, sources, and narrative form cannot fully capture on its own.
