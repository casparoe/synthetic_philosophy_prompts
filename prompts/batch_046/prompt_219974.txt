As a retired physiologist who has only recently begun serious study of philosophy of mind, I find that contemporary defenses of a broadly Cartesian view of animals often rely on an analogy I have encountered repeatedly in both popular science writing and philosophical discussion: an animal navigating its environment is like an automobile’s cruise-control system—sensors detect deviation from a set point, a comparator computes error, and actuators correct course, all without the system representing the road, anticipating traffic, or experiencing anything like concern for its destination. The analogy is explicitly offered as a modernized version of Descartes’ clockwork automaton: behavior that looks intentional is really just closed-loop feedback, and the apparent flexibility of learning is merely parameter adjustment within the loop. Those who deploy it use it to license a specific conclusion—that attributing phenomenal consciousness, conceptual thought, or genuine moral status to non-human animals is a category mistake analogous to saying the cruise controller “wants” to maintain sixty miles per hour.

I want an audit of this analogy that takes a definite position rather than merely cataloguing pros and cons. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what in the animal maps onto the sensor array, the comparator, the set point, the actuator, and the absence of an interior monitor? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended philosophical use—for instance, whether the brain’s capacity to generate internal models that anticipate sensory input introduces representational content that a thermostat lacks, whether plasticity and developmental restructuring in animals break the “fixed loop” image, and whether the analogy quietly imports determinism or linear causation that modern neuroscience rejects. Third, say which of these disanalogies actually undermine the Cartesian conclusion that animals lack inner experience, and which are merely cosmetic. Fourth, evaluate whether the conclusion survives once the analogy is repaired: if we update the source domain to a modern adaptive predictive controller rather than a simple cruise-control loop, does the updated analogy still support the denial of consciousness, or does it carry the opposite implication? Finally, if the cruise-control analogy fails as a defense of Cartesianism, propose a better analogy for the relation between animal behavior and conscious mind—one that does not beg the question—and defend your choice. Please be committed: take a clear stance on whether the analogy can bear the philosophical weight it is asked to carry, and state what follows for how we should regard animal minds.
