Consider the following reconstructed passage from Hegel's Philosophy of Right, drawn from the sections treating international relations and the ethical idea of the state. The state is an individual possessed of sovereignty. In conditions of prolonged peace, the particular interests of civil society become fixed, private concerns dominate, and the ethical unity that binds citizens to the universal weakens. War interrupts this stagnation. By requiring citizens to risk life and property for the state, war demonstrates that the authority of the state is supreme over individual existence, and it restores consciousness of the universal over particularity. Between sovereign states, however, there is no superior judge or law; therefore war is the condition in which each state's sovereignty is either recognized or denied by another. The ethical significance of war lies not in violence itself, but in the willingness of individuals to subordinate their particular existence to the universal will of the state.

I am trying to understand how this view coheres with Hegel's broader claim that rational freedom requires self-determination according to universal, law-governed principles, since war appears to suspend precisely those legal norms both internally and externally. In particular, does the recognition achieved between states in war operate analogously to the master-slave dialectic—where recognition is achieved through a life-and-death struggle over sovereignty—or does it belong to an entirely different logical structure within Hegel's system? Furthermore, if a state permanently avoids this risk and remains in perpetual peace, does it forfeit not merely its vitality but its very status as an actualized ethical idea? I would appreciate help working through these tensions, especially regarding whether the ethical moment of war is a necessary condition for the state's rationality or merely a remedial exception to it.
