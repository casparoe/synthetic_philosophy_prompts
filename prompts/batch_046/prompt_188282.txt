I've just spent the evening in a long argument with a friend about whether race, gender, and money are genuinely socially constructed in anything more than a weak, conventional sense, and I've come to defend a specific, stronger version of the view: these categories function as what I call institutional attractors. They begin as collective representations and institutional rules, yet through sustained feedback—analogous to the looping effects found in studies of interactive kinds, but extending into biological substrates and economic distributions—they progressively reshape the very properties they purport merely to classify. The upshot, I think, is that for these particular kinds the distinction between discovering a pre-existing division and inventing one collapses in practice. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Which empirical findings—say, in behavioural genetics, behavioural economics, or developmental neuroscience—would genuinely undermine the claim that these classifications alter their substrates rather than simply selecting for pre-existing variation? Which philosophical objections—perhaps concerning causal overdetermination, the ontological status of feedback loops, or the risk of circular explanation—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? I also want to know which defeaters a committed defender of this view would concede as damaging, and which they would dismiss as misunderstanding the mechanism. Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by treating every observed substrate change as merely another turn of the loop—and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a defect for a claim of this kind? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated if that is where the argument genuinely leads.
