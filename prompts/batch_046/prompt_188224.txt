I'm a phd student in political philosophy working on civic republicanism and defence policy, and I've been trying to defend a kind of hybrid view that feels underdiscussed in the literature. The idea is that armed neutrality — think Switzerland or Sweden's historical posture — isn't mainly sustained by geography or diplomatic tradition, but by a specific institutional linkage: universal conscription functions as what I want to call constitutional risk-pooling. Because every citizen bears the direct burden of service, no social subgroup can externalise the cost of conflict onto others, which makes the threat of total societal resistance credible to potential aggressors and prevents the emergence of a separate military caste. Once you shift to a professional army, that risk-pool collapses: the state can project force or maintain neutrality without mobilising society, so neutrality becomes expensive in fiscal terms but cheap in social terms, and democratic control weakens because the armed force is structurally detached. I think this explains why Finland kept conscription even after joining NATO, but I'm definately not sure I'm not missing something big.
