I'm serving as a senior referee for an interdisciplinary journal that publishes both technical logic and philosophy of mathematics, and about twice a year we receive unsolicited submissions claiming to have derived contradictions from standard axiomatic systems—usually ZFC or Peano arithmetic. My co-editors want a reusable triage protocol that a non-logician can apply in under an hour to decide whether the claim deserves peer review or should be returned with a brief justification, and they have asked me to design it around the philosophical idea that an alleged foundational inconsistency is not like an empirical falsehood but like a structural failure claim: it must satisfy conditions of derivational transparency, semantic stability, and cross-system portability before it can be taken as a genuine threat to the axioms rather than as a local proof error or a hidden redefinition. The procedure needs to say exactly where it stops with a desk rejection, where it hands the decision to the board because values—such as institutional risk and disciplinary norms—outweigh the formal assessment, and what it explicitly cannot do, including establishing consistency positively or ruling out all future revisions.

Please construct a four-stage audit procedure—derivational reconstruction, semantic anchoring, system translation, and consequence calibration—that translates that structural-failure framework into a checklist a referee without advanced logic training can run, specifying for each stage the precise question, the minimal evidence that counts as passing, the failure mode that triggers rejection, and the judgment point where formal assessment ends and institutional choice begins. Include a small set of test questions, no more than three per stage, that can be applied quickly to the submission's central argument, and flag the cases where the procedure is indeterminate and requires human arbitration rather than mechanical scoring. Then apply the full procedure to three submissions: (a) one arguing that ZFC's axiom schema of replacement produces a contradiction when applied to collections defined through modal accessibility relations across possible models; (b) one arguing that Peano arithmetic fails because the induction schema does not cover indefinitely extensible number sequences generated by an open-ended constructive rule; and (c) one that technically passes all four stages but implies the collapse of basic real analysis, producing a recommendation for peer review that conflicts with the editorial board's strong preference to avoid publishing destabilizing foundational claims unless the derivation is already widely vetted. For the third case, state clearly what the procedure can and cannot resolve, identify the exact point at which values rather than formal logic must carry the decision, and include one worked example of a test question from the consequence-calibration stage that yields an answer the board will not want to hear.
