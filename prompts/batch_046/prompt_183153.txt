I need you to write a five-minute speech for a retirement ceremony. Most people recall Kant’s vision of a federation of free republics and a cosmopolitan right of hospitality. What struck me, however, is that he separates preliminary articles—immediate, concrete prohibitions that states must observe, such as maintaining standing armies, contracting state debts specifically to fund future wars, or forcibly interfering in another state’s constitution—from the definitive articles that actually establish a lasting peace. This matters because it means perpetual peace is not a single grand treaty or moment of harmony, but rather a long, unglamorous dismantling of the machinery that makes war easy, followed slowly by federated structures that do not erase sovereignty.

The occasion: I am speaking at the retirement of my former supervisor, Elena Vasquez, who spent thirty-five years in international arbitration and mediation. Roughly half the room are her professional colleagues—lawyers, diplomats, and a few scholars of international relations who will recognize a Kant reference—while the other half are family and neighbors with no philosophical background. The speech must land for both groups. I want to honor Elena without the false grandeur of saying she achieved world peace, which would sound naive to the experts and hollow to everyone else. Instead, I want the preliminary-versus-definitive distinction to do real structural work: it should shape what I can and cannot claim about her career. Specifically, I want to frame her legacy as preliminary labor—removing the incentives for escalation, refusing to let arms dynamics or debt pressures drive negotiations, and preserving the sovereignty of the parties rather than imposing solutions—thereby making space for the slower, federative work she never claimed to have finished.

Please write the speech. It should be polished, suitable for a mixed audience of experts and non-experts, and should avoid bibliographies, citations, or references to specific scholars beyond Kant himself. Do not quote Kant verbatim; paraphrase without quotation marks is fully acceptable. Make sure the philosophical idea shapes the rhetoric—for instance, by explaining why framing her contribution as preliminary work is more truthful and more respectful than framing it as final victory—and keep the tone affectionate rather than lecturing.
