Examination question:
Critically evaluate Epicurus's argument that death is not harmful to the one who dies. Your answer should address (a) the no-subject argument, which turns on the dissolution of the soul and the hedonist claim that all good and bad consist in pleasure and pain; (b) the symmetry argument comparing our attitude towards pre-natal non-existence with our attitude towards post-mortem non-existence; (c) the modern deprivation account, according to which death harms the deceased by depriving them of future goods they would otherwise have enjoyed; and (d) the asymmetry of causal dependence objection, which observes that pre-natal absence is not brought about by our present life, whereas death is its causal termination, and asks whether this undermines the symmetry claim without relying on the premise that harm must be experienced.

Student answer:
The Epicurean position rests on a hedonist axiology: pleasure and pain are the sole criteria of good and bad. From this, Epicurus derives his well-known claim that death is nothing to us. His reasoning is that when we exist, death is not present, and when death is present, we do not exist. Because harm requires a subject who experiences it, and the dead have no experiences, death cannot be a harm to the deceased.

Epicurus strengthens this with the symmetry argument, originally set out in the letter addressed to Menoeceus. He asks us to reflect on the eternity that passed before our birth. We do not regard that pre-natal period as an evil, despite its being a state of non-existence. By symmetry, we should not regard the post-mortem state as an evil either. The argument suggests that our fear of death is irrational because it treats two identical conditions — non-existence — differently.

Against this, the deprivation account, associated with the philosopher Thomas Nagel, contends that death is harmful not because it involves suffering, but because it cuts short a life that would have contained valuable experiences. The dead person is deprived of goods they would have possessed.

However, I think Epicurus can respond effectively. The deprivation account assumes that harm can occur without a subject to experience it, but this is precisely what Epicurus denies. Furthermore, the asymmetry of causal dependence objection — which notes that our pre-natal absence is not caused by our life, whereas death terminates it — fails to establish that death is experienced as bad; it merely highlights a structural difference between the two absences. Therefore, the symmetry argument remains intact, and the deprivation account must be mistaken in treating death as a harm comparable to injury or illness, since neither can exist without a subject, and death, in annihilating the subject, removes the very condition for harm.

One might object that death prevents the realisation of future projects. But Epicurus would reply that projects have value only for a living agent; once the agent is gone, there is no one for whom the unfulfilled projects constitute a loss. Thus, the argument succeeds, and death is not bad for us.
