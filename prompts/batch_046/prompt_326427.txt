I am a retired physicist who has spent the last three years studying early modern philosophy, and I am preparing to present a paper at an interdisciplinary seminar on Leibniz's moral philosophy. The audience will be about eighteen people: half are historians of philosophy specializing in the seventeenth century, deeply familiar with Leibniz's writings on justice and grace; the other half are philosophers of science and applied ethicists who think naturally in terms of systems dynamics and optimization but have little patience for theological metaphysics. I will defend the following thesis: that Leibniz's conception of justice—understood as charity exercised by the wise—should not be read as either a deontological constraint or a consequentialist maximization of universal happiness, but rather as a dynamic equilibrium process analogous to thermodynamic relaxation, in which moral agents continuously adjust local parameters of right and grace so that the global system converges toward maximal compossible harmony. I call this the equilibrium-reading of Leibnizian morality. I need a speaker's Q&A preparation sheet: the eight objections I am most likely to face, ordered by how damaging they would be to my argument, each phrased in the vocabulary and tone of the audience subgroup that would raise it. For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute response, a frank note on where that reply is weakest, and an indication of whether I should concede a point or hold firm. Include the one objection I am hoping no one asks—the one that strikes at the analogical core of my thesis—and sketch how I might survive it if it comes. The objections should target both the historical plausibility of attributing this systems-theoretic structure to Leibniz and the philosophical coherence of treating moral agency as parameter-tuning within a predetermined harmony.
