In popular explanations of consciousness, experience is compared to a live television program assembled in a single control room: sensory modalities arrive like separate camera feeds, an unseen director edits them into one continuous stream, and that stream is projected onto a screen watched by a solitary viewer—the self. This picture is often used to motivate a Cartesian-theater assumption: that somewhere in the brain there is a privileged point where disparate processes converge into a unified, present experience. Set against this is Dennett's multiple drafts model, which rejects any central projection room and treats consciousness as a distributed, parallel editing process with no authoritative master version.

Audit the control-room analogy. Provide an explicit correspondence table: identify what corresponds to the director, the camera feeds, the editing suite, the master tape, the screen, the viewer, and the real-time broadcast. Then specify the points of breakdown, with particular attention to three issues: temporal binding (whether consciousness requires a synchronous frame rate), the unity of the self (whether there must be one audience member), and the status of a "final cut" (whether there is a definitive version of experience that is "shown" to anyone). Examine what normative or metaphysical baggage the analogy imports—especially the idea of a single, spatially localized convergence point—and ask whether that baggage does the argumentative work of making the Cartesian theater seem unavoidable. Next, consider an injected variant: suppose there is no director, but instead dozens of local editing bays, each producing its own version of the program with slightly different cuts, delays, and narrations, and the "official broadcast" is determined only retroactively whenever an external query demands a report. How well does this modified analogy represent the multiple drafts model, and does it still smuggle in a hidden theater—perhaps in the very mechanism that selects which draft answers the query? Conclude by assessing whether the original broadcast analogy's implication—that consciousness requires a central "showing"—survives this audit, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. If the analogy fails, propose a more apt image for distributed, draft-based consciousness.
