I'm working through my own position for a seminar paper on moral demandingness and the limits of obligation, and I want a consistency check before I present it. I hold the following commitments: (a) supererogation is a real moral category, not reducible either to strong obligation or to mere permissibility—there are acts that are genuinely good beyond the call of duty and that merit praise without being required; (b) the boundary between duty and supererogation should be drawn by appeal to what can be demanded of any agent without undermining their moral agency or central life projects, roughly in the spirit of contractualist limits on demandingness; (c) collective harms, such as climate-related damage, do not generate precise individual obligations to remedy them, because the causal contribution of each agent is too diffuse and the cost of full individual compliance disproportionately high, though coordinated institutional action is clearly required; (d) nevertheless, individuals who voluntarily take on substantial personal costs to reduce such harms, far beyond what fairness demands, perform genuinely supererogatory acts that deserve moral recognition; (e) a society that comes to depend systematically on such voluntary sacrifices to secure basic goods or prevent serious harm is morally defective, because it replaces just institutions with contingent virtue; and (f) supererogatory motivation must be somewhat transparent to the agent—one cannot accidentally perform a supererogatory act—though I am unsure whether this transparency condition applies to the moral evaluation of the act itself or only to the praiseworthiness of the agent.

Do these hang together? Specifically, is there a hidden tension between drawing the duty line via demandingness (b) and denying individual obligations in collective harm cases (c), given that some critics argue that diffuse responsibility actually intensifies rather than diminishes individual duty when institutions fail? Is (d) compatible with (e) if widespread supererogation becomes structurally expected and thus loses its voluntary character? And is (f) consistent with (a) if we allow that unintended heroic rescue might still be supererogatory in evaluation even when the agent lacks the relevant transparent motivation? Please rank any tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging, identify which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and indicate the least costly revision—whether abandoning (f), reinterpreting (c), or modifying (e)—together with what that revision costs me elsewhere. In your response, please separate what is relatively settled in moral philosophy from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any single view—especially on the demandingness debate or on whether supererogation requires transparent motivation—as the established consensus.
