I'm preparing a long-form feature for an ideas magazine on the enduring philosophical dispute around Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor. To structure the piece, I need a taxonomy—constructed as a branching decision tree rather than a list of names—of the positions one can hold on the Inquisitor's charge that Christ offered humanity a freedom it is structurally unable to bear, and that authority therefore does well to exchange it for security, wonder, and obedience.

Build the tree from the logical space. Begin with three diagnostic questions that split the interpretive field without requiring prior knowledge of the scholarship:

- Is the burden of freedom primarily epistemic (we lack stable knowledge of the good), affective (responsibility generates intolerable anxiety), or metaphysical (choice presupposes a groundless self)?
- Does the Inquisitor's remedy—bread, miracle, authority—extinguish freedom or redirect it onto a collective object?
- Is Christ's refusal to perform miracles an assertion of negative liberty, a call to positive self-creation, or an ascetic acceptance of suffering?

From the answers, generate the branches. Each leaf must name the position (use standard labels where they exist—e.g., paternalist, libertarian-Christian, existentialist, unnamed) and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment: the implication its defenders rarely acknowledge.

Then map the full combinatorial space: identify which answer-combinations are unoccupied, and for each explain whether it is incoherent, merely unexplored, or concealed inside a named view that conflates distinctions your tree keeps apart. Flag any pair of leaves that remain indistinguishable on these questions.

The taxonomy must include at least four specific positions: one that accepts the Inquisitor's diagnosis of human psychology but condemns his authoritarian remedy; one that accepts both diagnosis and remedy but argues this betrays rather than fulfills Christ's intent; one that denies the diagnosis and holds that freedom is sustainable only under conditions of material scarcity; and at least one unnamed combination produced by answering the three questions in a way no standard interpretation does.

Keep the tree to no more than eight leaves. Write for an educated general reader, not for specialists. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading.
