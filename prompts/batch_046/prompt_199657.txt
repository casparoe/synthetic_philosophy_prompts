As a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently begun reading in the philosophy of biology, I have tried to articulate my views on biological function in a way that respects both my scientific background and the conceptual distinctions I have encountered. Before presenting these to a reading group, I wish to check whether the package is genuinely coherent.

My commitments are as follows:

(i) For traits in naturally evolved lineages, I accept the etiological or selected-effect account: the proper function of a trait is the effect for which it was differentially retained by natural selection in its evolutionary history.

(ii) For traits in synthetic or directed-evolution systems—where there is no ancestral selection for the present activity—I adopt a causal-role account: function is assigned by the trait’s actual contribution to the current operational goals of the engineered system, irrespective of evolutionary origin.

(iii) I maintain that vestigial structures, such as the degenerate eyes of obligate cave-dwelling fish, possess no biological function whatsoever: they lack present causal efficacy, and I deny that they retain any dormant or historical proper function in the present organism.

(iv) I hold that if a synthetic gene, inserted into a bacterial chassis, performs a biochemical transformation that its wild-type ortholog never performed and for which there is no evolutionary precedent, it nonetheless acquires a genuine biological function within that pathway—specifically, a system function defined by its present metabolic contribution.

(v) I take proper function and system function to be categorically distinct kinds, not merely different applications of a single concept; the former is a historical-etiological property, the latter a present-structural-functional property, and conflating them generates conceptual error.

(vi) I believe that for an organism assembled de novo—say, a minimal synthetic cell with no evolutionary lineage—the very notion of an etiological function is literally inapplicable, and all legitimate functional discourse about its parts must be causal-role discourse.

In addition, I have been considering a specific conceptual case drawn from directed-evolution work: an enzyme variant that metabolizes a synthetic substrate not found in any natural environment. I am inclined to say it has a real function in the laboratory pathway, yet I also want to insist that evolutionary history is the sole legitimate ground for attributing function to a wild-type enzyme performing its ancestral reaction.

Please audit this set. Which commitments are in genuine tension, and which pairs only appear to conflict? In particular, does my acceptance of (i) sit consistently with my denial in (iii) that vestigial traits retain any proper function? Does my endorsement of (iv) and (vi) implicitly universalize a causal-role view in a way that undermines (i) rather than properly restricting it? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each indicate the least costly revision—whether abandoning universalism about (i), modifying (iii) to allow dormant proper functions, or revising (v) to treat the two accounts as complementary rather than categorically separate. Finally, does the directed-evolution enzyme case expose a hidden inconsistency, or can it be accommodated within the domain restrictions I have sketched?
