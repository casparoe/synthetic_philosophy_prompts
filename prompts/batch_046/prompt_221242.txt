In the course of auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mind after retiring from experimental physics, I have been developing a modified version of Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle that tries to isolate whether the difficulty lies in continuity conditions or in the uniqueness of first-person reference. The scenario is this: rather than an instantaneous copy, Dennett's biological brain is replaced neuron by neuron with synthetic substrates that maintain continuous functional coupling with the remaining tissue. At the exact midpoint—when precisely half the system is synthetic—the synthetic portion is physically separated and runs in parallel with the biological portion, both preserving full psychological continuity with the pre-surgical subject and with each other. Both entities sincerely report, in the first person, being Dennett.

What is your actual credence—stated as a specific number between 0 and 1—that at this separation point there exist two genuinely distinct conscious subjects, each with an equally legitimate first-person claim to being the original Dennett, as opposed to one authentic subject and one derivative copy? Decompose the reasoning explicitly: assign a rough probability to (i) the correctness of psychological-continuity theories of personal identity versus brain-based or animalist alternatives; (ii) the permissibility of branching without a privileged best candidate given such a theory; (iii) the coherence of non-uniquely instantiating indexical first-person reference; and (iv) the claim that gradual coupled replacement introduces no hidden discontinuity prior to separation. Show how these terms combine into your final figure, identify which term you are least confident about, and describe what empirical or conceptual evidence—perhaps from split-brain clinical data or formal measures of information integration—would move your number most. I am not asking for citations or a survey of positions; I want the reasoning exposed with its weak points marked.
