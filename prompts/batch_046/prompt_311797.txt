I serve on my university’s Committee on Institutional Memory and Commemorative Practice, and we face a decision that has split the faculty senate and the alumni board. A donor has funded the digitization and permanent open-access publication of the complete disciplinary records from our university’s 1968–1972 student-occupation era—approximately 340 files—including not only the names and sanctions but also the deans’ handwritten affective annotations describing students’ emotional states during interrogations and the precise spatial layouts of the rooms where disciplinary hearings occurred. The donor requires publication within eighteen months and prohibits redaction of names. Meanwhile, a coalition of alumni—now in their seventies—has petitioned for what they call structured mnemonic closure: they do not deny the historical importance of the events, but argue that perpetual digital preservation, especially of emotional metadata, transforms historical documentation into an enduring instrument of exposure that violates a moral right to be forgotten, particularly since the university has formally repudiated the disciplinary framework of that era. Historians and several student groups counter that there is an institutional duty to remember state-adjacent repression, and that selective forgetting would constitute a form of archival paternalism that lets the institution sanitize its own record.

The philosophical stakes here go beyond standard debates about monuments or physical archives. They touch on the tension—associated with thinkers like Margalit, who defends shared memory as an ethical bond sustaining collective responsibility, and Rieff, who warns that modern therapeutic culture risks privatizing suffering to the point of erasing collective moral history—between a duty to preserve the moral texture of institutional wrongs and a case for allowing individuals to escape perpetual identification with past victimization. What makes this case particularly sharp is the digital medium itself: unlike a physical archive that requires physical presence to access, this database will be searchable, linkable, and capable of being mined for patterns that the original paper files never permitted, effectively creating what one committee member has called prosthetic memory—a form of remembrance that outlives and out-scales any natural human capacity to hold the past in proportion. The alumni coalition insists that this prosthetic quality changes the moral calculus: preservation is no longer remembrance but perpetual exposure.

Please draft the committee’s written opinion for circulation before our vote next Thursday. It should recommend a concrete course of action—full publication, structured closure with a defined retention and access protocol, or a conditional release framework—with a specific implementation timeline (e.g., months for expert review, conditions for access tiers, and a sunset clause if applicable). The memo must engage seriously with the alumni coalition’s position rather than dismissing it as anti-historical sentiment, and it must also address the historians’ concern that any limitation on access amounts to institutional self-exculpation. The reasoning should be formal enough that either side could see precisely why they lost, and it should explicitly mark where the decision turns on a value judgment rather than a factual determination—particularly regarding whether emotional metadata should be treated differently from administrative facts, and whether digital searchability creates a morally distinct form of remembrance from analog preservation.
