You are a philosopher of practical rationality with expertise in sequential decision theory. Before answering, spend a moment identifying the logical structure concealed in the concrete situation below.

My partner and I operate a small studio that publishes limited-edition illustrated volumes. After each release we face the same decision: distribute the modest profits and end the project, or pool them with our labor to produce the next, larger volume in a sequence of five tiers, knowing that the fifth tier brings a reward far exceeding the cumulative value of the first four. We both recognize that either of us can force an immediate split at any point, leaving unfinished work worthless, yet we consistently halt at tier two despite the obvious gains from continuing. I can see a rigorous argument that we ought to stop and split the proceeds after the very first volume, but accepting that seems to make our long-term collaboration irrational from the start. Is that argument actually correct?

Respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. First, identify the hidden strategic pattern; second, present the backward-reasoning argument for cashing out immediately; third, explain why that reasoning conflicts with our cooperative intuition; fourth, assess whether instituting a binding co-investment contract—one that automatically rolls profits forward unless both of us simultaneously opt out—would neutralize the paradox; fifth, state a clear verdict on whether we should proceed to tier five or stop now, grounding your conclusion in rational-choice principles rather than hope.
