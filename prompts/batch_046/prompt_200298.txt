Below are outline notes for an essay on the paradox of fiction. I take the familiar puzzle—that we feel genuine grief, fear, or pity toward characters and events we know to be unreal, yet we do not behave as if those beliefs were true—to be inadequately resolved by both “quasi-emotion” and “make-believe” accounts, not because they are wrong in detail, but because they mislocate the problem in the ontology of the feeling rather than in the structure of its intentional object.

My proposed framing introduces what I call sandbox-targeted affect. Rather than treating fictional engagement as a weakened or pretended form of belief-directed emotion, I suggest that the emotional system treats represented possibilities as modal simulations: scenarios tagged with an internal “within-the-fiction” index that blocks practical action-tendencies without suppressing phenomenological intensity. On this view, crying for Anna Karenina is not a “fake” grief that needs to be explained away; it is real affect genuinely directed at a possibility evaluated in isolation from our practical belief-set about the actual world. The paradox dissolves not because the emotion is counterfeit, but because its object is correctly indexed: we do not attempt to rescue Anna because the affect is tethered to a modal content, not to an actuality we take ourselves able to alter.

I apply this to two test cases. First, why learning that a tragic ending was “only fiction” does not retroactively erase the grief experienced during reading: since the grief was directed at the modal content under its fiction-index, discovering that the index refers to no actual state of affairs does not contradict or cancel the prior emotional evaluation; it merely confirms the boundary conditions under which the evaluation operated. Second, I address an asymmetry objection: if sandbox-targeted affect is genuinely emotional, why do we not experience comparable sustained grief for vividly imagined hypothetical disasters in our own lives? I propose that narrative framing supplies coherence conditions—causal closure, emotional arc, stable character identity—that stabilize the modal object, whereas private hypotheticals lack this scaffolding, causing the affect to destabilize and dissipate quickly.

Please comment on these notes. Specifically: Is the distinction between belief-targeted and sandbox-targeted affect philosophically coherent, or does it merely rename the standard tension between cognitive and conative responses? Does the appeal to narrative scaffolding genuinely explain the asymmetry with private hypotheticals, or does it risk circularity by defining “fiction” as “that which supplies scaffolding”? And are there obvious objections—from theories of emotional intentionality, from accounts of action-blocking, or from phenomenological descriptions of absorption—that I should anticipate and answer?
