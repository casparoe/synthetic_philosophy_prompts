I serve on my university's Joint Committee on Research Partnerships and Cultural Stewardship. We must decide on a proposal from our ecology department and an external agricultural cooperative: the cooperative seeks to license detailed plant-cultivation protocols documented in our ethnobotanical archive—collected under agreements that prohibit "commercial exploitation"—to develop a drought-resistant crop variety. In exchange, the cooperative offers full funding for open-access digital preservation of the archive, permanent community veto over patents derived from the data, and direct revenue-sharing with the source communities. The compromise requires reinterpreting "commercial exploitation" narrowly: the cultural narratives and ritual contexts remain fully protected and non-commercial, while the isolated biochemical and agronomic parameters are licensed for adaptive agriculture. Some committee members argue this is a legitimate gains-from-trade arrangement—communities gain institutional security and influence they lack now, while researchers gain resources—while others hold that allowing any profit-linked use of archived knowledge violates the integrity of the original agreement regardless of the safeguards. Draft the committee's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressing specifically whether restructuring the boundary between "cultural knowledge" and "derived agronomic data" constitutes a defensible moral compromise or an impermissible conceptual gerrymandering that undermines both value systems.
