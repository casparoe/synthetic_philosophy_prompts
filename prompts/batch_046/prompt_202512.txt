I've been reading about illusionism -- the idea that phenomenal consciousness isn't real, just a kind of mistake our brains make. There's a specific argumentative pattern I keep running into and I'm not sure if it has a proper name.

First case: someone says seeing red is just the brain's "user interface" -- there's no real redness, just a useful fiction. You ask, okay, but if it's wrong shouldn't I be able to peek behind the curtain? They say no, that's the clever part: the interface is designed to hide that there's nothing behind it, and your attempt to peek is just more interface running.

Second case: illusionists cite people's reports -- "it feels like something to be me" -- as the data that needs explaining. But then they say introspection is part of the illusion too, so those reports can't be trusted. So the reports are both the problem and part of the mechanisim that hides the problem.

The pattern is an illusion defined partly by being self-sealing: any attempt to check it gets absorbed as part of it. Is there an established concept for this exact structure? Not illusionism broadly, but specifcally the self-sealing feature where resistance to being seen-through counts as a defining trait? How's it different from plain unfalsifiability? And if there's no name, can you coin one with clear edges -- what's in, what's out? Keep it to one paragraph, max ~120 words, bottom line first.
