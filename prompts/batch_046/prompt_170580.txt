I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy of science or quantitative genetics, but I have been trying to understand what heritability actually means conceptually—not merely the formula, but what it implies about causation and the possibility of intervention. In software architecture we sometimes distinguish an interface contract fixed at compile time from a runtime environment that supplies configurable parameters; this led me to interpret heritability estimates (for example, reports that cognitive traits show heritability around 0.6 to 0.8 in twin studies) as a kind of variance budget. I picture the genome as specifying a “base implementation” that accounts for roughly 70% of phenotypic variance, while the environment contributes only the remaining 30% of runtime state. From this framing, it seems contradictory when geneticists insist that high heritability does not entail that a trait is resistant to environmental change. If the genetic architecture fixes 70% of the variance, would not any intervention be constrained to operating within the residual 30% parameter space?

I want to locate the precise conceptual error in this analogy. Is the mistake primarily statistical—confusing broad-sense heritability (H²), which includes dominance and epistatic effects, with narrow-sense heritability (h²), which isolates additive genetic variance and governs response to selection? Or is there a more fundamental philosophical mistake in treating population-level variance components as if they partition causal influence within a single individual, thereby suppressing genotype-environment interaction and covariance? Specifically, does my “base class versus runtime configuration” model implicitly deny reaction norms—the fact that the same genotype can yield different phenotypes across environments—by assuming that genetic and environmental contributions are modular and non-interacting?

Could you clarify where the architectural analogy collapses, particularly with respect to why heritability is environment-specific and why a trait with high heritability in one population might be easily modified under a different environmental regime? I would prefer not to be told only that correlation is not causation; I am looking for the deeper conceptual clarification about variance partitioning, interaction effects, and the limits of inferring individual-level malleability from population-level statistics.
