Before I evaluate competing reconstructions of Zhuangzi's philosophy of death, I want the adequacy conditions set out explicitly. Write the desiderata an interpretation must meet: which specific responses to death in the text it has to accommodate (the drumming and singing at his wife's death, the butterfly dream's erosion of the self/other and living/dead boundaries, the seasonal-transformation analogy), which distinctions it must not collapse (between accepting natural transformation and being emotionally indifferent to the dead, between epistemic humility about what death is like and metaphysical certainty that it is annihilation), which broader commitments from the Zhuangzi it must remain consistent with (the practice of fasting the mind, the critique of the completed heart, the ideal of wandering freely), and which comparative results it must not violate without argument (for instance, that Daoist transformation is not interchangeable with Stoic apatheia or Epicurean extinction of consciousness). Give the motivating passage or conceptual reason behind each. Then flag any pair you take to be jointly unsatisfiable -- in particular, whether accommodating both his response to Hui Shi's death and his response to his wife's death demands an unstable doctrine rather than a coherent view. Briefly score only three readings: the natural cyclical transformation interpretation, the radical dissolution of personal identity interpretation, and a phenomenological equivalence of life and death interpretation -- but keep the weight on the criteria. Finally: if this task rests on a false premise -- for example, if Zhuangzi does not present a unitary theory of death but rather a set of performative provocations, or if demanding cross-textual consistency is anachronistic -- say so plainly rather than constructing criteria to fit.
