I serve on our university’s Committee on Institutional Memory and Public History, and we must recommend a single course of action by next month’s board meeting regarding a $4 million donor proposal that has forced us to confront, in practical terms, how an institution should live with its own past. The donor offers to fund a permanent, unalterable digital archive and exhibition space—the “Founders Monument Project”—explicitly designed to present heroic, emulative narratives of the university’s origins so that present students and faculty feel themselves part of an enduring tradition worthy of imitation. In direct response, our faculty historians and student government have jointly proposed an alternative “Critical History Laboratory,” which would apply the same resources to a continuously revised, deconstructive exhibit treating every foundational narrative as provisional and subject to dismantling in light of present justice concerns. Meanwhile, our university archivists have submitted a separate brief arguing that neither approach serves institutional life: what is genuinely needed, they claim, is a purely antiquarian repository—complete, uncurated, and without narrative framing—that preserves every document regardless of whether it inspires, critiques, or merely accumulates.

The memo I will circulate must recommend a concrete institutional design—specifying governance structure, access rules, curatorial authority, and a time-bound review mechanism—rather than merely declaring a winner. It should treat the donor’s desire for monumental continuity and the students’ demand for critical dissolution as the two strongest competing considerations, giving each an honest accounting of what it risks losing if the other prevails, including the subtle but serious worry that institutionalized critical history tends eventually to congeal into its own orthodoxy, while pure monumentality becomes dead weight that no living community can carry. The reasoning should draw on the practical taxonomy of monumental, antiquarian, and critical relations to the past not as an academic ornament but as a framework for deciding what kind of life this institution is attempting to sustain, and it should be written so that board members with no background in the philosophy of history can apply the recommendation directly—without asking for bibliographies, external citations, or further scholarly consultation.
