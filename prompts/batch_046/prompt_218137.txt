I am writing an essay for my philosophy elective about whether values that belong to entirely different domains can ever be genuinely compared, and I have encountered a conceptual distinction that my course materials do not address clearly. Philosophers sometimes distinguish between values being incommensurable—lacking any common unit of measurement—and being incomparable—such that neither value is better, worse, or exactly equal to the other. I want to understand whether introducing a structural feature of values creates a stronger or distinct form of incommensurability.

Specifically, consider a rational agent who must distribute limited resources between commitments that have what I will call threshold-structured value and commitments that have continuous-structured value. A threshold-structured commitment is one where falling below a specific level eliminates the value entirely—such as maintaining a linguistic tradition or honoring a solemn promise—whereas a continuous-structured commitment improves proportionally with every increment, like reducing pollution or expanding literacy access. The conceptual issue is this: when both structures appear in the same choice situation, does the presence of a threshold on one side block any rational comparison with the continuous side, or does it merely establish a lexical priority that can still be incorporated into an overall ranking?

Please explain whether this structural distinction genuinely illuminates the incommensurability problem, or whether it collapses into a weighted cost-benefit analysis once priorities are assigned. In particular, I would like to know whether a defender of value pluralism would argue that the difference in internal structure—not merely the content of the values—prevents aggregation across domains, and whether that position requires accepting that rational choice can be fully valid within each value domain yet globally indeterminate. If the structural distinction does real philosophical work, which feature of the comparison is responsible, and what happens to the argument if we treat the threshold itself as just another quantitative parameter?
