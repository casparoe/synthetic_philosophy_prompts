Following a recent episode of severe vertigo in an anechoic chamber—where, deprived of visual and auditory reference points, my own rotation felt irreducibly real despite the absence of any external frame—I have become convinced that the metaphysics of space is not a settled museum piece but a live conceptual problem. I am preparing the final examination for my upper-level metaphysics and philosophy of physics seminar, and I need a single 90-minute essay question on Newton's bucket argument and relationist responses that students cannot answer by reciting a standard summary. I want the question to inject a specific, under-discussed variant: the hollow-cylinder universe, in which a single rigid hollow cylinder rotates about its axis in an otherwise entirely empty cosmos. Students must determine whether a relationist can explain the dynamical bulging of the cylinder's walls by appealing solely to the internal distance-relations among the cylinder's own constituent parts, or whether that explanation either presupposes an absolute metric structure that makes those internal relations determinate, or collapses into a Machian appeal to distant masses that are ex hypothesi absent. The question should force precise separation of three analytical targets: Newton's original inference from concavity to motion relative to absolute space rather than relative to water or bucket; Mach's relational reply and its dependence on the distribution of distant matter; and the prospects for a structural relationalism that treats the metric field as relational yet ontologically robust, specifically in this minimal case where no distant bodies exist.

Please provide the following:

The essay question itself, phrased so that students must take and defend a position, not merely summarize.

A model answer of approximately 700 words, organized around three required moves: (1) reconstructing Newton's argument with explicit attention to why concavity indicates absolute rotation rather than relative motion between water and container; (2) assessing Mach's distant-mass response and demonstrating precisely why it is unavailable or inadequate in the hollow-cylinder scenario; (3) evaluating whether an internal-relational explanation—grounding rotational dynamics solely in the geometry of relations among the cylinder's own parts—avoids substantivalism or secretly reintroduces absolute structure through the determinacy conditions of the metric. The answer should identify the circularity risk in using internal geometry to ground rotation without prior determination of which parts are at rest, and should conclude with a defensible judgment about whether structural relationalism survives in the minimal case.

A marking scheme totaling 25 marks, with explicit allocations for each of the three moves above (with higher weight on move 3), plus marks for argumentative clarity and for correctly identifying the specific weakness in the relationalist reply. For each allocated point, include a one-line note on the characteristic B-level mistake—such as accurately describing Mach's principle but failing to apply it to the minimal universe, or describing internal relations without addressing the circularity objection. The scheme should clearly distinguish an A answer—which must treat the circularity problem explicitly and take a reasoned stand on structural relationalism—from a B answer that summarizes Newton and Mach correctly but treats the hollow-cylinder twist as peripheral.

The prompt should not request bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or specific page references, and should depend only on knowledge available without online search. Do not include verbatim quotations from Newton, Mach, or other historical sources unless you have fetched and verified the exact text; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks.
