Two researchers in a working group on longtermist ethics debated whether insect welfare should dominate resource allocation. Here is their exchange, reported by a colleague who did not take sides.

(1) Elena argued that the scale argument is decisive: with roughly 10^19 insects alive at any moment, even a 0.01 probability of sentience multiplied across that population yields expected moral weight that dwarfs farmed vertebrates.

(2) Marcus replied that this commits a "disjunctive explosion": if low probabilities multiplied by vast numbers are sufficient, then soil microbiomes, synthetic processes, or even quantum events might also qualify, and without a principled boundary the argument proves too much.

(3) Elena said the boundary is not arbitrary; it is anchored empirically. Insects possess nociceptors, centralized neural architectures, and evolutionary continuity with sentient lineages, giving a non-arbitrary reference class within which the scale multiplication applies.

(4) Marcus conceded the filter but said it transforms the argument's logic. Originally it was "vast N compensates for low P"; now it is "biological similarity raises P within a reference class, and vast N amplifies it." That is an analogical-scale hybrid, not a pure scale argument, and the analogical component is weak across deep phylogenetic distance.

(5) Elena responded that there is no contradiction: the biological filter sets the domain, and within it scale determines priority. Farm animals also pass the filter, but there are only about 10^10 of them versus 10^19 insects, so scale still breaks the tie.

(6) Marcus countered that this assumes welfare is linear and additive and that insect phenomenology has comparable intensity. If there is a steep moral gradient that discounts alien phenomenology, or if welfare is non-linear across individuals, then multiplying headcount by a tiny intensity factor could collapse the expectation, leaving the analogical premise as the only load-bearing element.
