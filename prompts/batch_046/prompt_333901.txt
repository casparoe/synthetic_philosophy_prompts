I hold that a value-independent low-impact criterion for AI can be constructed by measuring only structural perturbation: compare the causal graph of world-states with and without the agent present, restrict divergence metrics to non-normative physical variables such as energy distributions, spatial configurations, and connectivity patterns, and require that the agent's presence not alter the graph's topology beyond a fixed threshold outside a precisely delimited task corridor. The claim is that this yields an objective "side-effect" boundary without requiring a full specification of human values. What would actually count against this position? I want the objections that should move a committed defender, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: formal results showing that any such divergence measure either collapses into value-ladenness or is too permissive to constrain behavior; cases where structurally minimal perturbations cause serious harm that the metric misses, or where structurally large perturbations are benign; and intuitions about which environmental changes we genuinely care about that fail to map onto topological differences in the graph. Which of these defeaters would a structural-footprint theorist concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss by sharpening the formalism or narrowing the corridor? I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability—say, by allowing post-hoc adjustments of the task-corridor boundary—and whether that insulation is a vice for a criterion meant to guide real system design.
