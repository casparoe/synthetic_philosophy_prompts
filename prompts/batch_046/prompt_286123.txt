In my grad seminar on social ontology we've been stuck for weeks on whether Lewis's account of convention actually explains how norms evolve, or whether evolutionary game theory just describes something totally different. Everyone accepts Lewis's basic setup - arbitrary solutions to coordination problems sustained by common knowledge and mutual expectations. And everyone accepts that evolutionary models can stabilize behavioral regularities without anyone reasoning about coordination. The stall comes when we ask: if a population converges on a punishment norm through replicator dynamics, with no agent ever representing 'we're coordinating on this,' is that a Lewis convention? One camp says yes - the functional role is identical, common knowledge is just distributed in the population dynamics rather than in individual heads. The other camp says no, you've lost the normative structure: for Lewis, I follow the convention partly because I expect you to, and I know you expect me to, but in the evolutionary story I'm just running a copied strategy with no such recursive expectation. What's wierd is that both sides agree on all the empirical predictions and agree that punishment norms are real. So is this just a verbal dispute about the word 'convention,' or is there a substantive disagreement about whether evolutionary dynamics can generate genuine normativity? And if it's substantive, what kind of evidence or conceptual distinction would actually move it - do we need a formal result showing evolutionary equilibria can't support counterfactual reasoning about deviation, or is the issue that we're asking evolutionary biology to do philosophical work it was never meant to do?
