Explain what this suspension consists in, distinguishing Abraham's position from both the tragic hero (Agamemnon) and the knight of infinite resignation. Then evaluate whether de Silentio's account leaves room for any ethical critique of Abraham's silence—perhaps along the lines of what recent commentators call an ethical remainder, where suspended norms leave a trace rather than being fully annulled—or whether the suspension is total.

student answer (verbatim, including spelling):

The ethical is the universal domain of Hegelian Sittlichkeit, where the individual is always mediated through social norms and can justify her actions to others. Abraham suspends this universal for a higher telos—his direct, singular relation to God as the absolute. Unlike Agamemnon, who sacrifices Iphigenia for the welfare of the state and can therefore speak, explain, and be understood within the universal, Abraham has no mediating term; he acts in silence because the universal literally cannot comprehend his obligation. The knight of infinite resignation is different: he gives up Isaac for the universal, accepting the loss with infinite sorrow, but he remains within the ethical. Abraham goes further and makes the movement of faith, expecting to recieve Isaac back by virtue of the absurd.

That said, I think de Silentio is endorsing a kind of moral particularism here: since the ethical is suspended for the absolute relation, any individual could in principle claim a direct divine command that overrides universal duty, which collapses the distinction between faith and fanaticism. Still, de Silentio allows for a partial ethical critique via the idea of an ethical remainder—Abraham's silence violates the intersubjective conditions that even religious existence presupposes, because Sarah and Isaac are still ethical subjects with claims on him. So the suspension is real but not total; it operates inside a residual ethical frame that keeps Abraham accountable.
