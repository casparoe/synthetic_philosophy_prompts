I'm trying to figure out if there's one principle behind my gut reactions to these pain cases, or if I'm just being inconsistent. Here are my verdicts.

One, normal acute pain — stubbing your toe — feels awful and makes you pull back. I think the badness is right in the experience; you can't really seperate the hurt from the 'this is bad' part.

Two, pain asymbolia, usually from parietal damage. Patients say 'yes that's painful' but they don't care, don't withdraw, even laugh. For them I want to say it's not actually bad — the sensory signal is there but the badness is missing.

Three, congenital insensitivity to pain. They never feel anything. I think they're missing something genuinely bad — not that they suffer now, but they've lost a protective mechanism that involves badness. So the absence of pain means the absence of a specific kind of bad experience.

Four, someone on heavy opioids during surgery. They feel the cutting and pressure but it's 'distant,' like it's happening to someone else. They don't suffer. Here I feel the pain is technically happening but it's not bad for them right then. Similar to asymbolia but chemically induced.

Five, a weird hypothetical — pure affective phantom pain. The patient has no localized burning or stabbing, just overwhelming dread and wrongness in a limb, but denies any sensory quality. They suffer a lot. I think this IS bad, and maybe even counts as pain, but I'm unsure.

Six, a trained practitioner who feels full sensory intensity and unpleasantness, but has cultivated acceptance. They say 'it hurts badly, but I'm okay with it hurting.' For them I think it IS bad — the badness is real — they're just not fighting it.

So my pattern seems to be: badness tracks the affective-motivational part, not the sensory part. But cases three and six are hard to square. In three there's no affective part ever, and I say they're missing a bad thing. In six there's full affective unpleasantness, and I say it's bad, but they don't avoid it. Meanwhile in case two there's sensory but no affect, and I say it's not bad. So maybe the principle is that pain is bad iff it carries a negative affect that normally motivates avoidance?

But wait — in case five there's affect and suffering without sensory, and I want to call it bad and maybe pain. In case four there's sensory but suppressed affect, and I say not bad. That fits. But case three: congenital insensitivity means they never have the affective part either. So they're missing the capacity for that specific badness. That still fits.

The real tension is between case two and case six, I think. In both, the person doesn't withdraw or resist. In two I say 'not bad' because the affect is gone. In six I say 'it is bad' despite not resisting, because the affect is present. So the principle might hold: badness equals presence of negative affect with motivational pull, regardless of actual behavior.

But there's another tension: case five has affect without sensory. If I say that's pain and bad, then pain isn't defined by sensory quality. But in case two there IS sensory quality but I deny it's really bad pain — or do I deny it's pain? I said 'the sensory signal is there but the badness is missing.' I didn't say it's not pain, just that it's not bad. So maybe I think pain can exist without being bad (case two), but badness in the pain family requires affect.

Actually, here's the hard pair: in case three I say missing pain is missing something bad. But if pain can exist without being bad (case two), then why is missing all pain a loss of badness specifically? Maybe it's just loss of sensory information. So there's a conflict: is pain necessarily bad, or not? Case two suggests not necessarily. Case three suggests yes, it's a specific kind of badness that's missing.

Also, case four vs case two: both have suppressed affect, but one is neural damage, one is chemical. I treat them the same, but maybe I shouldn't?

Anyway, I want to know: is there a coherent principle? Probably something like 'pain is bad in virtue of its intrinsic affective character, but not all instances of the sensory state count as full pain if that character is absent, except that the capacity for it is itself valuable because it protects'? That sounds messy.

Please tell me what principle best captures this, state it precisely, say what it predicts for cases I haven't mentioned — like someone with asymbolia who is intellectually terrified of their injury despite feeling no hurt, or a masochist who seeks out intense sensory pain with full affect present. And be honest about which of my verdicts have to be dropped to keep the principle simple. I don't want a laundry list of theories, just the best one or two candidates.
