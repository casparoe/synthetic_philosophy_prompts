As a novelist preparing a work that treats collective cognition with philosophical seriousness rather than mere allegory, I have been attempting to clarify my own commitments regarding whether an ant colony—such as the one Hofstadter imagined in his ant fugue, sometimes called Aunt Hillary—might genuinely possess a minimal form of mind. Before I proceed, I should like an audit of whether these commitments hang together.

Here is where I have landed:

First, I believe that an ant colony can, in principle, instantiate a non-metaphorical mind: not merely that we speak of it as thinking, but that there is something it is like to be that colony, however alien and dim.

Second, I reject panpsychism in any form that attributes consciousness to individual ants, or to neurons taken in isolation. Consciousness, on my view, is genuinely emergent and requires a specific scale of functional integration.

Third, I hold that consciousness requires some form of temporal integration—what I would call narrative continuity—without which there is no unified subject of experience.

Fourth, I am a functionalist: the substrate is irrelevant provided the right patterns of information flow, feedback, and predictive processing are present.

Fifth, I think that if collective minds exist, they possess what I term a fugue-structure: continuous and responsive, yet lacking autobiographical unity or reflective self-awareness. They are minds, but not selves.

Sixth, I deny that consciousness automatically entails moral status. A collective mind might exist without the capacity for suffering or for having interests that can be harmed, and therefore without generating direct moral obligations.

Seventh, despite my functionalism, I find it deeply implausible that a computer network running analogous algorithms could be conscious in the same sense. I cannot shake the intuition that biological organisation, or at least some analogue of living metabolism, plays an essential role.

Please examine these for tension. Which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which only appear to conflict, and how severe is each tension? I should particularly like to know whether my functionalism in the fourth commitment undermines my resistance in the seventh, and whether my demand for narrative continuity in the third is compatible with the fugue-structure I describe in the fifth. If a principle must be sacrificed, which is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity, and present your answer in British English.
