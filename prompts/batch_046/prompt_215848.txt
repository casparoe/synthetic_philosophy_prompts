In the long-running exchange over Bernard Williams’s integrity objection and the “one thought too many” case, the literature has reached an impasse that looks like repetition with refinement rather than resolution. On one side stand defenders of impartial moral theories—mostly consequentialists, occasionally Kantians—who reply that Williams confuses the level of justification with the level of deliberation: an agent need not run an explicit calculus to save a friend, and having an immediate, direct emotional response is fully compatible with that response being justified, at the reflective level, by an impartial principle. They add that “integrity” is better understood as a virtue governing how one integrates commitments, not as a side-constraint that blocks trade-offs. On the other side stand Williams’s sympathizers, who insist the objection was never about deliberative speed or psychological transparency but about the structure of practical identity: if a ground project is partly constitutive of who one is—being this particular friend, pursuing this particular vocation—then a moral theory that treats that commitment as fungible with any other commitment of equal weight has already misrepresented what it is to have it. Both sides have circulated refinements since the 1980s—distinctions between agent-relative reasons and agent-relative permissions, appeals to “separability,” moves that recast integrity as a feature of character rather than a constraint on action—without either camp conceding the core point.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is driven by an unacknowledged disagreement about what I will call indexical binding: whether a ground project binds an agent in virtue of its particular, non-substitutable content (this friendship, that artistic practice) or in virtue of its functional role (being-a-friend, being-an-artist, where role-fillers are interchangeable if they produce equivalent value). The defenders of impartial theories tend to treat commitments as role-fillers that can be justified impersonally; Williams’s side treats them as essentially particular, so that replacing one with an equally valuable substitute does not preserve integrity but dissolves it. If this framing is correct, part of the disagreement may be verbal—both camps use “integrity” and “ground project” to name different structures—while another part is substantive: they genuinely disagree about whether practical identity is constituted by particular attachments or by the holding of general roles.
