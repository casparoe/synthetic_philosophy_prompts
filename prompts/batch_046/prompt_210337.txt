In our philosophy-of-science study group, two members — call them Maya and Leo — invented a hypothetical peer-review case to test judgment aggregation rules and then found themselves unable to agree on what the case shows. They imagine a three-person panel evaluating a controversial argument in metaphysics. Each panelist independently judges three propositions: (P) the argument’s first premise is conceptually coherent; (Q) its second premise avoids a fallacy of equivocation; and (R) the argument is therefore sound, where R is taken to be jointly entailed by P and Q. After voting, a discursive dilemma emerges: a majority affirms P, a majority affirms Q, but a majority rejects R and declares the argument unsound overall.

Maya argues that collective rationality requires premise-based aggregation: the panel should combine the majority judgments on P and Q and derive R, even if this contradicts the direct majority vote on R. She claims that allowing a group to endorse premises while denying their logical consequence is a failure of coherence that no aggregation rule should permit, and that any departure from this opens the door to arbitrary outcome manipulation.

Leo responds that this treats all judgments as equally authoritative, ignoring epistemic division of labor. He suggests the panelists might be domain experts on overall argument soundness — hence reliable on R — but relatively untrained in detecting specific conceptual errors — hence unreliable on P and Q. So conclusion-based aggregation, which respects the direct vote on R, actually tracks expertise better. Maya counters that Leo’s appeal to expertise is ad hoc: if we can always discount premise-level judgments whenever they conflict with a preferred conclusion, the discursive dilemma dissolves into mere skepticism about logical reasoning, and aggregation rules lose all normative force.

Leo then offers a refinement meant to split the difference: suppose each panelist assigns explicit confidence weights to their judgments — averaging, say, 0.9 confidence in R but only 0.55 confidence in P and Q. Leo proposes a weighted premise-based rule that aggregates judgments only when confidence exceeds a threshold, arguing that this preserves logical coherence without forcing the group to accept premises it barely trusts. Maya objects that this merely relocates the arbitrariness: there is no non-circular way to set the confidence threshold, and selecting it so that the premises fall below it while R remains high is just conclusion-based aggregation disguised by formal machinery. Leo replies that collective rationality should maximize expected epistemic accuracy rather than enforce logical form unconditionally, so if weighting improves accuracy, coherence should bend to it.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate Maya’s coherence objection, Leo’s expertise defense, Maya’s ad-hocness charge, Leo’s confidence-weighting proposal, Maya’s relocation objection, and Leo’s accuracy-over-coherence reply — indicating for each whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or somewhere in between, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing — for instance, whether there is a principled distinction between premise-level and conclusion-level competence, or whether the dilemma reveals something about the limits of majority rule itself rather than just about aggregation mechanics.
