I am a retired natural scientist who has recently begun studying philosophy in earnest. I should like a poem — specifically a sestina — that treats the paradox of tolerance not as a political slogan but as an epistemic difficulty: the norm that a scientific community ought to tolerate dissenting hypotheses, yet if that tolerance is extended without limit to demonstrably destructive or fraudulent claims, the conditions for rational enquiry collapse. Use six rotating end-words — limit, extend, dissent, suppress, enquiry, collapse — arranged in the standard sestina pattern (six stanzas of six lines, followed by an envoi of three lines, each containing two of the words). Let the rotation do structural work: across the stanzas, extend should appear progressively nearer to suppress and collapse, so that the form itself traces how tolerance moves towards its own negation. The envoi should form one continuous sentence using all six words, making the circular logic visible. Write in blank verse with occasional slant rhyme, in a tone suitable for closing a philosophy-of-science seminar. After the poem, provide a brief note separating what is settled — that unlimited tolerance is logically self-undermining in principle — from what is genuinely contested, including whether institutions have a duty to suppress intolerant speech and whether Karl Popper’s formulation conflates personal virtue with procedural rules. Flag any liberties taken with Popper’s original argument, especially regarding the conditions he specified for suppression, and do not present contested normative conclusions as though they were consensus.
