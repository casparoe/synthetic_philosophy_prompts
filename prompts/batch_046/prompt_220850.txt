I sit on my university's predictive-intervention oversight board. Our student-success office runs a model that sorts incoming students into reference classes—demographic and academic profiles—to predict mental-health crisis risk and trigger mandatory wellness check-ins. The designers defend this as standard statistical reasoning: a student is an instance of a class, and the class frequency determines the appropriate intervention threshold. A student has appealed her assignment to a high-risk reference class; she accepts the data inputs but argues that a recent, unrecorded change in her family finances and study conditions places her in a different class with a far lower base rate, and that being compelled into counseling on the basis of a class she no longer belongs to violates both fairness and predictive accuracy. Half the board wants to reject the appeal—redefining reference classes case by case collapses the statistical rationale—while the other half wants to allow an individualized reclassification pathway. Please draft the board's decision memo recommending a concrete policy (approve the current model without appeals, approve with a structured reclassification pathway, or suspend the program) with a clear time frame for implementation. The memo should take the philosophical reference-class problem seriously: does an institution have the authority to treat an individual as a token of a statistical type she disputes, and can the model remain valid if reference classes become negotiable? Give the strongest argument for the position you reject.
