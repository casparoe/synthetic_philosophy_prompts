As a high school student working on an independent study in decision theory, I keep returning to the Allais paradox: the familiar pair of lotteries in which most people prefer certainty in one framing and risk in another, violating the independence axiom. What puzzles me is not the experimental pattern itself — it has been replicated for decades — but why the philosophical and economic debate has barely moved. Since the 1950s we have seen rank-dependent utility, regret theory, ambiguity-aversion models, and various non-expected-utility frameworks, yet the core dispute remains intact: are people irrational, is the axiom too demanding, or is expected utility simply the wrong language for real choice? My working theory is that the stalemate is not empirical or mathematical at all, but structural. One camp seems to treat decision theory as a normative discipline that sets consistency conditions for idealized agents with state-independent utility, while the other treats it as a descriptive project that must accommodate how actual agents evaluate outcomes under uncertainty — two different questions that happen to share a vocabulary. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a hidden substantive disagreement underneath — perhaps about whether independence is defensible only for small-stakes choices, or about whether revised formalisms can preserve normative force without collapsing into psychology? I want an honest diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, not a survey of models: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of answering different questions under one name? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction that separates normative from descriptive evaluation, a formal result reconciling the Allais pattern with a revised axiom system, or should we conclude that the impasse is permanent because the two sides share notation but not a subject matter?
