I was formalizing what “deference to objective chance” actually requires beyond Lewis’s Principal Principle, and I built an argument that ends up making direct observational memory impossible whenever you know a chance law. I think there is a scope slip or an equivocation between forward- and backward-looking chances, but I cannot name the exact step. Here is the chain:

(1) The Principal Principle (PP): if you know the objective chance of E is x and your other evidence A is admissible, your rational credence in E should be x.
(2) Evidence is admissible relative to a specific chance law L only if it is probabilistically independent of E conditional on L.
(3) Now suppose you do not know L; you have a prior over competing chance hypotheses (fair coin vs. biased coin). Your total evidence includes observed frequencies.
(4) By (2), those frequencies are inadmissible relative to any particular L (since under L, specific outcomes are dependent on the trial), but they are exactly what Bayesians use to update beliefs over L.
(5) Call this the Hierarchical Deference Requirement (HDR): apply PP within each L, but update over L using Bayes’ rule on the same frequencies.
(6) HDR works for predictions: your credence in the next outcome should be the expectation over L of ch(E|L).
(7) But HDR also requires that, within each L, your credence about past observed outcomes remains fixed at their objective chances, regardless of having witnessed them.
(8) So if L assigns ch(heads)=0.5, then even after seeing ten heads in a row, your credence that the first flip was heads—conditional on L being fair—must remain 0.5.
(9) Of course you know it was heads, because you observed it. So your unconditional credence should be high, driven by your updated belief that L is not fair.
(10) Now consider the limiting case where you are certain of L (you manufactured the coin and verified its fairness). By (1) and (7), your credence about each past flip, conditional on L, is still 0.5, and since you are certain of L, your unconditional credence is 0.5.
(11) Thus, if you are ever certain of a chance law, direct observation of outcomes under that law becomes epistemically null: you must assign credence 0.5 to “the first flip was heads” even after watching it.
(12) This proves too much—observational memory should survive knowledge of the law—so one step must fail.
(13) I want to keep (1) and (5). Where is the mistake? Is “admissible” misapplied in (7) because past outcomes fall outside PP’s domain? Is there an equivocation between forward-looking and backward-looking chances? Or does (9) show that certainty in L is impossible once you have observed outcomes, rendering (10) an empty case?

Please separate what is settled in the literature on PP and admissibility (e.g., Lewis’s original formulation, the standard problem cases involving outcome-evidence) from what is genuinely contested or framing-dependent (e.g., whether a hierarchical extension like HDR is coherent, whether PP applies to past events, whether observational certainty can coexist with law-certainty). Do not present a contested interpretation—such as that PP governs only future events—as the consensus view.
