Design an upper-level philosophy-of-art seminar exam prompt (90 minutes, approximately 1,500 words expected) that requires students to compare Tolstoy’s infection theory of art—understood as the view that art succeeds when an artist transmits a sincerely felt emotion to an audience with such clarity that the audience is genuinely affected—with a counter-model you should label as the vertical-transmission thesis: the claim that art’s primary value lies not in immediate emotional infection but in preserving and transmitting complex emotional-cognitive structures across generations, often at the cost of immediate accessibility to contemporary audiences. The exam question must direct students to identify a specific internal tension: Tolstoy insists that good art must be immediately accessible (the infection must be direct and universal), yet he simultaneously praises traditional religious and folk art forms whose authority derives precisely from being embedded in long traditions that modern, secular audiences no longer share. Students should be asked to adjudicate whether Tolstoy can consistently maintain accessibility as a necessary condition for good art without excluding the traditional works he praises, and they should defend one of three positions: (a) Tolstoy should abandon the accessibility condition, (b) he should deny that the traditional works are genuinely good art by his own lights, or (c) the infection model can be modified to accommodate vertical transmission without losing its core. Provide a model answer of about 600 words that (i) reconstructs the infection theory in your own terms, (ii) articulates the vertical-transmission challenge, (iii) locates the tension in Tolstoy’s evaluation of religious art, and (iv) offers a reasoned judgment with one specific modification proposed if option (c) is chosen. Then provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points—reconstruction of infection theory (5 marks), articulation of vertical-transmission challenge (5 marks), identification of the internal tension (5 marks), evaluation of the three positions (7 marks), and coherence/defense of the chosen resolution (3 marks)—with a one-line note for each on the typical mistake that costs marks (e.g., conflating vertical transmission with mere historical influence, or summarizing Tolstoy’s biography instead of analyzing the conceptual conflict). The prompt should be answerable without recourse to specific page citations or bibliographic references.
