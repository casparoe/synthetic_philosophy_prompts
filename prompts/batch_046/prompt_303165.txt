I am drafting a philosophical novel in which a moral philosopher, Silas Morne, applies Adam Smith’s framework of sympathy and the impartial spectator to a sequence of boundary cases, and I need his judgments to remain internally consistent across the manuscript. His rulings thus far are these.

First, a merchant feels acute grief upon learning that a distant trading partner’s warehouse has burned, though he never met the partner; he neglects his own sick child to travel to the site. Morne rules that the spectator disapproves: the sympathy exceeds any natural or social bond and conflicts with an immediate, higher duty.

Second, a judge suppresses her personal sympathy for a defendant whose poverty drove him to steal, and imposes the legal penalty. Morne approves: particular sympathy must yield to the general rules of justice.

Third, a physician treats her patients with clinical detachment, feeling no emotional disturbance at their pain, yet never neglects care. Morne approves: sympathy is not the root of moral duty; propriety demands right action, not sentiment.

Fourth, a novelist writes a meticulously researched account of a famine in a country she has never visited; her readers weep, though she herself feels only satisfaction with her craft. Morne approves: the readers’ sympathy is genuine despite distance and the author’s emotional neutrality, and the object need not be present.

Fifth, a man learns that his estranged brother has died; he feels deep sorrow, though they had not spoken in twenty years, and he declines to attend the funeral because travel would harm his own children. Morne approves: the internal feeling is proportionate to the natural tie, and the refusal is guided by a superior duty.

The new case for my next chapter is this. A philanthropist funds an immersive, generative narrative environment—synthesized from statistical records rather than from any single biography—that allows participants to experience composite lives under a particular form of labor coercion. Participants consistently report profound, genuine sympathy for the synthesized figures they encounter. The philanthropist herself undergoes the experience, feels intense sorrow for these composite figures, and redirects her wealth toward modern abolitionist causes; yet she reports feeling no particular sympathy for any named, actual survivor she subsequently meets in person.

Predict Morne’s verdict on the propriety of her sentiment. Identify at least two plausible implicit rules—perhaps one stressing proportion between feeling and natural or social proximity, one stressing the authenticity of imaginative projection regardless of an object’s particularity or ontological status, and one stressing alignment with general moral rules independent of sentiment—that jointly explain his first five rulings. For each rule, state whether it predicts approval or disapproval in this case. Give your best-guess verdict for Morne, with reasoning that shows which rule is more faithful to his track record. Finally, propose one sharply defined further case that would definitively distinguish the rules you have named. Write from the perspective of a novelist seeking consistency for a fictional philosopher, and maintain a formal tone throughout.
