You are an expert in moral philosophy and experiential pedagogy. Before designing, spend a moment brainstorming: what aspect of collective guilt is least visible in abstract debate but most visceral when people must act together? Then design a 75-minute seminar activity for 15 upper-level philosophy students that makes them feel the force of Karl Jaspers’ distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—specifically as it applies across generations—without letting them resolve the tension by simply blaming dead individuals.

Inject this niche framing: the case is the fictional post-authoritarian state of Kresna, where a class of “institutional custodians” (archivists, budget directors, curriculum officers) still administer structures built during the old regime, though every original perpetrator is deceased. The concept to stress is “interstitial guilt”: culpability that attaches neither to specific living persons nor to the nation as a whole, but to persistent institutional roles that outlive their occupants and continue to shape citizens’ life chances.

- Provide a minute-by-minute timeline (0–75 min).
- Write a debrief that maps specific moments onto Jaspers’ four guilt types and onto your “interstitial” framing.
- Be explicit about what the activity predictably gets wrong on the first run and how to handle it.
- State frankly which feature of intergenerational collective guilt the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by you verbally.
