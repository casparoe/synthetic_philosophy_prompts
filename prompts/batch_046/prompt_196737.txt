hey, retired chemist here, got into philosophy of history after I retired. I've noticed a pattern in how nations tell stories about fights they clearly lost, and I can't tell if there's a real term for the exact shape.

Example one: after a civil war, the losing side spends decades insisting they weren't beaten on merit but by sheer numbers and factories. They build monuments to the commanders who kept fighting long after it was hopeless, and anyone on their own side who had pushed for a negotiated settlement gets rewritten as a coward or villian — even when the deal they wanted was pretty reasonable.

Example two: a small group holds a mountain fortress against a giant empire, refuses to surrender when it's obvious they'll all die, and centuries later that mass suicide is taught as the founding moment of national identity. Not despite being a total defeat, but because of it. The people who argued for accepting terms and saving lives are barely mentioned, except as traitors.

So the pattern I'm seeing is this: an avoidable military failure gets turned into proof of moral purity precisely because it was avoidable and they took the hard road. The defeat is framed as both inevitable (we were outmatched) and freely chosen (we picked honor over survival). Meanwhile the internal pragmatists get erased or demonized.

I know people talk about 'lost cause' mythology for the first case, and there's something called the Masada complex for the second, but I'm asking about the specific narrative machinery that both share — the double move of inevitable-outcome-plus-voluntary-sacrifice, plus the retroactive casting of dissenters as enemies. Does that exact combo have an established name? And where are its edges — for instance, Dunkirk was a retreat with survival, not a last stand; does the pattern apply there, or is that a different beast?
