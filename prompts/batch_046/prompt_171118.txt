After retiring from experimental physics, I have been trying to understand the alignment literature on iterated amplification and distillation. The central claim is that a difficult reasoning or evaluation task can be decomposed into subproblems small enough for a human or a distilled model to solve, then reassembled, preserving both competence and alignment. Critics reply that alignment is not compositional: decomposing a value-laden judgment fragments the very coherence that made it aligned, while defenders insist that any non-decomposable problem is by definition intractable for scalable oversight.

The debate has persisted for years without convergence, despite broad agreement on the formal setup. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is largely about what "preservation" requires: one side treats alignment as output-conformity (the aggregate recommendation matches what the unamplified system would endorse), the other treats it as process-fidelity (the reasoning path at each node tracks the original system's values). But I am unsure whether this is the real fault line, or whether there is a hidden empirical claim about whether human cognition is actually decomposable in this way, or even a conceptual mistake in assuming that "competence" and "alignment" are properties that can be jointly measured across levels.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is? Do the sides differ over facts about cognitive architecture, over standards for what counts as preserved alignment, over the applicability of compositionality to normative reasoning, or are they answering different questions under the same name? Is there an ill-posedness in asking whether decomposition "preserves" both, and if so, what distinction — perhaps between functional decomposition of a task and epistemic decomposition of a justification — would clarify it? Finally, given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress: a formal result about the non-transitivity of value coherence, an empirical study of human decomposition in ethical reasoning, or a reframing of the question?
