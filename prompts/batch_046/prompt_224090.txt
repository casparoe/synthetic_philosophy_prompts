Consider the distinction between causal inevitability—where a macro-historical outcome is overdetermined by antecedent conditions such that no plausible counterfactual intervention at intermediate nodes could have diverted it—and narrative inevitability, where an outcome appears unavoidable solely because historians retrospectively privilege the actual causal chain, systematically underweighting near-miss branches that were ex ante equally probable. If one accepts that most familiar claims of historical inevitability (for instance, regarding the dissolution of the Western Roman Empire or the geographical concentration of the Industrial Revolution in Britain) are best interpreted as narrative rather than causal inevitability, what are the most important implications for (i) the methodological norms governing the construction of counterfactual historical scenarios, (ii) the attribution of moral responsibility to agents operating within apparently deterministic structural constraints, and (iii) the epistemic status of "turning point" narratives in pedagogical and scholarly historiography? In particular, address whether accepting this distinction forces historians to abandon the very concept of a turning point, or whether it can be rehabilitated under stricter conditions, and identify which of these three domains suffers the deepest revision.
