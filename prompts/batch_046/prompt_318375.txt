I am composing a novel about a composer who, after fifteen years of private revision, has finally completed a single sonata and now faces a binding choice: she may premiere it and thereby commit to performing that identical work every year for the remainder of her life, or she may burn the manuscript and begin anew. She experiences the decision not as a career calculation but as an existential contract—should she sign it, she believes she is consenting to an unbroken cycle in which every future Sunday morning will structurally reproduce this one, not merely as habit but as a fated repetition of the same hour. She asks whether it is more authentic to embrace the loop out of love for its particular content, or to affirm it precisely as an infinite cycle regardless of content; conversely, she wonders whether destroying the work truly liberates her or merely substitutes the repetition of regret for the repetition of performance. I seek guidance on how to resolve her dilemma without reducing it to generic advice about risk or fulfillment: what philosophical structure governs the distinction between accepting repetition because one wills the specific act and accepting it because one wills one's destiny to recur eternally, and does breaking the cycle genuinely escape the burden or only transform its form?
