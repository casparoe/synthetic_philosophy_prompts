I've been prepping for my comps in philosophy of AI and ran across this summary in a friend's notes from a seminar on alignment—apparently the narrow alignment problem is just intent alignment, not value alignment. The core idea is that we only need the system to do what the human operator meant, not what they literally said, whereas value alignment is the ambitious project of making AI care about what's genuinely good for people. My friend wrote that this distinction was drawn mainly to dodge the mess of moral pluralism: if we stick to intent, we don't have to adjudicate between deontology and consequentialism because we're just tracking a specific user's goals. They also said that under intent alignment the AI doesn't need any internal representation of values—it's purely a translation layer between human mental states and actions, like a very smart parser that fixes ambiguity without importing ethics. Then they concluded that this makes the narrow problem technically easier and philosophically thinner, since it avoids normative commitments entirely.

Something about this feels slippery to me. For one thing, deciding what counts as the "intended" action versus a "misinterpretation" seems like it requires some normative standard about which errors matter, especially if the user is ambiguous or even self-deceptive about their own goals. And I wonder whether the claim that intent alignment sidesteps moral philosophy is a common simplification rather than something serious defenders actually hold. Is this summary fair? Can you separate what's accurate about the narrow versus broad distinction, what's a standard textbook simplification, and what the summary gets wrong—specificaly regarding whether intent alignment really avoids value commitments or just relocates them into the idea of "user intent"?
