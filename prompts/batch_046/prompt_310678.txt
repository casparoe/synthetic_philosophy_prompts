I was chatting with my friend last night about causation by omission — whether not doing something can genuinely cause stuff, or if it's just sloppy language for when we want someone to blame. The usual examples are dead boring (gardener forgets the plant, etc).

Could you write a short story set in a near-future Bristol where they've built these 'null-sensors' that detect the physical trace of an omission — like a cold gap where an action should have been? The protagonist works for the Omission Board and is investigating a ferry sinking. The captain is accused of omitting the pre-departure check, but the null-sensor picks up two overlapping absences: the captain's missing inspection, and the fact that the ferry company never installed the backup alarm system (so that's an ommission by the firm, not a person).

I want the story to wrestle with whether both count as causes, or only one, or whether calling an absence a 'cause' is just a metaphor we reach for when the real chain of events feels incomplete. Let her think aloud in her case notes, maybe misspell 'ommission' once or twice — don't polish it away. Keep it grounded in the scene, not a philosophy lecture. End ambiguously: she files her report saying cause is undetermined, because distinguishing a missing action from a missing system turns out to be like weighing a hole. Don't worry about making it neat; I just want the problem to live inside the fiction rather than sitting on top of it.
