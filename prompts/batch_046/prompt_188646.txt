Write a taxonomy of positions on the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, structured as a branching tree with 6–8 leaves. Each internal node should pose a concrete judgment—e.g., about the transplant surgeon who kills one healthy patient to harvest organs for five dying ones, about pulling the trolley lever, or about a public-health policy that imposes a 0.1% fatal-risk vaccine to prevent a pandemic—phrased so a respondent can answer without knowing philosophical labels. The root split should ask whether it is ever permissible to impose an identifiable harm on one person in order to aggregate benefits across others. From there branch by: (i) whether the number of beneficiaries makes any moral difference at all; (ii) if numbers do matter, whether aggregation is restricted to ex-ante risk policies or extends to ex-post selection of actual victims; and (iii) whether the core objection is contractualist (each affected person must be able to consent to the reasoning) or distributive (treating people as replaceable value-containers violates justice). The leaves must include standard act-utilitarianism, a Rawls-style distributive objection, Taurek's equal-claim / numbers-irrelevant view, Scanlon's contractualist objection, a Nozick-style rights-based inviolability position, and an ex-ante-only risk-aggregation hybrid. For each unoccupied combination of answers—such as accepting ex-post aggregation while denying that numbers matter—note in one sentence whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate (for instance, certain contractualist and rights-based positions may converge on the surgeon case). Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or philosopher names in the questions themselves; sort by what the respondent says about the cases. Keep the deliverable suitable for a rationalist blog handout: concise, precise, and free of encyclopedia-style exposition.
