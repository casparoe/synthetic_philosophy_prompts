Prepare a speaker-defense sheet for a moral-philosophy workshop presentation. The speaker defends a modified contractualist resolution of the aggregation problem in rescue cases: instead of Scanlon's framework of individually justified objections that risk either non-aggregation or hidden summation, the speaker proposes a "serial pairwise comparison" procedure governed by a non-additive representational constraint. Under this procedure, the principle for allocating rescue is tested by comparing, in sequence, the objection that each of the five potential victims could raise against being sacrificed for the one, against the objection the single victim could raise against being sacrificed for each of the five—without ever cardinally weighting or interpersonally summing burdens. The speaker argues that this preserves contractualism's core rejection of utilitarian aggregation (no summation of well-being across persons) while yielding the correct verdict in standard five-versus-one cases, and that it avoids collapsing into Taurek-style pure non-aggregation because the pairwise structure allows a principled stopping rule rather than a numbers-blind veto.

The audience is a moral-philosophy workshop with: two specialists in Scanlon-style contractualism (deeply familiar with reason-based rejection, the role of generic reasons, and Parfit's aggregation critique); two philosophers from the anti-aggregation literature influenced by Kamm and Taurek, who regard any procedure saving the five as a betrayal of the intuition that numbers do not count; and one formal philosopher skeptical that moral theories can be repaired with procedural patches without either collapsing into consequentialism or becoming ad hoc. They care about whether the fix smuggles in aggregation under a different vocabulary, whether it is internally motivated by Scanlon's own account of reasonable rejection, and whether it can handle asymmetrical risk or partial rescue without arbitrary line-drawing.

Draft the prep sheet in spoken-objection form: list the objections in the order they are likely to arise, each phrased as that specific subgroup would actually phrase it in the room. For each objection, provide (a) a concise reply deliverable in about two minutes, (b) a brief note identifying the weakest point in that reply or what must be conceded, and (c) a label indicating whether it comes primarily from the contractualists, the anti-aggregationists, or the formal skeptic. Order them by combined likelihood and damage to the thesis.

Specifically include objections addressing:
- the charge that serial pairwise comparison is merely cardinal aggregation disguised as ordinal comparison (likely pressed by the formal skeptic or a contractualist);
- the worry that the "representational constraint" banning cardinal burden-weighting is unmotivated within Scanlon's reason-based framework and looks like an external patch (contractualist specialist);
- the Kamm-style objection that any decision procedure yielding "save the five" has already abandoned the genuine non-aggregative intuition that the number of people affected is morally irrelevant;
- the Parfit-style concern that if reasons can be compared across persons in pairwise fashion, contractualism loses its distinctive refusal to make interpersonal comparisons of reason-strength;
- a technical objection about how the procedure handles unequal risks, partial rescue, or cases where pairwise comparisons yield cyclical results.

Finally, identify the single objection the speaker is most hoping nobody asks—the one that, if pursued, exposes the deepest internal tension in the position. Describe the likely form of that question, and sketch a defensive reply that acknowledges the tension explicitly without abandoning the thesis.

Keep the format practical and oral: objections should sound like actual seminar questions, not written essay prompts. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or demands for specific page references. If you refer to Scanlon's framework, paraphrase rather than quoting. Do not invoke highly obscure technical apparatus that a small language model would be unlikely to have encountered in training.
