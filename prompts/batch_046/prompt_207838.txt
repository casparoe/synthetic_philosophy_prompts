Next month I am defending my paper at an interdisciplinary seminar in political theory and constitutional law. My thesis is that the standard epistocratic challenge—exemplified by the argument that democratic voting should be weighted or restricted by individual competence—commits a category error. It treats democratic participation as an instrumental aggregation of individual knowledge about policy outcomes, when in fact democratic legitimacy derives from what I call cognitive sovereignty: the non-alienable collective capacity of a polity to author the norms that bind it. On my view, this sovereignty is not reducible to the summed epistemic competence of voters, so individual voter ignorance does not by itself undermine democratic authority. At the same time, I do not defend pure majoritarianism. I propose institutional epistemic veto points—expert bodies with the limited power to block legislation that violates established factual constraints (for example, basic budgetary arithmetic or public-health baselines)—without allowing those bodies to determine normative ends. The veto is structural, not legislative; it preserves democratic authorship of values while preventing collective self-harm through demonstrable error.
