Consider a proposed inheritance-tax mechanism that applies a dynastic-duration multiplier: estates persisting within one family line across three or more generations face exponentially rising marginal rates, whereas first-generation wealth is taxed lightly. Heirs may reduce liability by endowing need-based scholarships, but only up to a ceiling tied to the estate’s calculated intergenerational mobility deficit—the gap between the heirs’ projected socioeconomic trajectory and a counterfactual in which the capital had been distributed as universal basic capital at each generational transfer.

What implicit theory of value and equality does this composite policy presuppose? Does it treat dynastic wealth as an independent moral wrong, or merely as a reliable proxy for unequal opportunity? Is the scholarship offset an internalization of social cost compatible with entitlement views, or does it presuppose a luck-egalitarian commitment to neutralizing only unchosen advantages? Reconstruct the implicit framework, distinguish what the mechanism strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify internal tensions—particularly whether the mobility-deficit metric assumes a baseline of perfect capital equality that no credible theory endorses, and whether allowing private scholarship endowments to count as tax credits conflates charity with public justice. Note also which elements remain philosophically underdetermined, staying compatible with, for example, a relational-equality account or a fair-equality-of-opportunity view.
