Here are my gut verdicts on harming one to save others, and I want to know whether one principle—perhaps a refined doctrine of double effect—captures them, or whether I must drop a verdict.

Case 1: Flip a switch to divert a trolley from five people to one. Verdict: permissible; the death is foreseen but not the means.

Case 2: Push a large person off a bridge so his body stops the trolley and saves the five. Verdict: impermissible; his body is the means.

Case 3: Kill one healthy patient and distribute his organs to save five dying ones. Verdict: impermissible; the killing is the means.

Case 4: Bomb a weapons factory, foreseeing that nearby civilians will die, though their deaths do not cause the factory’s destruction. Verdict: permissible; side effect, not means.

Case 5: Give a dying patient in agony a dose of morphine knowing it will suppress respiration and hasten death by minutes, with the sole aim of relieving pain, where the respiratory suppression is mechanistically inseparable from the analgesia. Verdict: permissible.

Case 6: A hospital’s automated allocation protocol withdraws a ventilator from a terminally unconscious patient—who will die within hours regardless—to free it for five patients with acute reversible failure. No individual intends the death; it is a bureaucratic byproduct of the rule, yet the patient’s death is causally necessary for the machine to become available, and the protocol was designed with this sequence in mind. Verdict: impermissible, despite the absence of individual malicious intent and despite the structural parallel to Case 4.

Is there a coherent account—distinguishing causal means from causal prerequisites, or agent-relative from institutional intention, or perhaps introducing a condition about direct bodily involvement—that delivers all six? If not, which verdict should I overturn and why? Also, state precisely what the best principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a self-driving car programmed only to minimize total fatalities swerves away from five pedestrians into a barrier, killing its lone passenger, where the passenger’s death is an unavoidable physical consequence of the swerve but not the causal mechanism by which the pedestrians are saved.
