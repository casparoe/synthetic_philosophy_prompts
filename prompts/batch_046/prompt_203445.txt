In my philosophy of moral psychology reading group, we have spent months circling the situationist critique of virtue ethics—the line running from Doris’s 2002 argument that global character traits are empirically vacant, through Harman’s essays on behavioral variability, and into recent meta-analyses of the replication literature—without anyone changing position. The situationists continue to cite the Good Samaritan study and Milgram-style obedience experiments as proof that behavior is driven by situational features rather than stable dispositions; the virtue ethicists continue to respond with local-trait reconstructions, the practical-wisdom defense, and the skill analogy developed by Annas and Stichter, according to which virtue is a competence structure that produces different performances in different environments rather than a uniform behavioral cause. Every rebuttal is now familiar, and the discussion feels stalled.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not mainly empirical—both sides largely accept the psychological data—but structural and partly conceptual. Specifically, I suspect the disagreement turns on an unstated divergence in explanatory frameworks: the situationist treats “character” as a predictive disposition that should yield cross-situational stability, while the virtue ethicist, especially on the skill model, treats it as a normative competence analogous to linguistic competence, which manifests differently across contexts without being falsified by that variation. If so, the dispute may be partly verbal—about whether “trait” must mean “behaviorally uniform cause”—and partly about which project owns the concept: descriptive personality psychology or normative moral education. At the same time, there may be a hidden normative disagreement about whether moral formation should aim at stable dispositions or at flexible, context-sensitive judgment, which neither side argues for directly because each believes it is arguing about empirical psychology.

Can you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement? Is it a substantive empirical dispute in disguise, a verbal disagreement about “character” and “trait,” two different questions—one psychological, one ethical—running under a single heading, or a bedrock clash about the aims of moral education that neither side can argue for? And, crucially, if your diagnosis is correct, what would count as genuine progress? Would it require applying a rigorous competence-performance distinction borrowed from linguistics to moral psychology; an empirical result showing whether expert moral practitioners display cross-domain stability; a conceptual clarification that separates predictive explanation from normative assessment; or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and that virtue ethics and situationism are simply answering different questions with shared vocabulary? Give me the diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions, and be explicit about what, if anything, could actually move the debate.
