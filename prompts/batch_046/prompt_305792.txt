I just spent the better part of an evening in a long, circular argument with a friend about whether terms like “Boomer,” “Millennial,” and “Gen Z” denote anything ontologically substantial, or whether they are merely administrative fictions sustained by census categories, marketing departments, and nostalgic memoirs. We kept returning to the boundary problem: a person born in December 1996 and another born in January 1997 share nearly identical technological, economic, and geopolitical environments, yet they are routinely sorted into different generational “boxes” with different attributed temperaments and political destinies. To try to clarify what we were actually disagreeing about, I proposed a rough tripartite ontology of cohorts—demographic aggregates (statistical clusters fixed by arbitrary birth-year ranges), experiential kinds (groups unified by shared formative disruptions such as entering adulthood during the 2008 financial collapse or the pandemic’s school closures), and narrative kinds (categories kept alive through media discourse, institutional labeling, and collective self-identification practices). My friend insisted that if generations are real kinds, at least one of these three must possess essential properties, while I argued that the reality of experiential kinds might actually undermine the reality of demographic ones, since the causal mechanisms that shape a cohort—say, exposure to a collapsed entry-level labor market—operate continuously across calendar lines rather than clustering discretely at 1997. Could you explain the conceptual landscape here in some detail? Specifically, is there a defensible account of cohort ontology that allows for genuinely shared formative experience without requiring sharp natural boundaries, and does recognizing narrative kinds as partly constitutive of the cohort reinforce or dissolve the claim that generations are more than demographic noise? I am not asking for sociological data, a literature review, or citations; I want to understand whether the “boundary elasticity” between administrative cutoffs and lived historical discontinuities makes generational realism incoherent, or whether a non-essentialist but still real kind-structure can accommodate the fuzziness without collapsing into pure convention.
