I'm presenting my dissertation chapter in a joint history and political theory seminar next Thursday. The thesis I am defending is that the canonical Hobsbawm-Anderson framework for understanding modern nations — that they are relatively recent "inventions" sustained by "imagined communities" — obscures a more precise structural distinction between mnemonic, bureaucratic, and spectacular modes of collective identity formation. I argue specifically that much of what historians classify as "invented tradition" in nineteenth-century European state-building is better understood as bureaucratic retrojection: the projection of modern census categories, linguistic classifications, and land-registry data backward onto pre-modern populations, creating an illusion of continuous ethnic identity that is actually an artifact of archival practice. The audience will be six empirical social historians of early modern Europe who are deeply suspicious of grand typologies, one political theorist who is a committed defender of the standard Andersonian account, and one anthropologist specializing in oral ritual transmission who will be alert to any reduction of non-state mnemonic practices to bureaucratic effects. They value primary-source fidelity and will object that my framework imposes anachronistic concepts onto archival material. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody raises: the anthropologist's challenge about whether bureaucratic retrojection can genuinely account for ritual continuity in communities that never experienced centralized state archiving. I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I need arguments tailored to this room.
