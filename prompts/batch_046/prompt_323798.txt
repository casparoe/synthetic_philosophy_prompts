A colleague in my philosophy reading group — a retired engineer who reads widely in archaeology — offered this summary of the literature on societal collapse: "Collapse is best understood as a unified, measurable failure mode. When a civilization's net energy surplus drops below the level required to maintain its administrative hierarchy, structural integrity is lost: writing systems disappear, monumental construction stops, and population declines sharply. The Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island all exhibit this identical pattern, and in each case the prior institutions were not replaced by functionally equivalent structures, which means the societies objectively failed rather than merely transformed. Critics who say these cultures only changed form are ignoring quantifiable indicators — energy return on investment, settlement density, bureaucratic record continuity — and treating organizational death as a matter of interpretation, like insisting a collapsed lung is just reconfigured respiration."

Having spent my career in the natural sciences but only recently turned to conceptual questions in history and philosophy, I find the thermodynamic analogy persuasive, yet I worry it may conflate different archaeological records or overstate a consensus that does not exist. Is this a fair summary of how scholars actually treat collapse in these three cases? Please distinguish what reflects genuine agreement, what is a reasonable simplification, and what misrepresents either the empirical record or the philosophical question of whether "failure" can be defined without reference to a society's own aims and values. I would prefer the response in American English.
