In my philosophy of economics seminar we have spent the better part of a term circling the same problem about revealed preference and the ontology of utility, without either side conceding ground. One camp—mostly economists and formally inclined philosophers—holds that utility is nothing more than a numerical representation of a consistent choice ordering: if an agent’s selections satisfy the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference across a well-defined domain, then we have said everything true about her utility, and talk of mental satisfaction or subjective welfare is at best redundant, at worst metaphysically spurious. The other camp—mostly philosophers drawing on decision theory and phenomenology—insists that utility must be anchored in some mental or experiential state, because otherwise we cannot distinguish a genuinely satisfied agent from one who has merely been conditioned into a consistent choice pattern; they point to framing effects, preference reversals between joint and separate evaluation, and cases of adaptive preference in which an agent consistently selects deprivation yet reports no corresponding satisfaction.

The stall is peculiar: both sides now accept the same experimental literature—framing violations of the kind studied by Kahneman and Tversky, the Allais paradox in risk preferences, and even recent dynamic-choice data showing reversals when an identical menu is presented as an opt-in versus an opt-out default. They agree these phenomena occur, and they agree that standard revealed-preference theory needs amendment, whether via reference-dependent utility functions, context-dependent representations, or explicit mental-state supplementation. Yet the amendment debate never advances. The structuralists treat framing as a change in the choice environment that alters the revealed ordering, requiring only a richer formal representation of the agent-context pair; the intentionalists treat the very same data as proof that choice behavior tracks something other than a stable mental utility—namely contextual cues, social conditioning, or errors—so that mental states remain indispensable for explaining why the behavior counts as irrational rather than merely different. Each side accuses the other of changing the subject, but neither can point to an empirical result that would settle the matter, because any behavioral result can be redescribed either as a shift in revealed preferences or as noise masking an underlying mental state.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden verbal dispute about whether the term utility should apply only to mental representations, or a substantive metaphysical disagreement about whether mental states cause choices in a way that makes them indispensable to explanation? Could there be a hidden formal question—perhaps about whether any extensionally adequate choice representation can be uniquely decomposed into mental-state plus environmental parameters, or whether underdetermination runs all the way down? Or are the sides simply answering different questions: one about predictive representation, the other about explanatory causation and normative assessment? And if your diagnosis is that the disagreement is bedrock—say, a clash of intuitions about whether explanation must terminate in subjective experience—what would even count as progress? Would it require a conceptual distinction between agent-indexed and context-indexed utility, a formal impossibility result about representational uniqueness, or a change of question altogether?
