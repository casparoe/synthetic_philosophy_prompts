I am leading a single seventy-five-minute workshop for graduate students in heritage conservation who have no prior background in nineteenth-century philosophy. The idea they must be able to apply—not merely name—is Nietzsche’s distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, drawn from his second Untimely Meditation, specifically in the context of a preservation dispute. Do not explain Nietzsche’s theory to me; instead, plan the explanatory arc.

First, lay out the dependency order: which of the three modes must be introduced first, which second, and which last, with the reason each step requires the previous one and why presenting them as a flat taxonomy would fail for this audience.

Second, specify the one concrete case that should carry the entire explanation: the debate over whether to preserve or demolish a large post-war social-housing estate that was celebrated in its era as an architectural monument and later condemned as an urban-planning failure. State exactly what properties this case must possess and what it must lack—particularly why it works better here than the standard classroom examples of Renaissance Rome-revival or local folklore archiving—and identify the precise moment in the sequence where the case should be introduced.

Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order the students will form them: for instance, that critical history is only negative debunking, that monumental history is uncomplicated hero-worship, and that antiquarian history is mere nostalgia. For each, specify the exact point in your sequence where it should be intercepted and defused, and what brief counter-example or reframing does the work.

Fourth, propose one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that reveals whether a student has grasped the life-serving function of each mode rather than having only memorized the three labels.

Finally, state explicitly what portion of Nietzsche’s broader argument—such as the physiological necessity of forgetting, the pathology of historical hypertrophy, or the relationship to the will to power—I should openly set aside, name as out of scope, and tell the students we are not addressing.
