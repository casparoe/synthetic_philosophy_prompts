Take the argument from divine hiddenness, roughly: if a perfectly loving deity exists, that being would want a personal relationship with all capable humans, which requires belief; yet reasonable non-belief is widespread and apparently not culpable; therefore such a deity probably does not exist. Restate this argument step by step in mechanism-design vocabulary, treating the deity as a principal who selects an information structure over agents with heterogeneous priors about the state (the deity's existence), where belief is an action with state-dependent payoffs and "love" is interpreted as an objective function over agent welfare. For each component — the normative claim that belief should be widespread, the diagnosis of hiddenness as a failure of the mechanism, and the inference to non-existence — identify what translates cleanly, what translates only by distorting either the theological claim or the economic concept, and what drops out entirely (for example, the phenomenology of doubt, the moral value of freely chosen belief, or the distinction between epistemic and affective relationships). Conclude by judging whether the untranslatable residue reveals a limitation of mechanism design for religious epistemology, or whether it shows that the original argument smuggles in unstated assumptions — such as a particular social-choice function or welfare criterion — that a mechanism designer would reject as implausible or non-incentive-compatible.
