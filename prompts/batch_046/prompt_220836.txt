Consider a modified Thomson's lamp in which the switch at stage n is governed by a deterministic but non-computable rule—specifically, the lamp is on at stage n if and only if n lies in a predetermined non-computable subset of the natural numbers—so that after the one-minute supertask is completed there is no algorithmic way to determine the lamp's final state. Suppose Zeno of Elea, familiar only with his paradoxes of infinite division and the impossibility of traversing an actual infinity but unacquainted with modern logic or computability theory, is shown this device and asked whether it possesses a determinate state (on or off) at the limit. Predict, in exactly five numbered points with no introduction or conclusion, how he would argue from his own commitments, addressing in particular whether the lack of a computable decision procedure reinforces his original objections or introduces a difficulty he would dismiss as foreign to the nature of motion and time. Each point should be one or two sentences.
