I'm preparing a feature on how philosophers classify emotional experience, and I keep encountering a three-part taxonomy: emotions as evaluative judgments about what matters (in the tradition of Nussbaum or Solomon), as perceptions of bodily change (from James-Lange-style accounts to more recent interoceptive models), or as sui generis feelings that carry their own intentional direction. Recently, however, I've been speaking with cognitive scientists who frame emotional states within hierarchical predictive processing—treating affect as essentially precision-weighted interoceptive inference about visceral signals. From that vantage point, the old philosophical divisions seem like different descriptions of the same neurocomputational routine: a "judgment" becomes a high-level prior, a "feeling" is the prediction error, and "perception" is the inference mechanism itself. Does this mean the conceptual boundary between judgment, perception, and feeling theories has genuinely collapsed, or is there a settled philosophical objection—perhaps concerning intentionality, phenomenology, or normative evaluation—that preserves the taxonomy regardless of the underlying brain mechanism? In your response, please clearly separate what is broadly accepted across both philosophy and cognitive science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any single interpretation, especially a reductionist neurocomputational one, as the established consensus.
