As a retired experimental physicist who has lately been auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mind, I keep encountering a comparison that my study group treats as almost axiomatic: the brain is a computer, and more precisely, the relation between mind and brain is structurally analogous to that between software and hardware. The analogy is usually pressed into service for a fairly ambitious conclusion—namely, that mental states are in principle substrate-independent, and that once we map the algorithmic architecture of cognition we could realize the same mental life in silicon or in any other medium that implements the correct computational structure. Yet in my own reading I have come across a more granular formulation that my colleagues rarely examine: the brain not as a generic von Neumann machine but as a continuously running, metabolically self-repairing analog system that performs predictive coding through recurrent, error-minimizing loops—something closer to a self-rewiring field-programmable analog array with continuous-time dynamics than to a digital processor with discrete clock cycles and separable storage.

I would like you to audit this refined analogy with some rigor. Please lay out an explicit correspondence table: in the brain, what plays the role of the central processor, the memory store, the input-output interface, the algorithm or program, and the physical wiring that modifies itself in real time? Then identify precisely where the mapping fractures. Does the brain possess anything genuinely analogous to a stored program that can be copied independently of the physical device, or to a synchronized clock cycle that discretizes operations? Does the comparison quietly import features—such as the possibility of perfect replication without biological continuity, or the existence of a sharp distinction between data and mechanism—that the neural target genuinely lacks? Most importantly, I want to know whether the substrate-independence conclusion survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy itself was carrying the entire argumentative load. If the brain-computer comparison ultimately cannot license that conclusion, what alternative analogy—perhaps drawn from non-equilibrium thermodynamics, distributed sensor networks, or analog signal processing—would better capture the mind-brain relation without overreaching?
