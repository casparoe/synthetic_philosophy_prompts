I hold that national myths organized around lost causes divide into two structurally distinct kinds: those that commemorate resistance to annihilation without surrender, and those that commemorate the defense of an unjust social order against legitimate reform. The first kind—exemplified by Masada as refusal of enslavement and by Dunkirk as strategic preservation—can be integrated into democratic identity because the defeat carries no claim to restitution. The second kind—exemplified by the Lost Cause of the Confederacy—generates what I call grievance recursion: a feedback loop in which the myth demands redress for a wrong that was never done to a deserving party, thereby perpetuating political grievance across generations. Kosovo in 1389 complicates the taxonomy, since it is narrated simultaneously as martyrdom against Ottoman conquest and, in modern Serbian nationalism, as proof of stolen sovereignty; I am unsure whether it belongs with Masada or with the Confederacy. What would count against this view? I want an honest assessment of its exposure. Which historical interpretations of Masada, Dunkirk, Kosovo, or the Confederacy would undermine my categorization? Which comparative cases—say, the Warsaw Uprising, the Alamo, or the defense of Constantinople—have I ignored or misclassified? Which conceptual distinctions must hold for my taxonomy to survive—between defeat and betrayal, between myth and history, between collective trauma and collective grievance—and which psychological or sociological findings about collective memory would collapse them? Which defeaters would defenders of grievance-based myths themselves concede, and which would they dismiss? Rank these from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, tell me whether this position is genuinely risky or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a theory of national memory.
