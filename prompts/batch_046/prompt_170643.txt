Write a Shakespearean sonnet—abab cdcd efef gg—spoken in the first person by one of Leibniz’s unactualized striving possibles: an essence that tends toward existence within the divine understanding but was excluded from the actual world because it could not be combined with the set of compossible essences that together maximize overall perfection. In the octave, let the speaker claim membership in a counterfactual world that avoids a specific, named local evil present in ours—perhaps a particular historical atrocity or a destructive natural event—presenting that world as apparently richer in harmony and justice. At the volta in line 9, shift the speaker’s tone from grievance to structural recognition: its own exclusion is not an injustice but a combinatorial necessity, since the best world achieves the greatest possible variety of being precisely through constraints on which essences can coexist. Design the rhyme scheme so that the A and B rhymes concern logical and modal relations—compatibility, combination, exclusion, inclusion—while the C and D rhymes concern moral or aesthetic evaluation—good, evil, perfect, lacking—so that the formal pairing itself enacts the tension between compossibility and the problem of evil. The closing couplet should articulate the paradox that the world containing the most being necessarily contains the absence of some beings, leaving open whether that absence is the hidden price of perfection or its quiet indictment; do not resolve the tension. The poem should be metaphysically precise enough that a specialist in early modern philosophy recognizes the operative distinction between absolute possibility and hypothetical necessity in the speaker’s final acceptance, and it should stand as a complete poem for a reader unfamiliar with the Theodicy.
