Here's an argument my study partner cooked up about Meno's problem and now I think it proves way too much, but I can't see the exit. (1) Knowledge is genuinely more valuable than true belief -- that's the whole Meno intuition. (2) So there must be some feature F that knowledge has and true belief lacks. (3) The usual suspects for F are justification, reliabilty, or maybe understanding. (4) But each of those is only instrumentally good: they help you get true beliefs in future cases, or defend them in conversation. (5) Once you've already got the true belief about p, having F doesn't change anything about p's truth, or about what you can do with it right now. (6) So F is like a warrenty on a car that already runs fine: it doesn't make the car drive any better. (7) Therefore knowledge isn't more valuable than true belief; it's just true belief plus a redudant certificate. (8) Even worse, since acquiring F costs effort and time, we should actually prefer lucky true beliefs over knowledge. (9) So the argument doesn't just solve Meno's problem, it inverts it: knowledge is worse. Where does this slip? I'd rather keep (1) and (3) if I can, but I'm open to losing (5) if that's where the rot is. Also, is there any version of F that isn't just instrumental?
