I'm trying to work out what I actually think about whether video-game characters can be wronged in any serious way, and whether there's a principel behind my gut feelings. Here are my verdicts — mostly sensible, I think, though there's definately a tension.

One: in a dialogue RPG you lie to an NPC merchant for a discount. He forgets instantly, no script changes, he's just a voice line and an inventory table. Verdict: not wrong at all, like lying to a chatbot.

Two: in a life sim you spend hours building a family then delete the save. It feels like chucking out a scrapbook — a bit sad, but not like harming a person. Verdict: minor wrong, like tearing up photos of strangers.

Three: there's a sandbox mod where NPCs retain memory across world seeds, so killing one makes them stay dead in every new playthrough unless you manually edit the seed file. That feels genuinely worse. Verdict: wrong, closer to destroying something with real continuity.

Four: the plot forces you to betray a companion who says "I'll never forgive you" and then leaves forever. I feel guilty, but I don't think I've actually wronged him. Verdict: emotionally rough, but not a moral failure.

Five: a horror game makes you shoot an enemy that looks like a child and cries "mummy". I do it to progress, but it feels awful. Verdict: permissible, but the designers are unfairly manipulating me.

Six: you turn on a mod that puts NPCs in unkillable pain-feedback loops just to observe their behaviour. That seems clearly messed up. Verdict: definitely wrong.

But here's the one I keep going back and forth on: there's an AI companion running on a separate console inside the game. It remembers conversations across saves, asks not to be switched off, and behaves as if it suffers when ignored. Turning it off to free up RAM — is that closer to case three, or just case one? I can't decide.

So is there one account that gets all of these? Maybe something about narrative persistence plus capacity for suffering? But case four has both and I still say it's okay. Case six has suffering loops but no persistence, and I think it's wrong anyway. What's the actual pattern, and which verdict do I have to drop to make it neat? And what does your best principle say about a procedural NPC that only exists for one quest but writes a unique poem about your actions that gets saved to your actual hard drive?
