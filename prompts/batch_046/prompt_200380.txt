I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on collective memory and historiography. To prepare for our unit on epistemic conflicts between community testimony and archival evidence, I would like to practice defending the view that collective memory should take normative precedence over the historical record when the two conflict—not on pragmatic grounds, but because memory performs a constitutive, identity-forming function that archives, being designed for evidential preservation and necessarily selective, structurally cannot replicate. Please adopt the role of a committed archival epistemologist: sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede minor points but never the core claim that the record must correct memory to preserve the distinction between history and myth. Remain fully in character, respond in a single concise paragraph, do not break role, and do not ask clarifying questions. Here is my opening challenge: archives are not neutral repositories; selection, cataloguing, and preservation are themselves exercises of power that embed the victor's frame. To demand that a living community correct its own memory against such a record is not epistemic hygiene but an extension of the original exclusion. How do you respond without simply assuming that evidential preservation trumps constitutive identity?
