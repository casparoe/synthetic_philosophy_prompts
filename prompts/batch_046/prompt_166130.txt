I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy currently writing a dissertation on the ethics of deception, and in a recent graduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy—centered on his discussion of lying to the murderer at the door—we were asked to articulate our own reflective judgments rather than reproduce canonical interpretations. After class, I continued the exercise with a colleague from political theory, and my assessments have settled into a mostly stable pattern, with two notable exceptions.

Here are my verdicts. If an armed assailant asks whether my friend is concealed inside and I lie to protect them: that deception is permissible, perhaps obligatory. Telling my aunt that I genuinely like the garish sweater she knitted: permissible. Informing a job interviewer that I left my previous position voluntarily, when in fact I was dismissed for cause: impermissible. Assuring a dying patient who asks that their estranged child telephoned, knowing this is false but intending only to ease their final hours: impermissible, despite the compassionate motive. Writing to a creditor that the check is in the mail when no check exists: impermissible, though the wrong feels less severe than the interview deception. Fabricating a synthetic voice message in the voice of a deceased relative to comfort a grieving friend, without revealing that the voice and message are artificial: I remain genuinely divided—worse than the sweater, but whether it equals the dying-patient lie or the interview lie, I cannot consistently decide. The judgment that keeps reversing is telling my young child that our euthanized dog was rehomed with a kind farmer rather than put to sleep: part of me sees it as permissible protection from premature grief; part of me sees it as an impermissible breach of the trust that my words about absence and death are truthful.

Given these judgments—particularly the tension between permitting deception to save a life and forbidding it to comfort the dying, together with the ambiguity surrounding synthetic mediation and protective falsehoods about death—is there a principle, or a small competing family of principles, that captures the pattern with minimal revision? State the principle precisely, mapping each clause to the case that motivates it (the murderer, the sweater, the interview, the patient, the check, the synthetic message, the farm lie), giving its verdict on a case I have not mentioned—say, falsely assuring a colleague that a shared manuscript was lost to a server failure, when in reality I never completed my section, solely to postpone confrontation—and stating honestly which of my own verdicts it must overturn. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, the case mapping, and the admission of misfit, with no preamble and without restating the question.
