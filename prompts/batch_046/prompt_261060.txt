I am a novelist composing a philosophical essay for a literary quarterly, and I should be grateful for your critical remarks on both its conceptual architecture and its prose. The piece addresses the scale argument in debates about insect sentience: the contention that, because insects exist in such staggering multitudes, even a vanishingly small per-individual probability of consciousness generates an overwhelming aggregate moral burden. Rather than rehearsing the familiar probabilistic calculus, I introduce what I call the "narrative dispersion problem." I propose that moral consideration depends not solely on the presence or absence of subjective experience, but on a minimal condition I term "phenomenological accessibility"—the capacity for a life to be integrated within a coherent narrative structure, whether first-person or third-person. Insects, on this view, present a distinctive difficulty not because we possess conclusive evidence against their sentience, but because their existence is distributed across such immense spatial and temporal scales that any attempt to represent them as discrete subjects dissolves into statistical abstraction. I therefore argue that the scale argument commits a category error by treating sentience as a fungible quantity that can be multiplied like biomass, when in fact moral weight requires a unit of narrative coherence that vast multitudes inherently undermine. I then consider the objection that this framing merely projects an anthropocentric, literary prejudice onto metaphysical reality, and I attempt to rebut it by distinguishing epistemic accessibility from ontological status. I also draw an analogy to the representation of crowd scenes in realist fiction, where individual interiority is necessarily sacrificed to collective motion, and I ask whether this comparison illuminates the ethical issue or merely distracts from it. Would you comment specifically on whether my "narrative dispersion" framing genuinely advances the philosophical debate or instead obscures the underlying probabilistic reasoning with metaphor? I should also welcome observations on whether my treatment of the scale argument's proponents avoids strawmanning, on the coherence of my distinction between accessibility and status, and on where the language might be tightened without sacrificing its literary register.
