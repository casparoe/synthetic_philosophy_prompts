I am preparing a programme on moral philosophy for an interdisciplinary seminar, and in our recent discussions we have examined Adam Smith’s account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in The Theory of Moral Sentiments. The students understand the core idea—that we correct our partial sentiments by imaginatively placing ourselves in another’s position and appealing to an internal observer—but they have raised a difficulty that Smith could not have foreseen. They observe that modern digital platforms do not merely mediate sympathy between individuals; they aggregate, rank, and amplify emotional responses through algorithmic systems that present a computed collective reaction as though it were a neutral moral judgement. One might describe this as a distributed impartial spectator: not the disciplined internal judge within a single breast, but an externally synthesised average of millions of partial sympathies, weighted by engagement metrics and fed back to users as authoritative ethical guidance.

If Adam Smith were alive today and confronted with this architecture—where the correction of sentiment is outsourced to an algorithmic aggregation rather than achieved through personal imaginative labour—how do you suppose he would evaluate it? Would he consider it a legitimate extension of the social refinement of sympathy through mutual observation, or would he condemn it as a corruption of the impartial spectator precisely because it replaces individual accountability and nuanced moral discernment with a quantitative metric? I should be grateful for your reasoning, particularly on whether Smith would regard such a system as preserving the corrective function of sympathy or as flattening the gradations of moral approbation into an anonymous, mechanised verdict.
