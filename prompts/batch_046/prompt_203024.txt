I was talking with my roommate about politics and she mentioned that dirty hands idea — like how presidents or mayors sometimes have to lie or do shady stuff for the greater good. I tried to map out the logic and now I think I've built something that proves way too much, but I can't spot the crack.

Here's my chain:

(1) Dirty hands says a leader can break a normal moral rule if breaking it stops something way worse.
(2) But people say there's also moral residue — the bad act still stains you even when it was totally necessary, so you don't get off scott-free.
(3) If the stain is real, then we can treat it like a real cost. The leader takes on some moral damage, and in exchange they prevent some harm.
(4) Once it's a cost, you can compare it to the benefit directly. If one lie creates 1 unit of moral damage but prevents 100 deaths, that's clearly a good trade.
(5) But there's no natural wall at 100. If 1 unit for 100 deaths is okay, then 2 units for 200 deaths is also okay, and you can keep scaling up.
(6) So a leader should just keep racking up moral damage whenever the math works — lying here, framing someone there, maybe bending one law — as long as they save more lives overall.
(7) That turns dirty hands into a corruption budget. The leader is running a moral debt account and should go into debt whenever the payoff is good enough.
(8) But that feels wrong. Most people who believe in dirty hands picture one awful emergency choice, then a lifetime of guilt, not a spreadsheet of how much corruption they can afford.
(9) So either moral residue isn't the kind of thing you can put on a scale, or the dirty hands idea collapses into "do whatever works."

Which step is broken? Is it that you can't quantify moral damage? Or is the mistake earlier, in assuming that "necessary wrong" is still wrong in the same weighable way? I really want to keep the idea that leaders sometimes have to do awful things for real reasons, but I don't want them to have a license to do awful things whenever it's convienent. What's the fix?
