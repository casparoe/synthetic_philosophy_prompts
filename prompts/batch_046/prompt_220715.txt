Since I retired from biophysics I've been trying to understand the philosphy behind how we actually do science, and I keep coming back to this weird split in peer review culture. In economics and climate science people defend models with blatantly false assumptions — point-mass agents, frictionless trade, paramaterized clouds that don't match microphysics — as long as predictions hold up. They cite Friedman's F-twist: unrealistic premises are okay if the model works. But in my old field and in pharmacology, reviewers reject that move. If your ion-channel model assumes a homogeneous membrane and gets the right current by accident, they call it curve-fitting, not science. They demand mechanistic realism.

So what philosophical commitments does that instutional double standard presuppose? Does it mean economics thinks explanation doesn't matter, only prediction? Or that different sciences have different aims built in? Can one coherent philosophy of science license both practices, or are we just being inconsistent across departments?

Reconstruct the implicit theory behind each side, say whether they're compatible, and tell me what peer review and funding priorites would have to change if we replaced the underlying view with pure instrumentalism or with structural realism. Don't just lay out options — take a committed position on whether the split is justified.

Also I keep going back and forth on climate models: we use parameterizations that are physically wrong but stabilize predictions.
