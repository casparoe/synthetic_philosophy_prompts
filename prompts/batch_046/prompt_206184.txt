I run a 90-minute graduate workshop in philosophy of social science with about 16 students who have seen basic game theory but not formal epistemic logic. Design a runnable in-class simulation—using only cards, sealed envelopes, and a point-payoff scheme—that makes the group feel why rationalizability can halt one step short of the rationalizable set when accessibility relations are reflexive and symmetric but fail transitivity. Specifically, students should occupy roles in a “broken-chain” information structure: each adjacent pair has mutual belief of rationality, yet no global common belief exists because knowledge does not propagate across the chain. The rules and message-passing constraints (not a lecture) should cause them to eliminate a dominated strategy locally, then discover collectively that they have stopped short of the globally rationalizable profile and cannot justify the final elimination without assuming the missing transitive link. Provide: the physical setup and materials; role assignments and hidden-type cards; a round-by-round timeline with decision points; a payoff table in points (no real money); instructions for the facilitator on how to reveal the broken chain only after the stall occurs; a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the formal distinction between pairwise mutual belief and common belief of rationality, and onto the iterative elimination procedure; an honest statement of what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., students inferring transitivity from physical proximity, or the facilitator accidentally repairing the chain); and a frank admission of which feature of the formal result—likely the proof that rationalizability equals the intersection of best-response sets under an equivalence relation—this embodied activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied afterward. Do not ask for citations or a bibliography. End your response with a single line beginning “Core gap:” that captures the one formal feature this simulation cannot reproduce.
