At a recent workshop on social ontology, two colleagues—Dr. Marcus Chen and Dr. Elena Vasquez—became locked in a disagreement about whether methodological individualism can survive what Vasquez calls “reverse emergence” in institutional persistence. I observed the exchange as a bystander and do not wish to signal a preference; I ask only for a point-by-point adjudication. Please write your response in American English.

Here is the exchange as it unfolded.

(1) Chen opened by defending methodological individualism as an ontological constraint rather than a mere heuristic. He argued that any explanation attributing beliefs, intentions, or causal powers to an institution is either reducible without remainder to the mental states and actions of its individual members, or it commits an unacceptable ontological bloating—treating relational structures as if they possessed minds. Even apparently collective phenomena such as institutional inertia, he insisted, must ultimately bottom out in individual-level coordination failures.

(2) Vasquez replied that this misses a specific class of cases she terms “reverse emergence”: every individual member of an institution may sincerely prefer structural change, yet the institution persists unchanged because of interdependent contractual and normative relations that are not properties of any single member. She claimed that in such cases the explanatory work is done by “system-level dependency webs” that cannot be captured by aggregating individual preferences without severe loss of predictive and causal information.

(3) Chen conceded that relational structures exist, but introduced what he called the “explanatory substitution thesis”: for any holistic explanation invoking these webs, one can in principle construct an isomorphic individual-level network description specifying every counterfactual dependency among members. Because the two descriptions are predictively equivalent, he argued, the holistic explanation is merely a cognitive shorthand and does no independent explanatory labor; treating it as causally autonomous is therefore a category mistake.

(4) Vasquez countered in two parts. First, she argued that predictive equivalence does not entail explanatory equivalence: if the individual-level description requires specifying an astronomically complex array of counterfactual dependencies that exceeds any feasible cognitive or computational capacity, then the holistic level functions as an autonomous explanatory layer—what she termed an “epistemic compression failure” at the individual level. Second, she noted that Chen’s substitution thesis tacitly assumes explanation must be transparent to human reasoners, yet many legitimate scientific explanations (for instance, in statistical mechanics) depend on macro-level properties precisely because micro-level descriptions are intractable, not because they are false.

They then asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves above from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then indicate who is right about which specific point, identify what—if anything—both parties are missing, state who is more right overall, and explain your reasoning. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
