As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have encountered Molyneux's problem — the question of whether an individual blind from birth who suddenly acquires sight can immediately identify shapes by vision that they previously knew only through touch. I should like you to construct a modern thought experiment that updates this scenario for a contemporary technological setting, adhering to the specifications below.

Please design a realistic, single-paragraph scenario (not science fiction) involving an adult who has been blind since birth and has learned to perceive three-dimensional objects through echolocation combined with a haptic sensory-substitution vest. This individual then receives a retinal prosthesis that provides partial visual input. The case must be engineered so that three theoretical positions yield distinctly different verdicts regarding whether the subject can immediately match visual representations to their existing spatial concepts.

The three positions are:
1. An empiricist associationist position (in the Lockean tradition), which holds that cross-modal recognition depends on learned correlations and should therefore initially fail.
2. A rationalist or nativist position (in the Leibnizian tradition), which holds that spatial concepts are unified across sensory modalities and should therefore permit immediate recognition.
3. A predictive-processing position, which holds that recognition depends on the brain's weighting of prior predictions against incoming sensory precision; on this view, the subject should exhibit above-chance but inconsistent matching, with performance varying according to contextual confidence rather than being uniformly present or absent.

After describing the scenario, please explain the reasoning each position gives, identify the specific structural feature of the case that produces the divergence, and describe what occurs if that feature is removed — for example, if the subject had never developed echolocation-based spatial mapping. Furthermore, please address how a defender of the predictive-processing view might reply to an empiricist who argues that the observed variability is merely noise attributable to insufficient learning time rather than a genuine feature of precision-weighted inference.

Please present your response in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or specific textual citations.
