I've been thinking about the debates over statues and street names not from any formal study, but from observing disagreements in my own town and trying to organize my own view. I have developed a small framework that divides commemorations into three social functions, and I have applied it to two cases, but I am unsure whether the reasoning holds together.

My framework is this. Commemorations are not all doing the same kind of work. Some are pedagogical markers: they exist mainly to teach a specific historical story, and they can carry context with them. Others are aspirational icons: they present a figure as an embodiment of values the community claims to uphold. Still others are territorial markers: they signal whose history and identity are treated as the default owners of a shared public space, regardless of any explicit narrative attached.

With this in mind, I consider two hypothetical objects in a town square. The first is a statue of a local industrialist who funded a major hospital but also organized violent suppression of a workers' strike. I think this statue should remain only if it is actively converted into a pedagogical marker—for instance, by placing an explicit, detailed account of both the hospital funding and the strike violence in direct physical relation to the statue—because an uncontextualized statue in a central square tends to function as an aspirational icon. The second is a street named after a widely read regional poet whose verse is still taught in schools, but who actively promoted an exclusionary nationalist ideology. Here I think renaming is required, because a street address is used daily by every resident regardless of their background, and a street name functions primarily as a territorial marker: the city is constantly reiterating that this poet belongs to its shared identity. Unlike a statue, a street name cannot easily be surrounded by counter-context, so transformation into a pedagogical object is practically impossible.

I worry that I am not applying my own framework consistently. I permit the statue to stay under conditions of reframing, but I demand the street be changed even if one were to post explanatory plaques at every corner. I tell myself this difference is justified because the ease and completeness of contextualization differs, but I have not defended why ease should determine the ethical outcome. I also wonder whether I am ignoring a distinction between active and merely symbolic harm: perhaps the statue causes direct harm to descendants of those injured in the strike in a way that the poet's street name does not, or perhaps the daily repetition of the street name causes a more diffuse but constant harm that the statue, being localized, does not. Furthermore, I have said nothing about who should decide—the affected community, the city council, or some broader democratic process—because I have focused only on the meaning of the objects themselves.

Would you comment on whether this three-part distinction—pedagogical, aspirational, and territorial—is coherent, or whether it collapses because every monument inevitably teaches something? Is my different treatment of the statue and the street ethically justified, or am I relying on an unstated assumption about contextualization that needs defense? And is there an important ethical dimension—perhaps about democratic decision-making, or about the difference between historical harm and present exclusion—that my framework misses because it treats commemorations mainly as carriers of meaning rather than as social practices?
