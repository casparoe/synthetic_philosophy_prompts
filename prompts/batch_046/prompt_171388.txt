I serve on my university's Committee on Interdisciplinary Research Standards. A joint proposal from the Department of Philosophy and the School of Environmental Studies asks the university to adopt a mandatory Many-Sided Reporting Protocol (MSRP) for all funded interdisciplinary projects that address contested empirical claims—such as climate-adaptation pathways, public-health trade-offs, or economic-forecasting models. Drawing on Jain anekāntavāda, specifically syādvāda (conditional predication) and nayavāda (partial standpoints or perspectives), the protocol requires that every final report present at least three non-contradictory descriptions (nayas) of the central claim, each qualified by syāt (rendered here as in-some-respect or perhaps), and explicitly prohibit the university from endorsing any single description as its definitive institutional position. The proponents further propose a novel Contextual Validity Index that weights each naya not solely by evidential strength but by the stakeholder proximity of the knowledge community holding it—so that, for example, a localized hydrological model and a global circulation model can carry different institutional weights without being ranked absolutely true or false. Opponents, primarily from the natural sciences and the legal-compliance office, argue that this institutionalizes contradiction, exposes the university to liability when contradictory recommendations are cited in policy briefs, and substitutes a metaphysical framework for empirical resolution. Draft the committee's decision memo—recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions—with a clear bottom line and an actionable time frame for implementation or revision. The memo should treat anekāntavāda as a serious epistemological position rather than a decorative multicultural reference: explain why syādvāda might protect against dogmatic overreach without collapsing into relativism, engage honestly with the strongest opposing consideration (likely the liability and scientific-transparency objection), and specify what conditions—if any—would make the protocol acceptable, such as restricting it to explicitly normative or predictive domains rather than purely descriptive ones. The reasoning should be formal enough to circulate to the Senate and concrete enough that the proponents can revise their proposal in direct response.
