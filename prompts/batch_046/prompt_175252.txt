I've been thinking that Arendt is mostly right about political lying being destructive not because it violates some duty to truth, but because it erodes the shared factual ground that makes collective action possible — but I want to push this further and say the real damage is something like mnemonic drift: once a lie gets baked into archieves, monuments, and school curricla, it stops being a false belief people hold and becomes a false script people perform, and that performative layer is way harder to correct than simple misinformation. So my view is that the harm of political lying is mainly this structural misalignement between official memory and lived experience, not the individual epistemic harm.

What would count against this? I'm not after the usual people-need-accurate-beliefs-to-coordinate line — I want defeaters that target the performative/mnemonic part specifically. Which historical cases or empirical findings show that embedded lies just dissolve without leaving structural residue? Are there arguments from collective memory research or institutional epistemology showing that official narratives are always already performative, so my distinction between belief-based and script-based harm collapses? Also, does this view risk being insulated into unfalsifiability — if every damaging lie eventually gets institutionalized, then of course it produces mnemonic drift, but if it doesn't, I can just claim it wasn't the harmful kind? I'd like the counterarguments ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including what defenders of this view would themselves concede, and an honest call on whether the insulation problem is actually fatal for a view of this kind. Maybe mention if there's anything in Arendt's own texts about the persistence of lies that I should be worrying about.
