In the literature on peer disagreement—conciliationism versus steadfastness—I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure that I cannot locate precisely within the standard taxonomy. I will describe it through three concrete cases, then ask what, if anything, it is called.

Case A: Two climatologists with identical training and access to data disagree about equilibrium climate sensitivity—one estimates 2.8°C, the other 4.2°C. Most philosophers I read treat this as a straightforward instance of peer disagreement and ask whether each should lower confidence.

Case B: Two normative ethicists with identical logical training disagree about whether act-utilitarianism is correct. Here, philosophers often say conciliation is inappropriate, because the disagreement concerns values rather than empirical facts, and there is no independent metric by which to split the difference.

Case C—this is the one that puzzles me: Two methodologists disagree about whether frequentist or Bayesian inference should govern the evaluation of a clinical trial. They share statistical training and data. One party, when pressed to conciliate, does not defend her first-order statistical judgment. Instead, she recharacterizes the dispute as being about the very norms of evidential reasoning—claiming that because they disagree about what counts as a decisive error, they are not peers in the relevant sense, and therefore conciliationism does not apply at all. Meanwhile, in Case B, the same philosopher who blocked conciliation in Case C now insists that the ethicists are genuine peers and should split the difference, arguing that normative disagreement is just disagreement about weights assigned to shared values.

The pattern I see is not ordinary steadfastness, which appeals to first-order reasons for holding firm. Nor is it a genuine meta-disagreement about epistemic norms, because the framework-level claim is introduced selectively: it is invoked precisely when conciliation would be costly to the speaker's object-level view, and withdrawn when it is not. The speaker treats the boundary between object-level peer disagreement and framework-level incommensurability as movable according to strategic need.

My questions: Is there an established term for this selective elevation of a disagreement to the meta-level in order to insulate an object-level position from conciliation? If not, how should it be distinguished from (i) ordinary steadfastness, (ii) genuine disagreement about epistemic norms, and (iii) the motte-and-bailey move, given that the framework claim and the object-level claim are not a defensible core and an indefensible extension but rather two independent commitments deployed situationally? And if there is no established concept, what would be the precise conditions for applying it—specifically, how do we tell the difference between a legitimate appeal to framework disagreement and this insulating maneuver?
