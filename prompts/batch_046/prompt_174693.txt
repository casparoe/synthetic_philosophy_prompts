Consider an epistemologist whose verdicts on testimonial knowledge are jointly consistent with both reductionist and anti-reductionist frameworks. Her judgments are these: she denies that a hearer knows a historical claim solely on the word of one highly reliable expert; she grants knowledge when independent sources within a shared disciplinary framework converge on the same claim; she denies knowledge when acceptance rests purely on institutional authority, with the hearer grasping nothing of the evidential basis; and she grants knowledge when the hearer can articulate a minimal defeasibility condition, but denies it when the testimony is treated as unrevisable. Now consider this case: during an operation, a surgical resident accepts a senior surgeon's authoritative identification of a tissue sample as malignant. The resident has not examined the slide herself, has no immediate access to an independent pathologist, and cannot specify the histological feature that distinguishes this sample from benign analogs—though she knows the surgeon's track record is excellent and recognizes that she could, in principle, request a second opinion after the procedure. Predict whether she would say the resident knows. Identify which of her prior verdicts is the closest analog, say whether her track record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, and if so, specify the principle she would be adopting with each choice—for example, a reductionist requirement that the hearer possess some individual evidential access versus an anti-reductionist allowance for authoritative division of cognitive labor. Finally, propose one further case that would force her to choose between those underlying rules.
