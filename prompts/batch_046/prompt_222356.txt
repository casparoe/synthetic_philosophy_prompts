You are grading a graduate-level exam in philosophy of science. The exam poses the following two-part question.

Part A: Nelson Goodman’s “new riddle of induction” is often described as a transformation rather than a direct solution to Hume’s problem. Explain precisely what Goodman transforms the problem into, clarifying the distinction between justifying induction in general and selecting among competing inductive hypotheses. Then explain the function of “entrenchment” in Goodman’s account, being careful to distinguish it both from mere psychological habit and from syntactic simplicity of the predicate itself.

Part B: Consider a predicate “zind” defined as follows: an emerald is zind at observation time t if and only if, in case it was mined north of the equator, it is green when t is before 2040 and blue when t is after 2040; if mined south of the equator, it is green at all t regardless of date. A student argues that a mining cooperative operating exclusively north of the equator has, in its entire history, applied “zind” only to green emeralds, so “zind” is as entrenched in that cooperative’s practice as “green” is in broader scientific practice. From this, the student concludes that for the cooperative’s future predictions there is no rational or inductive basis to prefer “green” over “zind,” and therefore that Goodman’s appeal to entrenchment fails to resolve the riddle for localized linguistic communities. Identify the two most serious errors in this reasoning, and explain whether either error actually undermines Goodman’s broader claim that entrenchment distinguishes projectible predicates.

Here is the student’s answer, presented in full for evaluation.

Goodman basically agrees with Hume that we can never rationally justify induction, but he shifts the question to which predicates we actually use. The answer is entrenchment: a predicate is projectible if it has been used in lots of past successful predictions. “Green” is entrenched globally because everyone has used it; “grue” is not because nobody uses it. For the cooperative in Part B, since they only mine north of the equator, they have only ever seen green emeralds described as zind. That means “zind” has exactly the same entrenchment history for them that “green” has for us. So within that community both predicates are equally projectible, and Goodman cannot say they should prefer “green” unless he smuggles in global linguistic norms that his own theory rejects. The mistake people make is thinking entrenchment depends on the logical form of the predicate, but Goodman shows it depends only on usage history, so syntactic complexity is completely irrelevant. Because the cooperative’s usage tracks green perfectly within their domain, there is no inductive difference between predicting “all future northern emeralds are green” and predicting “all future northern emeralds are zind.”
