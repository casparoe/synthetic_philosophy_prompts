In several jurisdictions, defense attorneys may introduce neuroimaging evidence showing that a defendant's action was preceded by pre-conscious neural initiation—of the kind studied in Libet's readiness-potential experiments—to argue for diminished culpability, yet courts systematically reject or exclude the same category of evidence when it might suggest heightened rather than reduced responsibility, such as neural patterns interpreted as stronger unconscious predispositions toward harm. What implicit theory of moral agency, control, and causal relevance does this asymmetrical institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the underlying view: does it hold that unconscious neural determination undermines freedom selectively, or that responsibility tracks something independent of causal origin? Assess whether the practice is philosophically coherent, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and describe how sentencing procedures would need to change if replaced by a uniform view—whether that view treats all pre-conscious neural determination as exculpatory or treats none of it as relevant to blame.
