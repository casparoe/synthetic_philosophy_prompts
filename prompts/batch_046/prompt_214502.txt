The argument about Chesterton's fence in institutional ethics has reached, in my view, a peculiar stasis. The Burkean conservative holds that inherited practices—procedural rituals, unwritten norms of deliberation, the layered architecture of common-law doctrine—carry a form of practical knowledge that exceeds any explicit justification we could now construct; removing them before understanding why they stand is therefore epistemically reckless. The opposing camp—call them the reconstructivists—grants that practices have functions, but insists that a function is only a reason to preserve something if it can be articulated in the normative vocabulary available to present agents; otherwise we are merely deferring to historical accident under the guise of wisdom. Both camps discuss the same examples—the restructuring of the UK Lord Chancellor's judicial functions, the replacement of jury trials with bench trials in certain jurisdictions, the codification of unwritten constitutional conventions—yet no exchange has moved anyone. I suspect the deadlock is conceptual rather than empirical: the sides do not disagree about what institutions do, but about whether an institution's "doing" can be separated from "being justified in terms that present agents explicitly accept." Is that diagnosis right, or is there a substantive disagreement hidden beneath—perhaps about the actual cognitive limits of institutional designers, about which cases count as data for institutional wisdom, or about bedrock intuitions regarding authority and change?
