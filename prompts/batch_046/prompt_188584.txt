I have been auditing a seminar series led by an environmental historian—let us call her Dr. Voss—who rejects the undifferentiated label “collapse” and instead classifies past societies as either undergoing discontinuous failure or reconfiguration with continuity. So far she has delivered four verdicts that I am trying to systematize without reducing them to a single slogan. For the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean (c. 1177 BCE) she rules discontinuous failure: Linear B administration, palace redistribution economies, and long-range bronze trade vanish abruptly, and the intergenerational transmission of scribal and bureaucratic expertise appears to break entirely. For the Classic Maya lowlands (c. 800–900 CE) she insists on reconfiguration: despite the abandonment of southern ceremonial centers and a marked demographic contraction, hieroglyphic and calendrical traditions persist in the north, maize agriculture continues, and trade networks reroute rather than dissolve. For Rapa Nui she returns to discontinuous failure: the competitive moai-building hierarchy and the long-distance navigation knowledge that sustained it are lost, the population crashes sharply, and the old social order leaves no functional successor institution. For the Indus Valley de-urbanization (c. 1900–1300 BCE) she argues for reconfiguration: standardized urban planning and weights-and-measures fade, yet rural agricultural and craft practices continue with little demographic rupture, suggesting a decentralized adaptation rather than a systemic crash.

She has not yet addressed the Norse Greenland settlements—the Eastern and Western Settlements abandoned by the fifteenth century—and I want to predict whether she will classify them as discontinuous failure or reconfiguration. More importantly, I want to identify the implicit rule she is running, because two interpretations seem equally consistent with her four judgments. One interpretation, call it the Institutional Memory Rule, holds that failure occurs precisely when high-complexity, specialized knowledge—writing, navigation, centralized legal codes—is permanently lost without local equivalents. The other, call it the Integration-Decoupling Rule, holds that failure occurs when a society’s central political-ecological coupling mechanism—palace redistribution, competitive chiefdom prestige economies, trans-regional trade dependencies—disintegrates rapidly and cannot be replaced by smaller-scale substitutes, regardless of whether fragments of knowledge survive. These rules diverge on Greenland: under the Institutional Memory Rule, the erosion of Latin ecclesiastical literacy and European pastoral techniques in an isolated setting might signal failure, yet the persistence of oral Norse traditions and possible Inuit contact might point to continuity; under the Integration-Decoupling Rule, the severing of the Norwegian trade lifeline and the collapse of the pastoral-church integration mechanism point clearly to decoupling, hence failure. Which prediction best coheres with her track record? And what further hypothetical case—perhaps a society that retains its writing system but loses its trade network, or one that loses its writing but preserves its exchange relationships—would most cleanly separate the two rules? I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I am asking for the conceptual anatomy of her implicit taxonomy and the precise point at which it would split.
