The discussion made me think immediately of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, which I've been re-reading for personal interest rather than coursework, and I found myself wondering whether Victor Frankenstein's secretive experiments constitute exactly the kind of pseudoscientific practice Popper describes, or whether the novel actually complicates the demarcation by showing that a research programme can be technically unfalsified yet ethically and practically bankrupt. I'm trying to work through this for my own essay, not for any grade, since the topic has become unexpectedly personal: my background is in biology, and the novel's portrayal of empirical ambition without peer accountability feels uncomfortably close to questions I face about transparency in my own field.

Specifically, I want a reading that applies both Popper's falsificationism and Lakatos's framework of research programmes—particularly the distinction between progressive and degenerating programmes—to Shelley's narrative of Victor's creation and abandonment of the creature. Victor claims an empirical breakthrough, yet he refuses to articulate risky predictions, suppresses replication, and interprets the creature's violence through improvised moral explanations rather than allowing his core theory about animating dead matter to face direct testing. Does this make his work pseudoscience by Popper's lights, or does Lakatos's concept of a degenerating programme, with its ever-expanding protective belt of excuses, capture Victor's retreat into isolation more precisely? At the same time, the novel frames his failure as hubris and abandonment rather than purely epistemic error. Where does the philosophical lens illuminate Victor's refusal to share methods, his destruction of the unfinished female creature as a refusal to extend the programme, and his final pursuit of the creature into the Arctic? Where does it distort the text's concern with responsibility, sympathy, and the limits of human knowledge?

I'm not asking for citations or a reading list—I am working away from my university library and want to avoid guessing at sources. Rather, I want an analysis that takes both the philosophy and the novel seriously: what the work shows, dramatizes, or complicates about falsificationism that pure argument misses, and where an honest critic must admit the framework runs out of explanatory power. Does Shelley inadvertently vindicate Popper by dramatizing the dangers of an unfalsifiable, immunized science, or does she suggest that the demarcation problem itself misses the moral dimensions of scientific failure?
