I was at a philosophy of mind meet-up last week and someone dropped the line "the self is just a convenient fiction, not a controller" to shut down a talk about whether an AI could ever have a first-person perspective. They said it meant there's no inner homunculus directing thoughts, just predictive processing loops and language games, so asking "who experiences" is a catagory error. But I think that slogan is smuggling in about four different theses and I'm trying to map them.

Can you separate the precise claims hiding in "the self is just a convenient fiction, not a controller"? I want a menu, not one answer. At least: (a) there's no Cartesian ego-substance, only skandhas or brain-states; (b) the pronoun "I" is a grammatical placeholder with no referent, like "it" in "it rains"; (c) there's a minimal phenomenological self that experiences, but the narrative "controller" self is a post-hock fiction with no causal power; (d) experience is centerlessly integrated — there is awareness but no owner, like light without a lamp — which would make "who experiences enlightenment" literally unaskable; or (e) the whole claim is therapeutic, about reducing ego-clinging, with zero ontological commitment. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, defensible, or flat false, and specifically whether it supports dismissing questions about who experiences or whether AI could be that subject. The meet-up guy seemed to slide from "no controller" to "no subject whatsoever," and I want to know if that's a legit entailment from Buddhist anatta or just a bait-and-switch. Lay it out like a checklist I can actually bring back.
