I'm taking an advanced seminar on comparative memory politics and transitional justice, and I'm trying to work out where conceptual research in this space should actually go rather than just cataloguing national differences. The four cases we keep returning to are Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan's divided institutional and societal approaches to its wartime past, Turkey's official denial of the Armenian genocide alongside local and civil-society archival work, and the American South's recent shift away from Lost Cause monumentalization toward removal and counter-memorial practice. Most existing comparison either applies a uniform transitional-justice framework or describes policy divergence without explaining what conceptual work the comparison is doing.

I want to focus on a specific problem that seems underdeveloped: the dynamics of what we might call nested mnemonic obligations—when a society's official and unofficial efforts to address one historical injustice generate structural, but not automatically legitimate, pressure to address structurally analogous but politically and causally distinct injustices. In Germany, intensive Holocaust remembrance coexists with far more ambivalent public engagement with colonial violence in Namibia; in Japan, state-level acknowledgment of the atomic bombings and some wartime suffering often operates alongside suppression of comfort-women and forced-labor narratives; in Turkey, state denial of the Armenian genocide exists alongside Kurdish and Alevi memory activism that uses analogous evidentiary methods but receives radically different state responses; and in the American South, the recent dismantling of Confederate monuments has reopened, but not resolved, questions about indigenous dispossession and slavery's differential memorialization.

Given a researcher with perhaps two years and limited primary archival access—so the work must be conceptual and comparative rather than deeply archival—which open questions in this space are most worth prioritizing, and why?

First, is there a defensible principle that distinguishes legitimate mnemonic differentiation (treating different injustices differently because of genuinely distinct causal structures, victim groups, or state responsibilities) from illegitimate selective amnesia? Can such a principle be articulated without presupposing a fully universal theory of historical justice, and what would it imply for the four cases?

Second, how should comparative work weigh vertical, state-led memory regimes against horizontal, subnational, or civil-society practices—particularly in federal or decentralized systems such as Germany's Länder-level education policies, Japan's prefectural museums and local textbook controversies, U.S. state-level monument governance, or Turkey's provincial archives? Is the real conceptual action at the national level, or does comparing subnational variation reveal something that national narratives obscure?

Third, does the comparative study of these four cases support a useful concept of reparative time—the idea that societies adopt structurally different temporal orientations toward the past, such as working-through, strategic forgetting, active denial, or perpetual monumentalization—or is this mostly an academic taxonomy with little payoff for understanding policy change or political conflict?
