Having spent the bulk of my career in experimental physics studying open thermodynamic systems, I have recently turned to philosophy of metaphysics and find myself drawn repeatedly to the statue-and-clay problem of material constitution, though I wish to work through it as a conceptual exercise rather than as a historical survey. I am particularly struck by an intuition drawn from my former field: macroscopic artifacts, like physical bodies, rarely if ever maintain genuinely static part-composition, since atomic exchange with the environment occurs continuously and form is preserved through dynamic equilibrium rather than through the enduring presence of particular constituent particles. If we treat the clay not as a fixed lump but as a slowly turning-over region of matter—maintained in a stable pattern analogously to biological homeostasis—what are the most important implications for the classic claim that the statue and the clay coincide spatially yet differ in their modal properties and survival conditions? I want you to consider whether this "dynamic maintenance" framing weakens the standard argument that differing persistence conditions prove non-identity, or whether it instead pushes us toward a relational or process-based ontology in which artifacts are not enduring substances but temporarily stabilized flows of material. Please avoid offering bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke highly technical formal apparatus or obscure named doctrines; instead, develop the reasoning from the conceptual structure of the problem itself, engaging seriously with both the metaphysical options and the physical intuition that material constitution is almost never genuinely static.
