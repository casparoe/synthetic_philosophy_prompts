I am preparing notes for a graduate seminar on early modern metaphysics and have found myself stuck on a particular step in Berkeley’s master argument, specifically the exchange in which Philonous presses Hylas about the conceivability of an object existing without being perceived. In the passage I am working with—drawn from the first dialogue between the two characters—Philonous asks whether Hylas can form the idea of a tree standing in a garden with no perceiver present. Hylas concedes that he can form such an idea, but Philonous responds that in forming it Hylas is himself perceiving an idea in his own mind; since the tree is nothing over and above an idea, it is therefore present to a mind after all. Hylas tries to distinguish between the idea he presently entertains and the tree’s supposed independent existence outside all minds, yet Philonous insists that whatever is conceived must exist as an idea in the conceiver’s mind, and hence cannot be genuinely unperceived.

What makes this difficult for me is a conceptual distinction I have been trying to apply, one that distinguishes between counterfactual phenomenal presence—the representation of an object as available to be perceived—and occurrent phenomenal presence, in which an object is actually given in present consciousness. I want to know whether Berkeley’s reasoning tacitly collapses these two modes, or whether it only establishes that any representation requires an occurrent idea in the representing mind, leaving untouched the counterfactual status of the object represented. More precisely, does the argument depend on an unstated occurrentist premise about mental representation—namely, that every act of conception is itself an episode of actual perception—or can it be reconstructed using only the thinner claim that ideas depend for their existence on being perceived by some mind, without specifying whether that dependence must be temporally immediate? If the latter, then Hylas might reply that he is indeed having an occurrent idea of the tree, but that this idea represents the tree in a counterfactual state of being unperceived, and I cannot tell from the text whether Philonous has already blocked that reply or whether he needs an additional premise to do so. Could you help me understand exactly which move in the passage eliminates the counterfactual option, and whether that move is doing more philosophical work than Berkeley makes explicit?
