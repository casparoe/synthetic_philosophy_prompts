You are a philosopher with expertise in early modern metaphysics and political economy, and also a careful literary critic. Before answering, take a few minutes to think step by step: list the major scenes in The Merchant of Venice that turn on contracts, debt, and the substitution of one value for another; then recall Berkeley’s immaterialist account of money—especially his claim that money possesses no intrinsic value but operates as a circulating sign or counter for labor and goods, together with his argument that money should be barren and that interest-bearing loans violate this nature by making value appear to generate itself ex nihilo. Now read the play through this framework. Be precise about scenes, but do not treat the play as a mere illustration: show where the drama resists the philosophical reading—for example, whether Shylock’s human suffering and the play’s sympathy for him undercut Berkeley’s confidence that clarifying money’s immaterial nature resolves moral conflict.
