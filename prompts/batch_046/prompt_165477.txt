I'm giving the opening remarks next tuesday at a small workshop we're running at the local historical socity — it's called "Republics in Slow Motion" and the room will be about half historians, half local activists, plus a couple philosophy grad students who showed up because they heard there'd be talk about democratic legitimacy. I want to use the fall of the Roman Republic as a framing analogy, but not the tired "Rome fell and so might we" line everyone has heard a million times. Instead I want to focus on this specific structural pattern: in the late republic, the Senate never technically abolished itself or lost its legal authority, but it gradually lost the ability to actually decide things because magistrates kept using procedural extensions and emergency mandates to hold onto armies and provinces, and each individual extension was legally defensible but together they meant real decision-making had moved outside the constitutional framework while the old institutions kept performing their rituals. I think there's a parallel to modern democracies where formal democratic institutions stay intact — elections, courts, legislatures — but decision-making power slowly migrates to administrative bodies, private contractors, or emergency frameworks, leaving citizens with the feeling that they're still particpating in self-rule even though the actual levers have moved. The philosophical point should do real structural work: if this analogy holds, then the problem isn't just "bad leaders" or "low voter turnout" — it's a kind of institutional ventriloquism where the voice of popular sovereignty is still heard but the mouth has changed. So the speech needs to avoid easy prescriptions like "vote more" or "elect better people" because those miss the structural issue, but it also can't be pure doom-mongering since the whole point is that the institutions are still there, just hollowed out. It needs to land for the activists without losing the historians, and especially not patronize the philosophy students.
