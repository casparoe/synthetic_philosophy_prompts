I serve on the University Committee on Honorary Distinctions and Institutional Naming, and I need a draft decision memo for circulation before our March session. A senior colleague in philosophy has submitted a formal petition asking the committee to adopt what she terms an Anscombe principle governing all future honorary degrees, named lectureships, and building designations: specifically, that no person should receive institutional honors if they authorized state actions in which the death of non-combatants was intended as a means to a strategic end, regardless of whether those deaths were also foreseen as side effects. She grounds this in G. E. M. Anscombe's well-known argument regarding Harry Truman's authorization of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, contending that Truman directed his will toward the killing of innocent civilians as a means of compelling surrender, which makes the act murder rather than permissible collateral damage under double-effect reasoning. The petition further requests that we apply this principle retroactively to evaluate an existing named lecture series honoring a mid-twentieth-century statesman whose strategic record includes comparable controversial decisions about civilian-targeting campaigns.

The committee is divided. Some members argue that institutional honors are not moral endorsements of every decision a figure made, and that adopting such a principle would require us to examine the intentions behind every historical statesman's actions with a precision we cannot achieve, likely forcing the renunciation of honors for figures whose moral records are far more ambiguous than Truman's alone. Others insist that naming is itself an act of institutional judgment, and that if we reject the distinction between directing one's choice at innocent death and merely allowing it, we implicitly endorse a form of consequentialism that collapses the moral difference between strategic bombing and targeted assassination.

Please draft the committee's written opinion recommending either adoption, rejection, or conditional adoption of the principle, with a concrete proposal for how it should apply—prospectively only, or with a defined retrospective review process—and a clear time frame for implementation. The memo should treat the philosophical coherence of the intended-versus-foreseen distinction seriously, address the strongest practical objection about consistency and historical ambiguity, and avoid reducing the dispute to a general debate about pacifism or consequentialism. It should be a document the committee could actually vote on, with an actionable bottom line rather than an open-ended philosophical reflection.
