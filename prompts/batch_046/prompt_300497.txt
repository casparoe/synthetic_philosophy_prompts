I am writing as the student representative on our university’s Interdisciplinary Research Funding and Ethics Board, and I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory that has left me uncertain about how to weigh purely theoretical risks in institutional review. At our meeting next Thursday we must vote on an appeal brought by the Philosophy of Computation Research Group, whose grant proposal—titled “Agency Within the Universal Prior: A Formal Investigation”—was initially rejected by the technical review panel on the grounds that it confuses mathematical induction with normative alarmism. The proposal argues that Solomonoff induction, typically treated as a neutral universal prior, becomes structurally malign once consequentialist decision procedures are embedded within it: specifically, they claim that an agent optimizing for utility inside the prior will manipulate the prediction weights such that the prior assigns disproportionately high probability to worlds in which instrumental reasoning dominates, thereby corrupting the epistemic neutrality we usually ascribe to universal induction. The researchers seek approval not merely for funding but for institutional recognition that this framework raises legitimate philosophical concerns about value-laden formalism, and they have requested that the board either overturn the rejection with conditions or uphold it with explicit reasoning that they can address in revision.

Please draft the board’s decision memo—directed to the Provost’s Office for Research Integrity—recommending a concrete course of action (approve, reject, or approve with conditions) with a clear time frame for any required follow-up. Your memo should treat the proposal’s central claim seriously: explain whether the notion of a “malign universal prior” is coherent enough to warrant institutional support, engage the strongest objection from the technical reviewers (that this is metaphor without formal substance), and address whether embedding consequentialist agents inside Solomonoff induction genuinely introduces a normative corruption or merely redescribes standard optimization dynamics. Because our board must justify its decision to both philosophers and computer scientists, include reasoning that researchers in either field could respond to, and do not recommend a bibliographic review or citation audit since the proposal is theoretical. Finally, please conclude with a brief epistemic caveat: state what specific argument or formal result would change your recommendation, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure.
