I am presenting in my graduate seminar, Concepts of Intelligence: Philosophy and Computation, a thesis I developed in my final-year philosophy coursework. My claim is that what is commonly called "the alignment problem" is not one problem but three with distinct conceptual structures, and that conflating them produces both philosophical confusion and misguided engineering priorities. Specifically, I distinguish: (1) the specification problem—how to translate an informally specified human intention into a formal objective without hidden surrogates; (2) the stability problem—how to prevent an agent's effective goals from drifting under self-modification, distributional shift, or recursive improvement; and (3) the authority problem—which human values or procedures determine the target of alignment when those values conflict, and whether that determination is itself part of the technical task. I argue that interpretability research addresses (1), robustness and corrigibility address (2), and democratic or meta-ethical theory addresses (3), and that treating them as a single "alignment" problem leads researchers to apply technical fixes to normative questions and normative debates to technical ones.

The audience is a mixed seminar of about ten: philosophy Ph.D. students who have read Bostrom's superintelligence argument and are skeptical of singularity rhetoric but take conceptual analysis seriously; computer science Ph.D. students working on RLHF and mechanistic interpretability who regard philosophical taxonomy as an obstacle to practical progress; and the seminar convener, a philosopher of science who cares about whether distinctions do explanatory work. They know the standard literature on reward hacking and instrumental convergence, but none are decision theorists or AI safety specialists.
