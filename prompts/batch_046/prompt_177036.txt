Consider a political philosopher whose judgments on voting ethics I have recorded across five cases: (1) in a safe-seat legislative race where her local candidate is certain to win by a wide margin, she holds that citizens still have a duty to vote; (2) in a referendum where a harmful measure is certain to pass regardless of her vote, she holds that citizens should still cast votes against it; (3) in an election where both viable candidates endorse policies she regards as gravely unjust, but one is clearly less harmful, she advises voting for the lesser evil; (4) in a context where voting requires public identification and credible retaliation against dissenters exists, she excuses non-participation; (5) in a party primary for which she is not a registered member, she denies any duty to register solely in order to vote.

Now a new municipal charter introduces cascading preference delegation: voters may cast a direct ranked ballot or assign their vote to a registered preference bloc that pools votes through a published algorithm. A voter who trusts Bloc A's algorithm on most issues must, to join it, publicly endorse Bloc A's charter, which includes a housing-policy position she considers unjust. All direct candidates are unacceptable to her, though one is marginally less harmful; direct votes alone are unlikely to reach the winning threshold without bloc aggregation, but assignment gives her vote real weight.

Predict her verdict on whether the voter should assign to Bloc A, vote directly for the least harmful candidate, or abstain. Identify at least two competing principles that fit her first five judgments—one treating voting as an act of sincere individual judgment and one treating it as an instrument of collective resistance—state what each predicts here, give your best-guess recommendation with the reason for preferring it, name the closest analog among cases (1)–(5), and specify the further case that would distinguish the two principles.
