Rather than comparing Gilligan's or Noddings's ideas to Kantian universalism on the page, I want a staged simulation that makes relational agency feel unavoidable: participants should discover that they are not autonomous choosers distributing goods by principle, but nodes in networks of dependency whose obligations shift with proximity, history, and emotional labor. Design a structured deliberation or crisis simulation—set in a small community facing a sudden resource shortage—where each participant holds an assigned relational role (e.g., primary caregiver for a dependent with high needs, distant acquaintance with a hidden vulnerability, institutional gatekeeper bound by protocol) and must allocate limited tokens representing both material aid and attentive presence over three rounds. The rules should be arranged so that applying a universal distributive rule produces a visible, collective breakdown—burnout, missed dependencies, or network fracture—while pure partiality to one's assigned intimate also collapses the interdependent system because hidden obligations surface late. Include specific roles, materials or token mechanics, a timeline with decision points, and a payoff structure that uses only symbolic or social stakes. Be explicit about which aspect of relational moral agency—perhaps the embodied, temporal depth of actual caring relationships—this token-based format cannot reproduce, and tell me exactly how I should supply that gap in the debrief rather than pretending the simulation captured it. Finally, note what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this—likely role confusion or participants defaulting to utilitarian calculus despite the rules—and how to intercept it without lecturing. No introductory lecture should occur; participants should learn the term "care ethics" only after they've lived through the dynamics, and the prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
