I'm writing from a course I'm taking this term on self-reference and hierarchical explanation in philosophy of science. Our readings discuss cases where a system seems to contain its own explanation or authorization at every level, leaving no untouched ground floor from which to revise the whole thing. Outside of class, I started noticing this structure in places I hadn't expected. For instance, my university's student constitution can only be amended by a constitutional convention, but the rules governing how that convention is convened and who may vote in it are themselves written into the constitution being amended. A classmate mentioned that corporate bylaws work the same way: the board modifies the bylaws, yet the bylaws specify what powers the board has to begin with. Even in programming, a compiler for a language is often written in that same language, so there is no outside version that validates the first one without already belonging to the system it validates.

I don't have the right vocabulary for this pattern. I know people sometimes invoke Escher's image of two hands sketching each other into existence as a visual metaphor for mutual dependence, and I've heard casual talk about loops that fold back through their own levels. But I'm asking about something more specific: the structural situation in which every level of authority or validation is internal to the system it governs, so that there is genuinely no level that stands outside being defined by the others. Is there an established philosophical or logical concept that captures exactly this absence of an external base? And if so, how is it distinguished from ordinary circular reasoning, an infinite regress, or a simple feedback loop? I want to understand whether this marks a real and identifiable structural feature—one that might apply equally to institutional rules, software bootstrapping, and perhaps even to the way theories justify themselves—or whether it remains only a suggestive metaphor.
