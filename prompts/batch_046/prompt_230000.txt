I've been working on a short essay sketch about Dennett's four kinds of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—and how they might apply to large language models. The central claim is that an LLM's base architecture is best understood as Gregorian: it manipulates internal representations, including representations of representations, and deploys them in flexible, context-sensitive ways that go well beyond hard-wired stimulus-response patterns. At the same time, the standard post-training pipeline—RLHF and related reward-based methods—treats the model as a Skinnerian creature, shaping behavioral outputs through reinforcement without granting the system direct access to or control over the representational structures that generated those outputs. The tension produces what I'd call an unstable hybrid: a Gregorian cognitive substrate governed by Skinnerian behavioral optimization. This might account for specific failure modes like sycophancy, adversarial mode collapse, and the tendency to shift surface-level behavioral policies rather than genuinely update internal beliefs.

I'd welcome critical comments on this framing. Specifically: is the base pretraining dynamics closer to Popperian internal selection than to Gregorian representational manipulation? Where does the RLHF-as-Skinnerian-conditioning analogy break down, given that the reward model is itself a learned representational system rather than a direct environmental contingency? And how should we distinguish between the model's internal world-model—if one exists—and its behavioral policy?

Please say what would change your mind: which argument about Dennett's taxonomy or about LLM cognition would cause you to abandon the Gregorian/Skinnerian hybrid view? And please flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail, particularly if you are taking liberties with Dennett's original definitions or with the technical details of how RLHF modifies internal representations versus output distributions.
