I'm running a philosophy-of-history grad seminar — 18 people, one 75-minute block. Design an in-class simulation that makes them feel path dependence, but specifically the tension between weak path dependence (once initial conditions are set the outcome is basically predictable) and strong path dependence (a genuinely contingent branch where a small flip sends history down a different track). Frame it as a round-based resource game: groups of 3-4 manage water rights in a made-up valley called Meridian. Each round they vote on one of three infrastructure rules — diversion, cooperative reservoir, or market auction — with payoffs in paper tokens. At round 3 inject a 'noise card' that forces one table to switch rules regardless of preference, while others stay. At round 5 allow unanimous 'reform' but the token cost scales with how many rounds they've already accumulated under the current rule. The mechanics should be arranged so that some tables converge back to the same equilibrium despite the noise (weak dependence) while others get locked into a stable but suboptimal pattern they can't escape without massive loss (strong dependence). They should feel the lock-in physically — counting tokens they can't afford to lose — before anyone names the idea.

Then plan the debrief: map round 3 onto contingency, round 5 onto accumulated-commitment lock-in, and the divergence across tables onto the weak/strong distinction. Tell me what will probaly go wrong the first run — likely students treating the noise card as unfair rather than structural, or tables colluding. And be frank about which part of the philosophical problem the game can't reproduce: specifically, the counterfactual evaluation of whether the same outcome 'would have arrived anyway' without access to the full causal graph, which you'll need to supply verbally at the end. Keep materials to paper, pens, and one deck of cards. No QWERTY, no VHS vs Betamax — too familiar. Don't include any bibliography, reading list, or citations. Just the rules, payoff structure, debrief, and failure predictions.
