I teach a philosophy of history seminar of about ten graduate students in a single 90-minute session. Design a structured deliberation—not a lecture or essay—through which participants feel the conflict among Nietzsche’s three modes of relating to the past—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—especially the point at which each mode, pursued rigorously, undermines the conditions for the others and for life itself.

Frame it as a curatorial role-play: the group serves as the board of a fictional Institute for Synthetic Ecology (1924–1971), whose founder was simultaneously a visionary ecologist and a systematic forger of field data. Divide students into three factions, each assigned one Nietzschean mode and given a confidential brief. The monumental faction must construct a heroic, usable past; the antiquarian faction must preserve every document that establishes institutional continuity; the critical faction must expose and dissolve the fraudulent foundation.

Impose a zero-sum rule: there is exactly one display case and a mnemonic budget allowing only three artifacts to survive; anything excluded is permanently destroyed. Provide specific artifacts that serve double or triple duty—for example, the founder’s original falsified field journal, which the antiquarians need for continuity, the critics need for exposure, and the monument-builders must suppress. Give each faction a hidden veto over one artifact. Allow 35 minutes of negotiation, then a binding vote. Reveal the Nietzschean labels only after the vote and the symbolic destruction of the excluded items.

In your response, include: (1) the precise rules, roles, artifact descriptions, and negotiation structure; (2) a debrief that maps each crisis in the deliberation onto the corresponding mode and its specific disadvantage for life; (3) a frank prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time—for instance, participants reducing the conflict to an ethics debate about scientific fraud rather than to the value of history for life—and how to correct it; (4) an explicit admission of which feature of Nietzsche’s argument—say, the physiological or cultural conditions under which one mode becomes necessary—the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply in the debrief.

Keep the design conceptually precise, avoid bibliographies or citations, and do not invoke obscure secondary literature.
