As a journalist covering the intersection of quantum physics and philosophy of mind, I have recently been revisiting Erwin Schrödinger’s late essays on consciousness, particularly his conviction—shaped by Advaita Vedanta—that individual awareness is not ontologically plural but rather a localized expression of a single, universal mind. In conversations with readers about this view, a persistent confusion emerges: several interlocutors assume that if consciousness is genuinely unitary in this Vedantic sense, then the very phenomenon of intersubjective disagreement—say, between Bohr and Einstein over the interpretation of quantum mechanics—must be either illusory or reducible to one mind arguing with itself. From that premise they infer that Schrödinger’s philosophy effectively dissolves the measurement problem not through decoherence or hidden variables, but by eliminating the multiplicity of observers altogether, leaving only one self-transparent consciousness whose “collapse” renders all other experimental records phenomenologically redundant. This seems to conflate a metaphysical claim about the identity of awareness with an epistemological claim about the structure of scientific knowledge. Could you clarify whether Schrödinger’s Vedantic monism actually entails that empirical disagreement is conceptually impossible, and whether he intended this unity of consciousness as a literal interpretation of quantum formalism—perhaps treating superposition as the undivided state of a single subject—or rather as a parallel metaphysical conviction that leaves the physics and its intersubjective practice entirely intact? I am especially interested in whether there is any coherent reading of his position that preserves the reality of distinct laboratory records and scientific debate without collapsing into either solipsism or a mystical denial of empirical collaboration.
