Consider Dennett's intentional stance: we treat an entity as a rational agent with beliefs and desires in order to predict its behavior, without committing to any particular internal mechanism. Now apply this framework to artificial systems that contain explicit representational structures—call them belief-registers—encoding propositions about the world, yet lack the integrated motivational or affective architecture that binds belief to action in biological agents. In such systems, belief attribution appears to split into two practices that do not clearly coincide. One is representational attribution: we identify the register encoding "P" and say the system believes P. The other is predictive stance-adoption: we forecast outputs by treating the system as if it holds beliefs that rationalize its behavior, even when the relevant registers are epiphenomenal or overridden by optimization dynamics. Explain the conceptual tension between these two modes of attribution. Is the split a genuine problem for extending the intentional stance to AI, or does it reveal that belief attribution was always disjunctive—part representational, part functional? More specifically, can a system with fully transparent belief-registers but no unified conative architecture coherently be said to possess beliefs in the sense that makes stance-adoption predictive and explanatory, or does the transparency and architectural fragmentation cause the stance to collapse into mere mechanistic description on one side and unfounded anthropomorphism on the other? Address whether the intentional stance remains a single practice when applied to such architectures, and what conditions would have to hold for belief attribution to be unified rather than bifurcated.
