I serve as the undergraduate representative on our university’s Committee on Intellectual Pluralism and Campus Expression, and we must vote by next Friday on whether to grant funding and reserved venue access to a registered student organization, the Epistemic Sovereignty Collective, for their proposed semester-long series, “Excluding the Excluders.” I am writing this from my coursework in political philosophy and philosophy of science, and I ask you to draft the formal decision memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting; it should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, deny, or approve with explicit conditions and a fixed review timeline, such as ninety days—and it should be written as a document the committee could actually act on.

The Collective’s proposal is not ordinary political advocacy. Their explicit aim is to practice what they call “framework-level intolerance”: they want participants to identify and reject specific methodological commitments—falsifiability, universalizability, and what they term “disembodied objectivity”—whenever these commitments appear in course syllabi or public university discourse, on the grounds that these commitments function as mechanisms of epistemic domination that suppress non-Western and indigenous knowledge systems. Crucially, their application states that they will not extend procedural courtesy—no shared panels, no joint publications, no speaking slots—to any student group or individual who defends the inclusion of those commitments in university life. They justify this exclusion by invoking the paradox of tolerance: they claim that allowing defenders of “hegemonic rationalism” to participate in pluralist spaces is itself an intolerant act that reinforces structural violence, and therefore that genuine tolerance requires being intolerant of such defenders.

Our expression policy protects student organizations unless they directly incite violence or discriminate against protected classes; it does not address “epistemic exclusion,” or intolerance directed at frameworks rather than at persons. The committee is divided. Some members see this as a direct application of the paradox: if we fund a group that refuses dialogue with others, we risk eroding the very conditions of pluralism. Others argue that the Collective targets harmful methodological frameworks, not people, and that treating framework-critique as equivalent to agent-directed intolerance is a category error that risks forcing marginalized students to tolerate their own epistemic erasure.

Your memo should treat the Collective’s position seriously: explain precisely why their invocation of the paradox might succeed or fail, but do not dismiss it by merely restating our legal policy. At the same time, include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest argument on the side you reject. I also want the reasoning to engage a conceptual distinction I have been developing in my coursework—between agent-directed intolerance (targeting persons) and framework-directed intolerance (targeting epistemic commitments)—and to ask whether the paradox applies symmetrically to both. Please also consider whether a recursive problem arises: if we tolerate the Collective’s intolerance on the grounds that it protects pluralism, do we undermine pluralism in a different register? The memo should avoid requesting citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and it should not depend on highly specialized literature that assumes access to niche debates in philosophy of science. Instead, it should reason through the issue as a self-contained philosophical analysis that committee members without specialist background can follow, but with sufficient conceptual precision that it does not read as generic advice about free speech.
