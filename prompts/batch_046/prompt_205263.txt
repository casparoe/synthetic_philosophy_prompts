I am auditing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on logic and metaphysics, and I need you to design a 90-minute take-home exam essay for students who have already encountered the standard literature on vagueness. To force genuine conceptual analysis, please inject the following niche framing: treat the paradox as structurally analogous to a continuous-deployment pipeline that rejects code commits whenever a static-analysis confidence score falls below a precisely configured threshold θ. The pipeline must act, yet any exact value of θ is arbitrary—shifting it by 0.001 produces no change in the code’s semantic properties—while leaving θ undefined renders the pipeline useless.

Draft one exam question that asks students whether importing this “operational threshold” model into metaphysics shows that vagueness is purely pragmatic, demonstrates that vague predicates have hidden sharp boundaries, or collapses into a contextualism that conflicts with the principle of bivalence in formal logic. The prompt should require students to construct and defend an argument, not to produce a taxonomy of positions.

Then provide the following, with explicit constraints:
- The exam question itself, aimed at upper-level undergraduates, with an expected answer length of roughly 1,200 words and a 90-minute time limit.
- A model answer of about 800–1,000 words, organized around five specific philosophical moves that a top-scoring response must make.
- A marking scheme totaling 20 marks, allocating specific marks to each of the five moves, plus a one-line note on the characteristic misunderstanding or omission that should cost marks for each move.
- Specify that no bibliography, no author citations, and no reading lists are required or permitted; students must reason from the framing and the conceptual structure alone.
- Ensure the difficulty comes from identifying the crux: a candidate who has only memorized theory names should be unable to score more than about eight marks.

Make the prompt formal and polished, but preserve the voice of someone without philosophical training who is nevertheless precise about system-design analogies. Do not include multiple-choice items.
