I serve as a clinical ethics mediator in a hospital system and must classify, several times monthly, whether staff disputes present genuine moral dilemmas in the strong philosophical sense—where two obligations remain genuinely binding after decision, producing unavoidable moral residue—or merely apparent conflicts resolvable by ordering, scope clarification, or further evidence. Write a reusable procedure my team can run in about twenty minutes: ordered questions with criteria for a sufficient answer at each step, explicit stop points yielding a negative classification, and clearly marked judgment calls that cannot be proceduralized. Ground the distinctions in the actual philosophical debate—prima facie versus absolute obligations, epistemic versus structural conflict, value incommensurability, and the presence or absence of moral residue—without requiring theoretical training. Apply the procedure to three cases: a Jehovah’s Witness refusing transfusion for a minor child; a triage protocol directing withdrawal of a ventilator from Patient A (admitted first, equal clinical priority) to save Patient B; and a staff member’s religious objection to administering a legal standard-of-care drug that conflicts with an institutional mandate. The second case should yield a negative classification despite staff intuition, and you should defend that result and note what would overturn it. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot settle, where it is likely to misfire, and what a practitioner must supply that the checklist cannot.
