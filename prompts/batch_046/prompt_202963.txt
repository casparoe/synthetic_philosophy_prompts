For a graduate seminar in legal philosophy or political theory—twelve to fourteen students, ninety minutes—design a structured deliberation with assigned roles and hidden payoffs that makes participants feel the conceptual pressure of the Hart-Devlin debate on legal moralism before either name is spoken. Invent and use an unfamiliar but concrete scenario: algorithmic mourning, a practice in which people maintain persistent, interactive AI simulations of deceased loved ones. The scenario should be built so that it produces widespread moral revulsion and is said by sociologists to erode shared ritual structures that maintain social trust, yet it causes no direct physical or psychological harm to identifiable victims and involves fully consenting participants.

Divide the group into three rotating roles. Legislative Drafters must write a penal statute using only the justificatory vocabulary assigned to them on a confidential brief—half receiving a harm-based framework that permits criminalization only for tangible injury or coercion, half receiving a social-cohesion framework that permits it when a practice threatens the shared moral fabric that binds the community together. Affected Citizens live through the scenario via short first-person vignettes: one experiencing the comfort of the practice, another experiencing exclusion and shame within their religious community because they refuse it, a third observing the gradual disappearance of communal funeral rituals. Public-Reason Auditors hold veto power over any statute that relies on contested metaphysical claims about proper grief or the good life, forcing drafters to translate their justifications into terms acceptable across deep disagreement.

Structure the rules so that participants construct the argument sequence themselves. In round one, drafters propose statutes for algorithmic mourning; auditors strike down cohesion-based drafts unless they can be reframed without appeal to controversial comprehensive doctrines. In round two, the class learns that tolerance has prevailed: the practice remains legal. At that point, apply a hidden payoff—the class physically splits into two non-communicating subgroups for ten minutes, each using only internal vocabulary, to simulate the social fracture that the cohesion framework predicts. In round three, drafters must defend either the split or the suppression of the practice, now knowing that suppression requires overriding the audit and accepting the fracture-prevention rationale.

The deliverable should include: the role cards with explicit voting and veto rules and the specific penalties or rewards tied to them; the three escalating vignettes that move from private revulsion to institutional erosion; and a debrief that maps round one onto the harm principle, round two onto the public-reason constraint, and round three onto the disintegration thesis. The debrief should predict the predictable failure mode—participants will conflate visceral disgust with demonstrable harm—and explain how the facilitator should handle it without resolving the debate. It should also be frank about which feature of the cohesion view, the empirical claim that societies actually disintegrate when shared moral boundaries erode, cannot be reproduced in a classroom simulation and must be supplied directly by the facilitator. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid framing the exercise through the historically overused examples; the aim is to make the conceptual architecture of legal moralism felt through an unfamiliar case.
