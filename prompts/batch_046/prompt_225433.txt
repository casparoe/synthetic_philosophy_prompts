I'm preparing a conceptual paper on institutional misalignment, and in discussions with colleagues I keep finding that Goodhart's law is treated as a self-evident explanation for any policy failure, while the more precise literature on specification gaming—especially the distinction between metric corruption and reward hacking in organizational settings—is rarely applied with real discipline to actual cases. I want to correct that in my own thinking by working through a single, well-documented episode in detail: the introduction of the four-hour emergency-department waiting-time target in the UK NHS around 2004, and the subsequent institutional responses, including ambulance queuing outside hospitals, the creation of temporary observation wards, and adjustments to clock-stopping protocols.

Please analyze this episode through both lenses—Goodhart's law in its canonical form, and the specification-gaming framework that asks whether agents are exploiting a poorly specified reward function rather than merely reacting to a distorted metric. Map the actors precisely (trust managers, clinical staff, regulators), specify what the measure was, what it became a target of, and what the payoff structure actually incentivized once funding and performance ratings were tied to the metric. Then identify which observed behaviors count as genuine specification gaming and which are better described as strategic adaptation or organizational learning, and say where the theory is silent—did it predict the specific form the gaming took, or only its existence? Crucially, do not stop at a balanced survey of interpretations: take a committed position on whether this case confirms Goodhart's law in a strong explanatory form, reveals that the law needs an auxiliary theory about organizational culture to do real work, or actually undermines the law by showing that culture can suppress metric corruption despite intense targets. Defend your verdict with reasons drawn from the institutional details, and be explicit about any point where you think the model has nothing useful to say.
