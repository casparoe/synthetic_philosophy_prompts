I'm currently enrolled in an advanced undergraduate seminar on the metaphysics of social categories, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about seven minutes—at an interdisciplinary panel our university is hosting next Thursday titled "What Holds Political Sides Together?" The audience will be split: on one side, philosophy and political theory students who have read the standard literature on ideological taxonomy and are skeptical of any claim that "left" and "right" name anything real; on the other, staff, local journalists, and community organizers who came to talk about polarization but have no patience for academic taxonomy debates. I want the speech to turn structurally on a specific, somewhat unconventional thesis: that the left-right distinction is real, but not because the two sides hold different values or policies—rather, because they operate with incompatible forms of institutional justification. Specifically, I want to propose that left justifications are "recursive"—they must include an account of why the speaker is entitled to make the demand—while right justifications are "lineal"—they appeal to an unbroken chain from an origin or a final purpose. This difference in the form of warrant should determine what I can and cannot say: for instance, why dialogue between the sides often fails at the level of question rather than answer, why common ground is impossible at the level of justification even when outcomes overlap, and whether there is any meaningful sense in which the distinction could dissolve without either side abandoning its basic logical structure. The speech must avoid naming specific theorists or texts, must not request a bibliography or further reading, and must land for both the specialists and the non-academic half of the room without sounding like a lecture disguised as an address. Can you write these remarks for me?
