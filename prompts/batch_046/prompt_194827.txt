I have been working through Wittgenstein's remarks on language games and family resemblance in a small reading group, and we have reached an impasse that feels less like a clash over evidence and more like a failure to identify what kind of disagreement we are having. One faction treats family resemblance as a positive, if loose, ontological structure: categories such as games are held together not by a shared essence but by a criss-crossing network of overlapping similarities, and philosophical explanation should map that network without reducing it to necessary and sufficient conditions. The opposing faction insists that family resemblance is purely therapeutic—Wittgenstein is not substituting one taxonomy for another but dissolving the craving for essentialist analysis altogether, so that asking what really binds the members of a category together is already to misunderstand the point. Both sides invoke the same analogies—the rope composed of overlapping fibers, the diverse practices we call games—and both concede that essentialism is untenable; yet after sustained discussion we have not advanced on whether categories possess any internal structure beyond our practical linguistic uses. My working hypothesis is that the stall conceals a deeper divergence about what philosophical explanation owes us, specifically whether abandoning an essence requires supplying a replacement account or permits a quietist withdrawal, but I am unsure whether this is correct or whether the disagreement is verbal, metaphysical, or a case of two questions being pursued under one heading.
