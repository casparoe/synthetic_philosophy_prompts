I am a PhD student in modern European history and philosophy, serving as the graduate-student representative on the curatorial-ethics advisory board of a public history museum. The board must deliver a formal decision memo by next Friday regarding a proposed permanent gallery, Bureaucratic Form and Mass Violence, which would juxtapose primary Holocaust documentation—transport schedules, camp administrative records, and survivor testimony—with structurally analogous bureaucratic artifacts drawn from other twentieth-century contexts of mass death, including colonial forced-labor administration and the logistical records of a subsequent genocide. The designers, drawing on Zygmunt Bauman’s argument that instrumental rationality and bureaucratic division of labor operated as necessary though not sufficient conditions for the genocide’s scale, intend to hold the administrative mechanism constant across cases so that visitors can grasp the functional role of modern organizational form without the exhibit asserting moral equivalence. Opposing board members, including representatives from the survivor-advisory council, invoke the uniqueness thesis associated with the German Historikerstreit: they contend that framing the Holocaust through administrative comparison normalizes it into a generic pathology of modernity, erasing the specific, ideologically driven intentionality of racial eliminationism that distinguishes it, and they urge outright rejection. Please draft the board’s decision memo—addressed to the museum director and the design team—recommending approval, rejection, or approval with binding curatorial conditions, specifying a concrete course of action with an implementation time frame. The memo should explain what philosophical stakes about comparability, uniqueness, and modernity are actually in play, engage the opposition’s strongest argument honestly rather than dismissing it, and present reasoning precise enough for the designers to respond technically yet accessible to board members without specialist philosophical training. In your response, please state how confident you are in the recommendation you advance and identify the specific point—whether historical, philosophical, or curatorial—about which you are least certain.
