I am preparing material for an upper-level ethics course I am auditing, and I keep returning to a conceptual knot that sits at the center of debates about moral conflict but is rarely unpacked with the precision I need. The knot concerns whether what we call a genuine moral dilemma is best understood as a logical impossibility within our normative framework—evidence that our obligations are inconsistent—or as a phenomenologically real situation in which two binding demands remain fully in force even after we have acted. To make this concrete, consider the pairing of two commitments that are often held together but may pull in opposite directions: first, that moral reasons have a comparative structure (we can recognize one demand as weightier than another without either losing its normative status), and second, that the experience of being torn tracks an objective feature of the situation—namely, that an obligation we fail to meet does not evaporate but leaves what has been called a moral remainder or tragic residue. The difficulty is explaining how both commitments can be true without collapsing into either a simple cost-benefit calculus on one side or a flat contradiction in deontic logic on the other.

What I need is an explanation of the conceptual architecture that would allow genuine dilemmas to exist without rendering the normative system incoherent. In particular, I want you to address whether preserving consistency requires us to deny that both obligations are genuinely binding, or whether we can instead reconceive bindingness so that an unfulfilled obligation transforms rather than disappears—perhaps into a retrospective duty of acknowledgment, repair, or structured grief. I am less interested in a survey of named positions than in a careful mapping of the pressure points: where exactly does the principle that ought implies can apply with full force, and where does it fail to capture the logic of tragic choice? Please treat this as a conceptual exercise rather than a historical one—no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations are necessary—but the reasoning should be tight enough that someone familiar with deontic logic and moral phenomenology could follow the moves without relying on external references.
