I've been trying to understand the demandingness objection to consequentialism—especially Bernard Williams's point that requiring agents to constantly maximize the good alienates them from their own projects and relationships. It strikes me that recent "moderate" or "cost-sensitive" versions of consequentialism—those that allow agents to assign extra weight to their own interests or to stop optimizing once a threshold is met—are trying to have it both ways. If the core commitment of consequentialism is that one ought always to produce the best overall outcome, then building in a personal "prerogative" or a satisficing cutoff seems like inserting a contradiction: it's as if the theory says "maximize, except when maximizing is too costly for you," which sounds less like a refined moral theory and more like an admission that the original theory was false. So why do philosophers treat these modified versions as serious rivals rather than as straightforward concessions that the demandingness objection has won? And if the real problem is that people psychologically cannot sustain constant self-sacrifice, shouldn't that be a reason to reject consequentialism entirely rather than to patch it with what look like ad hoc exemptions? In your answer, please separate what is settled about the structure of these objections from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and don't present a contested interpretation—such as whether moderate consequentialism is genuinely coherent or merely a retreat—as if it were the consensus view.
