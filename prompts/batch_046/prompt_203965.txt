My thesis is that emotional responses to fiction—fear at a horror film, grief over a novel's death—are not irrational because they never involve belief-like representational commitments about fictional entities in the first place. Instead, I argue they run in a "sandboxed" mode: functionally decoupled from our belief-formation and action-guidance architectures, analogous to how a containerized software process can share libraries with the host system without altering its global state. I contend this resolves the paradox without requiring us to posit "quasi-emotions" or pretend beliefs; the emotional machinery executes with its own local validation rules, producing genuine affect that simply doesn't feed into our world-modeling pipeline the way fear of a real threat would.

They will care most about whether my analogy obscures phenomenology, whether "sandboxing" is empirically tractable, and whether decoupling actually explains why fictional emotions can spill over into real-world moods and behaviors. Include the one question I'm dreading—likely something that asks why my sandbox model doesn't collapse into a general theory of counterfactual reasoning or pretend play, undermining its specificity to fiction. Throughout, separate what is genuinely settled in the philosophy of fiction literature from what remains actively contested among specialists; do not present my sandbox view, judgment theory, predictive-processing accounts, or Walton-style make-believe as if any were the consensus.
