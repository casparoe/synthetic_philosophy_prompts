Some philosophers of biology press an analogy between a species and a single organism: just as an organism is a spatiotemporally continuous individual with interdependent parts, a species is said to be a lineage with organisms as its parts. The analogy is used to argue that taxonomy tracks historical individuals rather than shared essential properties, and therefore that biology does not carve nature at its joints in the classical sense. Can you set out the explicit correspondence—what plays the role of birth, death, reproduction, and functional integration—then identify the points of breakdown, particularly regarding hybridization, horizontal gene transfer among microbes, and the lack of any centralized developmental program? Finally, assess whether the anti-natural-kind conclusion survives these disanalogies, or whether it relies on importing an ideal of discrete boundaries that phylogenetic networks already undermine.
