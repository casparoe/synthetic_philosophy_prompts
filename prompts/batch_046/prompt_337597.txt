I'm on the review panel for our uni's cross-disciplinary research ethics board, and we've got a proposal that's sitting in this awkward space between philosophy of rationality and behavioral econ. The PI wants to run a modified Luring Lottery — you know, the Hofstadter-style setup where people are invited into a lottery that only pays if both enter, testing whether superrational agents coordinate without talking. But they've bolted on what they call a "Platonia framing": participants first read a short philosophical prompt about rational cooperation, then face a one-shot choice between a guaranteed $40 or entering a pool where mutual entry splits $120, but if you enter alone you get $10 and the other person gets $80. The twist that makes it weird is the counterpart-ontology condition: half the pairs are human-human, a quarter are human-LLM (the LLM is primed to reason superrationally), and a quarter are human-random-algorithm. Participants are told their partner "might not be human" but they don't know which bucket they're in, and debriefing only happens after.

The board's split. One faction says it's basically just game theory with a philosophical garnish — the deception is minimal, it's one-shot, and comparing human vs LLM cooperation is genuinely intersting for theories of superrationality. The other faction says injecting a philosophical framing about rationality right before a money decision is a form of cognitive manipulation that breaks informed consent, especially since the Platonia-style payoff structure is designed to make people feel irrational for not cooperating. There's also a methodological worry: if people don't know whether they're facing a reasoning agent, then measuring "cooperation" is really measuring trust in unknown ontology, not testing anything about human rationality.

We need a formal memo recommending approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions. It should lay out actual criteria the PI can respond to — not just "fix the ethics form." I want the memo to take seriously the strongest objection: that this study doesn't actually illuminate the Platonia dilemma because mixing ontology conditions with a philosophical framing effect means you can't separate whether people are being superrational or just reacting to being philosophically tested. But I also want it to acknowledge that if the PI can isolate the framing effect from the counterpart-uncertainty effect, there's real value in seeing whether superrational coordination collapses when the other might not be an agent at all.

Please don't ask for citations or a reading list — just write the memo from the logic of the case. Keep it casual, maybe a typo or two, and make sure there's a clear bottom-line recommendation with honest treatment of the losing side.
