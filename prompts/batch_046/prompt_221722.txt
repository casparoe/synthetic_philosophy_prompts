I'm drafting a scene in which my protagonist, a committed moral particularist, delivers a seminar argument that I meant to serve as a reductio of her own view, yet the chain now strikes me as suspiciously tight. Here is the argument I have her present: (1) Particularism holds that moral reasons depend on holistic narrative context rather than on repeatable, principle-governed features. (2) If a reason's force is genuinely holistic, it is inseparable from the total configuration in which it operates; altering any background element, however minor, can change what counts as a reason. (3) Therefore the reason that justifies an act in one narrative configuration cannot be assumed to justify any act in another configuration, even one that duplicates every observable feature of the first, since the unobservable narrative remainder necessarily differs. (4) But if reasons never travel across configurations, then moral learning is impossible: judgment in one case gives no guidance for any other case. (5) Moreover, assessing the moral status of a configuration requires already knowing which features within it carry reason-giving weight; yet particularism denies that we can identify those weights except by reference to the whole, which includes the very moral status we seek to determine. (6) So particularism renders moral judgment both non-transferable and circular. (7) Since we plainly do learn from past moral experience and avoid circular reasoning, particularism must be false, leaving generalism as the only viable account. I want the protagonist to resist this conclusion, but I need to know whether the argument is genuinely sound. Where does it go wrong, if it does? If I must choose, I would preserve (2) and (4) and abandon whatever drives the slide into (6), but I want to understand exactly what that costs.
