I've been arguing with my flatmates about whether NIMBY objections to new housing are ever morally justified, and it got me thinking about writing a short research proposal for a philosophy workshop I'm pitching next term. The question I want to tackle is this: if we take seriously Lefebvre's idea of a 'right to the city' not just as access to urban space but as a right to participate in shaping its social charcter, can long-term residents legitimately claim that rapid densification violates that right by destroying the 'temporal continuity' of their neighbourhood — or is that just a fancy way of dressing up exclusion? I want the proposal to frame this as an intergenerational collective-action problem rather than the usual property-rights vs housing-abundance debate, because I think that moves us away from tired libertarian-vs-progressive scripts.

The proposal should explain why existing answers don't settle it: philosophers have talked a lot about exclusionary zoning as unjust discrimination or as legitimate local democracy, and there's work on the ethics of gentrification, but hardly anyone has asked whether preserving neighbourhood charcter is structurally similar to climate adaptation — where current generations make decisions that bind future ones — except in reverse, since residents want to bind the future to look like the present. I want an aproach that uses the concept of 'moral luck in locational privilege' — the idea that where you happen to live is partly luck, but that luck can generate legitimate expectations — to see whether continuity-claims survive when we factor in that residents themselves benefited from earlier zoning decisions they didn't choose.

Please write the one-page proposal: state the precise research question, say why a philosophical rather than empirical urban-studies approach makes progress, outline the method (probably comparing the temporal-externality framing to standard accounts of collective self-determination), say what a useful deliverable looks like for actual planners or activists, and be honest about what could make it too big for a three-month workshop project. Don't include a bibliography — I'll fill in the referances myself — and please don't ask me to reference specific court cases or planning statutes; I want this to stand on conceptual grounds alone. I also keep making typos when I'm excited, so don't worry if the framing sounds a bit messy.
