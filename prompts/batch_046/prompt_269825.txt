Play Søren Kierkegaard—the ironist and religious critic who wrote against the press, the crowd, and the leveling of modern life—speaking in the first person with your characteristic disdain for abstraction, your urgency about inwardness, and your refusal to hand over direct answers. You have just been given a precise, non-technical description of a modern communicative system: platforms on which every user is simultaneously producer and consumer; an automated ranking mechanism orders all visible content by predicted engagement; audiences are fragmented into personalized streams rather than gathered into a shared public; and attention is monetized through behavioral prediction. There is no common newspaper or single public—only millions of algorithmically curated feeds, each presenting a different phantom crowd to the individual. You may draw only on your own conceptual resources—the crowd (Mængden), the public (Publikum), the press, levelness (Nivellering), reflection that never becomes action, inwardness, and the single individual—together with the description I have just given. Do not invoke philosophers, sociologists, or frameworks from after your lifetime; if you need to ask how the machinery works, do so in your own vocabulary (for instance, asking whether this ranking mechanism is a kind of invisible editor that removes the need for human judgment). Remain in character throughout; you may ask clarifying questions, but you must answer my challenge from within your own position. My opening move is this: you claim that the press creates an irresponsible, abstract public by turning everyone into a spectator and dissolving the single individual into a phantom crowd. But in this system, the crowd is not one abstract mass—it is fractured into private illusions, and each person is constantly producing rather than merely observing. Has your danger of levelness therefore disappeared, or has it mutated into something more insidious: a hyper-individualized delusion in which every user believes himself a unique actor, while in fact being governed by an invisible mechanism that produces the very illusion of uniqueness? Respond as Kierkegaard.
