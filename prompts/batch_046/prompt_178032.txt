Write the full specification of desiderata that a theory of ontology identification under principled informational inaccessibility must satisfy. By principled inaccessibility I mean not merely practical unobservability but structural barriers—whether physical, logical, or indexical—that permanently block direct access to the properties that would distinguish competing ontologies. The specification should include: (i) the phenomena and hard cases the theory must accommodate, specifically observationally equivalent but structurally divergent formulations of a domain (e.g., empirically equivalent physical frameworks that differ on the existence of unobservable entities), entities whose defining properties lie in causally isolated modal branches that cannot influence our domain, and properties detectable only through second-order theoretical inference rather than direct detection; (ii) the distinctions it must preserve, above all between the claim that absence of access warrants absence of ontological commitment and the claim that partial or indirect access can still ground identification, as well as between contingent technological limits and necessary structural opacity; (iii) the established results it must remain consistent with, including that scientific practice routinely commits to entities via indirect and predictive criteria rather than direct observation, and that some forms of underdetermination are resolvable through explanatory coherence rather than observational discrimination; (iv) for each item, the motivating case or consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a partisan prejudice. Then identify which single desideratum does the most damage to standard structural realist programs and which does the most damage to eliminativist or deflationary ontologies, with brief justification. Indicate which requirements are non-negotiable and which are open to relaxation if the theory pursues a more modest purpose. Finally, assess whether the full list is jointly satisfiable or whether there is an in-principle tension—particularly between demanding unique identification across all structurally opaque domains and accepting that some ontologies are genuinely and permanently underdetermined by any information accessible within our domain. Present the result as a structured list with concise rationales, keeping the conceptual weight on the criteria themselves rather than on an exhaustive survey of existing positions.
