A recent philosophy blog post summarizes evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics—particularly versions developed by Richard Joyce, Sharon Street, and Michael Ruse—in the following terms. Evolutionary psychology shows that our core moral judgments, spanning both deontic beliefs about wrongness and aretaic beliefs about virtue, were shaped by natural selection for social coordination and kin altruism rather than by tracking mind-independent moral properties. Because selection operates solely on behavioral outcomes and is causally blind to normative facts, the evolutionary origin of a moral belief offers no evidential support for its truth. This creates what the post calls an "explanatory gap": moral realists must either deny that evolution fully explains our moral beliefs, or concede that those beliefs are epistemically unjustified. The standard realist reply—a "third-factor" explanation—attempts to close the gap by proposing that evolution selected for general rational capacities, and that rational reflection independently converges on moral truths. Yet the post argues this reply collapses, because it implicitly treats moral truths as causally available to influence evolutionary outcomes, contradicting the realist's own claim that normative properties are non-causal. The post therefore concludes that realists face a dilemma: they must either accept that evolutionary history undermines all moral justification, or adopt an implausible causal role for moral facts. From this, it draws a further conclusion: evolutionary debunking renders normative ethics methodologically bankrupt, since any ethical theory built on intuitions shaped by selection lacks independent epistemic standing.
