I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on rational decision theory, and we've been working through the logic of geometric rationality and the Kelly criterion—specifically, when maximizing the expected logarithm of a resource is justified over maximizing expected value or expected utility. Our professor has given clear verdicts on a range of cases, but I'm trying to determine what implicit principle is governing them.

Here are the rulings so far. First, on a standard long-horizon investment problem with liquid, fungible capital and no absorbing bankruptcy condition, she endorsed full Kelly betting. Second, on an otherwise identical investment problem but with a hard legal floor—if net worth falls below zero the investor is permanently barred from future trading—she rejected Kelly and insisted on a conservative, utility-based criterion, arguing that multiplicative reasoning collapses once an absorbing boundary removes the agent from the sequence. Third, on a scientist allocating fungible grant funding across independent, recoverable projects over a career with no institutional penalty for a failed project, she endorsed a Kelly-style allocation of research resources, treating the budget as multiplicatively compounding. Fourth, on a strategic interaction—specifically a repeated coordination game where one party's loss reshapes the other party's payoff matrix—she rejected geometric rationality altogether, saying that interdependence makes the multiplicative model inapplicable because one's own growth rate is no longer an independent variable. Fifth, on an institutional endowment with perpetual horizon and no mandatory withdrawal schedule, she endorsed maximizing the geometric mean of real returns.

The new case is this. A philosopher is deciding how to distribute her working time over the next ten years between writing a sequence of reliable, moderately successful books—low variance, linear accumulation of professional standing—and devoting a substantial block of time to a single, highly ambitious project that, if it succeeds, would fundamentally restructure her field's conversation, but if it fails, would be largely ignored. Crucially, she can continue writing the reliable books after a failure; there is no absorbing boundary that ends her career. However, professional standing is not fungible: it does not divide like capital, and a failed ambitious project does not merely reduce a quantifiable stock but alters the qualitative conditions under which her future work is received.

What should our professor say about this philosopher's allocation? Is it a case for geometric rationality—treating reputation as multiplicatively compounding and therefore allocating time according to a Kelly-like rule—or does the non-fungibility and qualitative structure of intellectual reputation place it outside the domain entirely? Which of the earlier cases is the closest analog—the recoverable grant-funding case, or perhaps the strategic-interdependence case if we treat the academic field as a responsive environment? More generally, I want to know which rule best fits all five verdicts—perhaps one that restricts Kelly to quantifiable, fungible resources regardless of compounding, or one that permits it whenever outcomes are multiplicatively structured and recoverable regardless of fungibility—and which further case would definitively separate those two rules. I'm less interested in a bare prediction than in the carving: exactly where the boundary lies, what the philosopher's situation is not, and what principle we would be adopting either way.
