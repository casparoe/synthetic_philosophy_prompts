In recent defenses of participatory governance, commentators frequently analogize Deweyan democratic inquiry to open-source software development: many distributed contributors, no central authority, errors corrected through collective testing, and policy improved iteratively rather than designed top-down. The analogy is deployed to argue that democratic decision-making should be decentralized, tolerant of failure, and resistant to expert gatekeeping—effectively licensing an epistemic anarchism said to flow from Dewey’s experimentalism. Audit this analogy precisely: provide the correspondence table (what plays the role of contributors, maintainers, version control, bugs, forks, and rollbacks), then isolate the disanalogies that matter for the intended anti-gatekeeping conclusion (for instance, the irreversibility of enacted policy versus revertible code, the absence of a shared objective function across citizens, and asymmetries of power among “contributors”). Specify which dynamical features of open-source collaboration the analogy quietly imports—such as meritocratic reputation mechanisms or the ability to abandon a failing branch—that democratic politics structurally lacks. Finally, assess whether the conclusion that Dewey’s model supports decentralized, failure-tolerant governance survives once these breaks are explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative weight. If it collapses, propose a more accurate analog for Dewey’s conception of social inquiry drawn from his actual account of cooperative intelligence and warranted assertibility rather than from software metaphors.
