I'm writing a paper for a seminar on distributive justice and family policy, and I've become fixated on a design feature that seems philosophically loaded but rarely discussed in the literature. In most countries with paid parental leave, the entitlement is structured as a continuous block—say, twelve or eighteen months—that must be taken in one sustained absence, with only a small partner quota reserved for the other parent. There is almost never an institutional pathway to fragment that same total entitlement into smaller units distributed over many years: for instance, working four days a week with protected status for a decade, or taking every Wednesday off until a child reaches school age. At the same time, professional workplaces—especially in law, medicine, and academia—maintain stop-the-clock provisions that treat a full-year absence as a neutral pause in career evaluation, while treating sustained part-time or intermittent schedules as evidence of diminished commitment, often without proportional safeguards for promotion or tenure.

The result is an institutional architecture that pushes families toward sequential, full-time caregiving by one parent at a time, rather than toward parallel, shared, part-time caregiving integrated into both parents' working lives. What philosophical commitments about career value, care labor, and gender equality does this architecture presuppose? Must one assume that professional capital accumulates only in continuous, indivisible units and that fragmentation destroys it? That care is best delivered in intensive, exclusive blocks rather than distributed over time? That equality between parents is satisfied by allowing each to take a discrete turn, rather than requiring simultaneous restructuring of both careers? Or can the practice be justified under several rival theories—liberal neutrality about family form, a capability approach focused on real options, or even a conservative view of natural gender roles? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value embedded in the design, carefully distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and tell me what a workplace and leave system genuinely committed to dual-earner, shared-care family life would have to change structurally—not merely in the length of leave, but in its temporal form and in the metrics by which professional advancement is assessed.
