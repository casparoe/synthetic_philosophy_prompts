I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with Dr. Marcus Chen, whose recent monograph argues that the collapse of the Roman Republic should be read not primarily through economic polarization or populist demagogy, but through a process he terms "institutional fatigue": the gradual normalization of extra-constitutional remedies—such as the senatus consultum ultimum and the deployment of private armies—until the republican architecture could no longer absorb internal conflict without open violence. Chen draws an explicit analogy to modern democratic erosion, especially the decay of informal norms surrounding judicial independence and peaceful transfers of power, yet he insists the comparison fractures at precisely the point where modern states possess a bureaucratic permanence that Rome entirely lacked.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond a summary of the thesis and press on its historical and normative mechanics. I want at least one question that interrogates his classification of the senatus consultum ultimum: does he treat it as an already-illegal emergency measure or as a formally constitutional instrument, and how does that distinction shape his analogy to modern executive overreach? Include a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that Rome's citizen-militia structure made such measures uniquely dangerous—if he concedes that uniqueness, how can the analogy still carry normative weight for modern democracies with professional armies and permanent administrative states? Another question should challenge his dismissal of economic drivers: what does he say to historians who emphasize the Gracchan land reforms and the rise of the latifundia as the real engines of political violence, and does his model of institutional fatigue have anything to say about whether economic stress accelerates or merely accompanies normative decay? I also want a question that anticipates his probable distinction between his concept of "institutional fatigue" and the familiar framework of "constitutional hardball" in contemporary political science—he will likely claim his idea captures a slower, less intentional erosion—followed immediately by the follow-up asking whether that very slowness makes the decay harder to detect and therefore harder to correct. Finally, include one question about what evidence could genuinely weaken his view: is there a modern case of democratic backsliding that recovered largely because economic conditions improved, which might suggest that institutional norms are more resilient than his fatigue model allows?
