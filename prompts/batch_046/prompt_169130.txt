Write a formal closing address for the plenary session of a symposium marking three decades since the publication of the final report of a truth commission modeled on South Africa's—one that traded amnesty for full disclosure, produced an authoritative public record, and left a deeply divided legacy among survivors, jurists, and historians. The audience is deliberately mixed: international-law scholars who defended the amnesty-for-truth framework; survivors and family members who regard that framework as an institutional betrayal that usurped their personal right to forgive or withhold forgiveness; and the archivists and legal historians who have spent decades determining which testimony remains sealed and which may be disclosed. The philosophical idea that must do real structural work—not mere decoration—is the distinction between answerability to victims (a direct, interpersonal moral relation that no institution can discharge on another's behalf) and accountability to history (a mediated, public relation secured through record, disclosure, and institutional acknowledgment). Your speech should honor the commission's achievement in establishing the latter—an authoritative record that makes perpetrators answer for facts—while explicitly denying that institutional amnesty achieved, or could achieve, the former. It should address the archivists' labor of disclosure and redaction not as bureaucratic housekeeping but as the ongoing practice of maintaining that boundary: opening the record to future citizens without forcing survivors into a posture of reconciliation they never chose. The address must comfort the non-philosophers in the room without saying anything false about the moral status of amnesty, and it must satisfy the experts that the distinction is being held rigorously rather than collapsed for rhetorical ease. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from the commission's report; if you refer to its structure—the amnesty committee, the victim hearings, the reparations recommendations—do so in paraphrase. Keep the register polished and appropriate to an academic-commemorative occasion.
