A colleague and I co-direct an oral-history archive that has completed 847 recorded life stories since 1962. Last week she observed that, if the project eventually reaches tens of thousands of entries, it is oddly improbable that we happen to be directing it right near the beginning—around the 0.008% mark—rather than closer to the middle of the sequence. From this she argued we should revise downward our expectation of the project's total lifespan: probably it will never grow large, and we should plan for institutional closure within our lifetimes rather than endowing it in perpetuity. I replied that every director necessarily occupies some position, and being near the start only feels surprising if we have already assumed a long future, which seems circular. She then raised a further difficulty: the rank itself is unstable. Depending on whether we count only fully transcribed interviews, partial drafts, or people who consented but died before recording, our position shifts from 847 to somewhere between 620 and 1,140—each yielding a different prediction about the total pool. We are now genuinely divided on whether this kind of positional reasoning carries real epistemic weight, and on how the ambiguity in defining our reference group should affect our planning. Can you help us identify the philosophical structure of this argument, assess whether it is valid or commits a subtle fallacy, address the reference-class problem embedded in our counting rules, and advise how—if at all—we ought to let our temporal position in the sequence inform long-term commitments? I am not looking for generic nonprofit-management tips; I want to know whether there is a genuine principle here that should change how we think about the archive's future.
