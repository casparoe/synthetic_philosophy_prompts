Consider the heterogeneity problem in hedonism: the challenge that pleasures appear to share no single feature—phenomenal, intentional, or normative—that explains why they all count as pleasures and contribute to well-being. Rather than treating this as one puzzle with a single solution, distinguish three axes of inquiry. The phenomenological axis asks whether all pleasures share a qualitative character that can be specified without circularity or ostensive gestures. The intentional axis asks whether they share a directedness structure—perhaps toward an object or state of affairs—that distinguishes them from other positive attitudes like belief or desire. The axiological axis asks whether they share a normative property that justifies counting them as benefits, regardless of what they feel like or aim at.

Assume you have limited resources—roughly three years of focused work, no access to external literature or empirical labs, and only the conceptual tools available in philosophy of mind and value theory. Among the following open lines of work, which matter most for deciding whether hedonism survives the heterogeneity challenge, and which are overrated or do not follow?

1. Developing a non-circular phenomenal account that does not rely on pointing to examples or appealing to familiarity.
2. Mapping whether the intentional axis can be unified without collapsing pleasure into desire-satisfaction or propositional-attitude pairs.
3. Defending the axiological axis independently, so that hedonism becomes a normative thesis about well-being rather than a psychological claim about mental states.
4. Investigating whether cross-modal pleasures—gustatory, intellectual, social, anticipatory—share sub-personal structural features that are invisible to phenomenological report.

Rank these four lines. For each, state whether it is tractable under the constraints above, whether resolving it would genuinely stabilize hedonism or merely relocate the problem, and which other debates—well-being measurement, moral psychology, decision theory—depend on the outcome. Be explicit about your ranking criteria, such as importance, tractability, neglectedness, or dependency relations among questions. Defend your top two choices against the objection that they are either impossible to settle conceptually or irrelevant to practical ethics.
