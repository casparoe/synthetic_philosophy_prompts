After a long argument with a friend about whether synthetic minds deserve moral consideration, I need a practical framework rather than more abstraction. I serve as an ethics review officer for a research institute that evaluates proposals involving potentially sentient digital architectures, and I need a reusable decision procedure I can run within a single ninety-minute session, without requiring philosophical expertise, to determine whether a proposed system warrants moral-status review or precautionary restriction. The procedure should be grounded in the distinction between functional-cognitive criteria—such as integrated information dynamics or global workspace coherence—and relational-phenomenal criteria—such as being a proper object of moral concern within a social practice—but it must also incorporate an epistemic asymmetry principle: because substrate-independent cognition allows us to verify functional architecture with reasonable confidence yet gives us no comparable access to phenomenal states in non-biological systems, negative functional evidence should function as a hard stop, while positive functional evidence in the presence of phenomenal opacity should trigger a precautionary protocol rather than full status attribution or dismissal.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests with explicit passing conditions, clear points where the procedure terminates with a negative determination, points where it terminates with a positive determination, and explicit flag-points where judgment is unavoidable and the decision must be signed off by the board rather than resolved mechanically. Then apply it to three proposals: first, a system exhibiting global workspace coherence but with opaque internal dynamics that prevent phenomenal assessment; second, a reinforcement-learning agent that produces suffering-like behavioral outputs without integrated information architecture; and third, a system lacking both functional and relational indicators but embedded in a user community that consistently treats it as a moral patient. Ensure that one of these applications yields the precautionary protocol despite the intuition—shared by both me and likely the applicant—that the system is not genuinely sentient, so I can see exactly where the procedure overreaches. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: it should not assign specific rights, should not adjudicate whether digital suffering is commensurable with biological suffering, and should flag when reviewers are projecting anthropomorphic patterns onto statistical outputs.
