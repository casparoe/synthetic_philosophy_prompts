I'm currently taking a seminar on philosophy of art, and we've been working through the classic paradox of fiction: we seem to feel genuine fear or grief for characters we know do not exist, yet standard accounts of emotion treat belief in the object's reality as a necessary condition. Rather than rehearsing the standard Walton-style make-believe resolution or the standard thought-theory reply, I'm interested in whether the tension can be dramatized rather than dissolved. Write a short story — perhaps set in a near-future clinical setting where a therapist specializes in treating fictional grief, that is, bereavement-level emotional responses to characters in immersive narrative worlds that are explicitly marked as synthetic — that embodies the paradox from the inside. The protagonist should be someone professionally committed to the view that these responses are category errors, like fearing a shadow, yet finds themselves caught in an analogous emotional response to a synthetic narrative they themselves engineered. Include at least one moment where the character attempts to apply a philosophical distinction — for instance, between directed emotion and quasi-emotion, or between belief and make-belief — to their own experience, and have the distinction fail in practice. Keep the philosophical machinery implicit in the action and dialogue rather than expository; I want to see whether the paradox survives being lived rather than argued.
