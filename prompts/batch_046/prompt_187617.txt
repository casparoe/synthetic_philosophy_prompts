Consider the following argument about collective moral memory, which seems to show that any duty to remember past injustices is self-defeating. (1) If a community owes victims a duty of remembrance, it must preserve the victim's narrative in a form that remains accessible and authoritative across generations. (2) But preservation is never pure: archives require selection, monuments require interpretation, and rituals require repetition that gradually transforms meaning; over time, natural decay and necessary revision are unavoidable. (3) Any selection, reinterpretation, or decay that allows details to fade is a form of forgetting—call it curatorial forgetting—because it removes some aspect of the original wrong from active communal awareness. (4) Therefore, every institutional attempt to fulfill the duty to remember necessarily violates it through unavoidable curatorial forgetting. (5) If the duty is unconditional, it demands the impossible; if it is conditional, then we need a principle that distinguishes permissible forgetting from impermissible suppression. (6) But any such principle must rank injustices by importance, which implies that some victims are legitimately forgettable, contradicting the universal moral claim implied by a duty of remembrance. (7) Hence the concept of a collective duty to remember either collapses into an impossible ideal or licenses selective amnesia. Where does this argument fail? Specifically, does the distinction between remembering that an injustice occurred and remembering it in a particular narrative form block the move from (2) to (3)? And if structured forgetting is sometimes morally required—for instance, to prevent the instrumentalization of trauma—does that undermine the victim's unconditional claim, or does it merely specify the form that remembrance must take?
