Read Its a Wonderful Life through the lens of the nonidentity problem. George sees Pottersville, where he was never born, and the people there are different individuals—Mary never married, Harry died young, and the town went to ruin. I want you to take that seriously rather than just saying everyone is worse off. Does George actually save specific people, or does he merely swap one set of future individuals for another? Look at the graveyard scene and Mary's spinsterhood: the film treats these as direct losses to George's absence, but if the people in Bedford Falls wouldn't exist without him, can he claim to have rescued them in particular? Where does the movie smuggle in a person-affecting morality that the nonidentity problem undermines, and where does it resist a purely impersonal reading? Also, separate whats settled in the literature—like the logic of replacement arguments—from whats genuinely contested, especially whether identity-affecting choices can be wrong if the replacements live good lives.
