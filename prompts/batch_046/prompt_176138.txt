I have been studying the philosophical conditions that render consent valid, with particular attention to deception, coercion, and competence. I have formulated what I take to be a necessary condition: consent is valid only if it is counterfactually robust across nearby possible worlds—that is, the agent would still have consented had every materially relevant fact been disclosed. On this view, any act of deception appears to vitiate consent automatically, since one can always construct a nearby scenario in which the concealed information alters the agent's decision. This strikes me as implausibly stringent; for instance, if a surgeon conceals an irrelevant personal preference, and the patient might hypothetically have objected on those grounds alone, does the consent genuinely collapse? My difficulty is this: if deception undermines consent by breaking counterfactual robustness, are we forced to place trivial omissions on the same moral continuum as coercion, or is there a principled way to distinguish autonomy-targeting deception from incidental falsehoods? I should be grateful for an answer in British English.
