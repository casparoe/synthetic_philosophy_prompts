Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full.

Nozick's experience machine shows that people care about more than pleasure. He imagines a machine that gives you any experience you want. Most people say they wouldn't plug in because they want to actually do things—like write a novel or raise children—not just have the feeling that they did. This proves hedonism is false, because hedonism claims well-being is just pleasure, but the refusal shows we also need real contact with the world. From this, Nozick concludes that well-being is best understood as an objective list: real friendships, real knowledge, and real achievements are necessary, and mental states alone are never enough.

Turning to the transparent machine: here you know you're in the simulation and you remember your real life, but you can still enjoy a happier simulated one. For Sumner's authentic-happiness view, this should actually be acceptable. Sumner says happiness counts only when it's about real things, and in the transparent machine your happiness is about real things—specifically, it's about your real knowledge that you're in the machine and your real choice to stay. So the authenticity condition is satisfied, and Nozick's objection doesn't apply here either. Therefore, both hedonism and authentic happiness survive the transparent-machine test, and Nozick hasn't really shown that mental-state theories fail—he's only shown that people have a psychological bias toward reality.
