I was working through a summary of Kant’s 1795 essay *Toward Perpetual Peace* that appeared in a course packet for an introductory political philosophy seminar, and I want to check whether I should trust its framing. The summary further claims that Kant does not treat these conditions as mere moral ideals, but as empirically inevitable outcomes: nature’s mechanism—above all the economic interdependence and self-interest of commercial republics—guarantees that despotic regimes will eventually collapse and that the federation will expand to encompass all nations. From this, the author draws a sharp historical conclusion: because modern international law lacks any binding world legislature and because cosmopolitan right in practice is restricted to temporary hospitality rather than global citizenship, Kant’s project has been definitively refuted by the record of the last two centuries. Finally, the summary treats Kantian republicanism as essentially identical to modern liberal representative democracy, implying that his argument applies without qualification to contemporary democratic states.

I am inclined to think the account captures the broad architecture of the preliminary and definitive articles, yet I suspect it quietly conflates several distinctions that matter for Kant—perhaps between a voluntary league of states and a coercive world government, between republican representation and direct democratic rule, or between a practical postulate aided by nature and a deterministic historical prediction. Could you audit this summary for fairness?
