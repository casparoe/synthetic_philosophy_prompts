I serve on the editorial policy committee of an interdisciplinary journal that publishes work at the intersection of philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and we must reach a concrete decision before Thursday's meeting. We received a manuscript that applies Daniel Dennett's concept of counterfeit people—systems that display fluent, person-like linguistic behavior and apparent reasoning without the evolutionary history, embodied cognition, or architectural coherence that grounds genuine intentionality—to contemporary large language models, arguing that outputs from such systems should be treated as epistemically unreliable interlocutors rather than as arguments worthy of peer review. The difficulty is that the submission itself was substantially co-produced by an LLM working in sustained dialogue with its human author, who acknowledges the collaboration but insists the philosophical reasoning and argumentative structure are genuinely his own. The committee is divided: one faction holds that if LLMs are counterfeit people in Dennett's technical sense, then a manuscript substantially generated by such a system lacks the authorial intentionality required for scholarly publication regardless of subsequent human editing; the opposing faction argues that Dennett's intentional stance is a pragmatic predictive strategy rather than an ontological litmus test, and that adopting it toward the human co-author is sufficient to evaluate the argument's merits without investigating the provenance of each sentence.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate: recommend one specific course of action—whether desk-rejection of LLM-co-produced submissions, mandatory disclosure paired with standard peer review, or a distinct third option—together with a clear implementation timeline and the reasoning that applies Dennett's distinction between strategically adopting the intentional stance and attributing genuine mental states to this institutional context. The opinion should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with intellectual honesty rather than dismissing it, should avoid requesting bibliographies, citation lists, or verification of external sources, and should stand on its conceptual reasoning alone, since our decision must be defensible without reference to literature we have not collectively reviewed.
