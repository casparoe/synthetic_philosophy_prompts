I came across a summary of the dirty-hands problem in politics—linked mainly to Sartre and later developed by Michael Walzer—that went like this. Political leaders must sometimes violate ordinary moral rules, such as lying or authorizing harm, to secure essential public goods. The summary drew a sharp distinction between excusable wrongdoing, where the agent remains blameworthy but is pardoned, and justified wrongdoing, where no moral wrong occurs at all; it placed dirty hands entirely in the second category. Because these violations are instrumentally necessary for collective survival, the leader incurs no genuine fault, and any talk of a moral stain is purely symbolic, functioning like a ritual apology rather than an acknowledgment of real guilt. From this, the summary concluded that democratic citizens ought to forgive such acts automatically, since holding leaders accountable would only disable necessary action, and that political ethics is therefore not a domain of genuine moral conflict but a separate system in which ordinary rules are suspended and replaced by a calculus of collective survival. Something about this feels too tidy, especially the claim that dirty hands involve no real wrong and the jump to automatic forgiveness. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what accurately reflects the dirty-hands tradition, what misrepresents it—particularly the treatment of moral stain and the excusable versus justified distinction—and whether the conclusion that political morality is simply suspended ordinary morality actually follows from the premise that good ends sometimes require wrong means.
