I sit on my city’s Commission on Civic Memory and Public Expression, and we are deadlocked over a permit request that feels like a philosophical problem I once studied but can’t fully name. A collective of artists has proposed a temporary “counter-monument” in our central plaza: a participatory audio installation that uses generative voice synthesis to let visitors “interrogate” reconstructed historical figures, but every generated response frames those figures as having committed hidden moral failures—fabricated confessions to corruption and cruelty—without any claim to historical accuracy. The artists argue this exposes how public veneration masks vice; opponents, including several commissioners, argue it is deliberately designed to corrode shared moral memory and that allowing it signals our collective standards of dignity and truth are disposable.

Our charter permits denial only for “substantial harm to public order or demonstrable injury to protected groups,” yet a faction insists we should invoke an implicit “moral fabric” interest: if an expression is engineered to erode the shared convictions that bind civic life, the city may refuse it a public platform even absent tangible harm. Others reply that this is exactly the confusion of disgust with damage that collapses liberal neutrality into legal moralism, and that without identifiable harm we overreach. I was discussing this with a colleague who mentioned a classic debate between liberal harm-principle reasoning and the view that society’s shared morality itself gives law a legitimate object, but I want the reasoning made fresh for our specific institutional context rather than recited by name.

Please act as a senior advisor in philosophy of law and institutional ethics. Think carefully, and begin by briefly mapping the conceptual tension—especially the distinction between suppressing expression because it violates shared morality versus suppressing it because it causes identifiable harm—and identify the strongest, non-caricatured version of the moral-fabric argument as it applies to synthetic, participatory art that targets collective memory. Then draft the formal memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting: recommend a concrete course of action (approve, approve with conditions, or deny) with an implementation timeline if conditions apply; provide reasoning the commissioners can actually respond to; and include an honest, serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Write it as an institutional document with a clear actionable bottom line, not as personal advice to me.

As you write, please state explicitly how confident you are in the recommendation and single out the specific point—whether about the nature of harm in symbolic corruption, the proper scope of state neutrality, or the interpretation of our charter’s “substantial harm” clause—about which you are least certain. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the reasoning should stand on common philosophical knowledge. If you reference specific thinkers or texts, do so only in general terms without verbatim quotation.
