I'm in a philosophy-of-literature seminar, and we've been working through the debate over authorial intention — Wimsatt and Beardsley's intentional fallacy, Hirsch's separation of meaning from significance, and the autonomy of the literary work. For my final paper, I want to test these ideas against Franz Kafka's The Trial, but I want to avoid the usual move of treating the text as a finished object with a recoverable authorial design behind it. Kafka left the novel unfinished; he instructed Max Brod to burn his manuscripts; and Brod preserved and arranged them, including deciding the sequence of chapters such as the cathedral scene. So the relevant "authorial intentions" here are not interpretive but existential — an instruction to destroy the text — and the published form depends on an editor's reconstructive choices that Kafka never endorsed.

Read The Trial through this framework. How does its unfinished, fragmentary condition — especially the ambiguous placement of the cathedral chapter and the abrupt, almost draft-like execution scene — bear on the question of whether meaning can be separated from authorial control? Does the intentional fallacy apply differently when the author's intended act is not to fix a meaning but to prevent the work from existing? Should Brod's editorial assembly be treated as an authorial intention to be ignored, as a separate intentional layer, or as evidence that the text we have is already a collaboration that makes the autonomy thesis more complicated? Identify scenes or structural gaps where the novel seems to solicit interpretation precisely because Kafka did not finalize them, and say where the hermeneutic framework clarifies those moments and where the novel resists being read as a mere illustration. Finally, does Kafka's attempt to erase his own work contribute something to the philosophy of textual meaning that standard arguments about authorial intention fail to capture?
