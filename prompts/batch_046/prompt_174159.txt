As a novelist constructing the moral architecture for a series of institutional characters, I find myself with a cluster of intuitive verdicts about indirect normativity and idealized deliberation that I cannot fully reconcile. I want to know whether they point to a single principle, and if not, which verdict I should surrender.

Here are my judgments, rendered as concrete narrative situations.

A surgeon refuses to harvest the organs of one healthy patient to save five, adhering to an absolute institutional prohibition on killing the innocent, even though she recognizes that the direct consequential calculus strongly favors the harvest. I judge her action correct.

A legislator supports a categorical ban on torture, and when faced with a ticking-bomb scenario in which torture would almost certainly prevent catastrophe, she declines to authorize it. Her reason is not merely the rule, but that her idealized deliberative counterpart—fully rational, fully informed, and reasoning behind a veil of ignorance about her social position—would reject torture as an admissible practice in any world. I judge her correct, and I think she is more correct than she would be if she had weighed this particular case on its direct merits.

A corporate whistleblower reveals systematic fraud despite knowing that the disclosure will destroy the livelihoods of innocent employees. She acts because her idealized self, reasoning without self-interest, endorses transparency. Here my verdict is also that she is right, but the reasoning feels different: it seems the direct and indirect endorsements converge rather than conflict.

A judge applies a rigid sentencing guideline—adopted through an idealized deliberative process among jurists—to a minor, first-time drug offense, producing a life sentence that is obviously disproportionate. She refuses to deviate, insisting that only rules with deliberative pedigree possess authority. I think she is wrong; she should have made an exception.

A teacher assigns grades according to a rubric developed through faculty deliberation designed to eliminate bias. A brilliant but persistently disruptive student receives a failing mark because the rubric contains no exception clause for exceptional intellectual contribution. The teacher applies it mechanically. I think she is wrong, yet I am less certain here than with the judge.

The case I keep revising is this: a hospital administrator learns that a dying patient’s estranged daughter has not, in fact, forgiven him. The administrator knows that telling him she has—an explicit falsehood—would grant him profound peace in his final hours. The hospital’s ethical code, adopted through extensive deliberative procedures, prohibits deception of patients in all circumstances. Yet if the administrator imagines herself in fully idealized conditions—rational, empathetic, unbiased by institutional loyalty—she suspects she might endorse this specific deception as an act of mercy, even while maintaining that deception should remain generally prohibited. Should she lie? I go back and forth.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes between rules that constitute an institution and rules that merely regulate conduct within it, or that separates reasons available in idealized reasoning from those that should be screened out—that accommodates all five verdicts? Or does accommodating the surgeon and the legislator force me to reject my intuition about the judge, or vice versa? And what should the resulting account predict for the administrator’s lie, and for a further case I have not mentioned: a citizen who breaks a just law—say, a prohibition on feeding the homeless—because her idealized self would endorse the feeding, but the law itself was adopted through perfectly legitimate democratic deliberation?
