I run a rationalist-adjacent reading group—mostly engineers and researchers with little formal philosophy background—and I want to teach the Meno problem in a single forty-minute session: specifically, why knowledge is more valuable than true belief if both guide action with equal success. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. First, lay out the dependency order: what conceptual piece has to be in place before the audience can feel the problem as a problem rather than a verbal quibble, and what prior assumption about belief and practical success makes the puzzle invisible until that piece is removed? Second, propose the carrying example: I am considering using a forecasting tournament in which one model produces correct predictions through overfitted spurious correlations, while another produces correct predictions through a calibrated causal world-model. Assess whether that example carries the philosophical weight, specify exactly what property it must preserve and what property it must lack so it does not collapse into a question about confidence or social justification, and if it is inadequate suggest one concrete alternative that is not the standard road-to-Larissa case. Third, map the misconceptions in the sequence they will arise—likely including "this is just about confidence or social credit," "operationally there is no difference," and "knowledge is true belief plus a decorative credential"—and identify the precise point in the arc where each should be headed off before it hardens. Fourth, design one closing question or exercise that distinguishes having grasped the value-gap from having merely memorized Plato's proposed resolution. Fifth, list what should be explicitly bracketed as out of scope—such as Gettier-style counterexamples, the circularity worry in the Meno's own answer, and virtue-theoretic replies—with a brief justification for each omission being safe at this level. Use American English, formal tone, and do not ask for readings or citations.
