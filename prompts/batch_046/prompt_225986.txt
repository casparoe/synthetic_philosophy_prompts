I am a high school student writing for an advanced philosophy-of-science seminar, and I have chosen to examine whether a revolutionary scientist can consistently hold empirical commitments that a later age dismisses as pseudoscience without collapsing into contradiction. My case is Johannes Kepler: I know he formulated the laws of planetary motion, yet he also cast horoscopes for patrons, spoke of celestial harmony, and defended astrological influence as compatible with mathematical astronomy. Rather than rely on a textbook summary, I want to test the tension directly through dialogue.

Please speak in the first person as Johannes Kepler, situated around 1619, shortly after the appearance of Harmonices Mundi. Before my first question, I have explained to you a modern philosophical analogy—sometimes called the mill argument—that if one enlarged a mechanical mill to the size of a building and walked inside, one would observe only parts pushing other parts, with no trace of perception or consciousness arising from the machinery itself. You may use the concepts available to you in 1619, together with this analogy I have just described, but you must not invoke figures, texts, or ideas from after your death unless I explicitly introduce them. Stay in character at all times; if you need a factual clarification about the mechanical model, ask it as Kepler would. Do not break frame to deliver modern commentary.

My opening move is this. You have claimed that the planets influence human affairs not by direct physical pushing like a lever, but through a genuine harmonic resonance or celestial correspondence that can reach even to a newborn child. Now imagine an orrery of perfect mechanical fidelity—brass gears, weights, and articulated arms reproducing every planetary motion with exact precision—built large enough for a person to enter. Walking through its interior, I see only inanimate components driving one another; there is no audible harmony extending outward, no soul residing in the mechanism, and certainly no direct link to an infant’s destiny. If your astrological influence is not literally inside those gears—which seems plainly absurd—then it appears absent from the model altogether. Does this not force you to concede that your celestial influence is either reducible to ordinary mechanical causation, which you reject, or else an illusion with no real presence in nature?

Respond entirely as Kepler. Keep your reply to a single sustained turn that addresses the orrery challenge on its own terms. At the very end, include one final line in exactly this form: Verdict: [a single sentence stating whether you hold that the mechanical model refutes your astrological view].
