In a recent working group with colleagues from military law and applied ethics—convened after our board received three conflicting proposals in a single week—I realized we have no shared framework for evaluating requests that treat enemy combatants differently based on the justice of their cause rather than on uniform combatant status. I serve as the senior ethics auditor for the legal-review panel of a multinational stabilization force, and these requests arrive roughly twice monthly, always under time pressure, with commanders expecting a structured recommendation rather than a philosophical essay. The proposals consistently fall into three domains: rules of engagement for lethal targeting, conditions of detention and interrogation, and post-conflict reintegration or amnesty eligibility. I need a reusable procedure that a panel without specialized philosophical training can run in under an hour.

The procedure must translate the moral equality of combatants—specifically the independence of jus in bello judgments from jus ad bellum assessments—into concrete evaluative steps, while also accommodating the revisionist challenge that unjust combatants may lack protective rights precisely because they are liable to defensive harm rather than because they wear the wrong uniform. For each domain, please lay out an ordered sequence of questions: what factual and normative conditions must be satisfied to proceed, where an insufficient answer triggers an automatic rejection, where it permits approval, and—crucially—where the procedure must stop and flag the decision as turning on values that exceed procedural determination. The checklist should preserve the distinction between status-based uniform treatment and liability-based discrimination, but it must also be explicit about which distinctions require epistemic access to a combatant's individual culpability, coercion, or mental state that field conditions rarely provide.

Apply the audit to three proposals: first, denying standard prisoner-of-war protections to all uniformed members of a state that has been formally designated an aggressor under international charter provisions; second, authorizing preemptive lethal strikes against uniformed soldiers of an unjust cause who are stationed in a rear-area logistics zone and present no immediate operational threat; third, granting preferential reintegration funding and amnesty to voluntary defectors from an unjust force while excluding conscripted soldiers who claim they were coerced into service. The procedure should deliver, in at least one case, a verdict that a commander motivated by retributive justice will find unacceptable—making the tension between procedural neutrality and moral intuition visible. Finally, state clearly what categories of judgment the procedure is structurally unable to resolve, particularly where evidence about individual consent, duress, or combatant intent is ambiguous or unavailable.
