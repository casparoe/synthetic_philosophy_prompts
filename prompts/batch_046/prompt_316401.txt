My study partner reconstructed Carroll's tortoise argument against the justification of deduction, added a twist about normative force, and claims it collapses into self-refutation—but I think the collapse happens in the wrong place. Here is the chain:

(1) For any deductive inference to be rationally justified, the logical rule it employs must itself be justified.
(2) A basic logical rule is justified only if it can be derived from more fundamental principles without already presupposing it.
(3) Carroll's tortoise demonstrates that deriving a rule like modus ponens requires applying modus ponens; hence basic rules cannot be non-circularly justified.
(4) If basic rules lack justification, then every inference depending on them—including all ordinary reasoning—also lacks justification.
(5) Therefore, if (1) holds, no human reasoning, including the reasoning in (1)-(4) itself, is rationally justified.
(6) But if the reasoning in (1)-(4) is unjustified, it provides no support for (1), which undermines the very argument that established it.
(7) Hence accepting (1) entails that (1) is unsupported, which is absurd; so (1) is false, and deduction requires no prior justification of its rules.

She concludes that logical rules are primitive entitlements rather than premises needing derivation. I suspect the slide occurs elsewhere—perhaps between treating unjustified inferences as irrational in (4)-(5), or between undermining an argument and falsifying its premise in (6)-(7). If I reject (1), I seem to abandon any philosophical account of why deduction is valid. If I keep (1) but reject (6), I must explain how an unjustified chain can still rationally support a conclusion.

Which step is the real fault? What does giving it up cost—particularly for the view that logical norms are premises, practices, or primitive entitlements? And does Carroll's regress actually threaten justification, or only the project of adding rules as explicit premises?
