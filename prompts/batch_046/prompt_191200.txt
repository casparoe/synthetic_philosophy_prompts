I'm prepping a grad seminar on Plato's ethics of the self — 10 students, one 75-minute session. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the specific problem Plato has with drunkenness: its not just that drunk people misbehave, but that the symposium functions like a stress-test for whether your virtue is actually stable or just held in place by social pressure. To keep it from being a standard "Plato on temperance" discussion, I want to inject the less-discussed asymmetry in Symposium and Laws: the drunk speaker often experiences his own words as profound revelation, while the sober observer can hear the exact same sentence as either genuine insight or empty noise depending entirely on the speaker's prior character. So design a structured role-play: split the group into "symposiasts" and "archons." The symposiasts have to defend a controversial ethical claim, but every round they get a new constraint that simulates lowered inhibition — first they can only speak in metaphors, then they must contradict their first statement without noticing, then they have to incorporate a randomly drawn "vice card" (e.g., flattery, envy) into their argument. The archons observe with a hidden scoring rubric: they award insight tokens to statements that survive a consistency check against what that speaker said sober in round one, and they can seal or discard testimony into the city record. No real money, just tokens that determine whose view gets entered into the final law code the group writes. Include rules, timeline, debrief questions that map rounds onto Plato's texts (especialy Alcibiades's speech and the Laws discussion of wine-testing for citizens), and an honest note on what can't be reproduced — probably the actual phenomenology of intoxication and the long habituation Plato thinks creates real virtue. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first run, like students playing the metaphor round for laughs and missing the epistemic point. Keep it runnable without any outside readings during the session. End with a single line beginning Verdict: that captures the one design feature that makes the asymmetry visceral rather than theoretical.
