I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of medicine working on how clinical institutions manage continuous biological variables. In cardiovascular practice, risk-stratification guidelines impose sharp numerical thresholds—blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg divides "pre-hypertension" from "hypertension," and specific cholesterol values trigger discrete treatment categories—despite the underlying biomarkers being continuously distributed with no physiological discontinuity at those points. The result is a binary eligibility structure in which a patient at 139/89 receives qualitatively different clinical recommendations from one at 140/90, reproducing the classic sorites pattern: if one unit of systolic pressure makes no categorical difference, then incremental accumulation should never generate a new diagnostic kind, yet medicine treats the boundary as decisive for therapy.

What philosophical commitments about vagueness does this classificatory practice presuppose? Does it require medicine to deny that conditions like hypertension are genuinely vague properties, or does it instead presuppose an epistemicist view that a precise, unknown boundary exists and the cutoff is merely a practical approximation? Could a contextualist or supervaluationist account explain these thresholds without ontological sharpness, or does the institutional reliance on binary treatment eligibility—treat versus do not treat—reveal a hidden commitment that vagueness must be resolved administratively rather than discovered? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics of medical risk classification, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and explain what clinical policy, resource allocation, and patient communication would have to change if we replaced the threshold model with a fully gradational, sorites-sensitive framework that refused all binary diagnostic categories and instead expressed risk and therapeutic indication as an unbroken continuum containing no eligibility discontinuities whatsoever.
