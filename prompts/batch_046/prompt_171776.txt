A peer reviewer for a history journal has consistently ruled on whether submissions count as history from below rather than elite- or state-centered history. Her verdicts: a study of one miller’s heresy trial reconstructed from Inquisition records, yes; a statistical survey of peasant tax revolts across provinces, no; an oral-history archive of factory workers, yes; a monarch’s biography drawn only from court memoirs, no; and a study of slave resistance reconstructed solely from plantation account books, yes, but only if the analysis centers enslaved actors’ inferred choices rather than owners’ categories. She must now judge a project that traces one unnamed nineteenth-century street sweeper’s daily route through a city using only municipal boot-repair logs, soil-composition maps, and public-bench wear patterns, with no named individual and no direct testimony in any source. Predict her verdict, articulate at least two competing rules that fit her track record—such as presence of subaltern voice versus absence of elite framing—identify the closest analog case, and name the further case that would best distinguish those rules. Respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
