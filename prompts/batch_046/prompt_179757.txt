I don't have any formal philosophy training, but I've been thinking on my own about an idea that seems to come up in ethics: maybe what I ought to do isn't just whatever I want right now, but what I would want if I were fully rational, fully informed, and thinking clearly—something I've heard called indirect normativity, where the authority comes from an idealized version of me rather than from my actual preferences. I think I see how this could work for ordinary practical choices, but I've run into a problem with a specific kind of decision that feels much closer to home. Suppose I'm offered a career opportunity that would require relocating my family. If I apply the idealized-deliberation test and ask what my fully rational, fully informed self would choose, I notice something strange: the idealized version seems to be stripped of the particular emotional bonds and commitments that make my actual spouse and children the people they are to me. The idealization improves my reasoning, but it also seems to erase the very relationships I'm trying to protect or prioritize. Write an essay arguing whether this kind of "relationship-erasure" objection defeats indirect normativity for intimate life decisions, or whether defenders can repair the view by restricting how far idealization is allowed to go. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but don't end with a non-committal verdict—reach and justify a clear conclusion. If you defend one side, explain what that implies for how an ordinary person should actually approach hard family choices without becoming an abstract reasoning machine.
