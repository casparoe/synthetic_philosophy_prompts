In the course of drafting a novel that interleaves translated Renaissance verse with contemporary narrative, I have been discussing the ethics of formal translation with a fellow writer in my workshop. She summarized Douglas Hofstadter's argument in his study of Clement Marot—Le Ton beau de Marot, I believe the book is called—as holding that a poem's rhyme scheme, syllabic meter, and stanzaic architecture are not decorative surfaces but the actual cognitive vessel of its meaning. Consequently, she said, a translator must reproduce those structural constraints exactly, sacrificing literal lexical accuracy when necessary; and because form and meaning are inseparable in this strict sense, Hofstadter regards any rendering that abandons the original rhyme and meter—say, a free-verse adaptation—as not a translation at all but a replacement object. She added that this is precisely why Hofstadter spent years producing successive versions of a single Marot poem: he was searching for the one correct structural analogue, the unique solution that preserved both form and sense without compromise. I feel there is an exaggeration here, especially the claim of a unique correct version and the dismissal of all non-formal rendering as betrayal, but I am not versed enough in translation theory to separate the distortion from the doctrine with confidence. Could you audit this summary? Please distinguish which parts fairly capture Hofstadter's insistence that form must be preserved alongside meaning, which parts misrepresent his attitude toward multiple valid translations or toward free-verse adaptation, and whether the conclusion that abandoning rhyme and meter necessarily produces a replacement rather than a translation follows from the accurate premises. I ask that you say how confident you are in each claim and single out the point about which you are least sure.
