I'm working through the boundary conditions of the reflection principle associated with Bas van Fraassen — the idea that a rational agent should align her present credences with those she expects her future rational self to hold, subject to exceptions. A philosopher I have been following applies it consistently across three cases: (1) a researcher who expects that after seeing tomorrow's data she will rationally lower her confidence in H from 0.9 to 0.4 should lower it now; (2) a researcher who expects sleep deprivation to irrationally inflate a belief should hold steady; and (3) a researcher who expects that in twenty years a conceptual revolution will lead her to reject her current framework, though she cannot now understand the reasoning, should stay near her present levels with only a modest temporal discount. Now consider a new case: in one year, she will voluntarily undergo a safe, well-understood cognitive enhancement that restructures her priors — without altering her evidence — so that her credence in H rationally falls to 0.3; she fully endorses this future restructuring, understands the mechanism, but cannot execute it in her present cognitive state. Predict her verdict on whether she should adjust her credence now, identify whether she is running a mechanism-sensitive rule (evidential updates only), an endorsement-sensitive rule (any endorsed rational restructuring), or a temporal-distance rule, and name the further boundary case — for example, an endorsed restructuring she does not understand versus an unendorsed evidential update — that would best distinguish the two leading interpretations. Please answer in American English.
