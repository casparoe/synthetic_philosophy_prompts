hey — i've been noticing this weird argumentative pattern in debates about how old nations really are, and i cant tell if it already has a name or if im just seeing things. here's the setup: a historian shows that some national symbol or institution — say a flag, a language standard, a founding myth — is actually a 19th-century invention. the defender doesnt fight that fact. instead they say 'sure, that expression is modern, but the nation itself is ancient; these are just how it manifests now.' then you ask: okay, but what is this ancient thing if you strip away all the modern manifestations — the census, the citizenship law, the standardized education, the very idea of a 'national culture'? and suddenly the 'nation' becomes this vague substrate of shared folkways that supposedly existed before any of those instutions. but then, when they need to prove the substrate existed, they point right back to... the institutions they just admitted were invented. its like the ontological ground keeps shifting: whenever one pillar is debunked, the 'real nation' retreats to whatever pillar hasnt been touched yet. is there an established concept for this specific shell game? i know its adjacent to hobsbawm's invented tradition and anderson's imagined communities, but those describe the invention itself, not this particular defensive dance. if there's no precise term, can we name the move and map its edges — like, how is it different from plain primordialism, and whats the difference between a legitimate historical continuity claim and this kind of retreat-to-substrate maneuver?
