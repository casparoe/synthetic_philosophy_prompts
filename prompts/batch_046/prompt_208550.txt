I hold that Darwin and Turing each executed a strange inversion of reasoning that undermines a longstanding philosophical prejudice: Darwin demonstrated that the appearance of design in biological form requires no designer's comprehension of function, and Turing demonstrated that the appearance of intelligent behavior requires no inner comprehension of meaning or intention. I take this inversion not as a mere empirical observation about evolutionary biology or computation, but as a normative constraint on the philosophy of mind and agency—specifically, that functional competence is sufficient for the ascription of intelligence, design, or moral status, and that requiring an accompanying subjective grasp or conceptual understanding is an unjustified metaphysical luxury rather than a necessary condition. What would genuinely count against this position? I want more than the familiar worry that consciousness remains unexplained, or that correlation between behavior and comprehension does not establish identity. I want to know which specific philosophical commitments—about moral responsibility, semantic content, aesthetic judgment, or the conditions for scientific understanding—would have to be false if competence without comprehension is truly sufficient. In which domains does the absence of comprehension genuinely erode the relevant status, and in which does it merely feel unsettling? Please distinguish the defeaters that would be fatal from those that are merely awkward, indicate which ones defenders of the inversion would themselves concede as damaging rather than dismiss as residual Cartesianism, and assess whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind.
