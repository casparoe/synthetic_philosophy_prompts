I serve as the senior interpretive designer for a traveling museum consortium that produces two-page explanatory frameworks for secondary-school exhibits on revolutionary episodes. Our curators—who have no archival training and must complete each framework in under forty-five minutes—need a reusable decision procedure for applying the standard triad of revolutionary explanation (structural class antagonism, internal ideological dynamics, and contingent institutional failure) to any episode, and for determining whether the episode exhibits the self-devouring pattern in which a revolution turns against its own architects. The deliverable must be a flowchart in prose: an ordered sequence of tests with clear stopping rules, explicit criteria for what counts as sufficient evidence, marked points where the decision is a normative judgment about narrative framing rather than a historical determination, and an honest account of where the procedure will misfire—particularly its tendency to confuse opportunistic purges with ideological ones. You should also inject one precise conceptual distinction not typically found in standard textbooks—specifically, the difference between trigger-contingency (a specific event that activates an already-loaded mechanism) and structural-contingency (an unrepeated institutional configuration that itself produces the outcome)—and build it into the third filter so that the procedure is genuinely usable rather than a generic checklist.

The procedure should run as follows. Filter one asks whether the episode produced durable changes in property relations or class power that survived the immediate violence; if the answer is yes, the class-struggle lens is retained, but the curator must then apply a sub-test: were the primary targets of internal violence members of the opposing class, or were they co-revolutionaries? Only the latter activates the self-devouring pattern, and here the procedure must require the curator to identify—without requiring original-language skills—three translated primary-source passages in which the accused are charged with corruption, deviation, or betrayal rather than with class membership. Filter two examines ideological dynamics through the purity-penalty loop, a mechanism you should define precisely within the procedure: revolutionary legitimacy is tied to an ever-narrowing core principle, so that holding power requires expelling those who previously embodied authority. The curator applies this by testing whether the justifications for purge invoke a doctrine of virtue, historical necessity, or corruption independent of social position, and whether the targets were defined by deviation from that doctrine; the procedure must include a concrete linguistic test—such as the presence in translated texts of terms rendered as purity or virtue or traitor-to-the-principle rather than designations of class enemies in a specifically social sense—that distinguishes ideological escalation from rhetoric masking class interest. Filter three addresses contingency through your injected distinction: ask whether altering one specific, non-replicable event plausibly reverses the outcome; if yes, check whether that event merely triggered an already-loaded structural or ideological mechanism (trigger-contingency, which does not dominate the explanation) or whether the outcome depended on an unrepeated institutional configuration that had no structural backup (structural-contingency, which does dominate). At each branch, specify the stopping rule: for example, if filters one and two both return positive but conflict—class consolidation explains the targets, while ideological language explains the justification—the procedure must halt and hand the decision to a senior historian or an ethics board, because resolving the conflict requires a normative judgment about whether to emphasize material interest or moral narrative in a public display. The procedure must also flag its own misfires: it will over-classify opportunistic purges as ideological when leadership uses purity-language cynically, and it will under-detect self-devouring in cases where class and ideology are genuinely co-constitutive.

Apply the procedure to three cases. First, run it on the Thermidorian Reaction in France in 1794: show how each filter operates on the fall of Robespierre and the purge of his allies, identify the conflict between filter one (a parliamentary realignment readable as class consolidation) and filter two (the explicit repudiation of Terror as corruption of revolutionary virtue), and state the procedure's final recommendation for the exhibit text—including an answer the museum staff will dislike, such as relegating class-struggle explanation to background context despite its popular pedagogical appeal. Second, apply it to the suppression of the sans-culottes between 1793 and 1794, testing whether the procedure correctly identifies the self-devouring pattern or misclassifies the violence as mere class consolidation against a popular faction; here, show exactly where the linguistic test of filter two succeeds or fails. Third, apply it to a hypothetical but historically plausible analog: in 1795, a revolutionary council purges its military committee not for ideological deviation but solely because a specific, unrepeated supply-chain failure during a siege made cooperation impossible; run the three filters, show whether the procedure returns structural-contingency without self-devouring or exposes its own inability to settle the case, and state precisely which judgment call remains unresolved and why the procedure cannot resolve it.
