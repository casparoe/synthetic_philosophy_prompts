I'm giving the opening remarks tommorow at this weird hybrid workshop we're running with the county planning office and the university poli-sci dept. The official topic is redistricting, but everyone knows it's really about the boundary problem — who actually belongs to the demos when you're drawing lines around people who share an infastructure system but don't share citizenship status. The room is split between urban planners who think in census blocks, philosophers who want to talk about affected interests, and a couple council members who keep saying if you live here you vote here like that's the end of the story.

I want the speech to hinge on one specific idea: democratic membership isn't a container you fall into, it's a relation you enter — specifically, a relation of reciprocal governance over shared conditions. This needs to do real structural work, not just be decoration, so I want to open with our local case: there's a neighborhood east of the river that's technically in the county, not the city, but they draw their water from our treatment plant and our board sets their rates without any representation. I don't want to solve it; I want to use it to show that every boundary line is already a moral decision about who owes whom democratic accountability. The speech should land for the planners without losing the philosophers, and it should leave the council members slightly uncomfortable rather than convinced. About four minutes, no bibliography, no as someone once argued moments — I need the concept carrying the weight, not the citation.
