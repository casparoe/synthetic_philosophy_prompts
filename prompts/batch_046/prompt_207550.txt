I retired from marine biology about five years ago and have been trying to get my head around moral philosophy since then. I've settled on a cluster of views that feel right to me, but I'm not sure they all hang together and I'd like someone to poke at them.

I'm a negative utilitarian in a loose sense: reducing suffering is the only thing with intrinsic moral weight, while producing happiness is at best instrumentally valuable. I think Peter Singer's expanding circle idea is basically right - moral progress means extending concern based on capacity to suffer, not species membership. I also think Benatar's asymmetry is correct: there's no moral reason to create happy people, but there's a strong reason not to create suffering ones. Still, I reject the extinctionist conclusion - I think a world with less suffering and continued existance is better than nothingness.

I think wild animal suffering is enormous and mostly ignored, but I'm skeptical about large-scale ecological intervention because ecosystems are too complex and our predictive models are weak. I think phenomenal consciousness is necessary for moral status, and I lean that insects probably lack it given their neural architecture, though I'm not sure. I also think we don't need to solve the hard problem of consciousness to do ethics, since suffering is functionally identifiable through behavior and neurobiology. And I should mention that I think current AI has no moral status at all, but if we ever build artificial systems with the kind of integrated processing we see in mammalian brains, that might change - its not a bright line for me.

Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which ones only look incompatible but dissolve once you look closer, and for each real conflict what's the cheapest revision that restores consistency. Order them by severity. Don't lecture me on negative utilitarianism in general - audit this specific set.
