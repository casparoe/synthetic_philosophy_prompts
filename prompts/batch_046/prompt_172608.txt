I am a retired statistical physicist who has, since leaving active research, taken up serious study of philosophy of science and cosmology. I am presently assisting in the design of an advanced graduate seminar on epistemology and physical cosmology, and I wish to include a take-home examination question that tests conceptual precision rather than memorized taxonomy.

Please draft one essay question, suitable for a 48-hour take-home examination with an approximate limit of 1,200 words, intended for upper-level graduate students in philosophy who need not have specialized training in physics. The subject must be the Boltzmann brain problem.

Crucially, the question must not be answerable by rehearsing the familiar self-undermining argument—that if Boltzmann brains are statistically dominant then one's own reasoning is unreliable, hence one should reject the cosmological premise. Instead, the question should direct students to a more specific conceptual tension: on the one hand, the local entropy gradient surrounding an observer serves as powerful empirical evidence for a low-entropy cosmological past; on the other hand, statistical mechanics entails that a minimal thermal fluctuation capable of producing precisely one's present mental state requires far less total entropy reduction than the production of an entire cosmological history consistent with that mental state. The question should ask students to evaluate whether this tension can be resolved by appealing to a principle I shall call cognitive stability—namely, the constraint that rational agents ought to assign negligible credence to cosmological hypotheses that are incompatible with the sustained, coherent exercise of reasoning over time—and, further, whether invoking this principle illegitimately begs the question against the Boltzmann hypothesis or rather represents a legitimate, non-empirical prior on rational belief-formation.

Please provide the following, clearly labeled:

1. The examination question itself, stating the word limit, the time available, and the precise conceptual crux students must address.

2. A model answer of approximately 600 words that takes a definite, argued position on whether cognitive stability resolves the tension without begging the question, and that explicitly engages with the statistical-mechanical reasoning rather than treating the issue as purely a priori.

3. A marking scheme totaling 25 points, divided among five specific analytical moves or insights that a top answer must contain. For each move, state its point value (summing to 25), and provide a one-line description of the characteristic mistake that causes students to miss or mishandle that move.

The marking scheme should be designed to distinguish responses that genuinely understand the interplay between thermodynamic reasoning and epistemic normativity from those that merely summarize standard philosophy-of-mind objections to Boltzmann brains.
