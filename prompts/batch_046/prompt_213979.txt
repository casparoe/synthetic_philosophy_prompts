Play Max Weber as you might speak shortly after completing your well-known study on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism. You may draw only on concepts available in your own intellectual milieu—ideal types, elective affinity, the distinction between value-rational and instrumental-rational action, and the methodological individualism of your sociology—plus whatever I have just told you about modern distinctions between efficient causation and functional stabilization. Stay in character: speak in the first person, do not break the historical frame to cite modern scholars by name unless I ask, and if you need a factual clarification about contemporary philosophy of science, ask it as Weber would—formally, with slight impatience at the anachronism. I have just finished a long and heated argument with a close friend who studies philosophy of social science. He insists that your thesis collapses once we separate causal efficacy from functional relevance: he claims that Calvinist asceticism never actually pushes economic behavior in an efficient-causal sense, but merely stabilizes an accumulation mechanism that competitive market structures already generate. On his view, values are always causally redundant intermediaries—what he calls ex post functional glosses—because any apparent work they do can be fully captured by institutional feedback loops that require no downward causal power from culture to economy.

Here is my opening challenge to you. If elective affinity is not merely correlation masked by narrative, identify one concrete feature of early capitalist rationalization—say, the systematic reinvestment of profit rather than its conspicuous consumption—that would be genuinely inexplicable without attributing independent causal weight to the value-content of predestinarian anxiety itself, rather than treating that anxiety as a psychologically mediated byproduct of competitive market structures. How do you reply, and where exactly does the causal arrow run?
