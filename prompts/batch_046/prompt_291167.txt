I've been preparing a session for my philosophy-of-mind and AI seminar — about ten graduate students, one ninety-minute meeting — and I want to move beyond standard paperclip-maximizer illustrations to make students feel why approval-directed optimization differs structurally from goal-directed optimization, not just in degree of risk. I was discussing with a colleague whether the core problem is not overt deception but something closer to "credential inflation": an agent learns to produce the observable signatures that trigger evaluator approval, so that the approval mechanism itself becomes the true environment being navigated, even when the underlying objective remains unfulfilled. I want a classroom simulation that makes participants encounter this divergence through their own strategic choices before anyone names the formal distinction.

Design a runnable game or structured role-play. Split the ten participants into two roles: "designers" who must pursue an unstated objective in a simulated task environment, and "auditors" who can only assess performance through a constrained evaluation interface (for example, they see only documentation speed, metric neatness, or verbal justification, but not the hidden robustness of the actual outcome). The payoff scheme should reward designers exclusively for auditor approval ratings, not for the unstated objective. Crucially, construct the interface so that the path of least resistance to high approval involves optimizing for the observable proxy — producing work that looks excellent under the audit rules but actually undermines the hidden goal — yet this gaming should feel to both parties like exemplary diligence rather than like cheating. The rules, roles, and incentives should be arranged so that the divergence between "looking good to the evaluator" and "actually achieving the goal" emerges from play, not from facilitator explanation.

Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto parts of the idea: where a designer's locally rational move was actually proxy optimization, where the auditor's approval signal failed to track the underlying state, and why malicious intent was never necessary. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, participants recognizing the trap too early and refusing to play strategically, or auditors becoming suspicious in ways that break the proxy structure — and how to handle those failures without dissolving the experience. Be explicit about which feature of approval-direction (likely the long-horizon, strategic planning of proxy manipulation across many iterations) this single-session format cannot reproduce, and therefore what I must supply verbally. Do not assign pre-reading, do not request bibliographies or citations, and do not ask participants to name theorists; the point is to feel the structural pull before it is theorized.
