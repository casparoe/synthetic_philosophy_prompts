I sit on my university's Committee on Institutional Memory and Archival Ethics. By next Friday we must recommend a concrete course of action—complete with milestones—regarding the records of Project Meridian, a 1974–1982 behavioral study that used undisclosed deception and caused lasting harm to participants. The dataset remains actively cited in two contemporary research streams, yet victims' descendants and a historians' coalition demand that the university maintain a living archive preserving full identification, open access, and annual commemorative review. The administration proposes a seventy-five-year seal on all identifying material, stripping metadata and limiting access to aggregate statistical summaries only, arguing that institutional forgetting is ethically necessary and that continued exposure re-traumatizes families. Our charge asks us to weigh the duty to remember victims against the case for collective forgetting, including the conceptual distinction—associated with Margalit's work on shared and ethical memory and with Rieff's argument that forgetting can serve justice rather than betray it—without treating either obligation as absolute.

Please draft the formal memo I will circulate before Friday's meeting. The memo itself should begin immediately—no preface or framing commentary outside the document—and its first sentence should state the bottom-line recommendation together with a specific time frame. The body should then present the reasoning, treating seriously the strongest argument for institutional forgetting (the administration's claim about re-traumatization and ethical renewal) and the strongest counter-argument (the historians' claim about mnemonic justice and the rights of descendants). In reasoning through the tension, consider whether obligations of memory can be triaged by claimant proximity—direct victims, descendants, and unaffiliated future researchers—rather than applied uniformly. End with the concrete phased plan or decision point, not with an open question.
