I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through philosophy of mind without the scaffolding of a formal course. Lately I have become absorbed in Douglas Hofstadter’s proposal that what we call the self is not a substance confined to one skull but a high-level pattern of recursive, self-referential information—something like a strange loop—that could, in principle, be instantiated with sufficient fidelity in another brain. I want to design a single ninety-minute discussion for a small group of fellow retired scientists and engineers, people who are fluent in feedback loops and information theory but have no background in philosophy, on the specific question of whether an “I” can be genuinely distributed across brains and what “persistence of the self in others” would require. I do not want a lecture delivered to me; I want an explanatory plan I can execute: the exact dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and why each relies on the one before it; the single carrying example that should bear the weight of the idea, together with a precise statement of what properties it must possess—complete substrate independence, a requirement for recursive self-reference in the receiving system, and an explicit failure condition when that recursion is absent—and what properties it must lack, particularly anything that makes the self sound like a file that can be emailed or copied like software; the predictable misconceptions this audience will form and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; a final check that reveals whether they have grasped the idea rather than merely assented to it; and an explicit list of what theoretical machinery should be named as beyond scope and left untouched.

Because my audience will arrive with two default assumptions—that consciousness is either a private light inside one skull or that “pattern talk” is merely a poetic way of saying we are computers—I want the plan to open with a framing that avoids both dualism and crude computationalism. Specifically, I want to inject the idea of fragmentary persistence: not the full duplication of one self-pattern into another brain, but the case where partial, overlapping reconstructions of the pattern exist across two or more brains, so that the question becomes whether “I” am distributed rather than duplicated. Map out the misconception sequence I should expect: first, the confusion of pattern-identity with physical similarity; second, the slide into thinking that distributed selfhood implies some form of telepathic access or shared experience between the brains; third, the objection that if the pattern is reconstructed elsewhere there are now two people with equal claim to being the original, which seems to dissolve identity altogether. For each misconception, specify whether it should be headed off before the carrying example is introduced, during the example’s elaboration, or immediately after, and describe the brief counter-move that neutralizes it without derailing the arc. Propose the one example that can carry all of this—rejecting, if you think it inappropriate, my temptation to use a computer virus replicating across servers, and explaining exactly why your alternative succeeds where that fails. Then design a single closing question that forces the group to apply the distinction between pattern-continuity and material-continuity directly to the fragmentary-persistence scenario; if they answer it well, they have understood. Finally, tell me explicitly which bodies of theory—whether formal accounts of personal identity, neuroscience of mirror systems, Gödelian self-reference, or other specialized frameworks—must be acknowledged as present but kept outside the ninety-minute boundary, so that I do not open a conceptual door I cannot close.
