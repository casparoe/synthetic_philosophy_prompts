I recently spent an evening arguing with a close friend about the moral significance of human extinction. She insisted that extinction can only be bad in virtue of the suffering and premature deaths it causes: if the species were to end peacefully, long after every current person has lived out a full natural lifespan, there would be no remaining harm to lament. I found myself resisting this, partly because I have been thinking about Samuel Scheffler's idea—sometimes called the afterlife conjecture—that human beings have a deep interest in the continuation of the human project and culture beyond their own deaths, an interest that is not reducible to concern for the welfare of specific identifiable descendants. But our conversation stalled because I could not clearly articulate whether this afterlife interest is genuinely a separate source of value, or merely a psychological quirk that philosophy has mislcharacterized as normative weight.

Please explain the conceptual architecture of this problem, focusing specifically on how Scheffler's conjecture is supposed to generate reasons against extinction that survive even when every death is voluntary and painless. In particular, I want to understand the precise distinction between person-affecting harms—those that wrong specific future individuals by preventing their existence or making their lives worse—and the kind of impersonal or collective harm that extinction might represent if we take the afterlife conjecture seriously. How does the non-identity problem bear on this? If future generations never come into being, there is no particular person who is made worse off by their absence, yet Scheffler seems to think we still suffer a kind of loss. Is that loss best understood as a frustration of our present preferences for continuity, or does it depend on a more robust claim about the intrinsic value of human culture, science, and narrative extending indefinitely? And if it is the latter, does that commit us to an implausible form of collective essentialism, or can the claim be defended in terms of the structure of human meaning-making without reifying the species as a metaphysical subject?

Please avoid citing specific texts, page numbers, or offering reading lists; I am looking for a conceptual map rather than a bibliography. I am especially interested in whether there is a coherent middle position between my friend's purely person-affecting view and a full-blooded commitment to the infinite value of humanity's future—a position that might ground our reluctance to accept extinction in the disruption of long-term projects we have already invested with meaning, without requiring us to treat every potential future person as having a pre-existing claim to exist.
