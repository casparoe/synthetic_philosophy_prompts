As a retired biologist who spent decades studying fixed action patterns in invertebrates before turning to philosophy in retirement, I have become preoccupied with Dennett's notion of sphexishness—the mechanical repetition of behavioral sequences regardless of contextual change—and whether it genuinely marks the boundary between determinism and free will. In my seminar discussions, I have constructed what I take to be a reductio that collapses the distinction entirely, but I cannot locate the fault. The argument runs as follows. (1) Sphexishness is behavioral rigidity: the execution of a fixed motor program that cannot be interrupted or modified by real-time feedback. (2) Free will, on any compatibilist account worth defending, requires the capacity to revise one's course in light of reasons, which is to say, non-sphexish flexibility. (3) Any apparently flexible response can, in principle, be implemented by a deterministic mechanism with a sufficiently large state-space and an extensive lookup table mapping every possible input vector to an appropriate output, without any genuine evaluation occurring at the time of action. (4) Therefore, the observable difference between sphexish rigidity and human flexibility is not a difference in kind—mechanism versus evaluation—but merely a quantitative difference in the size of the pre-encoded repertoire. (5) If sphexishness disqualifies a system from having free will, and human flexibility is just large-scale sphexishness with more entries, then determinism disqualifies humans as well, leaving only libertarian free will intact. (6) But if we reject (2) and allow that sphexish beings might possess free will after all, then the sphex wasp, whose burrow-inspection loop is exquisitely reason-responsive within its ecological niche, must be granted some minimal form of agency. (7) Consequently, either we accept that free will comes in degrees proportional to state-space size, which seems to trivialize the concept, or we admit that the sphexish/non-sphexish boundary was never doing the philosophical work we assigned it. I find myself inclined to preserve the intuition that the wasp lacks free will and that humans possess it, but I am unsure whether the cost is abandoning compatibilism, accepting a threshold-based repertoire-expansion criterion that feels ad hoc, or identifying an equivocation somewhere between behavioral description and mechanistic explanation in step (3). Which premise or inference should I surrender, and what does that surrender commit me to?
