Question: Michael Walzer argues for the moral equality of combatants (MEC), maintaining that soldiers on opposing sides possess equal rights to kill and be killed regardless of the justice of their cause. He grounds this in the separation of jus ad bellum from jus in bello and in the shared vulnerability of soldiers, who are not responsible for the political decision to go to war. Jeff McMahan rejects MEC through a liability-based account: unjust combatants lack justification for killing just combatants because they contribute to an unjust threat. Consider a conflict in which Side A wages a just war with an all-volunteer army, while Side B wages an unjust war using only conscripted soldiers who are fully informed of the injustice of their cause yet face severe punishment for refusal. Does the non-culpable, coerced status of Side B's soldiers restore moral equality between the sides? In your answer, clearly distinguish liability from culpability, and assess whether Walzer's separation of jus ad bellum and jus in bello can accommodate this distinction.

The pupil's answer is as follows.

Walzer defends the moral equality of combatants by insisting on a strict separation between jus ad bellum and jus in bello. Because soldiers are not responsible for the decision to wage war, and because they all face reciprocal dangers on the battlefield, they share an equal moral status irrespective of cause. This means that even a soldier fighting for an unjust side retains the same right to kill as one fighting justly.

McMahan rejects this via a liability-based critique. He argues that unjust combatants contribute to an unjust threat and therefore forfeit their right not to be killed; they are not permitted to kill just combatants, who retain a right of defence. For McMahan, permission to kill depends on the justice of one's cause rather than on one's institutional role.

In the present case, Side B's soldiers are conscripted, fully aware that their cause is unjust, and continue only because of severe punishment for refusal. They are therefore not blameworthy for their participation. Since McMahan's liability argument requires that unjust combatants be morally responsible for the threat they pose, these conscripts are not liable. Hence they do not forfeit their rights, and moral equality is restored between them and Side A's volunteers. Volunteer soldiers on an unjust side, by contrast, remain liable because they chose to fight.

This shows that McMahan's critique succeeds only against volunteer armies, and that Walzer's account is actually correct for conscripted forces. The equality of combatants is therefore not universal but depends on the mode of recruitment.
