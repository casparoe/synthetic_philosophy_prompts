I cover philosophy of science and conceptual history for the Ideas desk, and I am preparing a long-form feature on how Aristotle's sea-battle argument in De Interpretatione continues to structure modern debates over bivalence, branching time, and actualist modal semantics. For the essay's close, I want a villanelle—not ornament, but argument in verse. Use strict form: nineteen lines, iambic pentameter, two refrains that alternate through the stanzas and converge in the final couplet without resolving. One refrain should carry the actualist intuition that among all branching futures only one path is genuinely real, as though marked by a thin red line of actuality running through the tree; the other should carry the Aristotelian anxiety that if tomorrow's battle already bears a definite truth-value, then its occurrence is fixed and contingency collapses into necessity. Through the six stanzas, move from the branching image to the logical crisis, letting the rhyme scheme and refrain structure enact why the two positions keep passing each other. The closing couplet should place both refrains side by side, leaving the paradox intact. The poem must be accurate enough that a modal logician recognizes the actualist-possibilist distinction and that a reader of ancient philosophy recognizes the sea-battle problem, but it should contain no named scholarly citations, no bibliographic references, and no direct quotations from Aristotle's text. Write it so that a reader unfamiliar with the technical literature can still feel the logical tension, while a specialist sees that the form itself is doing the philosophical work.
