I am preparing a long-form feature for an intellectually general audience on how contemporary philosophers of meaning adjudicate lived experience when the classic categories—subjective fulfillment, objective contribution, and Camus-style absurdism—overlap and pull in opposite directions. To ground the reporting, I have reconstructed the implicit judgments of a contemporary analytic philosopher of value, whose recent essays deliberately avoid explicit system-building, across five edge cases. Her record runs as follows.

First, on the figure of Sisyphus: she holds that the labor is meaningful if and only if the agent consciously embraces its futility without secretly hoping for release; a Sisyphus who expects the boulder eventually to stay at the summit is living in self-deception and therefore not leading a meaningful life.

Second, on immersive virtual existence: she denies meaning to a life spent entirely within a fully convincing virtual environment in which the subject experiences deep creative and relational satisfaction, precisely because no causal trace extends beyond the simulation.

Third, on anonymous maintenance: she grants meaning to a worker who spends decades repairing infrastructure for a future civilization he will never inhabit and whose values he does not share, provided he understands the causal chain with accuracy, even though he feels no personal attachment to the outcome.

Fourth, on scholarly accumulation: she denies meaning to a scholar who spends a lifetime compiling an exhaustive catalog of grammatical errors in an extinct language, pursued with intense passion and with full awareness that the work serves no communicative, scientific, or aesthetic purpose.

Fifth, on ephemeral creation: she grants meaning to a sculptor who produces a monumental work knowing it will be destroyed immediately upon completion, never witnessed and never recorded; the sculptor feels neither satisfaction nor despair, yet continues out of an internal commitment to the act itself.

The new case I need her judgment on is this. A philosopher lives in total isolation, writing rigorous arguments that demonstrate the impossibility of objective meaning. She publishes nothing, expects no audience, and feels neither rebellion nor satisfaction—only a cold, sustained recognition that her conclusions are correct. She continues writing solely because logical consistency demands that she not abandon a valid line of inquiry without reason. The arguments, if encountered, would be sound, but they are not encountered, and she knows this.

If one is reconstructing her implicit framework, what should her verdict be on this sixth case if she wishes to remain consistent with her own precedents? Which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog, and is that analogy genuinely determinative? Identify at least two competing rules—perhaps one centered on teleological residue and another on phenomenological integrity, or any other pair that fit her record equally well—that yield different predictions here. For each rule, state the principle she would be adopting if she followed it, and say whether her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the case. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish these competing interpretations of her position.
