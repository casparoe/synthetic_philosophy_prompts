I'm drafting an internal review procedre for my office — I'm the senior sign-off at a regional science-policy board, and we recieve about a dozen submissions each month where local teams claim they've 'established knowledge' of some risk, then want to extend it straight into policy recommendations. The headache is always epistemic closure: they write 'we know X, so we know Y' without asking whether Nozick's tracking conditions survive the jump. I need a practical checklist my three reviewers can run in twenty minutes flat, working only from the paper in front of them, no philosophy doctorates, no searching online. It should take Nozick's sensitivity and adherence conditions and apply them specifically to entailment transmission — logical, causal, and statistical — with an explicit rule for when the nearest counterfactual world shifts far enough that closure should be blocked. Mark every step where judgement can't be eliminated, especially the 'modal proximity' call.

Fix the constraints properly: I have to sign off, errors affect funding allocations, and we have exactly half an hour per file. The deliverable should be ordered steps, then apply it to three submissions: (a) direct testing finds lead in a reservoir, so they claim knowledge the school downstream is at risk; (b) trial data shows a vaccine cuts infection by thirty percent, so they claim knowledge it will do the same in a rural area with broken cold-chain storage; and (c) a case where the base belief barely tracks truth but the entailment is rock-solid, meaning the procedure denies the extended claim and kills an easy, cheap policy win that my team will be annoyed to lose. Be honest about what it can't do — I think it can't settle disputes about which possible world counts as 'nearest' without domain expertise, and it doesn't cover testimonial chains or belief revision under new evidence. Oh, and I was arguing with a mate who did a course on Nozick and said adherence works differently across entailments; I'm not convinced, but build that ambiguity in as a marked judgement point rather than pretending it's settled.
