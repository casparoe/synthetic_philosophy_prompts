I've been turning over the torture-versus-dust-specks problem as a formal reductio and I'm stuck on whether it's genuinely valid or where the hidden slide occurs. Here is the chain, stated as precisely as I can manage it.

(1) If moral harm is cardinal, interpersonally comparable, and aggregates linearly, then 3^^^3 people each suffering a dust speck in the eye produces more total harm than one person tortured for fifty years.

(2) That conclusion is intuitively unacceptable; therefore linear aggregation across persons must fail.

(3) The natural repair is a non-Archimedean value structure—say, hyperreal-valued moral weights—where torture occupies an infinitely higher order of magnitude than a dust speck, so that no finite number of the latter can ever outweigh one instance of the former.

(4) On such a scale, the same reasoning applies at every tier: death is infinitely worse than any number of paralyses, paralysis infinitely worse than any number of broken bones, and so on, yielding an infinite lexical hierarchy of harm types.

(5) Moral reasoning then becomes lexicographic maximization: eliminate all harms at the highest present tier before addressing lower tiers, regardless of quantity.

(6) Now apply this structure to risk. A policy that carries a non-zero chance of causing one death must be rejected, no matter how many paralyses it prevents, because any probability-weighted death dominates any finite quantity of lower-tier harm.

(7) By induction, we must reject any action that risks even the smallest harm in a higher tier, however remote, even when it prevents arbitrarily large lower-tier harms.

(8) The result is total decision paralysis: no medical triage, no resource allocation across harm-types, and no risk-taking whatsoever is permissible.

(9) Nobody accepts that paralysis, so the non-Archimedean repair must be abandoned.

(10) But abandoning it returns us to linear aggregation, and therefore to the conclusion in (1) that 3^^^3 dust specks outweigh torture.

(11) So we face a dilemma: either accept the dust-speck domination, or accept moral paralysis. Both seem false.

My question: where does this argument go wrong? Is there an equivocation between ex post aggregation and ex ante decision-making? A scope error in applying lexicographic ordering to probabilistic contexts? Or is one of the apparently plausible premises—perhaps (3)'s inference from the failure of linearity to a full non-Archimedean hierarchy—simply the weak link?

If forced to preserve the intuition that torture dominates arbitrary dust specks, I would rather jettison (5)-(7) and pay the price of a restricted lexical ordering that applies only to certain outcomes; but I want to understand exactly what that price is. Please diagnose the flawed step, explain why it looked legitimate, and spell out the costs of the available repairs.
