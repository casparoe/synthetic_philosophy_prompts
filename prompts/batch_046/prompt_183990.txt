I am writing from an upper-level course on moral contractualism, and I have been working through Scanlon's claim that an act is wrong just in case it violates a principle that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. I keep running into the aggregation problem, but rather than asking for a bibliography or reading list, I want to test whether my own intuitions about concrete cases can be captured by a single refined principle.

Here are my verdicts.

First, in the standard single-victim case—one agent inflicts severe suffering on one other to secure only a modest benefit for herself—I think the victim can reasonably reject the principle that permits the act.

Second, in the distributed-committee case: one hundred independent local boards each enact a separate policy that imposes a barely noticeable burden on ten people, with no board aware of the others and no individual able to identify her contribution to the total harm. Individually, each board's principle seems rejectable only at a cost that outweighs the local harm; collectively, however, the burden is catastrophic. I think the global pattern is rejectable, even though no single board's action is.

Third, in the temporal-fragmentation case: the same person receives a harm spread across decades so that at no moment is the harm noticeable, yet the cumulative effect over a life is grave. I think the person can reject the principle if she considers her life as a whole, but not if she considers only her momentary states.

Fourth, in the anonymous-market case: millions of consumers each make a purchase that contributes an imperceptible harm to distant workers, and no consumer can trace her particular contribution. I think this feels different from the distributed-committee case—perhaps because anonymity breaks the conditions for reasonable rejection—though I am unsure whether contractualism can accommodate that distinction without smuggling in non-contractualist conditions.

Is there one contractualist principle that delivers all four verdicts? If the answer is no, which verdict should I overturn and why? Please state the principle precisely, indicating which clause handles each case, and say what it predicts for a further case I have not listed—say, individual carbon-emitting acts that collectively drive climate damage. Finally, please be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail: which argument or consideration would lead you to overturn your verdict, and whether your interpretation of "reasonable rejection" depends on a contested reading of Scanlon's framework that a defender of the view might dispute.
