I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for a philosophy reading group composed mainly of analytic philosophers with no prior training in Indian philosophy, and I want the group to leave with a precise grasp of Jain anekāntavāda—specifically that reality is irreducibly many-sided and that any single predication is only conditionally true (syādvāda)—without reducing it to epistemic humility, linguistic ambiguity, or Western dialetheism. Do not explain the doctrine to me; instead, plan the explanatory arc I should follow. First, lay out the dependency order: what must be in place before syādvāda makes sense—perhaps the Jain distinction between enduring substance (dravya) and transient mode (paryāya), or perhaps a preliminary argument about why exclusive predication fails for complex objects—and justify why that particular foundation has to come first. Second, identify the one concrete carrying example that should bear the whole session; it must involve an object that genuinely possesses incompatible properties without contradiction, and it must not be the overused parable of the blind men and the elephant; specify exactly what the example needs to include (e.g., a change in conditions that alters the predicate without altering the substrate) and what it must avoid (e.g., subjective differences in observer position) so that it illustrates conditional predication rather than mere perspectival limitation. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form or import: I expect they will treat anekāntavāda as a thesis about our cognitive limitations, as a claim about ambiguous language, and as a form of dialetheism that accepts true contradictions; for each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where I should head it off and with what conceptual distinction. Fourth, include one question I can pose at the end that reveals whether they have understood the ontological claim—about the structure of the object rather than about the limits of the knower—rather than just reproducing the logical form. Fifth, state explicitly what portion of the classical seven-fold predication schema (saptabhaṅgī) I should omit at this level, particularly whether to include the avaktavya mode, and why leaving it out protects the explanation from a specific misunderstanding. Finally, suggest one brief check for understanding, earlier in the session, that would catch whether they have already conflated many-sidedness with the idea that all competing descriptions are equally valid.
