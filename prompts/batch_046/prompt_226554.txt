My friend insists that Arendt’s claim—that extreme moral failure can arise not from ideological fanaticism but from unreflective bureaucratic conformity—depends on a face-to-face human bureaucracy that has now been replaced by technical systems. He argues that when harm is distributed across machine-learning pipelines, remote drone interfaces, and data-science teams who never meet the people affected, the psychological mechanism Arendt described has been structurally superseded by algorithmic opacity rather than by moral thoughtlessness.

I want a committed answer, not a survey of options. Assuming Arendt were alive today and had closely studied contemporary algorithmic governance—specifically automated immigration-risk scoring, predictive policing algorithms, and remote-targeting drone systems—would she maintain that the banality of evil still applies? Take a definite position. Would she locate the same kind of unreflective conformity in the data scientist who trains a model that systematically denies asylum applications without ever examining individual cases, or in the drone operator who executes strikes through a screen interface with minimal emotional engagement? Or would she argue that the diffusion of agency through algorithms creates a categorically different moral phenomenon, closer to what she described as rule by nobody?

Please defend one specific reading of her framework with reasons drawn from her study of Eichmann. State clearly which modern cases your reading covers and which it excludes, and be explicit about where you are extending her actual writings beyond what she addressed. Do not present a balanced menu of interpretations; commit to one and argue for it.
