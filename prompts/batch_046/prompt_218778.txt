I'm in this philosophy-of-rational-agency course and me and my study group were trying to map out the Löbian obstacle for a presentation. We ended up with this argument that seems to show any agent that trusts its own future reasoning is either omniscient or broken, which can't be right, but I can't find the exit. Maybe there's a modal slide between normative reflection and formal provability? Anyway here's the chain we built:

(1) A rational agent should satisfy reflection: if it knows it will eventually come to believe P after honest, error-free reasoning, then now it should assign P credence at least equal to its confidence in that reasoning.
(2) Write B(P) for belief and let T be the agent's internal proof system strong enough for arithmetic. The principle becomes B(B(P) → P).
(3) Löb's theorem says that in T, if T proves [B(B(P) → P) → B(P)], then T proves B(P) for every P.
(4) So if the agent actually implements reflection inside T, it derives B(P) for arbitrary P — full omniscience.
(5) But an agent believing everything is useless; one believing contradictions is broken. So rational self-trust is impossible.
(6) Even weakening reflection to only apply when credence is above 0.9, graded-modal versions of Löb still push every qualified belief through, so there's no stable middle ground.
(7) Therefore any self-modifying agent that wants to trust its future reasoning must either disable its arithmetic proof-checker or abandon reflection entirely, which feels like a crazy price.

I'd really like to keep (1) and (2) since they sound like basic rationality, but I'm willing to pay if I have to. Where does the argument slip? Which step is the one to reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Also please seperate what's settled logic from what's genuinely contested about real agents or AI systems, and don't present a contested view as the consensus — I have a feeling the jump from (2) to (3) occured to us as suspicious but we couldn't name why.
