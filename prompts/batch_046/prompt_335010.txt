I'm drafting the final essay question for an upper-level seminar in philosophy of value, aimed at students who have already read the standard literature on informational privacy and its legal history. I want a prompt that prevents them from simply rehearsing Westin's four states of privacy or the Warren-Brandeis tort framework, and instead forces them to work with a distinction I will build into the question itself: the difference between informational privacy, understood as an agent's control over the flow of data about herself, and what we might call epistemic shelter, understood as the social condition in which certain beliefs others hold about one remain unchallenged and therefore do not constrain one's practical reasoning. The essay should ask students to diagnose a tension in the common defense that privacy is valuable because it protects an "authentic self" — namely, that this defense often appeals to opacity about features that, if revealed, would not actually compromise autonomy in any informational sense. Students should then be asked whether epistemic shelter can carry independent value, or whether it necessarily collapses back into a derivative form of informational control, and what follows for whether privacy rights should be framed as individual entitlements or as social-structural goods.

Please write the exam question itself (suitable for a 75-minute in-class essay), a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer. The marking scheme should specify the exact conceptual moves an A answer must contain: (1) a precise characterization of informational privacy that distinguishes it from mere secrecy; (2) an articulation of epistemic shelter that does not reduce it to a preference for ignorance; (3) a diagnosis of why the "authentic self" defense risks conflating the two; (4) an argument either that epistemic shelter is independently valuable (with a plausible account of what that value consists in) or that it is not, with clear reasoning; and (5) an evaluation of the rights-versus-structure implication that follows from the answer to (4). For each of these five moves, indicate what a B-level answer typically does wrong — for instance, treating the distinction as purely taxonomic, or assuming that if epistemic shelter is valuable it must automatically ground an individual right. The total marks should be out of 20, with specific weights for each move, and include a brief note on the most common failure mode that separates B work from A work. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists in either the question or the answer.
