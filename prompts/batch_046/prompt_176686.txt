I am fluent in the vocabulary of modern consequentialist ethics—expected utility over world-states, agent-neutral versus agent-relative reasons, preference-aggregation rules, and side-constraint formalisms—but I know Buddhist ethics only at a high level. Restate the normative architecture of classical Buddhist ethics, specifically the interaction between cetanā (intention), kamma (action and its ethical quality), and the cessation of dukkha as the orienting goal, using only that consequentialist vocabulary. Do this systematically: translate the five precepts, the role of mental purification (citta-visuddhi), and the distinction between wholesome (kusala) and unwholesome (akusala) roots into terms of outcomes, expected value, or constraints. Then deliver the residue report: which elements translate without distortion, which require violence to either framework, and which claims about moral life have no counterpart in consequentialist language at all. In particular, assess whether the untranslatable residue—if any—points to a genuine limitation in outcome-based evaluation (for instance, the moral weight of present mental states regardless of future causal efficacy) or whether it is merely the friction of forcing a soteriological practice into a secular evaluative schema. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle Buddhist assumptions into consequentialist terms and call them equivalent.
