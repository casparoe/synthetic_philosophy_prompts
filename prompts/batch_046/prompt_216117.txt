I just finished a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend about Singer’s drowning-child case, and I want to know whether a specific move we kept circling has an established name.

We both grant the core intuition: if you can pull a child from a pond at trivial cost to yourself, you are required to do so. Where we split is on the inference to institutional rescue—say, sending money to an organization that prevents deaths overseas. My friend does not invoke psychological distance, the problem of large numbers, or the standard “too demanding” objection. Instead, he offers a structural claim about mediation: in the pond case, the agent stands in an unmediated causal relation to the rescue, whereas in institutional giving the agent’s contribution is dispersed through administrative layers, supply chains, and local intermediaries. He insists that this mediation does not merely reduce efficacy; it transforms the moral geometry of the act itself, turning a direct duty of rescue into something closer to participation in an opaque collective mechanism. He is careful to say this is not skepticism about charity effectiveness, nor is it the diffusion-of-responsibility problem familiar from collective-action theory; it is specifically an objection to treating mediated institutional aid as morally analogical to direct rescue.

I have noticed this pattern elsewhere in debates about rescue obligations—people accepting that distant suffering matters, yet denying that institutional giving inherits the moral structure of the pond case, and doing so on grounds that target the causal architecture of the act rather than distance, cost, or numbers. Is there an established concept or literature for this precise objection? If so, how is it delineated from the near/far distinction, from organizational skepticism, and from collective-action diffusion? If not, please coin a precise term, define it, and provide boundary cases that separate it from (a) plain doubts about charity efficiency, (b) the claim that rescue duties weaken with physical proximity, and (c) the idea that group membership dilutes individual responsibility.
