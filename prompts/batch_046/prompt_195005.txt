I am a doctoral candidate in metaphysics with a focus on personal identity and reproductive ethics, and I have been struggling to apply theoretical distinctions to a concrete disagreement that recently arose within my own family, which I think exemplifies a deeper philosophical problem about origins and essential properties, though I want to avoid mischaracterizing it with overly technical jargon. My older sister learned that she was conceived through in vitro fertilization using embryos that had been cryopreserved for several years, and that for a brief period a different embryo—formed from the same parents but with a different sperm and egg pairing—had been designated for implantation before being replaced due to a clerical revision in the clinic's records. She has become preoccupied with the idea that she is not the person she was meant to be, conceding that she is numerically the same organism that developed from the actually implanted embryo, yet insisting that there is a normative and metaphysical gap between who she actually is and who she essentially should have been. She claims that this gap affects her sense of belonging and her moral relationship to the family narrative built around the intended child, not because she lacks psychological continuity with herself, but because her specific gametic origin is constitutive of her true identity in a way that bodily and mental continuity cannot override.

Several relatives dismiss this as a psychological quirk with no philosophical weight, treating her concern as a confusion between causal history and essential properties applied to practical family obligations. Others feel she has identified something genuinely puzzling about whether reproductive origins—down to the particular cells involved—can be essential to personal identity independently of psychological or bodily continuity. I find myself uncertain whether she is articulating a coherent metaphysical claim about origin conditions, or whether she is conflating counterfactual siblinghood with a displaced self and misapplying that distinction to questions of responsibility and familial membership. Could you help me understand the conceptual structure of her position? Specifically, I want to know whether there is a defensible distinction between numerical identity and an essential origin property that would allow someone to be continuous with their actual body and mind, yet metaphysically displaced from their true self because of a different gametic origin, and whether such a distinction, if coherent, has any bearing on ordinary questions about personal obligation and belonging, or whether it collapses once we properly separate metaphysical essentialism from the conditions of psychological continuity.
