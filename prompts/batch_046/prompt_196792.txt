As a high school student drafting an essay on historiographical bias, I have become stuck on a pattern in my own judgments about Whig history—the temptation to write the past as an inevitable march toward present institutions and values. Rather than asking for a bibliography or citations, I want to test whether my intuitions form a coherent principle.

Here are my verdicts on specific historical judgments.

First, interpreting the Glorious Revolution of 1688 chiefly as an inevitable stepping-stone toward modern parliamentary democracy strikes me as plainly Whiggish, and I reject that framing.

Second, describing the development of double-entry bookkeeping in Renaissance Italy as an early rationalization of economic practice that eventually made modern capitalism possible strikes me as structurally similar—both link a past event to a present institution—yet I do not feel it is Whiggish in the objectionable sense; I accept it.

Third, evaluating the failure of the British Chartist movement as a "fortunate delay" that allowed working-class representation to mature gradually rather than through abrupt revolution strikes me as Whiggish, and I reject it.

Fourth, mourning the decline of Latin as a universal scholarly language in the eighteenth century purely as a loss of cosmopolitan intellectual community—without treating vernacular science as progress—strikes me as non-Whiggish, and I approve of this framing.

Fifth, I find a particularly hard pair. Calling the destruction of the Library of Alexandria a catastrophic setback that delayed modern science by centuries feels Whiggish to me, because it treats the present state of science as the proper endpoint of ancient history. But describing the spread of Gutenberg’s printing press as accelerating humanity’s path toward universal literacy and democratic participation feels acceptable, even though it also frames a past event as moving toward our present. I cannot reconcile why one is objectionable and the other is not.

Finally, I have not formed a clear view on this case: a historian argues that the Roman Republic’s refusal to extend representative institutions to its provinces was “a missed structural opportunity that eventually forced imperial collapse and delayed federal governance until the modern era.” I want to know what my principle, if I have one, should say about this.

Is there a single, precisely stated principle—perhaps one that distinguishes normative teleology from causal genealogy, or that turns on whether an evaluation is counterfactually robust—that delivers all five of my verdicts? Please state the principle, indicate which clause is motivated by which of my cases, predict its verdict on the Roman-province case, and tell me honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate without contradiction. If no unitary principle works, offer two competing principles, state which verdict each must sacrifice, and explain which sacrifice is more defensible.
