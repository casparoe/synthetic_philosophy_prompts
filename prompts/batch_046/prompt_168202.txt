Hey, I've been trying to work out my own view on collective memory after reading Margalit and some of Rieff's stuff on theraputic culture, and I'm running into what feels like a category mistake but I can't quite place it. Margalit's argument, as I understand it, is that societies have a duty to remember serious wrongs not because archives are useful but because forgetting breaks solidarity with the victims—it's a relational failure, not an epistemic one. Rieff meanwhile seems to treat modern forgetting as a kind of emotional hygiene: we suppress painful collective memories so the culture can stay functioning and avoid guilt paralysis. So far so good. But then I started thinking about actual "active forgetting" mechanisms—like when a government lets commemorative sites lapse, or applies privacy-style erasure rights to historical records of abuse, or just stops teaching a particular atrocity after a generation.

My instinct is that if Margalit is right, these mechanisms are all morally defective, because they literally reduce the resolution of collective memory. But that seems wrong too; surely the duty can't require infinite archival fidelity, or we'd be obligated to preserve every traumatic detail forever. So is the confusion here that I'm treating Margalit's relational duty as if it were a data-retention obligation? And if so, does Rieff's theraputic framing actually address the same thing, or is he talking about individual psychological coping while I'm smuggling in institutional information policy? Basically, I want to know whether there's a principled line between legitimate social forgetting and betrayal of solidaraty, or whether asking for a "line" already assumes the wrong model—like optimizing a retention curve—when the ethics is actually about who is being recognized, not how much is stored.
