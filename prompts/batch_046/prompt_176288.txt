I'm a high school junior in an advanced philosophy seminar, and after working through the classic paradox of Buridan's ass, I've adopted a fairly strict position: whenever reasons, desires, and expected outcomes are in perfect equilibrium—when there is genuinely no discriminating factor—rational choice is impossible. Reason, on my view, can only weigh differences; it possesses no internal mechanism to generate a decision from absolute parity. So any actual selection in such a deadlock must be caused by something exogenous to rational agency itself, whether a stochastic neural fluctuation, an arbitrary whim, or an external perturbation. What looks like "choosing without preference" is therefore either not truly preference-free or not truly rational.

I want an honest map of this position's exposure. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Specifically, could there be an endogenous tie-breaker—such as a second-order volition not to remain suspended in indecision, or a structural cost intrinsic to continued deliberation—that breaks the symmetry without smuggling in a first-order preference for one option over the other? If so, does that defeat my view, or can a defender absorb it by redefining what counts as "rational"? Which conceptual distinctions in action theory, which observed patterns in human decision-making, or which formal results in decision theory would count as genuinely damaging rather than merely awkward? I am also worried that my view collapses into a bifurcated determinism: choices are either reason-driven when differences exist, or causally forced when they don't. Is that collapse fatal, or is it a consequence I should simply accept?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of my position could legitimately dismiss it, and say whether this view is well-insulated or genuinely vulnerable at its core.
