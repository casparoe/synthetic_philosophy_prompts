Consider the institutional practice in international criminal law and human rights reporting of applying the label genocide only when prosecutors can demonstrate that perpetrators held a specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part according to the perpetrators' own classification, while treating mass violence of comparable scale and brutality as crimes against humanity or war crimes whenever that intent is ambiguous or absent, regardless of whether the victims were objectively targeted by ethnicity, religion, or other protected characteristics. This practice means that the moral and legal naming of an atrocity depends less on the experience or structural vulnerability of the victim group and more on internal documentary evidence of perpetrator psychology—sometimes producing the result that two virtually identical campaigns of destruction receive different names because one left explicit internal records and the other did not. Given that Raphael Lemkin introduced this legal category in the mid-twentieth century, the intentionalist gatekeeping has shaped not only courtroom charging strategies but also the politics of memorialization and international obligation. What philosophical commitments about collective harm, moral ontology, and the function of legal categorization does this naming practice presuppose?
