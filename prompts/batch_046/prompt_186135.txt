My verdicts on when extreme behavioral rigidity indicates a lack of free will, and whether flexibility always rescues it. I want to know whether a single principle organizes these judgments, what it predicts for cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts it requires me to abandon.

Consider these eight situations.

1. The sphex wasp: she drags paralyzed prey to her burrow, enters to check, then pulls the prey inside. If the prey is moved a few inches during her inspection, she drags it back out and repeats the check, looping indefinitely. Verdict: this is pure mechanical sphexishness; there is no free will here, and the rigidity is not redeemed by any hidden flexibility.

2. A person with severe, untreated OCD who checks that the stove is off exactly seven times before leaving home, feeling an overwhelming compulsion and unable to stop without extreme distress. Verdict: in the act of checking, free will is absent or severely compromised; the behavior is driven rather than authored.

3. A factory worker who performs the same assembly motion thousands of times per shift, but who can stop to talk, adapt if a part is malformed, or quit the job entirely. Verdict: despite the surface rigidity, free will is fully present; the repetition is instrumental, not compulsive.

4. A large language model that generates text token by token, constrained by its weights and context window, able to vary outputs only within the statistical distribution it was trained on, with no capacity to reject its conditioning or initiate an unprompted global revision of its goals. Verdict: sphexish; no free will, though the surface variability can be large.

5. A sleepwalker who makes tea every night with identical steps, but on one occasion, finding the kettle missing, uses a saucepan without waking and continues the sequence. Verdict: I am inclined to say there is still no free will, yet the adaptive substitution feels different from the wasp's loop; it introduces a kind of flexibility that case 1 lacks, without seeming to add authorship.

6. A long-distance runner who has followed the identical ten-kilometer route at the same pace every morning for fifteen years, but who designed the regimen herself, can skip it at will, and occasionally varies the pace for aesthetic reasons. Verdict: fully free; the rigidity is self-imposed and transparent to her.

7. A contemplative practitioner who repeats the same mantra with perfect consistency for six hours, entering a state in which stopping feels psychologically impossible, though her body is physically capable of it, and she reports that she chose to enter this state and retains a background awareness that she could stop. Verdict: this is the case that troubles me most. The behavior is as rigid as case 2, yet I want to call it free, or at least not sphexish, because the rigidity is nested inside a prior and revocable authorization.

8. A chess program that, given identical board states, always selects the same optimal move, never experimenting or playing "for fun," but which can be redirected instantly by a human changing its evaluation function. Verdict: not free; its flexibility is entirely external and parametric, not internal.

The tension I cannot resolve is between cases 2, 5, and 7. Case 2 and case 7 both involve extreme repetition, a felt inability to deviate, and psychological pressure to continue; yet I treat case 2 as unfree and case 7 as free, or at least differently situated. Case 5 introduces flexibility without awareness, which seems to distinguish it from case 1 but not clearly enough to assign free will. Meanwhile, case 3 and case 6 show that rigidity itself is not the enemy: both are highly repetitive but clearly free.

So my question: is there a coherent principle that distinguishes sphexish rigidity from authored rigidity? I have been tempted by something like "free will is present when the rigidity is nested within a broader counterfactual capacity for arbitrary deviation that the agent recognizes as her own, even if unexercised" — but case 5 may satisfy part of this (there is adaptive capacity) without awareness, and case 4 might be reconstructed as having a kind of counterfactual capacity (different weights) that is not "its own." Can this principle be repaired so that it saves the distinction between cases 2 and 7, explains why case 5 remains doubtful, and does not simply collapse into "whatever I intuitively call free"? If not, which of my eight verdicts should be dropped?

Please also apply your best principle to three further cases I have not judged: (a) a musician improvising within the strict harmonic rules of a Baroque figured-bass tradition; (b) a social-insect colony that collectively shifts foraging routes when one path is blocked, with no individual ant capable of deviating from its local chemical rules; and (c) a human under deep hypnosis who carries out a post-hypnotic suggestion to count objects in a room with perfect repetition, but reports afterward that the counting felt "like mine" despite being triggered externally.

State the principle precisely, indicate which clause handles which of my original cases, note its verdicts on (a) through (c), and be explicit about which of my verdicts — especially among 2, 5, and 7 — it cannot accommodate without revision.
