I'm currently in a graduate seminar on welfare economics and the philosophy of value, and I've been developing a position that challenges the standard Robbins-style prohibition on interpersonal utility comparisons. Rather than treating utility as a private mental magnitude requiring an impossible universal unit, I want to defend the viability of what I call "phenomenal calibration protocols" — structured, cross-subjective procedures in which agents compare the experienced intensity of welfare-relevant states across standardized domains, producing relational cardinal scales anchored in intersubjective agreement rather than in an assumed intrinsic metric. On this view, the heterogeneity of tastes is a difference in preference content, not in the measurable dimension of welfare itself, and the calibration process is no more arbitrary than the standardization we accept in psychophysical measurement. I hold that this framework allows coherent social choice without collapsing into behaviorism or circular comparison, but I want an honest map of its exposure: what specific empirical findings about subjective report or neural reward signatures, what formal results in social choice theory, and what philosophical arguments about the privacy or incommensurability of experience would actually count against it? I am particularly concerned with whether there is a decisive objection showing that phenomenal calibration either smuggles in an unacknowledged interpersonal comparison at the calibration stage or reduces welfare to observable behavior, and I want to know whether defenders of strict ordinalism would concede that robust cross-personal predictive success in well-being markets or neuroeconomic correlation patterns should genuinely move them. Finally, I want to assess whether my view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a methodological failure for a normative framework of this kind.
