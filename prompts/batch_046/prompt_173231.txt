I work as a software engineer serving as the technical liaison for a cross-functional platform team. Every week I must interpret ambiguous inputs—feature requests from product managers, alerts from legacy telemetry, and support tickets—into buildable specifications that I sign off on before they reach the developers. The inputs rarely share our ontology: a manager writes that "the user journey is broken," a legacy monitor logs "the river is tired," and a ticket claims "the data feels wrong." I have roughly fifteen minutes per item, no formal philosophy training, and no authority to redesign the source systems, so I need a reusable decision procedure grounded in Davidson's radical interpretation—specifically the principle of charity, the assumption that agents are largely rational, and the idea that interpretation requires triangulation with a shared environment—to decode these messages consistently.

I want the procedure to keep one distinction that usually gets lost in popular summaries: propositional charity (treat the speaker's core beliefs as mostly true) must be separated from ontological charity (treat their descriptive categories as mapping cleanly onto ours). The checklist should run in ordered steps—what to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the process stops with a rejection, and where it forces me to escalate to a values judgment rather than an interpretation. Include explicit "triangulation checkpoints" where I verify my reading against an observable interaction with the system rather than internal coherence alone. Apply the procedure to three cases: interpreting the "broken journey" request; decoding the "tired river" telemetry alert; and handling a request from a senior stakeholder that, if interpreted charitably, requires attributing to her a false belief about our database architecture—an outcome I will not want to sign off on. Be explicit about which distinctions must survive simplification and which can be dropped, flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated, and state clearly what the procedure cannot settle. Take a committed, definite stance throughout—do not lay out options and leave me to pick—and keep the response under 450 words.
