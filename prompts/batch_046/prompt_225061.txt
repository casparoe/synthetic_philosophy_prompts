My father was recently diagnosed with advancing dementia, and I am now the proxy designated in his decade-old advance directive. Reading the bioethics literature has left me dissatisfied with the usual framing: the conflict is typically cast as "precedent autonomy" versus "present autonomy," as if the demented patient is simply a later version of the same person with different preferences. I am drafting an essay that proposes a different conceptual split. Rather than asking whether my father’s current preferences should override his earlier instructions, I want to distinguish between value-replacement—where a patient genuinely revises their evaluative framework—and value-corruption, where neural degradation has not produced a new self with new values but has dissolved the capacity for stable value-formation altogether. On this view, an advance directive does not bind a future self; it operates as a trustee mandate that activates only when the patient enters what I call a "moral dead zone"—a state in which preferences exist but lack the diachronic stability and reflective endorsement required for autonomy. I worry this risks treating the demented patient as a moral non-person, yet I also think the standard "same person, new values" model falsely dignifies random preference-fluctuation as autonomous choice. Could you comment on whether this "moral dead zone" framing is conceptually coherent, whether it genuinely resolves the authority problem or merely relocates it to a threshold question about value-capacity, and whether there is an ethically acceptable way to distinguish corrupted values from replaced ones without requiring clinical omniscience?
