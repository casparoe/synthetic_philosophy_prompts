I have recently returned to Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist critique of necessary causation—particularly his argument in the Tahāfut that fire does not burn cotton by any intrinsic nature, but that God creates the burning directly at each occasion—while watching a close family member move through an illness whose course seems entirely disconnected from every intervention we attempt. I want a poem in terza rima, roughly thirty to forty lines, that uses the form's interlocking rhyme scheme to enact the metaphysics: each rhyme reaching back to a previous line should initially feel like a causal chain linking one moment to the next, but as the poem shifts from clinical observation to philosophical reflection, that rhyme should come to feel like an imposed pattern on discrete, divinely authored events that have no necessary connection except through customary sequence. The poem should accurately reflect Al-Ghazālī's distinction between the philosophers' belief in natural necessary connection and his own view that all causation is direct divine action, with observed regularity being only habit rather than proof. It should not resolve whether this view offers consolation or cruelty in the face of unpredictable suffering, and it should avoid turning into either a sermon for medical interventionism or a shrug of passive acceptance. Instead, let the form itself—the rhyme that binds lines together without proving they must belong—carry the unresolved tension between pattern and denial of necessity.
