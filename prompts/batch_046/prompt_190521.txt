I hold a version of disjunctivism about perceptual experience: in genuine seeing, the subject stands in a direct relation of acquaintance to an external object, and there is no shared mental state between this case and a subjectively matching hallucination; the latter is not a different kind of experience with the same phenomenal core, but a failure or misfiring of the very perceptual capacity that, when successful, yields openness to the world. I think the argument from illusion illicitly assumes that subjective indistinguishability implies a common mental factor, and I am especially drawn to the idea that perception carries a normative presentational force that hallucination necessarily lacks, even if both feel similar from the inside. What would genuinely count against this position? Would the existence of epistemically frictionless hallucinations — cases where a subject rationally forms true beliefs from a hallucinated scene without any detectable error — show that hallucination can carry presentational force after all? And has disjunctivism been qualified into unfalsifiability, such that any phenomenological similarity is dismissed by fiat? If so, is that insulation a philosophical vice or a feature?
