My advisor in epistemology, Dr. K. Voss, has given the following verdicts on knowledge-attribution cases: ordinary perceptual belief that a cup sits on the desk—knowledge; the inference from knowing one has hands to knowing one is not a brain-in-a-vat—no knowledge; the grandmother who sees her healthy grandson via video call in a family that would conceal serious illness—knowledge that he is well; a Gettier-style true belief formed via a causally irrelevant false intermediate premise—no knowledge; and a measurement belief formed with an instrument she knows tracks truth, where the calibration standard she has not personally verified is in fact correct—knowledge. Next week she will consider a new case: a researcher uses a telescope she knows tracks stellar positions to observe a distant star, then calculates its distance using an equation she found only in a forged manual; the equation happens to be correct, the star is indeed at that distance, and she has performed no independent check. Predict Voss’s verdict, identify whether she is implicitly running pure Nozickian tracking with closure rejected, tracking conjoined with a no-false-lemmas condition, or a restricted closure principle that permits deduction only when intermediates are themselves known, and specify the one further case—distinguishing a false manual yielding a coincidentally true result from a true manual paired with an uncalibrated instrument—that would settle which rule she actually follows.
