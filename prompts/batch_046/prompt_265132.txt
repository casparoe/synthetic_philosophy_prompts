Since retiring from the lab I've been poking aroud in Galileo's letters and I'm struck by how often people lean on the 'two books' analogy -- nature and scripture are both authored by God, one in mathematics and one in plain words, so they can never genuinely clash. They use it to claim Galileo was essentially an early separationist, happy for science and religion to stay in their own lanes. But I'm not convinced that's what he actually thought. Can you audit the analogy for me? What's the mapping -- the author, the two volumes, the languages, the intended readers? Where does it break -- for instance, Galileo argued that if reason demonstrates a physical fact, then scripture must be reinterpreted, which sounds like one book overruling the other, not harmonious co-existence? And he spoke of scripture being 'acommodated' to common understanding, which the neat 'different syntax' version glosses over. Does the conclusion that Galileo believed in non-overlapping magisteria survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it all along? Answer in British English please, and no need for a bibliography -- I just want the reasoning laid out.
