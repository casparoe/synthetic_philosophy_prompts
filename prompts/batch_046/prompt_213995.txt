I've been running a small graduate seminar on the philosophy of privacy, and after working through the standard frameworks—informational control, contextual integrity, and autonomy-based defenses—we ended up in a place that felt both more interesting and less settled. Several of us were arguing that privacy's value might lie less in hiding information and more in sustaining what I'm calling "epistemic asymmetry": the deliberate, socially structured distribution of ignorance that allows roles to function, whether that is the physician who knows but does not disclose, the friend who respects a boundary not by concealing a fact but by not acquiring it, or the public figure whose necessary opacity makes democratic judgment possible. I want to close our final session with a poem that takes this idea seriously rather than treating privacy as a generic good, and I want the form itself to do philosophical work.

Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains alternating and finally converging in the closing lines. Let Refrain A carry the transparency imperative, along the lines of "What is hidden must be shown," and let Refrain B carry the asymmetry claim, along the lines of "What is shown must stay hidden." The repetition and rhyme scheme should enact the conceptual deadlock we reached in discussion: the two positions should pass each other through the tercets without resolving, and only in the final quatrain should they sit side by side, leaving the tension open rather than dissolving it into either a celebration of surveillance or a nostalgic defense of secrecy. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in privacy theory recognizes the role-based, knowledge-structural argument—rather than seeing "privacy" merely name-dropped—and should avoid sentimental resolution.

After the poem, provide a brief but committed note: defend the claim that epistemic asymmetry is indeed central to privacy's value, and explicitly flag where the poem takes liberties with standard accounts or leans on contested assumptions, particularly regarding whether all asymmetry is benign, whether social roles are stable enough to bear the weight the poem gives them, and whether the transparency imperative can ever be fully separated from the asymmetry it threatens.
