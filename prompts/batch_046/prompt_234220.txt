Two people in my study group, Alex and Blake, got stuck arguing about moral worth and acting for the right reasons. Alex brought up this case: Mia donates a big chunk to famine relief, but she only actually sends the money because she just saw a viral post calling wealthy people who don't donate "morally ugly," and she's terrified of being seen that way. At the same time, Mia genuinely beleives extreme suffering is bad and that she should help — it's just that without the shame trigger she probably wouldn't have moved. Blake said that's zero moral worth, full stop. The actual engine is vanity and image-management; her correct belief is just sitting there like decorative wallpaper. Alex pushed back: but if the shame only works because Mia already accepts that suffering matters — like, she wouldn't feel shame about being called "morally ugly" if she didn't think morality had anything to do with suffering — then isn't the moral belief doing causal work as a scaffold? Without it, the vanity wouldn't have latched onto this particular action. Blake said thats just counterfactual speculation, and even if true it only shows the moral belief is a necessary background condition, not an actual reason she's acting on. Then Alex flipped it: what about cases where someone acts from two motives, like wanting a tax break and genuinely caring? We'd usually say partial credit. So why treat shame as categorically diffrent from a tax break? Blake said tax breaks are external incentives, but shame is an ego-defense mechanism that actively competes with moral reasoning, so it's more like acting from spite. Then Alex asked about retroactive grading — if Mia later realizes she was partly vain, feels bad, and starts donating regularly out of real care, does that change how we should judge the original donation? Blake said moral worth is attached to the act as performed, not to her later self, but he suspects Alex is trying to smuggle in the idea that the original moral belief was "really" active all along, which doesn't follow. They went in circles. Who's right about what here? Specifically, does the scaffold argument grant Mia partial moral worth, is Blake right that shame is categorically worse than mixed motives like tax breaks, and is the retroactive point a red herring or does it actually undermine one of their positions? And what are they both missing about how to assign credit when motives are overdetermined?
