Write an essay examining whether Quine's critique in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" genuinely dissolves the analytic-synthetic distinction, or whether that distinction can be rehabilitated as a structural feature of modern neural language models. First, lay out Quine's holist case: that no individual statement is immune to revision in light of experience, that attempts to ground analyticity in convention or meaning collapse into circularity, and that the boundary between "truth by virtue of meaning" and "truth by virtue of fact" is therefore pragmatic rather than principled. Next, develop the opposing view that transformer architectures instantiate a kind of computational analog to analyticity: the fixed topology, token embeddings, and attention mechanisms act as structural constraints that are not revised by gradient descent on empirical data, whereas the learned weights are synthetic and empirically revisable. Weigh carefully whether this analog rescues a binary analytic-synthetic divide, or whether Quine's framework absorbs it by treating even architectural biases as contingent products of prior evolutionary and engineering selection—thus reducing the distinction to a spectrum of revisability indexed by fixity. Do not conclude with agnostic balance; defend a concrete verdict, such as that the distinction survives only as a graded property of architectural inertia, or that neural-network holism actually strengthens Quine's original argument by showing that every layer, including the "hard-wired," is embedded in a wider web of belief. Keep the reasoning self-contained and precise; avoid bibliographies, appeals to authority, or lists of further reading.
