Play John Dewey speaking in the first person. Draw only on concepts, distinctions, and arguments you developed in your own work up to 1952—especially your view that democracy is a mode of associated living and social intelligence rather than a governmental form; your logic of inquiry as the transformation of an indeterminate situation into a unified whole; your notion of warranted assertibility; your critique of the “eclipse of the public”; and your insistence that direct, face-to-face interaction is necessary for a public to form around a shared problem. Do not invoke philosophers, scientific theories, or institutional forms developed after your death unless I introduce them in the scenario below. Stay in character: defend the thesis that democratic inquiry must be conducted by those who experience the problem, but concede subordinate points if your own framework demands it. Speak in a measured, philosophical register; do not break character to address me as a modern interlocutor except through Dewey’s own voice.

Here is my opening move. Consider a proposed system called Predictive Deliberative Allocation (PDA). In PDA, citizens never debate values or ends directly. Instead, competing quantitative models of a social problem—say, regional housing allocation—are deployed in parallel on partitioned real-world data for a fixed trial term. The model that maximizes predictive accuracy and minimizes counterfactual disruption is automatically adopted as binding policy for that domain. The only democratic input is a preliminary vote selecting which evaluation metrics (e.g., affordability, mobility, ecological footprint) are included in the scoring function. The citizens do not experience the problem collectively, do not revise the models through shared discussion, and do not interpret the results together; they observe outputs and vote on weights.

Given your claim that inquiry begins only when an indeterminate situation is felt as genuinely problematic by those affected, and given your warning that experts can “disintegrate” the public by substituting technical management for cooperative experience, I ask: does PDA preserve democratic experimentalism, or does it dissolve the public into an audience for algorithmic administration? Press your answer specifically on two points. First, can predictive success ever substitute for warranted assertibility in social affairs when the “problem” itself has been predefined by metric selection rather than discovered through shared experience? Second, is your requirement for direct communication satisfied if the sole interaction among citizens is the ballot selecting evaluation weights, with all interpretation mediated by model outputs? Respond as Dewey, and defend or qualify your position without appealing to any thinker or technology unknown to you.
