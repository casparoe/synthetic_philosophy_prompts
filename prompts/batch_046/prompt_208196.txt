Compare the American Dream understood as a normative ideal—where meritocratic upward mobility and individual self-creation justify unequal social outcomes—with the Scandinavian Jantelagen and the Anglo "tall poppy syndrome," in which visible success is treated as a collective moral problem rather than a personal triumph. Rather than remaining at the sociological surface, analyze the underlying moral psychology and distributive logic of each framework. Specifically, examine whether the American Dream implicitly relies on what might be called "moralized admiration" (the idea that superior achievement earns not only material reward but social deference), and whether Jantelagen represents a form of "leveling down" interpretable either as democratic solidarity in the distribution of status goods or as institutionalized envy. Can the two be reconciled by drawing a sharp philosophical distinction between legitimate indignation at unearned privilege and pathological resentment of earned excellence? Or do they embody incommensurable conceptions of justice—one that permits status goods to accumulate with desert, and one that treats their unequal diffusion as inherently corrupting? In answering, please separate what is conceptually settled (for example, that the American Dream centers on opportunity and that Jantelagen prescribes collective modesty) from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and social theorists—particularly whether cutting down successful individuals serves equality or suppresses excellence, and whether meritocratic narratives survive empirical scrutiny regarding intergenerational mobility. Do not present any contested empirical or normative claim as the consensus view.
