I serve on my university's Faculty Senate Committee on External Funding and Ethical Partnerships, and I must circulate a formal advisory memo before our vote next month. The case concerns a proposed $2.4 million endowment from the Atlantic Foundation for Democratic Security to establish a research cluster entitled Comparative Regime Change and Post-Intervention Governance. The cluster's founding charter treats Kosovo (1999), Iraq (2003), and Libya (2011) as natural experiments in humanitarian coercion, organizing analysis around a three-axis taxonomy—multilateral authorization, consent of the target state, and duration of post-conflict occupation—to evaluate whether regime-change interventions can satisfy criteria of legitimate authority retrospectively. Although the grant agreement contains no explicit advocacy clause, the foundation's governing board includes former senior officials linked to the strategic planning of the 2003 Iraq invasion and the 2011 Libya air campaign. The central philosophical issue is what I have termed a moral hazard of retrospective legitimation: by subsuming these three contested cases within a single comparative normative framework, the cluster may inadvertently provide epistemic laundering for actions that lacked clear ex ante legitimacy, regardless of the critical intentions of individual researchers. Conversely, an outright refusal risks ceding the study of post-intervention ethics to institutions with far less reflexive distance from state power. Please draft the memo I will distribute: it should recommend a specific, actionable course—unconditional acceptance, rejection, or conditional acceptance with precisely defined charter amendments—together with an implementation timeline (for instance, a sixty-day negotiation window or an immediate recommendation to decline). The reasoning must treat the taxonomy itself and the retrospective legitimation problem as the primary stakes, not as peripheral policy concerns. Crucially, it must engage the strongest counter-argument in full: namely, that any conditional acceptance still implicates the university in normalizing a continuum between authorized and unauthorized uses of force, and that the scholarly distinction between studying and endorsing collapses once the comparative framework treats Kosovo, Iraq, and Libya as variable points on one spectrum. Do not rely on boilerplate conflict-of-interest language; the memo should read as a philosophically informed institutional judgment with a clear bottom line.
