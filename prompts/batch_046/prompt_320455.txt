I am a high school student taking an introductory political philosophy course, and during a recent seminar on Immanuel Kant’s essay on perpetual peace I got into a debate with a classmate that I have not been able to settle on my own. The discussion centered on what looks like an internal tension between two parts of Kant’s framework. In the preliminary articles, Kant prohibits certain methods of warfare—specifically the employment of assassins, poisoners, and breaches of surrender—on the grounds that they destroy the mutual trust required for any future peace, even when a state is fighting a purely defensive war. Yet in the first definitive article he requires a republican constitution in which citizens must consent to war, precisely because they will bear its costs and risks. My friend argued that these commitments pull in opposite directions: if citizens genuinely authorize hostilities through republican consent, they might rationally approve precisely the kinds of asymmetric, covert tactics that Kant forbids, particularly when facing an adversary that does not observe the same moral boundaries. I want to understand whether this tension is real or only apparent.

Please explain the conceptual issue by focusing on whether Kant’s argument implicitly depends on a strict distinction between the moral personality of the state—which remains accountable to republican citizens—and the moral status of individual combatants, and whether his cosmopolitan right of hospitality (Weltbürgerrecht) introduces an additional, independent constraint that resolves the conflict by extending moral consideration to foreigners outside the republic. I am especially interested in whether the framework holds together once we apply it to modern conflicts in which the line between state and non-state actors is blurred, or whether that application exposes a genuine contradiction rather than a superficial one. I do not want a historical summary, a bibliography, or a list of commentators; I want an intuitive but conceptually precise explanation of how these elements interact, with a clear verdict on whether the tension dissolves or persists, and why.
