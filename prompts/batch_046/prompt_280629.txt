I've been reading Fukuyama's end-of-history stuff lately — the original 1989 essay and then parts of Political Order — and I'm trying to figure out what actually matters for current work, not just whether he was "wrong" about democracy winning everywhere. There's this tension I keep circling back to that doesn't come up in the usual summaries: on one side you've got the Hegelian recogntion story, where history ends because liberal democracy solves the problem of universal recognition (thymos). On the other side, in his later institutional work, he basically says democracy doesn't stick without prior state-building, rule of law, and bureaucratic capacity — which sounds like the endpoint isn't universal at all, it depends on whether you can build specific institutions first, and that sounds more like contigent sociology than a philosophical endpoint.

If you had to advise a small research group with maybe three years of funding on conceptual political theory, which open questions stemming from this tension should they actually prioritize? I'm torn between three lines: (1) whether the recognition argument implies liberal democracy is the unique endpoint, or whether any regime providing universal recognition counts — which would make the endpoint plural and less specific to liberal democracy; (2) whether those institutional prerequisites (state capacity, etc.) undermine the original claim that history has a directional endpoint driven by human consciousness, turning it instead into a sociology of institutions that could stall or reverse; and (3) whether modern populism and identity politics should be read as thymos returning because recognition was never universalized, or as noise inside an endpoint that's already arrived. Can you rank these in terms of which is upstream of the others, which ones actually change how we think about democratic backsliding or state-building if resolved, and which are probably dead ends? I want reasoning about tractability and dependency — like, if we settled line 2, does that collapse line 1? And please don't just hand me a bibliography; I'm asking which lines deserve the years, not what to read. I was discussing this with friends over coffee and we couldn't agree if Fukuyama even meant these two parts to fit together, so I'm curious how that split should be handled conceptually, but mostly I care about the prioritization.
