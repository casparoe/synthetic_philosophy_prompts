In my philosophy of psychology reading group, the exchange between defenders of virtue ethics and proponents of the situationist critique has reached an odd standstill. Both camps now concede the core empirical findings—low cross-situational consistency in prosocial behaviour, the predictive power of situational variables over dispositional ones in Milgram-style and bystander studies, and the failure of global trait ratings to forecast specific actions. The virtue ethicists respond that virtue is not a 'behavioural uniform' but a complex of practical wisdom, emotional regulation, and responsive reasons; the situationists reply that this renders virtue empirically unfalsifiable and therefore explanatorily vacuous. Neither side has persuaded the other over two years of discussion, and the literature seems to replay the same moves: the ethicist invokes 'thick' concepts and narrative identity, the psychologist points to effect sizes.

I am inclined to think the impasse is not factual but conceptual: perhaps the two sides operate with different notions of what a 'trait' must do to count as real—one requiring robust behavioural uniformity, the other allowing highly context-sensitive expression mediated by moral perception. Yet I also wonder whether a hidden normative question is doing the work: the situationist treats the absence of cross-situational consistency as a failure of character, whereas the virtue ethicist treats the same pattern as evidence that character must be understood relationally rather than atomistically.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it a verbal dispute about the meaning of 'character trait'; a substantive disagreement about the standards of evidence appropriate to moral psychology; a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether human agency is fundamentally situation-responsive; or an ill-posed question that conflates descriptive psychology with normative evaluation? And given your diagnosis, is there a specific distinction, formal framework, or conceptual clarification—perhaps one separating 'trait-possession' from 'trait-expression', or distinguishing predictive validity from explanatory depth in virtue ascription—that could move the debate forward, or should we conclude that no empirical result could resolve it because the disagreement is fundamentally about what kind of explanation moral philosophy ought to seek?
