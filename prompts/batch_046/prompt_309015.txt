i'm a program officer at a science funder and i review maybe 8-10 cross-disciplinary proposals a month that claim they've either "reduced" some macro-phenomenon to micro-mechanisms, or that it's genuinely "emergent" and can't be decomposed. I need a reusable decision procedure i can run in ~20 minutes with no philosophy PhD, so i can sign off without guessing. The deliverable is a step-by-step checklist: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where i stop with a hard no, and where the decision has to be mine because it's values not facts. Ground it in what philosophy of science actually knows — mechanistic explanation, downward causation, predictive substitution — but translate it into things i can check in a methods section.

Build it around three filters applied in this order:
1. Decompositional fidelity: can they map the macro-property onto specific micro-parts without secretly using the macro-description?
2. Inter-level stability: if you intervene on the micro-parts, does the macro-pattern stay predictable, or does it collapse in ways the micro-rules don't cover?
3. Predictive substitution: does the micro-account reproduce the same range of outcomes, including edge cases and failures, or only the neat ones?

For each, define "pass," "fail," and "unclear," and flag wether "unclear" means "need more data" or "this is a judgment call about explanatory priorities."

Then apply it to three proposals:
A) Neuroscience: consciousness is "just" synchronized gamma oscillations in layer-5 pyramidal neurons; model predicts reportability but not subjective quality.
B) Ecology: forest fire resilience is emergent because burn patterns can't be derived from individual tree genetics even with full genomic data.
C) Behavioral economics: market crashes are fully reducible to individual traders' risk-aversion algorithms, with no institutional variables needed.

Make sure one gives me an answer i won't like — i usually think economic phenomena are emergent, so let the procedure come down hard in favor of reduction there, and explain why i have to live with it. Also include a worked "unclear" case if possible, or at least mark where the procedure stops and hands over.

Finally, be honest about what the procedure can't do: it won't settle wether emergence is ontological or just epistemic, and it won't catch a bad experiment that's been misllabelled as reduction.

Keep it practical, no citations, a typo or two is fine.
