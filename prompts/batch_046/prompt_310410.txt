Design an 80-minute classroom activity for 16 graduate students in a philosophy of technology seminar on impossibility results in algorithmic fairness. The participants should come away feeling—not merely understanding—that the tension between calibration within demographic groups and equalized false-positive and false-negative rates across those groups is a structural constraint, not a remediable design flaw, especially when base rates differ. To keep the exercise from reproducing standard workshop templates, inject the following niche framing: the scoring system must be an additively decomposable point-card (each feature contributes a publicly visible integer score, like a simplified risk assessment), and the same linear rule is applied in two simulated jurisdictions with different base-rate profiles; participants act as members of a multi-stakeholder certification board (vendor representatives, affected-community delegates, and independent auditors) who must approve the rule before deployment. Give explicit roles, a timeline broken into rounds, materials that can be printed on index cards, and a payoff scheme based on institutional credibility points rather than money. The rules and negotiation dynamics should force participants to discover for themselves that any point allocation they approve either breaks calibration in one jurisdiction or produces unequal error-rate balances, and they must then cast a binding vote on which constraint to formally abandon. Include a debrief that maps specific moments—such as the discovery that weighting a feature differently per jurisdiction violates the linear transparency rule—to the formal distinction between within-group calibration and across-group error-rate parity. Note honestly which feature of the impossibility the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the continuous probabilistic nature of real risk distributions and the statistical uncertainty around estimated rates) and state that the facilitator must supply that gap verbally. Finally, list what will predictably go wrong on the first run—for example, participants will try to resolve the conflict by inventing new features or jurisdiction-specific adjustments, and you should specify the facilitator rule that blocks both moves—and explain how to handle it without breaking the activity's momentum. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the prompt should be answerable entirely from reasoning about the structure of fairness constraints.
