I am currently preparing a presentation for a philosophy of language workshop on the semantics of conditionals, and I have become genuinely stuck on a point about Lewis's triviality results that my reading group keeps circling without resolving. We understand, in broad outline, that Lewis demonstrated how identifying the probability of an indicative conditional "if A then C" with the corresponding conditional probability P(C|A) leads—when combined with certain structural assumptions about logical form and the distribution of probabilities—to the collapse of all probabilities into the extremes of 0 and 1. Yet I am struggling to see exactly what conceptual lesson we are meant to draw from this collapse.

Specifically, is the result best understood as a direct refutation of the idea that indicative conditionals possess truth-conditions tied to probabilistic support relations? Or is it more narrowly a technical obstacle that can be circumvented by rejecting one of the auxiliary premises—such as the assumption that the probabilities assigned to conditionals are unconditional probabilities—while still preserving the core intuition that to believe "if A then C" is essentially to be disposed to assign high credence to C upon learning A? I am also wondering whether adopting a non-probabilistic semantics—say, a premise-set account or a restrictor analysis—genuinely escapes the force of the proof, or whether Lewis's argument can be reformulated to threaten those frameworks as well.

I do not need bibliographic pointers, reading lists, or citations to particular texts; I am working without ready access to a library database at the moment. What I need is a clear conceptual map of what is genuinely forced by the triviality result, which commitments must be abandoned to avoid it, and whether any robust middle ground remains for someone who wants to maintain that indicative conditionals are closely linked to rational belief revision without accepting full-blown probabilistic semantics.
