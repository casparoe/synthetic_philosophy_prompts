As a novelist drafting a historical fantasy that moves between the Warring States period and a modern frame narrative, I have been reading the Zhuangzi in various translations to understand how its author treats mortality not as an ending but as a continuous transformation. One episode in particular has stayed with me: Zhuang Zhou’s wife dies, and when his friend Hui Shi comes to console him, he finds Zhuang Zhou sitting with his legs spread wide, drumming on a tub and singing. Hui Shi asks whether this is not excessive, given that she was his wife and they had shared a life together. Zhuang Zhou replies that at first he grieved, but then he considered the changes of qi—how she had come from nothing, taken form, lived, and now returned to the vastness between heaven and earth, where she rests in the great room of nature. To weep further, he suggests, would be to misunderstand the course of things.

What I cannot quite grasp is the philosophical significance of the tub itself. In the narrative, the object is not a ritual instrument but something associated with domestic labor and storage, and its use in mourning seems deliberately incongruous. I am wondering whether this act is meant to illustrate that the death of the beloved dissolves not only social roles but also the very boundary between the one who mourns and the one who is mourned—that the drummer, by refusing conventional lament, enacts a kind of identity dissolution into the flow of qi. Alternatively, does Zhuang Zhou remain a fully distinct observer who has simply adopted an attitude of cosmic acceptance, with the tub serving only as a prop for that detachment?

Could you help me understand the passage’s logic regarding personal identity across death? Specifically, I want to know whether Zhuangzi’s view of transformation implies that there is no enduring self to be lost, or whether there is a persisting consciousness that merely witnesses the metamorphosis of others. I am working without access to secondary scholarship, so please explain the concept in your own terms, in American English, and do not recommend texts or editions for me to seek out.
