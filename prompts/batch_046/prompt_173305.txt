I teach an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar of twelve postgraduate students in a single ninety-minute session, and I am looking for a structured in-class simulation that makes the paradox of phenomenal judgement under epiphenomenalism feel like a live difficulty rather than a textbook curiosity. The central idea is this: if phenomenal consciousness has no causal efficacy, then the judgements we form about our own conscious states are produced entirely by physical processes that never involve those states; yet if that is true, it seems to erode the authority with which we can claim to know them. I want the participants to discover this tension through action — by being placed in roles where they must defend or challenge phenomenal reports under conditions of 'causal occlusion', a framing in which every report is mechanically generated from physical correlates but is presented as introspective knowledge.

Please design a runnable activity with assigned roles — for instance, a 'phenomenal reporter', an 'interlocutor', and a 'judge' — together with clear rules that prevent direct causal influence of experience on verbal output, a timeline that fits the ninety minutes, and an evaluation scheme that gives participants a stake in consistency without using real money. The rules should be arranged so that the group attempts to coordinate on whether the reports count as authoritative, and they should find that coordination collapses in a specific way — mapping onto the paradox — before I name the problem. Then provide a debrief that connects each moment of the simulation to the logical structure of the paradox, explains which premise of epiphenomenalism is being pressured at each stage, and is frank about which aspect of phenomenal judgement — particularly the first-personal immediacy that no sub-personal mechanism can replicate — the activity necessarily fails to reproduce and that I must supply in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as participants conflating the simulation's artificial occlusion with actual scepticism about consciousness, and how to correct it without breaking the framing. I prefer that no philosophers be named in quotation marks and that the prompt contain no request for further reading or citations; it should stand on its own conceptual architecture.
