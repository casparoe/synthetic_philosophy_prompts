Here's the student response:

Frankfurt's hierarchical model says freedom isn't just doing what you want, but wanting what you want. He splits desires into first-order ones, which are just wants for particular things, and second-order volitions, which are desires about which first-order wants should actually drive us. You have free will when your effective first-order desire lines up with a second-order volition you identify with. That's different from simple compatibilism, which usually just checks whether the action came from your own mental states without outside force, and doesn't care about determinism either way. Frankfurt is okay with determinism being true, but he thinks identification can still happen inside a deterministic system, so determinism doesn't automatically kill free will.

Van Inwagen's consequence argument says that if determinism holds, then every action follows from laws of nature plus past events we never controlled, so we never could have done otherwise, hence no free will. Frankfurt answers this with his Frankfurt-style cases involving an intervener who is ready to step in but never actually does. In these scenarios the agent still has alternate possibilities available — they could have acted differently if they had wanted to — yet they remain fully responsable because they genuinely identify with the choice they made. So the hierarchical model defeats the consequence argument: what really matters is internal identification with your desires, not whether determinism has wiped out every alternative path. Therefore Frankfurt shows that incompatibilism is wrong, since we can keep free will even in a world with no real alternatives.
