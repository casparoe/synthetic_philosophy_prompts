As a retired physicist who has recently begun attending a philosophy-of-science seminar, I find myself out of my depth but intrigued by debates that mix formal reasoning with conceptual judgment. At our last session, two fellow members—Nadia, an economist, and Leo, a mathematician—got into a sustained disagreement about the Ellsberg paradox and whether ambiguity aversion can ever be rational. I reported the exchange neutrally and want a point-by-point adjudication rather than advice for myself.

Nadia opened by arguing that ambiguity aversion is not a cognitive bias but a rational response to epistemic structure. In scientific practice, she said, an unknown parameter distribution is rarely uniform; ambiguity typically signals hidden dependencies or model misspecification. Choosing the urn with the known fifty-fifty composition respects one's actual epistemic position—there is a well-defined mechanism—whereas selecting the ambiguous urn forces an implicit commitment to one of many possible color distributions. The aversion, she claimed, tracks the cost of that model commitment, not mere pessimism.

Leo replied that this conflates normative decision theory with empirical psychology. The Ellsberg setup is deliberately stripped of downstream consequences: it is a single draw with no future inferences or learning. Within that formal structure, beliefs should be separable from values via a unique prior, and the sure-thing principle applies as a consistency condition on preference, not on belief formation. If one systematically avoids the ambiguous urn, one behaves as though assigning greater weight to unfavorable compositions—that is a hidden pessimistic prior, not epistemic humility. True suspension of judgment would yield indifference, not avoidance.

Nadia countered that the sure-thing principle itself presupposes a well-defined sample space over outcomes. In genuinely ambiguous cases there is no unique prior, so applying the principle is like using conditional probability reasoning without a measurable space. The rational agent should therefore avoid options whose evaluation depends on resolving the ambiguity. The known urn requires no such resolution; the ambiguous one does. Thus ambiguity aversion is consistent with rational belief management rather than a violation of it.

Leo then argued that even without a unique prior, ambiguity aversion implicitly picks out one specific resolution—effectively a worst-case analysis—while claiming not to have resolved anything. That is incoherent: one cannot simultaneously disclaim belief and act on a particular belief. Moreover, because the urn choice is one-shot with no learning or future commitment, invoking "model commitment costs" appeals to features outside the paradox. Within the formal structure itself, ambiguity aversion remains a pure preference anomaly with no rational foundation.

Nadia closed by conceding that in the bare one-shot case the epistemic-cost argument is weaker, but she insisted that human decision-makers never encounter such stripped contexts. Even in a single bet, selecting the ambiguous option requires framing the problem under some implicit parameterization, and that framing carries cognitive costs that rational agents should minimize. So ambiguity aversion persists as a sensible heuristic, even if it violates an idealized axiom designed for fully specified belief states.

Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether the sure-thing principle applies in ambiguous contexts, about whether ambiguity aversion implicitly assumes a worst-case prior, and about whether model-commitment costs are relevant inside the formal Ellsberg structure. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—perhaps a distinction between rational justification and psychological explanation, or an alternative framing neither has considered. Write in American English. Finally, state explicitly what argument or observation would change your verdict, and point out where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
