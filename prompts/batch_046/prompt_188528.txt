I'm taking a graduate seminar on evolutionary signaling theory, and over the past weeks my advisor has been giving quick, unscripted verdicts on whether particular behaviors count as genuine handicap signals in the strict Zahavian sense—wasteful displays that reduce fitness and that only high-quality individuals can bear—rather than as honest but non-handicap signals. So far she has said yes to the peacock's train, to stotting in Thomson's gazelles, and to the metabolically expensive courtship dances of male birds-of-paradise; she has said yes to conspicuous philanthropic naming rights in human societies, but only where the expenditure is irrecoverable and visibly wasteful rather than strategically convertible into reputation or future gain; and she has said no to cooperative alarm calling in meerkats and to the large nuptial gifts offered by male scorpionflies, which she treats as direct reproductive investment rather than quality-revealing waste. Her pattern is not fully transparent: she might be running a strict handicap rule, a condition-dependent index rule in the Grafen style, or some hybrid that requires both differential cost and strategic irreversibility.

The new case comes from a hypothetical but biologically plausible cooperative-breeding system discussed in our readings. In this species, subordinate helpers provide alloparental care at rates far above what their baseline physiology predicts they can sustain. The key finding is that the highest-quality helpers can maintain this elevated care with minimal fitness loss—in fact they recover faster—while lower-quality helpers who attempt the same rate suffer severe mortality and are quickly expelled from the group. The care level is not finely graded: a helper either commits to the high-rate regime or is driven out, and for the strongest individuals the expenditure is not especially burdensome, even though it remains lethal for the weakest. Given her prior verdicts, what should she say about whether this cooperative care counts as a handicap signal? Identify the two or three rules that best fit her track record, say what each predicts here, and argue for the best interpretation of her consistency. Which of her earlier judgments is the closest analog? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, state explicitly what principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—preferably in a solitary rather than cooperative context—that would force her to choose between the rules you have identified.
