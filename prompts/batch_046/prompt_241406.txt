Exam question: In clinical trial reporting, physicians sometimes state that "the probability of remission for a patient matching your profile is 34%." Explain how a frequentist, a Bayesian subjectivist, and a propensity theorist would each interpret this statement. Student answer: The frequentist interprets the 34% figure as the long-run relative frequency of remission within a precisely defined reference class—say, all patients with the same biomarker profile who received the drug in the trial. For the frequentist, this is not a property of any individual patient but a feature of the population distribution, and it only makes sense when the reference class is well-specified. The Bayesian subjectivist interprets the same figure as a rational degree of belief—specifically, the updated credence that a particular patient will remit, conditional on all available evidence including the trial data and any prior clinical knowledge. On this view, the 34% expresses an epistemic state rather than a feature of the world, and two clinicians with different priors could rationally assign different probabilities to the same patient. The propensity theorist, following Popper, treats the 34% as an objective single-case tendency or dispositional property of the treatment-patient system. Unlike the frequentist, the propensity view does not require repetition: it assigns a physical tendency to this particular patient under these specific conditions. However, like the frequentist, the propensity interpretation denies that probabilities apply to genuinely unique events outside any statistical framework, so in personalized medicine it still depends on grouping patients into classes that share relevant causal features. Therefore, the disagreement is largely verbal. The frequentist provides the statistical model, the subjectivist applies it with personal judgment, and the propensity theorist adds metaphysical language about tendencies. Since none of them deny the mathematical results of the trial, they are not in conflict but are simply emphasizing different aspects—statistical, epistemic, and metaphysical—of the same clinical statement.
