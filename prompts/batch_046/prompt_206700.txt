I was recently appointed to the procurement ethics committee for a large public university system, and I have been trying to rank our dining hall sourcing options by the total suffering they cause, including both farmed and wild animals. I have reached the following verdicts, but I cannot tell whether they reflect a single coherent principle or are simply inconsistent intuitions.

Case 1: Replacing our primary protein source from beef to conventional chicken and eggs would be a moral mistake. Even though beef production uses more land and emits more carbon, the number of chickens affected is orders of magnitude larger, and the intensity of suffering in standard broiler conditions makes the aggregate harm greater.

Case 2: Switching to a fully plant-based menu using conventional annual grains, legumes, and vegetables would be a clear improvement over either beef or chicken. Even though mechanized harvest kills wild rodents and birds, pesticides harm insects and soil fauna, and storage kills more, there is no intentional breeding of sentient beings for suffering, and the direct harm is lower in scale.

Case 3: A menu built almost entirely on perennial tree crops—nuts, fruits, olives, and avocados—would be better still, because minimal soil disruption and the absence of annual harvest machinery sharply reduce wild animal deaths, and there is no farmed animal suffering at all.

Case 4: A proposal to source protein exclusively from wild-caught, sustainably managed deer populations that are currently overpopulated and facing winter starvation. The deer live entirely wild lives; professional sharpshooters kill them quickly; and without the cull, many would die slowly of starvation and disease. I judge this option ethically preferable to the conventional plant-based menu in Case 2, because it replaces prolonged natural suffering with quick death, involves no industrial breeding, and avoids crop cultivation that kills wild animals.

Case 5: Serving lab-cultivated beef—produced with no animal suffering and powered by renewable energy—would be preferable to the wild deer cull in Case 4, because it eliminates killing altogether while still providing the nutritional profile we need.

My judgments now seem to pull in different directions. In Case 4 I prefer an option that intentionally kills wild animals in order to avoid the incidental wild deaths of crop agriculture, which suggests I weigh intentional or industrial harm more heavily than foreseen but indirect harm. But in Case 1 I reject chicken partly because of the sheer scale of individual lives affected, which suggests that scale should dominate over the intentionality distinction. Meanwhile, in Case 2 I accept that incidental harvest deaths are tolerable at the scale they occur, yet in Case 4 I appear to treat those same harvest deaths as serious enough to justify replacing them with intentional killing. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that turns on substitutive causation, replacement effects, whether a life is net-positive, or the difference between causing suffering and allowing it—that can accommodate all five verdicts? Or must one of them be abandoned?

Please state any principle, or a small set of competing candidates, with enough precision that each clause can be matched to the case that motivates it. Give your verdicts on two further cases I have not considered: (a) a diet centered on filter-feeding bivalves such as oysters and mussels, which have negligible central nervous systems, require no feed crops, but cause some aquatic bycatch during harvest; and (b) a diet composed primarily of surplus and food-waste products that require no new agricultural production, including both plant and animal items, where consumption does not increase supply. Be explicit about which of my five verdicts, if any, your best principle cannot accommodate, and explain why. Write the answer in American English.
