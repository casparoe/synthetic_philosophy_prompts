I have been turning over the problem of evil on my own, not in any classroom but just from reading and thinking, and I want to check whether my actual commitments hang together. I have a feeling they feel consistent in my head only because I have not forced them to meet each other directly. Here is where I stand:

1. If God exists, He is both all-powerful and genuinely good; I am not willing to weaken either of those claims or redefine them away.
2. Suffering is real, not an illusion. There are cases of intense, apparently pointless suffering—children dying of genetic diseases, widespread predation and disease among animals.
3. Human free will is real and valuable. I think God could allow moral evil—cruelty, injustice—rather than override human choice.
4. I reject what I call “direct” theodicies: any view that permits suffering because it directly builds character, tests faith, or produces spiritual growth. That treats a person as a mere instrument for their own improvement, and I think that is wrong no matter who does it.
5. I am open to “indirect” theodicies: suffering can be an unavoidable side effect of something genuinely good, such as a stable natural order or the existence of free agents. But I think any such explanation must satisfy what I have started calling the transparency condition—the reason must be graspable in principle by those who suffer, not permanently hidden behind divine mystery.
6. Natural evils—earthquakes, pandemics, birth defects—have nothing to do with human choice, so the free-will defense does not cover them.
7. The suffering of animals matters morally, yet animals do not exercise free will, so their suffering needs an explanation that does not appeal to human agency.
8. Despite all of this, I still feel drawn to the idea that a benevolent creator exists. I do not want to be an atheist, and I find it hard to shake the intuition that the world is ultimately ordered by goodness rather than indifference.

Please examine these commitments for real tensions, not just surface disagreements. I want to know which pairs actually pull against each other and how severely—especially tensions that are not obvious, where two views that each seem reasonable turn out to be incompatible in practice, or where one principle undermines another in a way I have missed. I also want you to flag any pairs that only look tense and dissolve on closer inspection. For each genuine tension, rank them by severity, identify the cheapest revision (which single commitment to weaken or drop), and say what that revision costs me. Finally, tell me which one revision would clean up the most problems across the whole set.
