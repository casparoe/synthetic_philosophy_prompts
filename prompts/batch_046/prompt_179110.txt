Next semester I present my dissertation prospectus to an interdisciplinary committee of archaeologists and anthropologists. My thesis is that "collapse" is not an empirical event but a retrospective narrative projection—specifically, that the Eastern Mediterranean crisis around 1177 BCE, the Classic Maya transformation, and Rapa Nui's restructuring should all be redescribed as "institutional phase transitions" in which societies successfully redistributed complexity into lower-density forms rather than failing. I introduce a distinction between systemic decoupling (the actual institutional change) and narrative collapse (the observer's imposition of causal coherence that links environmental stress to cultural death, treating the cessation of monument-building as equivalent to societal death). The audience includes a complexity-theory archaeologist who treats Joseph Tainter's cost-curve model as genuinely predictive, a resilience anthropologist influenced by adaptive-cycle ecology who already rejects "collapse" language but only on empirical grounds, and a Pacific historian who recently defended the view that Rapa Nui experienced genuine demographic crash rather than mere organizational shift. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each interlocutor would actually use, with notes on what I should concede, what I should hold, and how to reply within a five-minute exchange. Identify the weakest point in my own position—namely, that if local populations themselves adopted narratives of loss and discontinuity, my distinction between systemic decoupling and narrative collapse risks becoming merely semantic rather than substantive—and tell me how to defend it rather than hope it is missed. Finally, include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises: whether my framework can coherently distinguish an "institutional phase transition" from a genocide or forced displacement that has been narratively sanitized by survivors or by later historiography.
