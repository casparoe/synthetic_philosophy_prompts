Examination question:

Aristotle argues in Nicomachean Ethics VII.3 that the akratic agent acts against her own correct judgement, comparing her state of knowledge to that of a sleeper or an actor on stage. George Ainslie's picoeconomic model explains weakness of will through hyperbolic temporal discounting and intertemporal bargaining, wherein present rewards receive disproportionate weight and self-control is achieved by bundling future choices into a single package. Evaluate whether Ainslie's model genuinely illuminates Aristotle's account. In particular, consider (i) whether hyperbolic discounting explains a failure of judgement or merely a shift in preference, and (ii) whether Aristotle's distinction between universal and particular knowledge can be mapped onto the temporal structure of discounting.

Student answer:

Aristotle's analysis of akrasia in NE VII.3 shows that the weak-willed agent possesses the correct universal judgement—for instance, that sweet things are harmful—but fails to apply it to the particular case before her. This is not simple ignorance but a kind of "bound" knowledge, like that of a sleeper or a drunkard, where appetite prevents the knowledge from being actively exercised. Ainslie's hyperbolic discounting model offers a precise mechanism for this binding: as the reward draws nearer in time, its present value rises disproportionately, causing a preference reversal. The agent does not change her belief that excess is harmful; she merely applies a lower credibility weight to distant outcomes, treating the future harm as less probable rather than less valuable. Thus hyperbolic discounting captures the Aristotelian idea that rational evaluation remains intact while motivational structure shifts.

There are important differences. For Aristotle, the akratic acts contrary to her prohairesis—her deliberated choice—whereas in Ainslie's model the agent is always maximising intertemporal utility given her current discount curve. The conflict is therefore not within a single evaluation but between successive temporal selves. Furthermore, Aristotle's particular knowledge concerns concrete circumstances (this is sweet, this is now), not temporal distance per se. Ainslie's intertemporal bargaining explains self-control through bundling: the agent treats present and future choices as part of one package, flattening the discount curve. This parallels Aristotle's claim that the temperate person possesses a stable character that prevents appetite from overwhelming reason, though Aristotle emphasises habituation rather than strategic precommitment.

Overall, Ainslie provides a descriptive framework that makes Aristotle's "bound knowledge" empirically tractable, but he fails to capture the normative dimension. For Aristotle, the akratic is blameworthy precisely because she retains her correct judgement, whereas on Ainslie's account she simply has different preferences at different times, with no single judgement to violate.
