I'm trying to pin down a specific tension I've noticed in arguments comparing Buddhist ethics with consequentialist reasoning, and I want to know whether it already has an established name or framing. The case is familiar: a bodhisattva deceives a murderer to save lives, and defenders justify this by appealing to compassionate intention together with the magnitude of suffering prevented. Critics reply that the very mental act of weighing outcomes—counting lives saved against lives lost, computing comparative totals—introduces a form of calculative grasping or conceptual fixation that Buddhist moral psychology treats as a defilement, regardless of how pure the underlying compassion is. So the consequentialist justification that makes the action seem correct from the outside simultaneously threatens to corrupt the internal intentional state that Buddhist ethics takes as primary. This creates a reflexive problem: the more rigorously one justifies the act in consequentialist terms, the more one undermines the karmic purity that the justification was meant to protect. Is there a recognized term for this conflict—between a reasoning method and the intentional conditions it requires—in Buddhist ethical literature or in comparative philosophy? And how exactly does it relate to the standard debates about skillful means, which tend to ask whether harmful actions are permitted, rather than asking whether the reasoning behind them pollutes the agent?
