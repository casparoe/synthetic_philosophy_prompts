The philosophical debate over whether there is a morally decisive distinction between killing and letting die — and, more generally, between doing harm and allowing it — has run since at least the 1960s, with the familiar repertoire of direct cases (trolley switches, drowning children, bedside withdrawal) now thoroughly rehearsed. More recently, a narrower but sharper impasse has formed around what might be called protocol-mediated harm: situations in which an agent designs or sustains an institutional rule or automated system that, once activated, withholds life-saving aid under conditions the agent foresaw, without the agent physically intervening at the moment of harm. One side maintains that this remains a form of allowing, since the agent’s causal contribution is limited to establishing the conditions; the other side argues that authoring the protocol is itself an act of doing harm, because the agent has fixed the outcome in advance and merely delegated execution to a mechanism. Both camps accept the standard direct-case intuitions; both reject a flat consequentialist collapse of the distinction; and yet they have not moved in years. Is this stall substantive — rooted in competing metaphysics of causation or in different conceptions of moral agency — or is it verbal, turning on whether the term “do” can cover distributed or temporally extended actions? Or are the two sides answering different questions: one about individual culpability at the point of non-intervention, the other about the moral evaluation of institutional design? Diagnose the disagreement concretely, drawing on what each side actually says about agency and causal contribution. Present your own hypothesis about the nature of the stall as a hypothesis, not a verdict. And say what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction (for example, between “standing” and “triggered” allowing), a formal model of distributed agency, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no resolution ought to be expected.
