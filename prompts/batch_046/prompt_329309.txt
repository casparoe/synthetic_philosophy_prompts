As a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up philosophy of medicine and personal identity, I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a large teaching hospital, and I must circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. The case concerns a patient who, five years ago, executed a detailed advance directive refusing all life-sustaining measures should she ever suffer what the document calls "irreversible loss of autonomous agency," defined therein as a state in which no recovery of self-directed decision-making is medically probable. She is now in a complete locked-in state: fully conscious, cognitively intact by every available neuropsychological measure, and able to communicate only through vertical eye movement. Her family argues that the author of the directive implicitly modeled her future as unconscious or severely cognitively impaired, not as this precise phenomenological condition—fully present, suffering, yet physically imprisoned—and therefore that the "earlier self" never exercised normative authority over this particular future. The attending neurologists counter that autonomous agency requires the physical expression and execution of preference, which is permanently absent here, so the directive applies with full force. Our committee must recommend either withdrawal of artificial nutrition and ventilation within a defined time frame, or continued maintenance on the grounds that the directive's scope is misaligned with the actual state.

Please draft the formal opinion I will circulate: it should contain a clear, actionable recommendation with an explicit timeline, treat the family's phenomenological-mismatch argument with genuine philosophical seriousness rather than dismissing it as emotional resistance, and honestly address the strongest consideration on the losing side. In your reasoning, engage specifically with the problem of diachronic authority—whether an earlier self can bind a later self whose subjective experience falls outside the range of states the earlier self could have simulated with adequate fidelity. You should also evaluate, without treating it as dispositive, the so-called "phenomenological gap exception" recently proposed in bioethical discussion: the claim that an advance directive loses normative binding force when the targeted condition's subjective character lies beyond the predictive model embedded in the directive, regardless of whether the condition satisfies the directive's objective clinical criteria. The memo should be suitable for a body that includes clinicians, legal counsel, and non-specialists, and it should avoid appeals to authority that depend on citations or literature lists none of us can verify in session.
