I am taking a postgraduate seminar on applied normative ethics, and over the past term my study group has kept returning to the same impasse: we can describe what each of us values—autonomy, welfare, fidelity to tradition, distributive fairness—but whenever we try to negotiate a shared decision, for instance how to weigh a rights-based objection against a consequentialist calculation of harm, the conversation stalls not because anyone changes their mind, but because nobody can agree on what a rational concession would even look like. My supervisor suggested that before comparing existing theories I should write the full specification of what a theory of moral compromise between genuinely incommensurable value systems actually has to deliver, so that I can assess candidates against a standard I have made explicit rather than implicit.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of inter-value-system moral compromise must meet if it is to explain how rational trade-offs can occur between incompatible normative frameworks without reducing one side to a preference-ordering of the other. Give me the full spec: the phenomena it must accommodate, including cases where both parties retain their core commitments after compromise, cases involving what we might call sacred or non-negotiable values within one framework, and cases of unavoidable partial compliance where full adherence to either system is impossible; the distinctions it must not collapse, for example between a genuine moral concession and a mere strategic adjustment of preferences, between compromise that respects the autonomy of value-holders and compromise that amounts to coercive assimilation, and between inter-theoretic exchange and simple aggregation; and the consistency conditions it must satisfy, particularly regarding formal limits on rational preference fusion—whether Arrow-type impossibilities apply in normative space—and regarding compatibility with empirical results about how people actually treat sacred values in negotiation.

For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a camp preference, and clearly mark which are non-negotiable and which are likely preferences of particular theoretical camps, such as a demand for unique determinacy of the compromise outcome, or a demand that the result preserve Pareto efficiency across moral frameworks. Then tell me whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—whether there is a hidden structural result showing that rational moral compromise is impossible in principle, or whether the tension is merely practical. Finally, identify which single requirement does the most damage to popular existing approaches, whether contractualist, aggregative, or liberal-neutralist accounts, and briefly explain why, keeping the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on a full scorecard. I need the list so that I can see what the theory must do before I ask who, if anyone, has done it.
