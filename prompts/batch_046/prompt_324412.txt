I am a junior in my school's upper-level philosophy seminar, where about fourteen of us meet for fifty-minute sessions twice a week, and we have recently begun discussing metaphysics of personal identity. Last Thursday my instructor introduced anatta—the Buddhist denial of a permanent self composed only of impermanent aggregates—and I found myself arguing with a friend afterward that the view either collapses into triviality or asks us to pretend we don't exist; I want to feel, rather than merely accept, why the absence of a core owner is structurally unavoidable rather than emotionally optional.

Could you design a single-session classroom simulation for our group that uses tangible cards or assigned roles, includes a small-stakes payoff scheme that requires no real money, and arranges the rules so that participants physically run into the instability of first-person ownership—through exchange, loss, or reassignment—before anyone names the concept? Please provide concrete rules and materials, a brief debrief that maps the participants' moments of confusion onto the five aggregates without lecturing, a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how a facilitator should handle it, and an honest admission of which feature of anatta—perhaps the affective release of clinging or its soteriological aim—the exercise necessarily cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. Present this as exactly five numbered parts—setup, rules and stakes, the designed rupture, the debrief with failure notes, and the admitted limitation—each only a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
