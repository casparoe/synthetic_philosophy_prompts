I have been studying the institutional architecture of the Paris Agreement, specifically its system of nationally determined contributions, which allows signatory states with incompatible moral foundations—some framing climate obligations as debts to future rights-bearers, others as expressions of present stewardship, national prestige, or intergenerational reciprocity—to bind themselves to reciprocal emission targets without ever adjudicating their underlying ethical disagreements. In a seminar on political philosophy I am taking, a fellow student insisted that this is merely pragmatic diplomacy, but I am struck by what looks like a hidden philosophical premise: the framework treats moral commitments as if they can be partially traded, adjusted, and bundled with material incentives without anyone being required to concede that their foundational values are mistaken. It seems to presuppose that value systems, despite their incommensurability in theory, can yield something like gains from trade in practice.

What view of moral compromise, and of the relationship between ethical justification and institutional agreement, does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the design require that values are genuinely commensurable, or only that they can be bracketed for instrumental coordination? Carefully distinguish what the practice strictly requires—for instance, a meta-ethical claim about the separability of procedural fairness from substantive value convergence—from what merely accompanies it, such as contractualist reasoning or value pluralism. If this implicit theory is incoherent—if, say, treating a deontological duty as negotiable undermines the very authority that makes it a duty—then what would have to change in the architecture of international climate governance? Would we need to abandon reciprocal bargaining in favor of a single authoritative moral framework, or could the institution survive by reconceiving compromise as something other than trade? I want to know whether this form of moral compromise is philosophically sustainable or whether it hides an inconsistency that becomes visible only when we examine its presuppositions closely.
