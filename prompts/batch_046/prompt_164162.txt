I've been trying to make sense of what politicians and commentators mean when they invoke "the will of the people," especially in arguments about populism and democratic legitimacy. The phrase seems to rely on an analogy: a nation or electorate is like a single human being, and an election or referendum is like that being making up its mind. If fifty-one percent vote for a policy, that is treated as the collective "wanting" it, in roughly the same way I can say I want tea once I've settled the question for myself. The analogy does important work—it makes it sound coherent to say that a government "violates" the people's will, just as it would be strange for me to act against my own settled decision. But as I've thought about it, the mapping seems strained. In my own case, my will is not just a tally of internal impulses; it involves weighing reasons, holding conflicting desires, revising my view in light of new arguments, and eventually reaching a state that feels like mine. A collective election result, by contrast, is often a snapshot of preferences that can cycle—group A might be preferred to B, B to C, and yet C to A—without any single voter experiencing the outcome as a personal resolution. So I want to ask: if we press the analogy seriously, what corresponds to the person's reasoning process, to internal conflict, to changing one's mind, and to the coherence we normally demand of an individual? Where exactly does the mapping break, particularly regarding unity and continuity? What features of individual agency—authority over one's parts, persistence of identity, the capacity to revise—does the analogy quietly import onto the collective, and do those features actually exist there? Finally, if the disanalogies are severe, does the analogy still support the conclusion that a government is bound to follow the majority result, or does it reduce to a procedural convention with no deeper "will" behind it?
