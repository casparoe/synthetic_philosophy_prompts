I'm currently taking a seminar on democratic legitimacy and comparative constitutional design, and I've become puzzled by a specific institutional divergence that seems to carry hidden philosophical weight. In the United Kingdom, the monarch retains formal prerogatives—to appoint the Prime Minister and dissolve Parliament—even though these powers are governed by convention and have not been exercised against the democratic will for well over a century. By contrast, Sweden's 1974 constitutional reform removed the monarch entirely from the formal process of government formation: the Speaker of the Riksdag proposes the Prime Minister, and the monarch's remaining duties are strictly ceremonial, with no constitutional ambiguity about reserve powers. Meanwhile, Japan's postwar constitution assigns the Emperor acts in matters of state only with the advice and approval of the Cabinet, yet the Emperor still performs diplomatic and ceremonial functions that are constitutionally classified within that same category.

This pattern seems to presuppose more than a simple practical division between symbolic and political authority. What philosophical theory of sovereignty and democratic representation does the coexistence of hereditary office and democratic rule actually require? Does the Swedish arrangement presuppose that popular sovereignty must be monopolized by elected bodies, leaving no formal discretion whatsoever in a non-elected head of state, whereas the British arrangement presupposes that sovereignty can be symbolically embodied in a hereditary figure without being divided? Or are both configurations philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival accounts of legitimacy—such that they function mainly as pragmatic settlements rather than expressions of a coherent view about where ultimate authority resides? And what, if anything, does Japan's intermediate design, in which the Emperor remains constitutionally embedded in state acts but is fully bound by cabinet advice, reveal about whether symbolic neutrality can be maintained without either stripping all formal status or retaining ambiguous reserve capacity?

Please separate what is settled about these constitutional arrangements from what is genuinely contested among scholars of democratic theory and constitutional design, and do not present a contested interpretive view—such as the claim that monarchy necessarily undermines popular sovereignty, or conversely that it uniquely secures national continuity—as if it were the consensus.
