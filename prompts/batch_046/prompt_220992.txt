I'm in a seminar on forecasting and rationality where we've been working through the distinction—often associated with Philip Tetlock—between the inside view and the outside view in predictive judgment. The inside view proceeds by reasoning about the particular causal mechanisms of the case at hand; the outside view begins with the statistical distribution of outcomes in a reference class of apparently similar cases, then adjusts inward. A fellow student has proposed extending this framework into what she calls a theory of "epistemic modesty," arguing that the same dichotomy applies not merely to forecasting future events but to the calibration of confidence in one's own reasoning processes. On her account, taking an inside view of one's cognition means evaluating one's reliability through first-person reflection on the quality of one's arguments, evidence, and mental habits, while taking an outside view means treating oneself as an instance drawn from a population of reasoners operating under comparable conditions, and applying the known base rates of overconfidence, motivated reasoning, and domain-specific bias.

I would like you to compare the original forecasting distinction with this proposed extension into epistemic self-assessment. In particular, are they genuinely continuous—does applying the outside view to one's own beliefs involve the same methodological shift from case-specific reasoning to reference-class reasoning—or do they differ in structure because self-assessment involves a reflexive loop that prediction about external events lacks? The student also introduced a specific worry she named the "mirror boundary problem": the claim that using the outside view on oneself requires stepping outside one's own reasoning in order to judge it statistically unreliable, yet the judgment "I am likely biased in this domain" is itself produced by the very reasoning being evaluated, generating a kind of reflexive instability. Is this mirror boundary a substantive limitation on extending the inside/outside framework to epistemic modesty, or does it dissolve once we distinguish between object-level belief formation and meta-level calibration? Please address whether her extension preserves the core logic of the original distinction, where exactly the analogy breaks if it does, and what the mirror boundary problem reveals about the limits of self-application for statistical self-modeling.
