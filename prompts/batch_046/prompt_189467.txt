For a workshop on practical epistemology I am drafting my commitments on knowing-how versus knowing-that and want a consistency audit before presenting. My position: (a) knowing how is ontologically distinct from knowing that—practical knowledge is an agent-activity relation, not a mental representation; (b) nevertheless, any particular skill can be fully decomposed into propositional beliefs about conditions, instruments, and goals, leaving no mysterious non-propositional residue; (c) intellectualism fails not because practical knowledge lacks propositional structure, but because an agent can hold all relevant propositions and still lack the ability—possession conditions, not content, do the work; (d) in artificial systems that instantiate the same propositional structure computationally, there is knowing-that but no knowing-how, which confirms that the distinction turns on embodied agency rather than on representational content; (e) therefore, teaching a skill requires eventually inducing an embodied procedural state, and explicit instruction alone can never suffice, even in principle. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity, state precisely what each revision costs, and say whether abandoning (a) or (b) better stabilizes the package.
