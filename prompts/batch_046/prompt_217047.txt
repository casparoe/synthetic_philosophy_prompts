I am a sixth-form student presenting to my school's philosophy and politics reading group a paper arguing that Rawls's veil of ignorance should be extended to cover temporal position—whether one is born now or in a distant future—so that the difference principle governs intergenerational justice and obliges present societies to arrange institutions for the worst-off across generations, not merely within them. The audience knows A Theory of Justice well; most defend the standard domestic reading of the difference principle and will regard my extension as either a confusion of justice with benevolence or an illegitimate smuggling of utilitarian aggregation. One member has read Parfit closely and will likely press the non-identity problem. Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as these students would phrase them: which carry genuine philosophical force, which are merely rhetorical; what I should concede at the outset; what I must hold firm; the weakest point in my own argument that I ought to defend openly rather than hope they miss; and the one reply that must land if the proposal is to survive scrutiny. Also tell me the objection I am hoping nobody raises, and how to answer it if they do.
