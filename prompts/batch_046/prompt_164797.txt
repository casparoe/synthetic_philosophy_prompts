Consider the institutional architecture common in several welfare states: parental leave is structured as individually non-transferable entitlements with high wage-replacement rates, but these entitlements expire if not taken within a narrow window after birth, and public childcare subsidies are available almost exclusively during standard working hours and contingent on formal employment or education. At the same time, informal care—whether by grandparents, by parents working reduced hours over many years, or by neighborhood networks—receives no comparable state recognition or compensation. What philosophical commitments about gender equality, the value of care, and the boundary between family and market does this design presuppose? Does it treat equality as symmetric labor-market attachment rather than symmetric care burdens, and does it implicitly hold that care has standing only when it substitutes for formal employment? Reconstruct the implicit theory of autonomy and family embedded in the practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say what a policy that granted domestic care equal standing with market labor—without requiring parallel employment—would have to change.
