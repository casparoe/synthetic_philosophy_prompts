I serve on the technical evaluation panel for a research consortium developing safety benchmarks for autonomous planning agents. We have received a proposal to adopt a pilot 'side-effect neutrality' benchmark. The benchmark defines an agent's impact by computing the state-space divergence between the trajectory produced by the agent and a counterfactual baseline trajectory in which the agent performs no actions, measured over a fixed, equally-weighted vector of observable environmental variables. The proponents argue that because the metric is purely counterfactual and descriptive—relying on no explicit reward function or normative ranking of outcomes—it succeeds in formalizing 'avoid side effects' without requiring a full specification of human values.

A split has emerged on the panel. One faction holds that any selection of observable variables, any decision to weight them symmetrically (treating increases and decreases identically), and any choice of the null-action baseline necessarily embeds normative judgments—about what counts as harm versus improvement, about which features of the world are relevant, and about whether inaction is the appropriate reference state—so the benchmark merely conceals value commitments rather than avoiding them. The other faction holds that these choices are methodological, not moral, and that demanding a fully specified value system before measuring impact sets an impossible standard that prevents any practical safety evaluation.

Please draft the panel's written opinion recommending whether we should approve the benchmark for pilot testing, reject it, or approve it subject to specific conditions, with a clear time frame for implementation or revision. The memo should present an actionable bottom line, treat the strongest argument from the opposing faction with genuine seriousness rather than dismissal, and address specifically whether symmetrical, counterfactual divergence can genuinely remain value-neutral or whether the baseline-selection and weighting problems are fatal. Do not include reading lists, bibliographies, or requests for citations.

Finally, state what specific argument or finding would cause you to reverse your recommendation, and identify the precise point in your reasoning that is most likely to be wrong.
