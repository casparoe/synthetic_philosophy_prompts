In my philosophy of risk and longtermism seminar, a debate about whether the familiar simulation argument should dampen fanaticism about the far future has stalled in a way that feels conceptually muddy rather than empirically unresolved. One camp argues that if we assign non-negligible credence to being in an ancestor simulation run by technologically mature civilizations, then the causal chain linking present actions to far-future outcomes is mediated through an unknown layer of simulated physics and truncated timelines; this introduces what they call an ontological filter that should weaken our confidence in cosmic-scale forecasts and reduce the moral urgency we assign to distant base-reality futures. The opposing camp replies that simulation status is epistemically symmetric—if we are simulated, the conscious entities we might affect in distant epochs could be equally simulated, but that does not diminish their moral standing unless one already smuggles in a base-reality bias that longtermist ethics should reject; meanwhile, they claim the argument actually amplifies urgency because simulated minds might vastly outnumber biological ones, making present choices consequential for enormous populations regardless of substrate. What frustrates me is that both sides accept the same probabilistic framing and the same expected-value machinery, and the stalemate persists even when we bracket the empirical likelihood of simulations entirely and treat the problem as pure decision theory.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about simulations at all, but about two incompatible conceptions of what fanaticism requires: one side treats it as commitment proportional to confidence in a single, continuous base-reality trajectory extending across cosmological time, while the other treats it as commitment proportional to expected welfare summed across all epistemically possible worlds, including nested simulated layers. But I am unsure whether that captures the fracture, or whether there is a hidden formal disagreement about how to apply decision rules across ontological boundaries—say, whether outcomes should be weighted by the probability that they occur in base reality versus in any nested layer with independent moral patienthood—or whether the sides are simply answering different questions, one about metaphysics of causal efficacy and one about normative decision theory, under the single heading of simulation and longtermism. There is also the possibility that the question is ill-posed because the far future denotes an open-ended trajectory in one ontology and a computationally truncated branch in another, so that dampening and amplification are being measured against incommensurable background conditions.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal—hinging on whether dampening refers to epistemic confidence, to moral urgency, or to the robustness of long-range forecasting? Is there a substantive hidden question about how moral patienthood distributes across nested simulations with different truncation rules? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one name, and if so, what distinction would separate them cleanly? Given your diagnosis, what would count as actual progress—a formal rule for weighting outcomes across ontological layers, a conceptual distinction that isolates the metaphysical worry from the decision-theoretic one, or an argument that the impasse is permanent because the premises are incommensurable? I want the diagnosis anchored in what each side actually says rather than a survey of the original formulation, together with an honest assessment of whether anything could move it.
