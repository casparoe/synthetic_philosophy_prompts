I've been working through some of the institutional critiques of effective altruism that circulate in rationalist and adjacent spaces, particularly the tension between earning-to-give frameworks and what critics call institutional capture—where high-income career paths are said to reproduce harmful structures even when they generate donation revenue. In a recent conversation, a colleague who has read the popular critical literature but not the original philosophical defenses summarized the position as follows: effective altruism is committed to a strict counterfactual moral accounting in which an individual's ethical value is determined entirely by the donation differential they can produce relative to a baseline career; this necessarily entails that any income not donated is treated as an active moral loss, which in turn licenses the earning-to-give strategy by allowing people in damaging industries to offset harm through partial contribution. They added that the movement's well-known measurability bias—its preference for interventions with quantifiable outcomes—is not an epistemic caution but a strategic filter that systematically excludes structural and political reforms from consideration, because those cannot be expressed in standard metrics like quality-adjusted life years or cost-effectiveness ratios. I find the summary compelling in parts, but I worry it collapses several distinct objections into one and presents a standard critique as if it were an internal contradiction.

Could you help me audit this characterization? Specifically, which elements accurately reflect arguments made by serious institutional critics—say, regarding the moral psychology of offsetting or the allocation consequences of quantification—versus which are subtle misrepresentations of how defenders of earning-to-give or measurability actually reason? Does the inference that counterfactual evaluation necessarily entails treating non-donated income as a moral loss hold up, or does it conflate an evaluative metric with a theory of moral obligation? And regarding the measurability point: is it fair to say that the preference for quantifiable outcomes functions primarily to exclude structural reform, or does that charge depend on a particular view of what counts as evidence that defenders might reject? I am not looking for a bibliography, but I would like to know whether the conclusion—that this framework necessarily licenses harmful career retention—follows once the misrepresentations are corrected, or whether the accurate core of the critique points to a different kind of problem altogether.
