The modernist account of nations—associated with Hobsbawm’s analysis of invented tradition and Anderson’s argument about imagined communities mediated by print-capitalism—holds that the nation as we understand it is largely a nineteenth-century construct, with earlier collective identities being dynastic, religious, or local rather than national. The perennialist or ethnosymbolist counter-current insists that ethnic cores, shared memories, and collective self-naming extend back to the medieval or early modern period, citing vernacular literatures, chronicles, and pre-modern administrative records. In my reading the debate has stalled for decades: both camps cite the same sources, interpret them through opposite definitional lenses, and never converge. My hypothesis is that this is partly verbal—turning on whether “nation” requires what I will call reflexive demographic self-description, by which I mean an institutionalized practice in which a group describes itself in bounded, statistical, destiny-oriented terms rather than being categorized by rulers or clergy—and partly that the sides answer different questions, one about institutional form and one about subjective identification. Is that right, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about how widespread such self-description was before 1800? Are they divided by bedrock intuitions about continuity, or is the question ill-posed?
