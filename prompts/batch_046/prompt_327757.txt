I was discussing with colleagues over coffee whether our postgraduate metaphysics seminar on scientific ontology has become rather stale—students now arrive fluent in the tired realism-versus-nominalism script and treat the question of whether the sciences carve nature at its joints as if it had only two answers. For next term I want a sharper diagnostic instrument that forces people to locate themselves in logical space before they reach for a label.

Could you please design a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies positions on natural kinds not by the names philosophers wear, but by their answers to a sequence of concrete, branching questions? The tree ought to begin with whether a category counts as natural only if it figures in strict, exceptionless laws; then divide according to whether biological species are paradigm natural kinds or merely pragmatic heuristics; then according to whether chemical kinds such as gold depend on hidden microstructural essences or on stable macroscopic dispositional profiles; and finally according to whether psychiatrically defined kinds—for instance, major depressive disorder—should be admitted despite well-documented causal heterogeneity. Each leaf should carry the name of an actual position if the space is occupied—essentialist realism, homeostatic property cluster realism, promiscuous realism, or similar—or be marked 'unnamed', together with a single sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Please also flag any pairs of positions that the tree cannot distinguish, annotate unoccupied branches as apparently incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and present the branching logic with clear labels. Do not include any question that simply asks 'are you a realist?', and please write your response in British English.
