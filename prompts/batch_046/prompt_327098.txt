I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar, to be completed in 90 minutes. Draft one essay question on peer disagreement—conciliationism versus steadfastness—that cannot be answered by reciting the standard literature. Present a concrete scenario: a behavioral economist and a clinical psychologist both study whether a specific cognitive bias undermines free will; they share all relevant data, each regards the other as an epistemic peer on the first-order empirical claim, but they diverge because they operate within different methodological frameworks that define what counts as decisive evidence differently. The question should ask whether conciliationism applies to the framework-level divergence or only to the first-order belief, and whether steadfastness can be defended if the disagreement traces to a normative commitment about evidential standards rather than to a shared body of evidence evaluated differently.

Provide a model answer of about 700 words that takes a definite, committed position—do not merely map both views and leave the choice open. The answer should defend either a restricted conciliationism that applies only within shared frameworks, or a qualified steadfastness that distinguishes framework-level disagreement from genuine peer disagreement, and it should explain precisely why the rival view collapses or overreaches in this case. Then give a marking scheme built around four must-hit points, with marks assigned out of 20, and include a one-line note for each point on the specific error that typically costs marks there. The scheme should separate answers that solve the framing problem from those that revert to generic summaries of Elga-style or Christensen-style arguments. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the answer must stand on its reasoning alone. Make the prompt explicit that a committed stance with reasons is required, not an even-handed survey.
