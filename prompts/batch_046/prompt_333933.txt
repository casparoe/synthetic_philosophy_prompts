I have been thinking on my own about why time seems to run in only one direction—why we remember yesterday but not tomorrow, why broken cups do not reassemble, and why it feels as though the future is open while the past is fixed. I understand that physicists often point to entropy and the thermodynamic arrow, but that strikes me as describing a statistical tendency rather than explaining why my own experience is oriented. At the same time, I have read enough to know that the fundamental laws of physics are largely time-reversible, which makes it puzzling how any deep direction could emerge. I want to understand what a genuine explanation of time's direction would actually have to accomplish.

Please write the full list of desiderata—what any adequate theory in this area must be able to do. For each requirement, explain the case or consideration that makes it necessary: whether it is a physical result that cannot be contradicted (such as the second law or the reversibility of microscopic dynamics), a feature of conscious experience that must be accounted for (such as the asymmetry of memory and anticipation), or a conceptual distinction that must be preserved (such as the difference between time being fundamentally directed versus merely appearing directed because of boundary conditions, or between a local arrow confined to one region and a universal arrow). Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps—for example, whether compatibility with general relativity is essential or whether preserving a global present moment is a negotiable addition.

I especially want you to examine whether some subset of these demands is jointly unsatisfiable. Can a theory make the arrow fundamental rather than derived, derive it purely from entropy increase, and still respect the time-symmetry of the underlying physical laws? If there is a tension or outright contradiction among these, say so clearly; that is the part I care about most.

As one further constraint—not always discussed in standard introductions—the theory must be able to handle what I will call local arrow divergence: the hypothetical scenario in which two isolated cosmological regions evolve with opposite entropic gradients, and observers within each region attempt to compare notes. The theory must explain whether they would genuinely disagree about which temporal direction counts as the future, and it must do so without collapsing into contradiction or abandoning the reality of both regions' experiences.

Finally, briefly indicate how the main existing approaches—the boundary-condition or entropic account, a view that treats temporal direction as built into the fundamental structure of reality (for instance, through tense or becoming), and a causal theory that grounds temporal order in causal relations—measure up against your list. Do not give a full scorecard; keep the weight on the criteria themselves and on any conflicts among them, and tell me which single requirement eliminates the largest number of popular theories.
