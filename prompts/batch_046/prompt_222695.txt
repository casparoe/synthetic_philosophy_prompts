I'm a doctoral candidate in political philosophy working on democratic boundary problems, and I've been embedded with a working group drafting membership rules for the Upper Rhine Aquifer Governance Council, an inter-municipal body with cross-border jurisdiction over groundwater extraction. The staff face a steady stream of proposals—irrigation restrictions, extraction taxes, signage mandates—each affecting different combinations of residents, downstream agricultural users, cross-border commuters who work in the region but reside outside it, and corporate actors whose headquarters lie beyond the jurisdiction. They need a single reusable decision procedure, runnable by a non-specialist staffer in about twenty minutes per proposal, to determine who belongs to the demos for any given decision. The group insists on combining two familiar principles—all affected interests and all subjected to coercion—with a third criterion they have introduced themselves, which they call structural complicity: anyone whose routine economic practices are causally embedded in the implementation of the decision, even when neither direct welfare effects nor legal coercion apply to them personally, must be included if their exclusion would permit strategic externalization of costs.

Please operationalize this three-part framework into a step-by-step checklist. Specify the exact order of questions, the evidentiary threshold that counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the points at which the procedure issues a definitive membership determination and stops, and the precise conditions under which it must halt and refer the case to the council chair because the issue is one of values rather than fact. The procedure should be explicit about conflicts among the three conditions—particularly when structural complicity pulls in actors that the other two criteria exclude. Then apply it to three proposals: first, a summer irrigation cap for upstream vineyards that harms downstream farmers without legally coercing them; second, a mandatory bilingual signage ordinance that binds local businesses but primarily impacts non-resident cross-border commuters; and third, an extraction tax targeting multinational bottling firms whose daily workforce commutes into the region but whose shareholders and headquarters are abroad. For one of these applications, the procedure should yield a membership determination the working group is likely to resist—identify which one and explain why. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot settle and where it is most prone to misfire.
