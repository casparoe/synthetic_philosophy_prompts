I'm designing a graduate seminar on the interaction between trolley-style dilemmas and the doctrine of double effect, and I'm particularly interested in a class of cases that standard treatments tend to gloss over: what might be called mediated instrumentalization. By this I mean situations in which an agent saves several people by initiating a causal chain in which one person's body or labor functions as an essential bridge, yet the agent never physically touches that person—consider, for instance, remotely triggering a switch that causes a single maintenance worker to fall onto a track, blocking a runaway train and saving five passengers. The worker is not pushed, but his body is causally enlisted as a means.

With that framing in mind, please rank the following four reconstructions of the doctrine into tiers—tier 1 for the most defensible, tier 2 for workable but seriously flawed, and tier 3 for the least defensible—specifically with respect to their ability to explain why mediated instrumentalization is wrong (if it is) while permitting standard switch-diversion, without collapsing into ad hoc stipulation. The reconstructions are: (1) the classical mental-state account, which distinguishes intended harm from merely foreseen harm; (2) the causal-structural account, which defines a means as any harm that lies in the causal path producing the good; (3) the dignity-based account, which prohibits treating any person's vital interests as having merely instrumental value regardless of proximity or causation; and (4) the deliberative-representation account, which abandons metaphysical means-ends analysis and instead asks whether the agent's practical reasoning represents the harmed party as having value only as a tool. For each tier, provide a one-sentence justification that appeals either to internal consistency or to predictive power over intuitive judgments. You need not cite authors, texts, or page numbers; reason directly from the conceptual architecture of each position.
