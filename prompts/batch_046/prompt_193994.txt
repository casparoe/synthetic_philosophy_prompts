I am a novelist working on a long-form essay—tentatively called The Ritual Remains—that examines democratic backsliding through a lens I have begun to call procedural nostalgia. Rather than measuring erosion through the usual institutional checklists—court packing, media capture, electoral manipulation—I want to examine the strategic preservation and even intensification of democratic rituals, such as competitive elections, parliamentary debate, judicial review, and regular press briefings, precisely at the moment when those rituals have lost any practical capacity to alter outcomes. I am comparing four cases—Hungary under Viktor Orbán, Poland during the Law and Justice years, Turkey under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, and the contemporary United States—not to rank them on an index of democracy, but to ask whether the concept of backsliding has itself become a partisan label rather than an analytical category. In Hungary, the regime has openly embraced an illiberal democratic identity; in Poland, judicial reforms were framed as democratic corrections of an unaccountable judicial elite; in Turkey, majoritarian rhetoric absorbs dissent into a narrative of unified national will; and in the United States, the events surrounding January 6 and their subsequent absorption into ordinary partisan contestation seem to represent a different trajectory, one in which democratic vocabulary does not disappear but is incorporated into routine political performance. My central claim is that backsliding ought to be distinguished from democratic collapse: the former is characterized not by the abolition of democratic form but by its transformation into a kind of genre convention, a narrative structure that citizens and elites enact without expecting it to generate actual political change. I am concerned that this framework may collapse into mere metaphor, that comparing the American case to the others is either analytically reckless or transparently partisan, and that I may be underestimating the degree to which ordinary citizens in these regimes still experience constrained but genuine political agency.

Please offer comments on the essay's conceptual architecture, particularly on whether procedural nostalgia functions as a coherent analytical category or only as a literary flourish. I am especially interested in whether the comparative framework across Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States holds together or fractures under scrutiny, and whether my treatment of the American case successfully avoids the charge of partisan labeling that the essay itself critiques. I would also value observations on accuracy: do I mischaracterize the mechanisms of judicial capture in Poland, the nature of electoral competition in Hungary, or the relationship between majoritarian rhetoric and institutional practice in Turkey? Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether this reframing—treating backsliding as semantic and ritual preservation rather than straightforward institutional erosion—offers something genuinely new, or whether it unnecessarily complicates an already well-established literature.
