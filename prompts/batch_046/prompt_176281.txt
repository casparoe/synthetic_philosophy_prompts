I have drafted an essay arguing that if analogy is indeed the core of cognition, as Hofstadter and Sander maintain in Surfaces and Essences, then the familiar binary taxonomy of "surface features" versus "deep structure" should be replaced by a graded continuum of what I call "resonance densities." On this view, what we call a deep analog is not one that accesses hidden essences but one that activates a region of high cross-linking density within a conceptual network. The essay applies this framework to a specific case: expert mathematicians recognizing structural similarity between Galois theory and symmetry-breaking patterns in crystal lattices despite striking surface dissimilarity. I then propose that cognitive science might benefit from replacing the binary distinction with a graded "resonance field" metric—though I concede that I have not formalized how such a metric would be computed or validated. I also note that this framework risks inviting a misconception: if all analogies are merely matters of network density, then working-memory constraints and attentional selection might be wrongly treated as peripheral rather than constitutive.

Please comment on this essay outline and its central claims. I am especially interested in three questions: first, whether replacing the surface/deep binary with a graded continuum actually resolves a tension in Hofstadter and Sander's framework or merely relabels the same problem; second, whether the Galois-theory-and-crystal-lattice example genuinely illustrates high resonance density, or whether it depends on an idiosyncratic reading of one or both domains; and third, whether there is an obvious misconception—perhaps that a sufficiently dense network makes all analogies equally available, thereby erasing the role of attention and working memory—that my framework invites but fails to guard against.
