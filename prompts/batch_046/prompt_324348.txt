Construct a thought experiment that merges the Platonia dilemma with a luring-lottery payoff structure, but replace the standard mechanism of perfect logical correlation or omniscient prediction with a condition of graded, asymmetrical substrate transparency: two rational agents—co-auditors reviewing a distributed-ledger protocol upgrade—each execute their decision algorithms on separate nodes and, before committing to cooperate or defect, can inspect a noisy, truncated cryptographic hash of the other node's running source code. The noise is calibrated so that correlation is partial and asymmetrical: Agent A receives a somewhat clearer hash of B's algorithm than B receives of A's. The payoff matrix is a luring lottery: mutual cooperation yields a moderate shared reward; mutual defection yields nothing; unilateral defection yields a large payout to the defector and a severe penalty to the cooperator, with the defector's reward scaling upward in proportion to the opacity of their own source code to the other party. Keep the institutional setting realistic, the narrative confined to roughly one paragraph, and avoid any appeal to exotic physics, omniscient demons, or perfect predictors.

Then, in clearly separated sections, deliver the following: (a) specify the exact epistemic state—what each agent knows about the correlation between their choices and about the asymmetry in hash visibility; (b) derive the recommendation of Platonia-style superrationality for each agent, taking explicit account of the partial correlation and the asymmetrical transparency rather than assuming perfect symmetry; (c) derive the recommendation of causal decision theory, treating the hash-inspection mechanism as a causal process with no backward influence on the other node's execution; (d) derive the recommendation of evidential decision theory, treating the visible hash as statistical evidence about the other agent's likely choice; (e) isolate the single structural feature—whether the asymmetrical transparency, the opacity-scaled payout, or the partial correlation—that drives any divergence among the three theories, and explain precisely why removing that feature collapses the case back into either a canonical Platonia dilemma or a canonical Newcomb-style problem; (f) conclude with an honest defensive reply that a proponent of the theory that fares worst could mount, specifically addressing whether the graded substrate-access violates the symmetry prerequisites required for superrational reasoning, or whether it illegitimately severs an evidential link that decision theory ought to honor.
