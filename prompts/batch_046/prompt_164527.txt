I serve as the faculty representative on our university’s Committee for Interdisciplinary Research Governance, and we are required to circulate a formal decision memo before the board’s March session. The matter before us is the adoption of a permanent justification protocol for cross-disciplinary grant applications—specifically, whether our institution should require that every research claim be supported by further claims without termination, that justification terminate in a designated class of basic disciplinary approvals, or that validity consist solely in mutual coherence among already-approved propositions.

The immediate trigger is a stalled pilot collaboration between our Department of Cognitive Science and our Philosophy of Mind program. The cognitive scientists insist that empirical premises must bottom out in statistical bedrock—approvals that are not themselves justified by further disciplinary claims—while the philosophers maintain that any such bedrock is arbitrary and that justification must instead form a closed web of mutually supporting propositions. Meanwhile, an external auditor has argued that both positions illegitimately halt inquiry: the only intellectually honest architecture is an open-ended chain in which no claim receives final approval, with peer review continuing indefinitely as new premises and objections emerge.

We must recommend one of the three architectures—foundationalist termination, coherentist closure, or infinitist openness—as the binding institutional standard, together with a concrete implementation timeline proposing a phased rollout beginning in the fall semester. The memo should treat the strongest objection to our recommendation with genuine seriousness rather than dismissive summary. In particular, it must address what I will call the problem of justificatory fatigue: the institutional cost, in personnel hours and cognitive resources, of requiring that justification continue without termination, and whether that practical burden undermines the viability of the infinitist model even if it is epistemically pure. The recommendation must be fully actionable: it should specify which administrative office will oversee the protocol, what formally counts as a terminus or as a non-terminus, how disputes between disciplines will be adjudicated, and what concrete time frame governs the transition. Please draft the committee’s written opinion in formal memo format, with a clear bottom-line recommendation, reasoning that engages each architecture on its own terms, an honest account of why the rejected alternatives fail—especially addressing whether foundational bedrock can avoid arbitrariness and whether coherentist webs can avoid hidden circularity—and a brief implementation schedule.
