You advise a transitional-justice audit office that evaluates whether present-day state institutions bear collective responsibility for harms enacted by predecessor regimes across generations. Your task is to translate Karl Jaspers's fourfold distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt into an operational protocol that auditors without philosophical training can apply repeatedly. The protocol should take the form of an ordered decision procedure: state the questions in sequence, define what evidentiary finding counts as sufficient for each category of guilt, identify the exact stop-points at which the audit must return a negative determination, and flag the nodes where judgment is irreducibly normative rather than factual and must be escalated to an ethics board. The procedure must specifically address intergenerational transmission—distinguishing institutional continuity (budgetary lineage, archival custody, staff descent) from personal culpability—and must include explicit tests for whether a successor body has formally repudiated the original policies. Apply the protocol to two audit targets: a ministry of education that retains assimilation-era school records, employs lineal descendants of the original administrative staff, and has issued a public disavowal; and a regional heritage museum that curates artifacts from the same period, receives current state funding, and has adopted a critical exhibition narrative. For each target, show where the procedure produces an unwelcome result, and specify precisely what the protocol leaves unsettled—especially the boundary between inherited political guilt and metaphysical solidarity with victims.
