I am designing a module for an advanced philosophy seminar on rational choice under extreme uncertainty, and I want to clarify a conceptual tension that has come up repeatedly in our discussions. Please compare expected-value fanaticism — the thesis that rational agency requires maximizing expected value without any ceiling or saturation point, so that even astronomically remote possibilities of enormous good should dominate practical deliberation — with what I will call the doctrine of bounded moral salience. On this latter view, the normative force of an outcome is not determined solely by its magnitude multiplied by its probability; rather, it is also modulated by its position relative to the agent's epistemic and causal horizon. Once outcomes become so remote or so opaque that they fall outside the boundary within which the agent can form reliable intentions or bear meaningful responsibility, further increases in their scale do not generate proportionally stronger obligations, and may eventually generate none.

Specifically, I would like you to examine the disagreement along three axes. First, analyze how each framework handles low-probability, high-magnitude scenarios: does the fanatic's calculus treat a one-in-ten-billion chance of preserving an immense future value as lexically decisive, and if so, how does bounded salience resist that conclusion without simply reverting to risk aversion? Second, contrast their implicit conceptions of practical rationality — in particular, whether expected-value fanaticism depends on an agent-neutral, scale-invariant metric of value that bounded salience denies, or whether the dispute is ultimately about the cognitive and moral limits of human agency rather than about value itself. Third, evaluate whether bounded moral salience can maintain internal coherence without introducing an arbitrary cutoff point, or whether any such boundary collapses either into conventional threshold deontology or into a form of moral particularism that abandons general principles altogether. Write in a sustained, analytical style suitable for graduate students; do not provide a bibliography or appeal to specific scholarly authorities by name, but reason through the conceptual structure directly.
