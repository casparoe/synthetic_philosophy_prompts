I was arguing with a friend about democratic boundaries after skimming some old Dahl stuff, and I wrote out this chain that now seems fishy because it basically proves democracy is impossible if you take it seriously. Can you tell me where the slip is?

(1) A decision is democratically legitimate only if everyone whose core interests are significantly affected by it gets an equal vote — that's the all-affected principle, and it feels like a baseline requirement.

(2) Say a town council approves a chemical plant. The runoff hits a river that crosses state lines, and the carbon output contributes to global warming, so people hundreds of miles away and in other countries are affected.

(3) By (1), the demos for that decision should expand to include everyone globally who bears any meaningful risk from the plant or its climate footprint.

(4) But if the global population votes on whether this specific town gets a plant, then local residents are outnumbered by outsiders with no stake in local jobs or tax revenue — so local self-government is destroyed.

(5) So democracy requires both that the boundary expand globally (from 1-3) and that it stay local (from 4). These contradict, which means there's no consistent way to draw the demos for any real decision.

(6) But obviously countries have borders and elections happen, so (5) must be wrong. I think the problem might be an equivocation between 'affected in some causal chain' and 'affected in a way that generates a claim to participate,' but I'm not sure how to fix it without giving up (1) entirely. Which step should I reject, and does rejecting it leave democratic theory with a boundary problem it can't solve?
