I've mostly bought into the internalist line on reasons -- if a consideration can't in principle enter an agent's deliberation given their actualy cognitive architecture, then it just isn't a reason for them, not even an 'objective' one they're missing. I think this survives the usual thirsty-person counterexample because knowledge-access is part of architecture, not some external fact. But I'm worried I've qualified it into unfalsifiability. What would actually count against it? Skip the standard 'moral reasons for the selfish agent' reheated. Instead, I'm curious about cases where an agent's architecture gets partially rewired by an external system -- not changing beliefs, but altering how options get weighed -- and whether that shows reasons can be 'injected' without being internal to the pre-modification agent. Do split-brain results about hemispheric independence mess with this too? If one hemisphere acts on considerations the other doesn't endorse, does that make reasons architecture-relative in a way that collapses internalism into something trivial? Rank the defeaters: fatal, just awkward, or absorbable by tweaking what counts as 'the agent's architecture'. And be honest -- is the view actually exposed here, or have I just built a wall?
