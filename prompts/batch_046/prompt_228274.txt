My gut reactions on choice problems where expected value is undefined or infinite, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or I'm just being inconsistent.

Case 1: Classic St. Petersburg. I'd pay maybe $20, definately not infinity. So infinite EV doesn't mean "pay anything."

Case 2: The Pasadena game — payoff depends on a hidden bias paramter, and integrating over outcomes vs integrating over the paramter first gives different answers, leaving EV undefined. Here I feel paralyzed; I don't think there's a right price, not just that it's high.

Case 3: A 99.9% chance of $100, and in the remaining tail a St. Petersburg subgame. EV is still infinite, but I treat it like "take the near-certain $100" and ignore the tail. That feels different from Case 2, even though both involve undefinedness.

Case 4: Option A is $50 sure. Option B is identical except in one state with probability literally 0 (included in the model) you get -infinity utility, so B's EV is undefined or -inf depending on convention. I still pick B without hesitation.

Case 5: A moral version — save 10 people for sure, or save an undefined/infinite number if some cosmological paramter makes the multiverse infinite and every save propagates. I lean toward the 10, but I'm way less sure than in Case 4.

Is there a coherent account — maybe about whether the undefinedness is a physical ambiguity vs a mathematical artifact, or about dominance in every non-null state — that saves all five? Or which one do I have to drop? And what should it say about a truncation case: every finite cutoff of the payoff favors option A consistently, but the full limit is undefined?
