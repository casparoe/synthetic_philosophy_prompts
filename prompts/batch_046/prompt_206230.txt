In my seminar on moral psychology we have been tracking the taxonomy used by Dr. Elena Voss, who classifies other people’s normative judgments as either legitimate preferences, legitimate normative commitments, or illegitimate biases. Her published case evaluations are consistent and unrevised: (1) a chef who refuses to cook with cilantro because it tastes like soap is expressing a preference, since the judgment tracks her own sensory experience without imposing on others; (2) a hiring manager who rejects candidates with visible hand tremors because she finds them “unsettling,” even for a fully remote position, is displaying bias, because an affective response is projected onto a feature with no causal bearing on job performance; (3) a religious community that prohibits pork for reasons of ritual purity is engaged in a normative commitment, not a bias, because the rule applies symmetrically to all members within a coherent practice; (4) a teacher who grades essays in purple ink lower solely because she dislikes the color is biased, since the feature is arbitrary with respect to the grading criterion; (5) a parent who refuses vaccination based on a discredited belief about autism is biased, because the judgment purports to protect the child but rests on a false empirical link; and (6) a municipal arts council that rejects a proposed sculpture because members find it “emotionally draining,” while conceding its artistic merit and denying any harmful social effect, is expressing a preference, on the grounds that selecting shared aesthetic environments legitimately involves collective emotional response.

Consider now a new case for her taxonomy. A public transit authority mandates hard plastic seating on all trains, rejecting softer upholstered alternatives. The officials explain that soft fabrics “feel overly intimate and encourage a kind of emotional vulnerability among strangers that undermines civic distance,” and they apply the rule uniformly across every line and demographic. They acknowledge that passenger comfort is not a safety issue, that upholstered seats are actually cheaper to maintain, and that there is no empirical evidence linking upholstery texture to any measurable social or behavioral outcome. They further state that they would maintain the mandate even if they themselves were regular passengers on these lines.

Predict whether Voss would classify this as preference, normative commitment, or bias, and do so by identifying at least two distinct organizing rules that could equally well accommodate her six earlier verdicts but that diverge on this case. For each rule, specify what feature of the transit decision it treats as decisive and what classification it therefore yields. Then propose one further hypothetical case—different in domain but structurally parallel—that would best discriminate between these competing rules. I am less interested in a simple label than in seeing how the ambiguity between arranging shared emotional environments, universalizing an arbitrary affective reaction, and lacking functional justification plays out across her framework.
