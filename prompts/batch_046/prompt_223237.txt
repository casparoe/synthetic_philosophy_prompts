The speaker is defending a modified Hofstadter-and-Sander-style thesis at a cognitive-science seminar. The audience is about fifteen people: mostly predictive-processing and computational-neuroscience researchers, one embodied-cognition philosopher, and a computer-vision researcher. None are analogy specialists. The speaker’s core claim is that analogy-making is not merely high-level reasoning but the fundamental mechanism of cognition, operating via “slippage” between surface features and essential concepts. The speaker’s specific twist—call it “micro-analogy”—is that even basic perceptual object-constancy involves rapid analogical remapping of sensory categories, and that predictive-processing accounts of prediction-error minimization are best understood as a special case of this deeper analogical slippage, not the other way around. The speaker will argue that without allowing category-boundaries to “slip” at the perceptual level, no amount of precision-weighting can explain how the same object is recognized across radically different viewing conditions.

Draft the speaker’s Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by how damaging and how likely they are for this exact room, not for a philosophy-of-mind audience. For each: a concise two-minute reply, and an explicit note on where the reply is weakest or concedes ground. State up front what the speaker should concede before the objections arrive. Include the single question the speaker is hoping nobody asks—something that exposes the “micro-analogy” claim to an empirical or conceptual challenge it cannot absorb. Keep everything self-contained: no bibliographies, no requests for citations, and no reliance on obscure primary sources the speaker could not defend from memory. Make sure the prep sheet addresses the risk that “analogy” will be dismissed as an underspecified umbrella term in this crowd.
