I recently joined a regional land-use planning board, and we have been asked to review a proposal to drain a large freshwater marsh for agricultural conversion. The staff report evaluates the proposal through standard ecological metrics—biodiversity indices, carbon sequestration, and watershed function—but treats animal welfare as essentially outside the board’s mandate. In conversations with a friend who works in conservation biology, I realized that even among ecologists there is little agreement on whether high-density rodent or invertebrate populations in such marshes represent net suffering or net flourishing, and this ambiguity seems to disappear entirely from regulatory frameworks.

At the same time, I have been thinking about philosophical arguments—familiar in animal ethics but almost never applied to ecosystem management—suggesting that if wild animal lives are, on average, dominated by suffering, then destroying a habitat that supports high-reproduction, short-lived populations might reduce aggregate suffering by preventing those lives, particularly if the replacement land use generates less suffering per hectare. Of course, this ignores trophic complexity: removing a suffering-intensive prey base might increase suffering elsewhere through disrupted predator dynamics or starvation cascades. Given this framing, please describe the most important implications of adopting an explicit “net ecosystem welfare” evaluation criterion—one that weighs habitat destruction partly by its effect on total wild suffering rather than solely on biodiversity or human utility—for the procedural rules, evidentiary standards, and mitigation priorities a board like ours should use. I want to know which distinctions—between r-selected and K-selected dynamics, direct mortality versus indirect suffering across trophic networks, and welfare assessment across taxa with different cognitive architectures—must survive any practical simplification, where the framework demands data that planning boards cannot realistically obtain, and whether it ultimately offers a coherent alternative to conservationism or merely collapses into a development-friendly calculus dressed in ethical language.
