I teach a graduate seminar in political philosophy—fourteen students, ninety minutes. Design a classroom simulation that lets participants feel the core tension in lottocracy: random selection secures descriptive representation and insulation from party capture, yet it severs the accountability loop that citizens rely on in electoral democracy. To make this felt rather than merely discussed, build the game around an invented two-stage mechanism I call the shadow mandate. First, randomly draw six students to form a council; give them a hidden policy brief unknown to the other eight and charge them with proposing a binding rule for distributing a small but real shared resource—say, thirty minutes of discretionary seminar time or a pooled budget for texts. Second, the full class must vote not on the proposed rule, but on whether an unknown mandate could justify it, with the council forbidden to reveal the brief. Include explicit roles, rules, a payoff or incentive structure that does not use real money, a timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps the emotional dynamics of the vote—suspicion, acceptance, or demand for disclosure—onto the philosophical debate about authorization without election. Be frank about which feature of actual sortition—such as long-term institutional memory or iterative learning—this single-shot classroom exercise cannot reproduce, and predict what will go wrong the first time you run it and how to handle it.
