I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy of science and institutional epistemology. I now sit on the governance board of a national research funding body, and we face a recurring task: evaluating proposals to dismantle long-standing procedural safeguards—duplicate peer-review layers, mandatory replication archives, legacy conflict-of-interest disclosures, manual sanity-check protocols—that reformers present as obsolete bureaucratic friction. I want you to construct a reusable decision procedure grounded in the Burkean recognition that inherited institutions embody accumulated practical knowledge, together with Chesterton's fence principle, but adapted specifically to scientific institutions, where the fences are epistemic and procedural rather than physical.

Please frame the procedure around the idea that every institutional fence possesses both an explicit founding rationale and a set of latent coupling functions—hidden interdependencies with other mechanisms that may not be visible until the fence is removed. The practitioner using this procedure is a non-philosopher board member with limited time, who must apply it repeatedly, sign off on the result, and know exactly when to defer to expert judgment rather than algorithmic reasoning.

Your deliverable should include the following elements, in this order.

First, a step-by-step checklist that proceeds from identification of the proposed removal to final recommendation, specifying at each step what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence is required, and under what conditions the procedure terminates with a clear recommendation not to remove or to proceed only with caution. Mark explicitly the decision nodes where values rather than empirical facts govern the outcome, and where a human judgment call is unavoidable.

Second, define the three categories of latent coupling—functional redundancy, error-correction buffering, and normative signaling—that must be investigated before removal is permitted, with a brief diagnostic test for each.

Third, apply the complete procedure to three concrete cases:
(a) A proposal to eliminate the mandatory manual sanity-check log for AI-driven protein-folding predictions, arguing that automated validation has made it redundant.
(b) A proposal to abolish the double-blind peer-review layer for theoretical physics submissions, arguing that transparency improves accountability.
(c) A proposal to remove the legacy requirement that all clinical-trial data be deposited in a central archive within thirty days, arguing that private repositories are more efficient.

For each case, run every step of the procedure, show where it stops or proceeds, and identify the point—if any—where judgment supersedes the checklist. At least one case must yield an answer that a reform-minded board member would find unwelcome, and you must state clearly what the procedure can and cannot do in that instance.

Fourth, conclude with an explicit statement of the procedure's limits: the classes of institutional change for which it is not designed, the risks of false negatives and false positives, and the conditions under which the board should suspend the procedure and engage in direct philosophical deliberation rather than procedural evaluation.

Write in a formal, precise style appropriate for a scientific governance document, but do not cite specific philosophers beyond Burke and Chesterton, do not provide a bibliography, and do not ask for further reading. Do not quote any text verbatim; paraphrase all referenced ideas. Ensure that the procedure can be executed by a practitioner who has no access to online resources or specialized philosophical literature.
