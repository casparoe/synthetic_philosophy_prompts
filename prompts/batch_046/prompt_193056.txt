I am writing for a bioethics seminar, and I have become preoccupied with the long-standing stalemate between advocates of brain-death criteria and defenders of circulatory-death criteria. Both sides appear to accept the same clinical findings—persistent hypothalamic activity in some brain-dead patients, the reliability of apnoea testing, and the legal frameworks that recognise either standard—yet the literature shows almost no convergence over the past four decades. I am inclined to think that the disagreement is not empirical at all, but rather concerns whether 'death' ought to be defined as the irreversible loss of integrated organismal functioning or as the permanent disappearance of those mental and moral capacities that make life valuable. Is this diagnosis accurate, or does a hidden empirical dispute—perhaps about the neurological boundaries of consciousness or about the metabolic persistence of the organism—actually drive the division? Could it instead be a verbal disagreement, with clinicians using 'death' as an operational category and philosophers using it as an ontological one, so that each side answers a different question under a single term? I would like an honest diagnosis supported by what each camp actually argues, not a survey of positions. Furthermore, given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as genuine progress? Would a formal model of organismal integration resolve the matter, or is the impasse a sign that the concept of death is irreducibly plural and no single criterion should be expected? Please respond in British English, and avoid offering bibliographies or reading lists.
