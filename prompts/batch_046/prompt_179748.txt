Design a 75-minute workshop activity for sixteen graduate students in political philosophy or comparative politics, intended to make them feel the conceptual tension between Western transparency-based definitions of corruption and social systems in which obligation and reciprocity are constitutive of public office rather than deviations from it. Divide the group into two cohorts of eight: one cohort plays "bureaucrats" in a fictional ministry where career advancement depends on both formal examination scores and "sponsorship" from senior mentors who provide protection and discretionary resources in exchange for long-term loyalty; the other cohort plays "integrity auditors" trained exclusively in OECD-style metrics of impartiality, disclosure, and conflict-of-interest avoidance. Present both cohorts with an identical, concrete case: a senior official has used discretionary funds to cover a junior colleague's family medical emergency, and six months later expects that junior's support for a regional infrastructure bid that benefits the senior's kin network. Give each cohort hidden incentive structures—bureaucrats accumulate or lose "standing" within their role-network depending on whether they honour reciprocal claims, while auditors accumulate or lose institutional credibility depending on whether they approve non-transparent allocations—so that they must vote individually on whether to sanction the senior official before any joint discussion takes place. Arrange the rules so that both groups experience the very same act as either a moral failure or a failure to honour a legitimate obligation, producing a coordination breakdown that is not resolved by providing further factual information. Provide a timeline (ten minutes for role immersion, fifteen minutes for private deliberation, twenty minutes for the joint vote with hidden payoffs revealed, thirty minutes for structured debrief), materials (hidden role cards, standing-point ledgers, and a shared case dossier with identical wording for both groups), and a debrief that maps the moment of mutual incomprehension onto the conceptual dispute over whether corruption is best defined as private gain extracted from public office or as the instrumentalisation of relationships that ought to be governed by non-contractual reciprocity. State what will predictably go wrong on the first run—namely, that the auditors' vocabulary is institutionally privileged and will dominate the joint discussion unless speaking order is reversed—and explain how to handle it. Be explicit about which feature of the idea the simulation cannot reproduce: the historical specificity of the modern administrative state as a normative ideal rather than a descriptive universal, which the facilitator must supply verbally after the activity.
