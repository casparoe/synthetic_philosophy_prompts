A colleague is pressing what he calls the strict singleton hypothesis: exactly one concrete particular exists—call it U—and there are no other objects, no universals, and no relations as independent entities. He then offers the following argument, which he takes to show that the view eliminates the very possibility of distinct false beliefs, and therefore collapses into a kind of semantic solipsism. I think there is a hidden slide somewhere, but I cannot locate it.

(1) If the strict singleton hypothesis holds, then every singular term that refers denotes U.
(2) Any false belief about the world must either misattribute a property to U or presuppose the existence of something other than U.
(3) Consider two false beliefs: B1, that there are two cups on the table; and B2, that there are two tables on the table.
(4) In both cases, the belief is false because there are not two of the relevant kind.
(5) But since only U exists, "cup" and "table" cannot denote distinct non-U objects; at best they denote U under different descriptions, or they fail to denote entirely.
(6) If they fail to denote, then B1 and B2 contain empty terms. Under standard free logic, sentences with empty terms lack truth-values rather than being false.
(7) So for B1 and B2 to be genuinely false—not merely truth-valueless—the terms "cup" and "table" must have semantic values.
(8) Given (1), those values can only be U (perhaps via descriptive content that applies or fails to apply).
(9) Therefore both B1 and B2 reduce to the claim that there are two U's, or that U is simultaneously arranged in two incompatible cup-like and table-like ways.
(10) More precisely, the only falsehood available is the denial of uniqueness: "There is more than one U."
(11) Hence B1 and B2 are not distinct falsehoods; they are the same error expressed with different descriptive clothing.
(12) By generalization, every false belief about multiplicity collapses into this single falsehood.
(13) But this proves too much: it makes it impossible for one person to be wrong about cups and another wrong about tables in genuinely different ways. The content of error becomes uniform.
(14) He concludes that the strict singleton must reject standard semantics and treat all non-U vocabulary as strictly meaningless. But then he asks: if "cup" is meaningless, how could anyone ever form the false belief B1 in the first place?

Where does the argument go wrong? Is the slip in the move from (5) to (8), in the assumption that false beliefs require denoting terms, or in the inference in (11) that descriptive clothing cannot individuate content? If I have to choose, I would rather preserve the intuition that B1 and B2 are genuinely different errors than preserve the semantic uniformity in (11). But I want to know exactly what I am paying—does saving distinct false beliefs force me to abandon the strict singleton, or only to modify my semantics, and which premise is least costly to surrender?
