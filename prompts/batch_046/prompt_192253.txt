Compose a dialogue set during a workshop break, in which two philosophers of computation—Mara and Elias—debate the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis. Mara advances a modal-structuralist position: she argues that the thesis functions not as a falsifiable empirical claim about physical devices, but as a regulative conceptual boundary that defines effective calculability itself, so that the classic convergence of Turing machines, lambda calculus, and recursive functions reveals a fixed point in our conceptual framework rather than an inductive law of nature. On this view, any purported hypercomputer—whether analog, oracle-augmented, or exploiting exotic spacetime geometries—would not refute the thesis but would instead be reclassified as lying outside the domain of computation proper. Elias defends an empirical-inductive stance: he treats that same convergence as powerful abductive evidence for a contingent constraint on physically realizable processes, and he maintains that if a reproducible physical system consistently solved the halting problem for standard Turing machines, rational revision of the thesis would be preferable to redefining computation.

The exchange should be genuinely dialectical, with each speaker responding directly to the other's arguments rather than talking past them. Mara should press Elias on an epistemic circularity problem—namely, that any attempt to verify the thesis experimentally seems to rely on computational methods that already presuppose the very boundary being tested—while Elias should address whether that circularity is vicious or benign, perhaps comparing it to other scientific self-measurements. In turn, Elias should challenge Mara to account for the predictive success of the thesis in computer engineering if it is purely conceptual, and Mara should distinguish success within a framework from empirical confirmation of the framework itself. Neither character should be reduced to a strawman; each should acknowledge the opponent's strongest point and clearly state what their own position must sacrifice to maintain consistency. The dialogue should avoid bibliographic references, appeals to specific named texts or papers, and requests for citations, and should be written in polished American English.
