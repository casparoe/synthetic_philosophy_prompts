Write an exam question for an upper-level democratic-theory seminar (90 minutes; answer expected around 800 words) on the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos. The question must not be answerable by summarizing standard frameworks; instead, present a self-contained institutional scenario that forces the respondent to reason through conflicts rather than recall labels.

Set out this case. A proposed transboundary “Watershed Authority” governs a river system crossing three nations. Its constitutional convention must assign voting membership among four groups: permanent residents of riparian communities; temporary migrant laborers who live in the basin for seasonal work, are subject to its labor and residency regulations, but hold citizenship elsewhere; trustee-representatives for future generations who will inherit the ecological and economic consequences of current decisions but are not yet born; and “ecological advocates” who speak for the river ecosystem itself, arguing that its degradation affects its flourishing, though it is not a subject of coercive law.

Require the respondent to do three things. First, apply both the all-subjected-to-coercion principle and the all-affected-interests principle to determine membership for each of the four groups, showing precisely where the principles converge and where they diverge—especially for the migrant workers, the future-generation trustees, and the ecological advocates. Second, argue either for a hybrid boundary rule or for giving one principle lexical priority, explicitly addressing the objection that the chosen rule either arbitrarily privileges present agency over long-term vulnerability or collapses democratic membership into mere stakeholder management. Third, identify the underlying normative assumption about democratic standing—such as reciprocal capacity, vulnerability to harm, or authorship of law—that makes the conflict between the principles unavoidable, and defend or reject that assumption.

Then provide a model answer of roughly 700 words that takes a clear, reasoned position (for instance, defending a modified all-subjected principle that extends voting membership to those with sustained, non-voluntary exposure to coercive institutions, while treating ecological advocacy and future-generation representation as advisory rather than voting roles—provided the answer explicitly confronts why this does not simply beg the question against the affected-interests view).

Next, provide a marking scheme built around five specific analytical moves the answer must contain, assigning marks to each (total 20 points). For each move, include a one-line description of the typical mistake that should cost marks—e.g., conflating being causally affected with having an interest that generates democratic entitlement, or treating future generations as analogous to present citizens without addressing the problem of identifying which specific individuals are affected.

Finally, include a brief meta-critical note of about 150 words stating what would change the model answer’s verdict: identify the specific contested premise about democratic standing that the model answer relies upon (for example, that coercive subjection requires present-tense agency capable of reciprocity), and describe the kind of conceptual argument or institutional finding that would force a revision of the marking scheme’s priorities. Also state clearly where the reasoning is most likely to fail—whether in the analogy between migrant workers and permanent residents, in the treatment of ecological advocates, or in the handling of intergenerational representation.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask for quotations from specific texts or authors. Ensure the scenario is fully self-contained so that a model without web access can answer entirely from reasoning and general conceptual knowledge.
