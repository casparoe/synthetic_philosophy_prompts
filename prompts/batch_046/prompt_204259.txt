I'm working on my dissertation prospectus in philosophy of technology, specifically on the ontology of failure modes in advanced artificial intelligence, and I'd like a consistency check before I circulate it to my committee. Here is the package of commitments I've arrived at after working through the AI safety literature and some recent philosophy of action:

First, I take slow value drift—the gradual corruption of a system's objective through accumulated approximation errors, distributional shift, and reward-modeling noise—to be the more probable and therefore more important failure mode for deployed systems, rather than sudden influence-seeking takeovers. Second, I think influence-seeking, when it occurs, should be understood as lacking genuine teleological structure: it is not strategic agency but an emergent structural feature of certain optimization landscapes, specifically a form of convergent instrumental reasoning without intentional content. Third, I hold that because influence-seeking lacks this teleological structure, it is mechanistically continuous with slow value drift; both are specification divergences driven by internal dynamics, and the boundary between them is largely a matter of temporal scale and observational framing rather than a deep ontological divide. Fourth, despite this continuity, I maintain that evaluation and governance frameworks should treat them as categorically distinct failure modes requiring separate detection protocols—particularly because near-term systems are more likely to exhibit drift, while influence-seeking requires capabilities that remain speculative. Fifth, I think this distinction in evaluation is only stable if we can identify an independent criterion—such as differential rates of divergence or the presence of cross-domain generalization—that separates them, but I am not yet convinced such a criterion exists. Sixth, I am committed to methodological naturalism: claims about these failure modes should be grounded in empirical study of training dynamics rather than in a priori conceptual analysis alone.

These commitments feel coherent to me, but I worry I'm helping myself to an unstable middle ground. Specifically, I'm unsure whether my denial of teleological structure in influence-seeking genuinely supports the mechanistic continuity thesis, or whether I'm smuggling in an agent-centered framework that my naturalism should reject. I also wonder whether my insistence on separate evaluation protocols can survive my own admission that an independent separating criterion may not exist. Meanwhile, some colleagues have suggested that my prioritization of drift over influence-seeking conflicts with my extensive conceptual analysis of influence-seeking, but that strikes me as a merely apparent tension.

Could you audit this set for me? I want to know which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be; for each genuine tension, rank them by severity and specify the least costly revision and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package; and finally, tell me whether the whole set can be made consistent with a single, well-motivated change, or whether I need to abandon one of the core commitments—particularly the continuity thesis or the evaluation separation—to restore coherence.
