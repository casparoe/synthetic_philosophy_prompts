Student answer:
Epiphenomenalism treats conscious states like steam from a locomotive — they come from physical brain work but don't push back on the physical world. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is that if my feeling of pain doesn't cause my utterance "I'm in pain," then the utterance is just a physical event with no real link to the feeling. I could be systematically wrong about my own consciousness and never correct myself. Jackson raises this in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" when he argues that if qualia are left out of the causal story then we couldn't talk about them, yet we clearly do.

The mirror-judgment version makes the report reflexive: I'm not just reporting pain, I'm reporting that this state lacks causal efficacy with respect to the report itself. If the state is truly causally inert, then it can't be part of any causal chain leading to the utterance. So the common-cause reply — that one brain state produces both the feeling and the speech — fails here, because for the reply to work the feeling still needs to sit somewhere in the causal path. Once the report is about the state's own inertness, the brain is doing all the work and the feeling is just hanging there unused. That collapses the reply and shows epiphenomenalism is self-defeating: you can't consistently claim knowledge that your experiences are powerless.

So actually Jackson's argument proves interactionist dualism is correct — mental states must cause judgments about themselves, otherwise we wouldn't be able to discuss them at all.
