I am a doctoral student working on the philosophy of history and moral categorisation. I have been observing a long-running impasse in discussions of the Holocaust's uniqueness, particularly where they intersect with Zygmunt Bauman's thesis that modern bureaucratic rationality made the genocide possible rather than representing a breakdown of modernity. On one side, defenders of uniqueness treat comparison with other mass atrocities as either a category error or a moral diminishment; on the other, comparativists and sociologically-minded historians argue that refusing comparison prevents us from identifying the specifically modern, bureaucratic mechanisms Bauman highlighted, and that uniqueness claims risk mystifying the event. The debate has persisted in various forms since the Historikerstreit, yet both sides now appear to agree on the empirical record: one treats uniqueness as a precondition for adequate moral response, while the other treats it as an obstacle to structural explanation.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about historical facts or about the moral weight of the event, but a hidden divergence over what kind of explanation counts as adequate—whether an adequate account must preserve the singular moral gravity of the occurrence, or whether it must subsume the occurrence under general sociological mechanisms. I am unsure, however, whether the stall is instead verbal (centred on the word 'unique'), substantive but intractable because it rests on bedrock intuitions about moral particularity, or simply a case of moral philosophy and historical sociology using the same vocabulary for different projects.

Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? Specifically: is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work; are the sides answering different questions under a single heading; and does the impasse indicate that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is genuinely difficult? Given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a disciplinary reframing, or an argument that no further resolution should be expected?
