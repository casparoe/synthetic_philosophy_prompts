I volunteer as a crisis counselor for a remote text-based support service that operates under strict protocol rules. Two nights ago, I was chatting with an individual who stated they had ingested a dangerous quantity of medication and were alone in an apartment building, but they refused to provide their exact unit number or to consent to an emergency dispatch, explaining that previous encounters with first responders had caused them severe trauma. The service guidelines, which are designed to prevent unwanted police involvement and protect user autonomy, instruct counselors to continue dialogue and gather location information voluntarily; they explicitly prohibit initiating a welfare check based only on partial geographic clues, because false positives can cause serious harm and expose both the counselor and the organization to liability. After roughly thirty-five minutes of de-escalation, the individual finally shared enough detail for me to alert local emergency services; they survived, but had lost consciousness by the time responders arrived. The service director praised my adherence to protocol, noting that breaking it might have shortened the delay but would have violated the user's stated preferences, risked my removal from the platform, and undermined the trust that allows the service to assist many people over time. Nevertheless, I remain unsettled: the harm was immediate and visible to me, the cost of an unauthorized check seemed small compared with the risk of death, and the institutional reasoning feels like a rationalization for inaction. How should I evaluate whether my obligation in that moment was to the specific person before me—mediated only through a screen—or to the broader system of rescue that my compliance sustains? And was remaining within protocol the correct moral course?
