I am currently taking a module on philosophy of science and religion, and I have been trying to work out my own position on determinism, moral responsibility, and the interpretation of Einstein's writings. I have reached the following five commitments, but I am unsure whether they form a coherent package rather than a set of tensions I have not properly noticed.

(1) I accept a broadly Spinozist determinism of the kind often attributed to Einstein: I take free will to be an illusion produced by our ignorance of the underlying causal laws, and I believe that a Laplacian intelligence could in principle predict every human action. (2) I take Einstein's resistance to quantum indeterminacy—his well-known remark that God does not play dice—to reflect not mere epistemic caution but a substantive metaphysical commitment to an objectively real, causally closed universe. (3) Despite accepting determinism, I want to preserve moral responsibility, so I assume that some form of compatibilism must be correct, even though I have not settled on a specific account. (4) At the same time, I reject Frankfurt-style hierarchical models of autonomy, because I think they fail to block manipulation cases: an agent can possess fully aligned second-order volitions and still be the product of covert conditioning that undermines genuine authorship. (5) I also believe that Einstein's own ethical humanism—his pacifism and his appeals to individual moral courage—presupposes that people are able to choose otherwise in a robust, libertarian sense, which appears to sit uneasily with (1).

Do these commitments hang together? Specifically, does (3) rely on an account of autonomy that (4) has already undermined, and is the tension between (1) and (5) only apparent once we distinguish Einstein's personal ethics from his metaphysical determinism?
