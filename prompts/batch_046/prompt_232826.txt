I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of history, and I am trying to map the logical space of presentism without relying on the usual historiographical jargon. I want a decision-tree taxonomy—rooted in concrete epistemic commitments rather than in school names—that classifies positions on whether historians can escape the normative and conceptual standards of their own era. Please construct the tree so that each internal node is a diagnostic question a non-specialist could answer from first principles, and each leaf is a named or unnamed position with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment.

The root should ask whether the historian's present operates analogously to an unavoidable measurement frame (as observer-dependence does in physics) or analogously to a correctable instrumental error. From there, branch through three specific distinctions: (1) whether past social practices possess properties that are ontologically fixed prior to present interpretation; (2) whether diachronic calibration—the deliberate adjustment of one's present conceptual framework against the internal logic of past sources—is achievable to any non-zero degree; and (3) whether unavoidable present-dependence, if it exists, functions as an epistemic defect, a productive hermeneutic condition, or an ineliminable dual-status feature.

The tree must include leaves for strict presentism (all interpretation is present-projection); naive historical realism (the past is fully transparent if archives are complete); hermeneutic presentism (present frameworks are necessary and enriching); and calibrationism (partial insulation is possible but asymptotic, leaving a residual temporal parallax). Note any unoccupied logical combinations—such as full ontological realism coupled with a total denial of calibration possibility—and briefly diagnose whether the vacancy reflects incoherence, practical implausibility, or mere neglect.

Please flag any pair of leaves that your branching logic cannot genuinely separate, and phrase every node through concrete cases—for example, whether assessing a medieval guild's economic morality requires modern distributive-justice categories, or whether the guild's own normative vocabulary can be reconstructed with only negligible present contamination—rather than by asking whether one believes in presentism. Do not include bibliographies, named-author lists, or requests for citations.
