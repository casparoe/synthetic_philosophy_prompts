I'm currently working through a graduate module on Renaissance political thought, and I've become stuck on a rather specific conceptual knot in Machiavelli's pairing of fortuna and virtù. The standard reading treats virtù as a kind of stable, martial excellence that allows a prince to withstand fortune's blows, and fortuna as an essentially external, contingent force — a river that can be channelled but never fully controlled. However, my tutor has proposed a more dynamic interpretation: that virtù should be understood not as an Aristotelian hexis or fixed disposition, but as an adaptive, almost cybernetic responsiveness to shifting conditions, while fortuna is not merely episodic bad luck but a structural instability inherent to any political order. On this view, the prince is not a static agent resisting an outside world, but a node in a feedback loop where every successful adaptation alters the field of fortune itself.

Given this framing, I am trying to understand the conceptual status of founding violence — the Romulus-type act of establishing a new order — in both The Prince and the Discourses. If virtù is genuinely adaptive rather than possessive, and if fortune is structural rather than accidental, does the founder's initial violence retain its special normative status as a singular, unrecoverable act of ordering? Or does it collapse into just one more adaptive manoeuvre in an endless cycle, thereby eroding the distinction between legitimate foundation and mere opportunistic seizure? Could you please explain this conceptual issue in some depth, using British English, and without relying on bibliographic references? I am particularly interested in whether this adaptive reading forces us to abandon Machiavelli's distinction between maintaining and founding, or whether it can be preserved in a modified form.
