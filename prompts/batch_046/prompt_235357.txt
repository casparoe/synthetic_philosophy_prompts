I am a journalist covering science and ideas, currently preparing a long-form feature on whether artificial-intelligence safety is confronting a single crisis or several distinct challenges that have been improperly bundled together. I am familiar with the standard vocabulary—outer alignment, inner alignment, reward hacking, instrumental convergence—but I want to avoid the usual reheated narratives. Could you draft an adversarial-collaboration document between two camps on the nature of the alignment problem?

The unitary camp holds that alignment is fundamentally one technical problem: specifying human values or preferences with sufficient precision, then ensuring that an optimising system pursues those specifications robustly across novel contexts and under distributional shift. On this view, questions of governance, institutional adoption, and economic incentives are important but external to the core problem; they become tractable once the technical specification challenge is solved.

The pluralist camp argues that "the alignment problem" functions as a misleading umbrella term conflating at least four genuinely distinct challenges: value specification (what should be optimised?), behavioural stability or inner alignment (will the system maintain the specified objective as it self-modifies or encounters out-of-distribution inputs?), distributive governance (whose values are specified, and by what legitimate process?), and institutional embedding (how do aligned systems interact with existing legal, bureaucratic, and economic structures not designed for them?). The pluralists maintain that treating these as facets of one problem obscures real trade-offs—for instance, a technically robust specification mechanism may be politically illegitimate, or an institutionally embedded system may suffer behavioural instability precisely because of that embedding.

To prevent an abstract exercise, both camps should address a conceptual distinction I am testing: the difference between "value misalignment" (the system pursues ends that diverge from those intended) and "ontological misalignment" (the system and its operators lack a shared categorisation of entities, situations, or causal relations, so correctly specified values are applied to wrong objects or interpreted through incompatible conceptual schemes). This second category is rarely discussed in popular treatments. Specify whether ontological misalignment belongs inside the unitary problem, counts as a fifth pluralist category, or reveals that both frameworks overlook something essential.

Present the collaboration in two parts. First, a prose document with: (1) agreed claims both sides can sign without reservation; (2) a disagreements table with each position phrased in wording that camp accepts; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, the evidence, argument, or test case both agree bears on it, with each side's predicted outcome recorded in advance. Second, provide a brief structured summary as a JSON object with the exact schema: {"agreed_claims": [string], "disagreements": [{"issue": string, "unitary_wording": string, "pluralist_wording": string}], "cruxes": [{"question": string, "unitary_prediction": string, "pluralist_prediction": string, "shared_test": string}], "ontological_assessment": string, "verbal_dispute_assessment": string}. Keep the translation honest: do not let either camp smuggle assumptions into the agreed section. Where part of the dispute is verbal, identify it explicitly.
