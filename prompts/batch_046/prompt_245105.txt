After a long argument with a friend about whether death can genuinely be a harm, I have tried to write down exactly where I stand, and I want a consistency audit rather than a survey of the literature. Here is my package:

(1) I accept the Epicurean principle that death is not experientially bad: once I am dead there is no present subject to suffer deprivation, so the “no harm” argument succeeds for experiential welfare.
(2) Nevertheless, I hold that death is a real misfortune, because it permanently destroys the continuation of my projects, relationships, and the goods that constituted my well-being.
(3) I think the pre-natal/post-mortem asymmetry is only superficial: neither period is experientially bad, but death uniquely involves the truncation of an already-actualized life-narrative, which gives it a special misfortune-status that pre-birth absence lacks.
(4) I reject counterfactual deprivation accounts that compare me with a longer-lived counterpart in nearby possible worlds, on the grounds that personal identity is essentially tied to actual spatiotemporal history; such a counterpart would not be me.
(5) I also maintain that harm can be assigned retrospectively to a subject who no longer exists at the time of the harm, provided there is a continuous narrative thread linking the prior subject to the event—much as we judge historical injustices harmful to the long dead.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look tense, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity, and be specific about whether (4) undermines the misfortune claim in (2). Take a definite position: tell me exactly which commitment must go if I am to restore full consistency, or defend the coherence if you think none must go, but do not lay out options neutrally and leave me to pick. Give your reasons directly.
