I'm trying to work out whether there's a coherent moral principle behind my reactions to success and visibility across different cultures, particularly the tension between American-style celebration of upward mobility and the Nordic Jantelagen together with the Anglo tall-poppy syndrome. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases.

A tech founder who drops out of university, builds a company from nothing, and gives interviews describing his vision in grandiose terms: celebrating his achievement feels right, and I don't think he owes anyone false modesty, even if he is personally insufferable. A Norwegian biologist who makes a genuine breakthrough, then appears on television to explain its significance without constantly attributing everything to her colleagues: my immediate emotional response is that she is violating a humility norm and should have deflected praise, yet on reflection I think that response is unjust and she owes no one self-effacement. An Australian sports star who becomes the highest-paid player in his league and then buys an ostentatious waterfront property: the media and public tearing him down for "getting above himself" feels fair to me, but I struggle to explain why this is different from the founder case, since both are visibly enjoying the fruits of success.

A Swedish office worker promoted to senior management who now earns roughly twice her former peers; they gradually stop inviting her to social gatherings and joke about her "fancy" car: that social punishment feels clearly wrong, yet if she had bought the car specifically to signal status, part of me thinks she invited the resentment. Any society that quietly venerates inherited wealth while mocking people who rise from working-class origins and speak openly about it: this strikes me as hypocritical and morally worse than either pure merit-celebration or pure egalitarian modesty.

But the case I keep revisiting is a novelist who writes a memoir about escaping poverty, and in interviews emphasizes that she worked harder and made smarter choices than "people who just got lucky." Some part of me wants to cut her down for violating humility norms; another part thinks she is merely being honest in a culture that pretends structural luck dominates everything, and that punishing her for frankness is exactly the kind of envy-driven suppression Jantelagen describes. Is there a principle—perhaps about whether success is perceived as earned versus lucky, about whether visibility is instrumental versus expressive, about whether a culture already guarantees fair opportunity, or about whether the successful person is seen as competing with peers—that can capture most of these verdicts? Or must I drop some of them? In particular, does the principle that saves my reaction to the novelist also save my reaction to the cricketer, or do I have to concede that one of those gut feelings is just envy speaking?
