hey, im writing a feature on whether science carves nature at its joints or just sorts stuff for human convenience. trying to get my gut reactions straight before i interview anyone.

here are my calls. the periodic table—real carving, elements exist regardless of us. fermions vs bosons—same, deep physics. "invasive species"—definitely not, that's just human land-use talking. "planet"—i feel like this isn't a natural kind; pluto's reclassification was ontology theater, not discovery. but "star" feels genuinely joint-carving, even though we define stars partly by observable brightness. biological species are murky: wolves and dogs interbreed, but the split tracks evolutionary history, not just convention. mental disorder categories like depression in the DSM feel like symptom clusters for clinical utility, not discovered kinds like gold. now the hard pair: i think classifying whales as mammals was progress—getting closer to real evolutionary joints—but i also think the old "fish" category that included whales tracked a real ecological niche (aquatic, finned), and if ecology is part of nature, why wasn't that a joint? maybe because sharks and salmon are more different from each other than either is from whales?

the case i keep waffling on: viruses. biologists argue whether they're alive; some say they're just genetic material in a protein coat. if they're not alive, is "virus" still carving a real division in nature, or is it just a leftover bin for things that don't fit life?

what principle gets all this, or close? does joint-carving require supporting inductive inferences about unknown members? mind-independence? and be honest—which of my verdicts does it make me drop? probably the planet/star split, or the whale/fish tension. also tell me what your principle predicts for a case i haven't mentioned: do the clusters that an unsupervised neural net learns—say, groupings of images—count as carving nature, or just compressing human-labeled data?
