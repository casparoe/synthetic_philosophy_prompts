I'm noticing a recurring pattern in debates over term limits that I can't find a clean label for, and I'd like to know whether political science or institutional theory has already named it, or whether the phenomenon genuinely lacks an established term. The pattern unfolds like this: a legislature, council, or board is dominated by long-serving members who are criticized for institutional capture—deep ties to lobbyists, dependency on senior staff, and decision-making shaped by years of procedural habit. Reformers impose strict term limits, expecting that rotating in fresh representatives will break those patterns and restore responsiveness. But the newcomers arrive without institutional knowledge, without established staff relationships, and often without the procedural fluency to navigate complex budgets or regulatory frameworks on their own. As a result, they lean more heavily on permanent bureaucrats, career civil servants, long-tenured executive directors, and outside consultants—people who face no electoral accountability and no term limits, and who have been shaping policy continuously for decades. The elected careerists are replaced, yet the underlying institutional continuity does not weaken; instead, it seems to migrate downward into the administrative and advisory layer, where it is less visible and less accountable to voters. I have seen versions of this argued about state legislatures with strict rotation rules, about city councils with eight-year caps, and about nonprofit boards where rotating trustees depend on a permanent chief executive. Is there an established concept for this specific displacement—where restricting the tenure of elected officials doesn't diminish institutional entrenchment but shifts its effective locus to unelected permanent actors? If so, how precisely does it differ from ordinary bureaucratic capture, the revolving door, or principal-agent problems in delegation? If not, what would be the sharpest definition and the clearest boundary conditions—what would distinguish it from a simple failure of voter oversight?
