I'm writing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on what philosophers actually disagree about when they invoke Thomas Kuhn's incommensurability thesis. Rather than a survey of names, I need a diagnostic taxonomy — a branching tree — that takes a reader from concrete commitments to named positions, with each split labeled by the claim that separates it. The tree should begin with whether one accepts that paradigm shifts involve any failure of comparison at all. From there, branch by whether the incommensurability is local (affecting only a subset of theoretical terms) or global (encompassing entire conceptual schemes); whether it is primarily semantic, primarily normative (affecting standards of evaluation), or both; whether shared observational practices or predictive success provide a bridge independent of translation; and — this is the crucial, often-overlooked split — whether the absence of a common measure of success is a consequence of semantic incommensurability or a separate, independent failure. The leaves should include Kuhn's mature position, a Feyerabend-style anarchist reading, Davidson's rejection of conceptual schemes, Sankey's rational-comparison defense, and a pragmatist or instrumentalist stance that denies any deep metaphysical problem, plus any genuinely unoccupied combinations marked "unnamed." For every unnamed leaf, include one sentence diagnosing why it remains empty — whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Every node must be phrased as a question answerable without knowing which philosopher it points to, and you must flag any pair of named positions that this particular tree cannot actually distinguish. Keep the tree to no more than seven leaves, write in a formal, polished register suitable for an educated general audience, and avoid asking for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists: the point is logical mapping, not scholarly attribution.
