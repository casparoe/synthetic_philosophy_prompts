I'm a staff journalist at a science-and-ideas magazine that publishes two or three long-form pieces a week on neuroscience, psychology, and philosophy of science. I have about thirty minutes per study to decide whether it genuinely demonstrates mental causation—whether a belief, emotional appraisal, or high-level predictive model causes a physical change—or whether it merely correlates mental states with outcomes fully explained by physical mechanisms. I need a reusable, ordered decision procedure grounded in the causal exclusion argument and the contemporary debate over mental causation, operationalized for someone with no graduate training in philosophy.

The procedure should be a sequence of tests I apply to the methods and results sections in order, with explicit stopping rules: where it returns a clear decision not to cover the study as mental causation, where it requires me to flag the case for my editor because the distinction turns on values or unresolved philosophical disputes, and where it permits me to proceed with a pitch. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence I should seek in the paper, and where simplifying the philosophy risks misclassifying the study. Be explicit about which distinctions are being preserved—such as overdetermination, identity, and epiphenomenalism—and which are being collapsed for usability.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical studies. First, a forty-subject randomized trial reporting that cognitive reappraisal of stress lowers inflammatory cytokines, proposing prefrontal-amygdala connectivity as the pathway but not ruling out redundant peripheral mediators. Second, a clinical case series claiming that a stroke patient's explicit belief in recovery accelerates motor rehabilitation, with strong correlation but no proposed mechanism. Third, a study within a predictive-processing framework arguing that a subject's high-level generative model of bodily threat directly causes peripheral vasoconstriction, asserting downward causation without identifying any redundant neural mediator and treating the mental model as mechanistically irreducible.

For each case, report the procedure's output, including at least one case where the answer conflicts with my editorial instinct—likely that the study must be covered only as correlation despite its authors' framing. Finally, specify the procedure's hard limits: which categories of research it will systematically misread, which philosophical disagreements it cannot adjudicate, and the conditions under which I should abandon the checklist and consult an expert rather than sign off.
