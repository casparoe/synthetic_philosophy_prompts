As a journalist covering science and ideas, I was chatting with a colleague over coffee about risk and governance, and she summarized this concept she called the unilateralist's curse — she said it comes from a decision-theory paper about how groups fail to coordinate when anyone can act alone. Her version goes like this: in a group deciding whether to take a risky collective action, each person independently weighs the expected benefit to themselves against the cost spread across everyone; because the personal upside is large but the shared downside is diluted, every individual rationally says yes, so the action gets taken even though the group as a whole would have been better off if nobody did it. She also said it's basically the same thing as the prisoner's dilemma, except the defection option is replaced by going ahead unilateraly, and that this explains why climate geoengineering is inevitable — since any one country can deploy it, eventually one will, regardless of treaties. Then she added that the cure is just to make the action technically impossible for any single actor, which removes the curse entirely. Oh, and she threw in this extra bit: she claimed the curse only really bites when there's an excludability asymmetry — meaning the benefits are fully capturable by the actor but the harms are non-excludable — and that if you flip that asymmetry, say by making geoengineering benefits shared and costs localized, the logic reverses and you get a reverse unilateralist's curse where everyone waits and nothing happens.
