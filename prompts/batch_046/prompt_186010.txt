I'm trying to name a pattern I keep seeing in well-being philosophy and policy talk, but I dont know if it already has a term. There's this move where someone rejects that classic plug-into-a-simulation-for-perfect-fake-happiness scenario for themselves — they say they'd rather have real achievements and actual relationships, even if they feel worse — but then they support policies that basically build a distributed version of it. For instance, they might back urban 'happiness budgets' that replace public libraries with optimised relaxation zones because reported satisfaction is higher, or argue for subsidising immersive VR leisure instead of real-world skills programmes, on the grounds that at the population level welfare is just summed subjective experience so comfort-engineering is fine. They usually say this isnt hypocrisy: individual autonomy needs reality-contact, but collective design can maximise felt well-being. I've noticed it in some urban-planning literature and also in certain effective-altruism-style arguments about global mood enhancement, where the writer rejects hedonism personally but pushes for ambient well-being optimisation. Is there an established concept for this split? It feels near agent-relative versus agent-neutral reasoning, but that's too broad, and its not quite paternalism since nobody is forced into a machine — the environment just becomes one. If there's no specific name, can you coin one with clear boundaries: what counts, what doesn't, and how its seperate from plain hypocrisy or the standard simulation-objection? Please write in British English, using British spelling and phrasing. And dont just hand me a bare label — I want the carving, the edges, and which nearby ideas come closest.
