I'm trying to clarify my own position on whether fundamentality requires a unique, absolute bottom layer of reality. To that end, I want you to compare two conceptions of how grounding relates to fundamentality. The first is the hierarchical or "layer-cake" model: grounding is a strict partial order—irreflexive, transitive, and well-founded—so that all derivative entities ultimately depend on a single class of absolutely fundamental entities, and fundamentality is an intrinsic, non-relational property of that base. The second is an interdependent or "web" model: grounding is only locally directed, may be partial and mutual, and well-foundedness fails in at least some domains, so that fundamentality is relational and context-dependent rather than absolute; on this view, spatiotemporal structure or nomic relations might both ground and be grounded by the entities they organize.

Please compare these two models with specific attention to three issues. First, how does each treat the ontological status of spatiotemporal relations and structural properties—are they part of the fundamental base, or derivative, and can they play both roles? Second, what are the logical commitments of each view regarding transitivity, irreflexivity, and the possibility of circular or infinite descending chains of ground? Third, which model provides the more defensible account of fundamentality, and does the web model risk collapsing into a coherence-based ontology that loses the explanatory asymmetry grounding is meant to capture? Give your reasoning in detail, and do not treat either view as obviously correct—evaluate the trade-offs between explanatory power and ontological parsimony.
