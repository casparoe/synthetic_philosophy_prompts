I'm preparing questions for a long-form podcast interview aimed at rationalist-adjacent listeners. Next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher of formal epistemology who defends what she calls modular common priors: the view that Aumann's agreement result holds locally inside reasoning modules—say, empirical forecasting, normative evaluation, and strategic interaction—so agents can share a common prior within each module yet reach persistent, rational disagreement across modules. She applies this to explain deadlock among transparent experts, for instance climate modelers and macroeconomic forecasters, without attributing irrationality or hidden information to anyone.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the framework. I want at least one question each on the following three angles, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

(1) How the boundaries between modules are fixed without arbitrary stipulation—especially when a single belief, such as the reliability of a particular climate model, seems to sit in both an empirical and a strategic module. Anticipate that she will answer by appealing to functional role rather than content, and follow up by pressing whether that functional classification is itself prior-dependent.

(2) Whether her view collapses into standard hierarchical Bayesianism with hidden hyperpriors over module-selection, and if not, what formal difference remains. Anticipate that she will deny collapse by invoking non-commuting updates across modules, and follow up by asking whether non-commutation can be reproduced in a single global model with the right state-space structure.

(3) What empirical signature would distinguish her account from the simpler explanation that experts merely have different global priors shaped by different training data. Anticipate that she will cite systematic cross-domain prediction failures—cases where an expert updates correctly inside one module but fails to export that update to another—and follow up by asking whether those failures are observable from the outside or only detectable via first-person introspection.

Beyond those three, include further questions that press whether modular disagreement is genuinely rational or just a sophisticated form of compartmentalization; that ask what happens when agents become common-knowledge aware that they are operating in different modules; and that ask whether her framework ultimately undermines or rescues the normative force of Aumann's original result. Keep the tone precise and unscripted—no generic biographical questions, and no requests for bibliographies or reading lists.
