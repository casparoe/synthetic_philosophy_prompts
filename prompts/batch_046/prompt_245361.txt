You are a philosopher of mind with formal training in poetics and consciousness studies. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm: how can a villanelle’s alternating refrains and final coupled lines mirror the structure of the paradox of phenomenal judgment under epiphenomenalism—specifically, the way a claim about causal inertness and a claim about successful self-attribution keep returning unchanged, then are forced into proximity without synthesis?

Then write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by one quatrain, with two rhyming refrains (call them A1 and A2) that alternate as the final line of each tercet and then appear together in the closing couplet—spoken in the first person by a subject who fully accepts epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness.

The philosophical content must do structural work. Refrain A1 should express the causal inertness of phenomenal properties: that the experience plays no causal role in producing physical effects, especially not in producing the judgment about itself. Refrain A2 should express the successful phenomenal judgment: that the subject genuinely and correctly attributes the experience to themselves. Because both lines return exactly, the form should suggest that neither claim cancels the other; the final quatrain should place them side by side without resolution, leaving the tension open.

Inject the following somewhat niche framing: treat the judgment as causally orphaned. The physical brain produces the judgment through entirely sub-personal neural dynamics, with the phenomenal quality playing no part in that production; yet the intentional content of the judgment points precisely to that orphaned quality. The speaker should recognize themselves as correctly naming something that was never involved in the naming.

Requirements:
- Use concrete, precise imagery—neural signaling, the specific feel of a sensation, the act of reporting—rather than abstract dualist slogans.
- The poem must be accurate enough that a specialist in philosophy of mind recognizes the specific paradox—why phenomenal judgments seem to require causal interaction with phenomenal states, and why epiphenomenalism denies exactly that—rather than a generic meditation on mind-body problems.
- Do not cite authors, papers, texts, or dates; do not include footnotes, bibliographies, or suggestions for further reading. The poem must stand alone for a reader without web access.
- Do not resolve the paradox into either an illusionist shrug or a dualist triumph; end with the two claims coexisting in the same breath.
