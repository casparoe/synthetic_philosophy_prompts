I have been discussing with a colleague in my information-ethics seminar the analogies that circulate in debates over algorithmic profiling and data extraction. One that keeps recurring—both in popular writing and in some legal scholarship—is the comparison between a person's digital footprint and the contents of a private study or locked archive: the data are like books and papers arranged by an owner, and unauthorized scraping or inference is like breaking into that room, photographing the shelves, and copying files. The analogy is deployed to license a specific normative conclusion: that privacy violations are essentially trespasses against exclusive spatial control, and that consent should operate like a key—without it, extraction is a form of burglary.

As I work through a draft paper, I am trying to determine exactly where this mapping holds and where it collapses, particularly because the target domain includes phenomena that seem to have no counterpart in the source: data can be duplicated without being removed, combined across sources to generate inferred attributes that were never explicitly stored, and processed by distributed systems that never occupy or "enter" a physical space. There is also a deeper ontological question about whether the analogy smuggles in a particular view of value—treating privacy harm as damage to the integrity of parts under a single proprietor's gaze—when the actual harm might be relational, structural, or emergent in ways that a private archive cannot model. I need an audit that is precise about correspondences, not just a general dismissal.
