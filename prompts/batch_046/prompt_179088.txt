hey all, i've been messing around with this argument for my seminar on the varieties of free will worth wanting and now i'm genuinely stuck. a friend cooked it up and it feels like it proves way too much—maybe i miscopied a step, or maybe its just bad. here it is:

(1) The only kind of free will worth wanting is character-constitutive freedom: being the true author of the traits that actually drive your choices, not just having momentary leeway.

(2) If you're genuinely the author of trait T, then you must have formed T through a prior choice or at least a prior act of self-formation.

(3) But any prior choice that formed T had to flow from some already-existing traits—call them T1, T2, etc.

(4) So either there's an infinite regress of self-forming choices, or there's a first choice made with absolutely no prior traits behind it.

(5) A choice with zero prior traits is pure randomness; it can't be genuinely yours, just an accident.

(6) So nobody can ever be the author of their own character.

(7) But if you aren't the author of your character, then actions that flow from it aren't really yours in any robust sense—only in a thin causal one.

(8) Therefore none of the standard varieties—compatibilist, libertarian, or revisionist—actually deliver free will worth wanting.

this feels wrong, but i'd rather recieve (1) than (6). is the slip in (2), where "author" gets quietly equated with "chose"? or does (5) underestimate what a bare, trait-less choice could be—maybe it's not random but just radically self-creating? or is there a hidden modal slide between (6) and (7), assuming that un-authored traits can't still be "yours" in some other way? tell me which premise to reject and what that costs—especially if giving it up forces us to settle for a thinner compatibilist control that doesn't care about character at all.
