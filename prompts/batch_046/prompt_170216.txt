The issue is this predictive triage model we use — it sorts ICU candidates into reference classes by age, primary diagnosis and a couple of biomarkers, then gives a survival probability. Mrs. K is sixty-eight with severe heart failure, but she also carries a very rare genetic variant we'll call Q, which in the sparse literature seems to improve prognosis, except the only data are three case reports and one small retrospective study from 2018. The model puts her in the broad class 'HF-elderly-severe' with about 22% predicted survival, but if you include variant Q she falls into a much narrower class where the point estimate jumps to 61%, though the confidence interval is basically useless. Her family want us to treat the narrower class as decisive and therefore give her the last bed; the clinicians say thats just the reference class problem in practice — make the class narrow enough and anyone looks exceptional — and that using unstable subgroups destroys the consistency the algorithm is meant to provide. I need a memo for the panel recommending whether we approve an override for Mrs. K specifically, and whether we should adopt a general rule about what I'm calling 'reference-class turbulence' — that's when a patient sits at the intersection of several statistically valid classes with contradictory predictions, with no principled way to weight them — in future allocations. The memo needs a concrete recommendation with a time frame, and it should engage the family's argument seriously rather than just restating that the computer said no. I was chatting about this with my flatmate and she said it sounds like the liar paradox but with statistics, which I think misses the point, but if there's a quick way to explain why that comparison doesn't hold then feel free to include it — just don't let it take over the recommendation.
