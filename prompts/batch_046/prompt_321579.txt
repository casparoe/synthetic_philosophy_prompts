Write a proposal for a one-semester graduate seminar paper in philosophy of mind (about 6,000 words). The half-formed idea I want developed is this: defenders of higher-order theories of consciousness usually assume that the relation making a mental state conscious is a representational one—an explicit or implicit representation of that state—but in the cognitive-science literature on metacognition there is a robust distinction between metacognitive monitoring (explicit representation of one's mental states) and metacognitive control (regulatory adjustments to cognition that need not involve any explicit representation). I suspect that if we build a higher-order theory on control-relations rather than on monitoring-relations, we can avoid the classic regress objection and the problem of unconscious higher-order thoughts, but only by paying a price that has not been mapped: the theory may no longer be able to distinguish conscious states from expertly regulated unconscious ones, and it may collapse the distinction between phenomenal consciousness and functional self-regulation in ways that make the view empirically indistinguishable from some first-order or global-workspace alternatives.

Please structure the response in the following sections, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. The precise question (about 300 words): State the central research question exactly—can a higher-order theory of consciousness be reconstructed using metacognitive control rather than metacognitive monitoring as the constitutive relation, and does this variant avoid the standard objections without losing the explanatory advantages that motivate higher-order approaches in the first place? Specify the two or three sub-questions that must be answered for the main question to have a determinate answer, and say what conceptual work needs doing (e.g., clarifying "control" so it is not merely causal influence) before the argument can begin.

2. Why the existing positions leave this open (about 700 words): Explain how the standard actualist/dispositionalist debate within higher-order theory (e.g., Rosenthal-style actual-state views versus Lycan-style dispositional views) treats the higher-order component as uniformly representational. Show that neither side has seriously considered whether the relation in question could be non-representational yet still genuinely "higher-order" in the relevant sense. Also explain why first-order theories and global-workspace theories do not automatically settle the issue: they reject the hierarchy altogether, but they do not show whether a modified hierarchical view that replaces representation with control is viable or redundant.

3. The approach and method (about 1,000 words): Lay out the steps of the project in order. First, construct the control-based variant by defining the conditions under which a first-order state is regulated by a control mechanism that is (a) about that state in a non-accidental way and (b) operates without generating an explicit representation of it. Second, test this variant against three standard objections to higher-order theories: the regress problem, the problem of unconscious monitoring, and the "targetless" or misrepresentation problem (where a higher-order state exists but its target first-order state does not). For each objection, specify whether the control-based version dissolves it, relocates it, or inherits it in a new form. Third, compare the explanatory yield of this view against both standard higher-order theories and a representative first-order or global-workspace competitor on a specific phenomenon—e.g., the difference between subliminal perception and conscious perception of the same stimulus, or the phenomenon of "blindsight" with preserved metacognitive confidence. Say why a conceptual rather than empirical project can make progress here, and identify the exact point at which you would need to appeal to empirical findings and where you must stop.

Possible conclusions and decision conditions (about 800 words): Describe the three live outcomes—(i) the control-based view succeeds and offers a genuine third option; (ii) it succeeds in avoiding objections but only by collapsing into one of the existing views; (iii) it fails because control-relations cannot satisfy the conditions that make consciousness distinctive. For each outcome, list the specific consideration that would push you toward it (e.g., whether control can be genuinely "about" a state without representation; whether the view can account for the subjective "what-it's-like" character without smuggling in representational content). Be explicit about what you would have to concede in each case.
