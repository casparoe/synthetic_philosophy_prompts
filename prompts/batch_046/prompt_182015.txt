I spent thirty-five years in condensed-matter physics before turning, in retirement, to the philosophy of science, and I am now preparing a twenty-minute presentation for an interdisciplinary seminar titled Priority and Discovery: What Simultaneous Invention Reveals. My thesis is that celebrated cases such as Newton and Leibniz, or Darwin and Wallace, do not demonstrate that scientific discoveries are historically inevitable; rather, they exhibit what I call the underdetermination of formalism by phenomenon—that is, an empirical regularity can be captured by conceptually incompatible representational architectures whose differences are not erased by predictive equivalence but are instead path-dependent, permanently shaping the research programs that follow. I argue that Newton’s fluxional notation embedded an ontology of continuous motion particularly suited to celestial mechanics, whereas Leibniz’s differential calculus encoded a discrete, relational metaphysics that eventually made variational and field-theoretic methods more conceptually natural; analogously, Darwin’s analogy to artificial selection carried a gradualist, domestic-breeding framework that Wallace’s biogeographic determinism lacked, which is why Darwin alone could extend the theory to human cognition and sexual selection. I therefore propose replacing the standard question of temporal priority with a criterion of structural influence, distributing credit according to which formalization activated the subsequent theoretical lineage rather than according to who published first. The audience will be a philosophy-of-science reading group comprising two realist philosophers of mathematics who treat empirical equivalence as ontological identity, a historian of science committed to externalist explanations of the Darwin-Wallace convergence, and a mathematician specializing in the history of analysis. I need the objections ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased exactly as each of these interlocutors would raise them, together with notes on what I should concede without surrendering the core claim, what I must hold, and specifically the weakest point in my own argument—the charge that I am smuggling normative judgments about superior formalisms into a descriptive claim about historical path dependence—that I should defend actively rather than hope goes unnoticed. I also want to anticipate the objection I am secretly dreading: that Wallace’s explicit refusal to apply natural selection to human mental faculties proves he and Darwin never shared a single discovered idea, which, if correct, collapses my framing of simultaneous invention as one phenomenon with two architectures.
