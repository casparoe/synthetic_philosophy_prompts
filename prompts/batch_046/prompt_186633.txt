I'm putting together a grad seminar on self-knowledge and I keep running into the same wall—everyone wants to talk about whether introspection is 'realiable' in some global sense, and I think that framing just misses the point. There's this more specific, kind of weedy conceptual puzzle I want to lay out for the students: introspection seems to do okay when we're reporting the phenomenal quality of a state—like the sharpness of an anxiety—but it seems structurally unreliable when we're trying to report the causal genealogy of that same state, e.g. whether my belief that a colleague was being dishonest was actually driven by the evidence or by some subliminal affective cue I don't have access to. The puzzle isn't just that we confabulate reasons; it's why the failure is so selectively distributed.

Could you walk me through the conceptual issue? Specifically, I want an intuitive explanation of why unreliability might be built into the functional architecture of belief-formation rather than being a general epistemic defect of first-person access—maybe framing it through the idea that mental states aren't discrete inner objects we inspect, but nodes in a dynamic predicitive system where the 'inspection' mechanism is actually just another layer of post-hoc rationalization. Don't worry about citing the literature—my students can chase that down later. I just need the argument laid out clearly, with an eye toward whether this selective blindness actually undermines first-person authority or just relocates it.
