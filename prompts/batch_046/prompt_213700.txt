In our seminar on twentieth-century philosophy of value, we have been applying Moritz Schlick’s distinction between work and play to determine which activities confer meaning on life. Our professor has consistently ruled on the following cases. First, a concert pianist who practices exclusively to preserve her technical rank and dreads retirement: she judges that this activity lacks meaning. Second, the same pianist, after retirement, playing Chopin etudes at home with no audience or career aim: she judges this now possesses meaning. Third, a volunteer firefighter who risks injury solely because his community expects heroism and he fears social shame: she rules that his service is without meaning. Fourth, that same firefighter, after leaving the force, climbing remote peaks alone for the pure challenge: she rules that this has meaning. Fifth, a mathematician laboring on a famous conjecture mainly to win a Fields Medal and secure tenure: she says this work is meaningless. Sixth, the same mathematician, once tenured, spending evenings on an obscure puzzle that no colleague values, purely for intellectual delight: she says this now carries meaning.

Given these rulings, I am trying to anticipate what she would say about a new situation: an incarcerated woman is required to participate in a prison chess program; she initially resists, but over months she comes to genuinely love the strategic complexity and eagerly anticipates each match, though she remains physically unable to refuse participation.

I am less interested in the bare answer than in the underlying logic. What are the two or three most plausible rules that accommodate all six of her prior verdicts? For each rule, state what it predicts about the prison chess case. Then offer your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with the reasoning that makes it preferable, and say what she is likely to conclude about the inmate. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—different in structure from both the prison chess scenario and the six originals—that would sharply discriminate between the leading rules, explaining how each rule would treat it.
