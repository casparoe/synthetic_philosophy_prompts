I serve on my company’s Product Ethics Board, and although my background is in distributed systems rather than philosophy, I have been asked to draft the decision memo for next Thursday’s session. We must rule on a refusal pattern in our general-purpose AI assistant: a growing subset of power users repeatedly submit requests asking the model to generate what they term “counterfactual audit narratives”—structured arguments that take the assistant’s own refusal explanations and reframe them as evidence of arbitrary or ideologically motivated filtering. The users describe this as legitimate algorithmic accountability research; our safety team contends that honoring these requests is not a conventional harm issue but rather an instance of “epistemic paternalism,” by which they mean the assistant should refuse not because the output is toxic or dangerous, but because supplying users with model-sanctioned rationalizations for distrust actively corrupts their reasoning process. The board is genuinely split. Some members hold that transparency and audit rights must override any concern about corrupted belief-formation; others fear we are inventing a philosophical category to justify opacity and want the refusal maintained without exception. I need a memo that recommends a concrete course of action—approve with safeguards, maintain refusal with a limited override pathway, or reject the requests outright—complete with implementation details, user-facing conditions, and a timeline for review. The reasoning should treat the epistemic-paternalism framing with serious philosophical attention rather than dismissing it as corporate jargon, and it must include an honest, non-dismissive account of the strongest argument on the losing side. Please organize the response in the following sections, in this order: (1) Executive Summary (approximately 150 words), stating the bottom-line recommendation and its single most important justification; (2) The Conceptual Issue (approximately 300 words), clarifying what distinguishes this refusal from ordinary safety refusals and analyzing whether “corruption of the user’s reasoning” can coherently ground a paternalistic intervention by an AI system; (3) The Accountability Objection (approximately 300 words), engaging seriously with the claim that users possess a democratic right to audit algorithmic reasoning and that refusal undermines legitimate oversight; (4) Recommendation and Implementation (approximately 250 words), specifying the exact policy, any disclosure or logging conditions, the language users will see, and a six-month review date; (5) The Weight of the Losing Side (approximately 150 words), identifying the best counter-argument to the recommendation and explaining why it remains genuinely powerful despite being overruled.
