The reasoning was never purely about efficiency; it was meant to prevent treating any group's needs as fungible inputs into a collective calculus, preserving something like the inviolability of each assigned claim.

Here's the live case. We currently have twelve medical kits designated for Group A, twelve people facing a serious infection that is debilitating but, with standard care, not life-threatening. At the same time, Group B—two people—faces an acute, immediately lethal condition for which those exact kits are the only effective treatment available in our region. Our rule forbids us from taking the kits from A and giving them to B, because that would override A's assigned claim for the sake of saving more lives overall. Yet if we follow the rule, B dies, which seems like a far graver harm than the non-fatal suffering A endures. Critics in our collective argue that this reveals the rule to be incoherent: it protects individual claims so rigidly that it ends up permitting a much worse outcome—death—than a single, narrowly circumscribed exception would produce. Defenders reply that allowing even one exception dissolves the boundary between respecting people as ends and treating them as means, no matter how favorable the arithmetic.
