Please describe the most important implications of the documented divergence between WEIRD and non-WEIRD folk moral psychologies—specifically the finding that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic populations systematically overweight individualizing foundations such as harm and fairness while underweighting binding foundations such as loyalty, authority, and purity—for the tenability of moral objectivity, in light of a situation that has recently become personally relevant to me. I now regularly facilitate ethical deliberation in a multicultural professional environment where participants do not appear to disagree merely about the application of shared principles, but seem to be reasoning from genuinely incompatible moral grammars. I want to understand whether this psychological divergence should be taken as evidence that moral truths are culturally constructed and therefore not genuinely objective, or whether it remains compatible with a form of moral realism in which different populations possess differential cognitive calibration toward the same normative reality. In particular, address whether the observed modularity of moral judgment supports a pluralist realism, undermines universalism altogether, or instead points to a meta-ethical framework in which objectivity is preserved but moral knowledge is necessarily mediated by locally variable conceptual schemes. I am not asking for a simple choice between relativism and absolutism; rather, identify the specific philosophical commitments—concerning the nature of moral concepts, the epistemology of normative disagreement, and the conditions for rational moral persuasion—that are implicated if one takes the WEIRD/non-WEIRD divergence seriously rather than treating it as superficial noise around a single universal moral core.
