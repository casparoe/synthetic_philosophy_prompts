In a recent argument about why there is something rather than nothing, an interlocutor dismissed the possibility of an absolute “null world” by saying that “nothing comes from nothing.” He seemed to mean that concrete reality could not emerge from total absence, yet he immediately treated the existence of logical truths and mathematical structures as entirely unproblematic and in need of no explanation. I am trying to determine whether he was being consistent or merely relocating the mystery.

Please disambiguate that slogan into the precise theses it could be expressing in this debate. I want the menu to distinguish: (a) the kind of “nothing” in play—absence of concrete entities only, absence of spacetime, absence of every existent including abstract objects, or a state lacking all properties whatsoever; (b) the relation meant by “comes from”—efficient causation, metaphysical grounding, modal dependence, or conceptual entailment; and (c) the status claimed for the principle—empirical generalization, conceptual tautology, or a substantive axiom akin to the principle of sufficient reason.

For each distinct reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, or false; whether it actually undermines a null-world hypothesis; and whether it leaves logical or mathematical objects untouched or commits the speaker to explaining them too. Then say which reading he was most likely invoking, whether that reading supports his selective exemption for abstracta, and what residual problem remains once that exemption is granted.
