I've been chewing on this pattern in political ethics for a bit and I'm not sure if it's a named thing or just my own catgorization error. You set up an institution where there's a specific office — intelligence, corrections, some kind of 'fixer' — whose job is to do the morally sketchy stuff everyone else needs but wants off their books. Classic dirty hands setup, right?

But then there's this weird second-order effect I keep seeing: once that dirty role exists, the clean roles stop building in safeguards against the sketchy act, because they know there's a designated sink for the moral cost. Over time the clean actors start ordering or enabling the thing without feeling like they're getting dirt on themselves, since technically it's not them pulling the lever. It's not quite Walzer's dirty hands problem, because the leader here isn't personally wrestling with the choice — they're outsourcing the wrestling. And it's not just bureacratic diffusion of responsibility either, because the dirty office was deliberatly designed, not accidentally fragmented.

The thing I'm after is the institutional feedbac loop: the presence of the moral sinkhole actually lowers the barriers for everyone else, letting them stay pure by comparison. Does this have a name? Maybe something in the literature on moral division of labor, or institutional corruption? If there's an established concept that captures exactly this loop — where dividing dirty hands doesn't contain the dirt but redistributes the purity — I'd love to know. If not, how should we draw the boundaries: when does it stay within legitimate delegation, and when does it flip into this corrosive dynamic?
