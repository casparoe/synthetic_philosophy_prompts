Be precise: recognition requires that the other be both free and equal in status (call it the status-equality condition), not merely that they approve of you; and the object worked upon must be shown to mediate the slave's self-relation, not just serve as a tool. Here is one student answer (around 400 words):

The master defeats the slave in the life-and-death struggle and forces submission. He then receives recognition from the slave, but this fails because recognition only works under the status-equality condition: the other must be free and equal, not just approving. Since the slave is unfree, he's like a broken mirror—he can't confirm the master's freedom. Meanwhile the master never works; he only consumes, so he never sees himself in any lasting object.

The slave, by contrast, experienced the absolute fear of death, which dissolved his attachment to particular existence and revealed pure being-for-self. Then he works on objects and shapes them for the master. Because the master immediately consumes and destroys these products, the slave realizes his labor is ultimately meaningless, though the discipline still teaches him to delay gratification. By seeing his own skill endure in the object—well, in the habit he acquires—he achieves self-certainty without the master. Eventually he overturns the relationship and becomes the true master of himself, reversing the original domination. This is why labor is the path to freedom, and Hegel applies the same logic in his later writings on the state and citizenship.
