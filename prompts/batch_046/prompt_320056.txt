I'm a phd student in historical anthropology and I'm trying to nail down what I actually think about big-history narratives, going by gut reactions. Jared Diamond's continental-axis argument in Guns, Germs, and Steel feels like a real explaination, not just correlation with a story slapped on top. Harari's cognitive-revolution idea — that we started gossiping about strangers and that made large-scale cooperation possible — strikes me as legit. But when he uses the exact same framework to call capitalism a shared fiction about credit, it suddenly feels like a just-so story, even though the mechanism hasn't changed. Graeber and Wengrow arguing that Çatalhöyük was seasonal and mostly egalitarian, and using modern forager analougies to fill gaps in the archeological record, seems responsible to me. Then there's this preprint I came across proposing ritual debt cycles as the hidden engine behind every transition from foraging to farming to states; the author says there's no archeological trace because rituals don't leave carbon footprints, so absence of evidence is expected. That one feels wrong, but I can't tell if I'm just being inconsistent, since Graeber also uses absence of evidence sometimes. I also think comparing human history to evolutionary biology's major transitions is a useful heuristic, but saying societies literally undergo natural selection is a category mistake. Meanwhile, a historian argued the Industrial Revolution was really about an epistemic recategorization of nature in the 17th century, and backed it with three examples from natural philosophy — that feels way too thin for such a huge claim. Is there one principle behind all these verdicts, or am I just inconsistently applying some standard about mechanisms versus correlation? And whatever the principle is, what does it say about the case I keep flip-flopping on — whether Harari's capitalism-as-fiction claim is actually fine and I'm being unfair to it?
