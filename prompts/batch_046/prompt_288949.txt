I'm a high school student writing an independent paper in philosophy of social science, and I am trying to determine whether the claim that the world is becoming less violent expresses an objective fact or depends entirely on how one defines and measures violence. Here are my intuitive verdicts on specific cases. First, measuring only intentional interpersonal homicide per 100,000 people shows a clear long-term decline, and I take this as strong evidence that violence is genuinely decreasing. Second, measuring absolute annual war-related deaths without per-capita adjustment makes the claim false, since larger populations yield higher raw casualty counts even when individual risk is lower; I think this metric is misleading, yet I am not fully convinced it should be discarded entirely. Third, including structural violence—preventable deaths caused by economic inequality and institutional neglect—makes the historical trend ambiguous, but I hesitate to classify these as violence rather than as misfortune. Fourth, if one counts harm to farmed animals scaled per sentient-capita, violence appears to be rising sharply, though this feels like a category error unless the concept of violence is radically expanded. Fifth, counting state-sanctioned executions and lethal policing as violence produces different results depending on whether one normalizes by total population or reports raw incident counts. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving directness, intentionality, or a normative baseline—that determines which metrics are legitimate and whether there is a fact of the matter about global violence independent of metric choice? If no single principle can accommodate all five verdicts, which one should I abandon? Please also apply your principle to a case I have not listed: should cyberattacks that cause physical infrastructure failure count as violence, and should they be normalized by global internet-user population or by total world population?
