As a novelist, I am struck by how fiction makes social performance visible, and I would like a philosophical reading of The Great Gatsby through the lens of costly signaling and the handicap principle, pushed well past the obvious observation that Gatsby's parties are expensive displays. In particular, I am interested in whether Fitzgerald dramatizes what evolutionary biologists have called counter-signaling: Tom Buchanan's old money requires no spectacle, whereas Gatsby's new wealth must perform itself at ruinous cost, and whether the novel treats this asymmetry as a tragic evolutionary miscalculation or as a romantic authenticity that transcends fitness logic altogether. Does the green light operate as an honest signal of unattainable desire, or does Daisy's failure to read it expose the limits of handicap logic when applied to emotional rather than physical traits? Take both the novel's major scenes—the party descriptions, the shirts, the car—and the theory seriously, do not reduce either to an illustration of the other, and tell me where the work resists the reading and where it offers something about the cost of being seen that the scientific papers miss.
