I'm a science journalist who covers theoretical physics and its philosophical implications, and I've been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop hosted by a research institute. The audience is deliberately mixed: cosmologists and physicists working on temporal asymmetry, philosophers of science specializing in the metaphysics of time, historians of twentieth-century physics, and curators from a science museum that is co-sponsoring the event. The workshop is titled "The Geometry of Now," and its stated purpose is not to settle whether the block universe is true, but to examine whether the Rietdijk-Putnam argument—that special relativity's denial of absolute simultaneity entails that past, present, and future events exist with equal ontological weight—actually succeeds, and whether scientific practice can be conducted without presupposing either eternalism or presentism.

The organizers have asked me to structure the remarks around a specific constraint: I must welcome the participants and frame the workshop's stakes without using language that smuggles in either temporal flow or tenseless eternity as an unexamined premise. That means no references to "moving through time," to "the future being open," or to "all moments being equally real" as established fact. Instead, the philosophical idea should do real structural work: I need to explain why the denial of absolute simultaneity makes the ordinary vocabulary of planning, remembering, and predicting philosophically loaded, and then ask whether that vocabulary can be reconstructed in a way that survives the ontology it seems to require. The speech should be substantive enough that specialists recognize the argument's technical force—particularly its reliance on the relativity of simultaneity across inertial frames—yet accessible enough that the curators and historians are not alienated. I want to end not with a conclusion but with a framing question: if Rietdijk and Putnam are correct that there is no objective present, what becomes of the distinction between explanation and prediction in cosmology, and can that distinction be maintained without appealing to a privileged now?
