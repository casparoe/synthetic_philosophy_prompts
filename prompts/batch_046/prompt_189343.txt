Here is a consistency check I need before finalizing a course module on Marx and technological change. I have settled on the following package of commitments, and I want to know whether they form a stable position or whether I am quietly borrowing premises that cancel each other out.

(1) Marx's analysis in the Grundrisse fragment on machinery correctly identifies that progressive automation tends to reduce direct labor-time to a negligible factor in production, thereby undermining the capitalist form of valorization.

(2) The labor theory of value is false as a literal account of price formation and distribution, though it may retain normative force as a claim about the social origin of surplus.

(3) The tendency of the rate of profit to fall is a real structural dynamic, but its explanation lies in rising organic composition and competitive innovation pressure rather than in any labor-theoretic law of value.

(4) One can coherently reconstruct Marx's concept of surplus extraction for the contemporary digital economy—platform rents, data extraction, algorithmic management, attention monetization—without relying on a labor-time metric of value.

(5) Historical materialism is best treated as a methodological heuristic for tracing how technological forces reshape social relations, not as a deterministic teleology; its failure to generate accurate predictions about proletarian revolutionary consciousness is therefore not a decisive methodological defeat.

(6) If automation were to eliminate necessary labor entirely, capitalism would not necessarily enter a terminal crisis; it would more likely mutate into a rentier-technocratic regime sustained by artificial scarcity, intellectual property monopolies, and direct extraction from consumption rather than production.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension—not merely surprising together, but such that the usual motivation for one undermines the usual motivation for another? Which apparent tensions dissolve once the commitments are read precisely? For each genuine tension, rank its severity and specify the least costly revision: which single commitment should be modified or abandoned, and what the modified version would say. In particular, I am concerned that (1) and (6) pull in opposite directions about the fate of valorization, that (2) and (4) may be trading on an ambiguous notion of "surplus," and that (3) together with (1) may commit me to an explanation of capitalist crisis that I have explicitly disavowed. I also want you to identify whether there is an unstated premise I need to add—perhaps about the distinction between valorization and accumulation—to make (1) and (6) compatible without gutting either. Do not treat all conflicts as equally serious; order them by severity and say exactly what has to give in each case.
