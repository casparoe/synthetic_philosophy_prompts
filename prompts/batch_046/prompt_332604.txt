I'm designing the final exam for an upper-level seminar on autonomy, persuasion, and influence, and I need one essay question that students cannot answer by reciting familiar summaries of standard accounts—such as those found in the nudging literature or in well-known discussions of manipulation by authors like Goodin or Raz. Instead, the question should force them to work with a specific conceptual distinction that I'll embed in the prompt itself: the difference between influence that distorts an agent's reasoning environment (strategically altering what considerations appear salient or credible without the agent's awareness) and influence that merely enriches that environment (adding genuinely relevant considerations without concealment or selective framing).

The prompt should present a concrete case for analysis: a municipal public-health campaign that distributes personalized narrative videos in which local residents describe their recovery from a preventable illness. The videos are emotionally compelling and deliberately highlight severe outcomes, yet every statistic mentioned is accurate and no false medical claims are made. Crucially, the campaign does not disclose that the narratives were curated specifically to maximize emotional impact rather than to represent base-rate risk. Students must determine whether this counts as manipulation or as legitimate persuasion, defending their answer by applying the reasoning-environment distinction and by confronting a specific objection—namely, that if legitimate persuasion requires what we might call counterfactual transparency (the influenced agent's ability, upon learning about the influence mechanism, to still endorse the reasoning path taken), then nearly all targeted persuasive communication collapses into manipulation, since most audiences would reject being strategically selected for emotional priming.

Please write the exam question (calibrated for about 90 minutes, with an expected answer of roughly 600 to 700 words), followed by a model answer that demonstrates the precise moves required, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks. The marking scheme should identify five must-hit points, assign specific marks to each, and include a brief one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong on that point. The scheme should clearly separate an A-level answer—which must take a nuanced, qualified position rather than a flat yes/no verdict—from a B-level answer, which applies the distinction mechanically but fails to address the self-undermining problem posed by the transparency condition. Write in American English, do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid framing the assessment around obscure named theories that a well-prepared seminar student would not recognize.
