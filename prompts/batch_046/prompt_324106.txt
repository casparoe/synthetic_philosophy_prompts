I'm preparing a teaching session for a small group of technically-minded readers — the sort who have followed debates about AI consciousness and animal cognition but have no formal training in philosophy of mind — on the idea that sentience and moral patienthood come in degrees rather than being on-or-off properties, using the spectrum from insects to thermostats as the backbone. I don't want the explanation itself; I want the pedagogical architecture. First, lay out the dependency order: which distinctions must be in place before the gradient idea can even be motivated (for instance, separating functional feedback from experiential integration), and which must come only after the audience has already accepted that there is no sharp line. Second, propose the single carrying comparison that should run through the session — I'm weighing the contrast between a social-insect colony's collective decision loop and a thermostat's negative-feedback cycle, since both pursue homeostatic targets yet differ in something that might bear on moral status — and tell me whether that comparison illuminates the gradient or collapses into a false analogy, and if so, what should replace it. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably import: the binary "either conscious or inanimate object" framing, the neuron-count heuristic, the thermostat fallacy that any error-correction implies an interest, and the tendency to map the problem onto current large-language-model architectures. For each, specify the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the conceptual distinction that neutralizes it. Fourth, design the check for understanding: one task or question at the end that demonstrates they have internalized the gradation rather than just memorized the endpoints. Finally, state what should be deliberately omitted at this level — including any appeal to specific formal metrics of consciousness — and say so explicitly.
