I'm preparing a long-form feature for a magazine that covers moral philosophy and its intersection with ordinary life, and I keep returning to a tension in my reporting: the suspicion that consequentialism fails not merely because it asks too much of us, but because it seems to erase the very idea of being "off duty" morally. Please write an essay that takes up the demandingness objections to consequentialism through this specific framing. Rather than treating demandingness only as a quantitative problem—that the required sacrifices exceed some reasonable threshold—I want you to examine whether consequentialism suffers from a structural defect: it appears to abolish any protected space of "moral rest," turning every hour of life into an occasion for optimization and making any non-maximizing activity a kind of dereliction. To do this, distinguish carefully between "threshold demandingness" (the claim that the costs of compliance exceed what can be fairly expected) and "structural demandingness" (the claim that the theory cannot coherently recognize a boundary between moral duty and personal life, so that there is no legitimate category of being "morally at rest"). In weighing the objection, consider whether scalar adjustments, agent-centered prerogatives, or other modifications can preserve structural moral rest—say, allowing a parent to spend an evening with family rather than donating equivalent resources—without collapsing consequentialism into a non-consequentialist framework that fully privileges personal projects. Reach and defend a definite verdict: either consequentialism can be reformulated to accommodate structural moral rest while retaining its distinctive maximizing core, or the demandingness objection reveals a fatal flaw in the theory's conception of the moral agent. Do not end on a non-committal note; justify your conclusion with conceptual analysis and concrete illustrations drawn from everyday decision-making. Write for an educated general audience: avoid bibliographies, appeals to obscure literature, or citations; ground your argument in reasoning and clear examples, such as whether the family evening counts as permissible within the theory or only as a defeasible excuse that still marks the agent as falling short.
