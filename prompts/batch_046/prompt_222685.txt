I am preparing a paper for a course on comparative philosophy of mind, and I have been trying to work through a specific tension that my colleagues and I keep circling back to. On one side, the Theravada Abhidhammic account of anatta denies any enduring substrate—personal identity is nothing over and above a rapidly arising and passing stream of cittas (mind-moments), each causally conditioned but none belonging to a self. On the other side, contemporary analytic philosophy often treats personal identity as a matter of psychological continuity, where what matters for survival is not an underlying substance but the right kind of relational connections between mental states.

To push the comparison further, I want to explore a hypothetical technological duplication: suppose a complete mental profile is instantiated simultaneously in two parallel processing substrates, each maintaining perfect psychological continuity with the original stream and with each other. For the reductionist, this seems to create a branching problem—if identity is one-one, we face indeterminacy; if it allows branching, we seem to abandon the intuition that I am a single person. For the Buddhist, however, there is no substrate to be duplicated, only causal streams that are already conventionally designated. I am unsure whether the Buddhist framework actually dissolves the puzzle or merely relocates it into questions about karmic continuity and moral responsibility across divided streams.

Could you write a formal dialogue between two interlocutors—one a scholar of classical Theravada Abhidhamma and one an analytic philosopher defending a psychological-continuity reductionism—who debate exactly this duplication scenario? Both should be presented as fully competent: the Buddhist should not be reduced to a mystical denial of experience, and the reductionist should not be caricatured as naive materialism. They should engage in genuine back-and-forth, with each responding directly to the other's arguments about whether anatta offers a more elegant dissolution of the branching problem, or whether reductionism can appropriate the Buddhist insights without surrendering its normative commitments to moral agency. Please avoid bibliographic lists, citations, or requests for further reading; I am interested in the conceptual architecture itself. The tone should remain rigorous and philosophical throughout.
