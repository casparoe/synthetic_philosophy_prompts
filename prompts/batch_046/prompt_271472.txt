I serve on our university’s Committee on Institutional Research Funding in Mental Health, and by next Tuesday we must issue a formal recommendation on whether to approve a pilot grant for a campus counseling protocol explicitly built on tranquilist premises. The proposal—titled Absence-Only Therapeutic Design—treats the complete elimination of anticipatory craving and psychological suffering as the sole criterion of therapeutic success, and it deliberately excludes all measures of positive affect, life satisfaction, or what the investigators term eudaimonic flourishing. Their argument is that mental-health interventions should not impose a contested conception of the good life; if suffering and craving are absent, the therapeutic mandate is fulfilled, regardless of whether patients report joy, purpose, or engagement. This past weekend I had a long, unresolved argument with a close friend—who also sits on the committee—about exactly this philosophical point. She contends that funding the protocol would institutionalize a form of nihilism: a mental-health service that aims only at emptiness is indistinguishable from managed sedation, and it betrays our institutional obligation to help students live well, not merely suffer less. The rest of the committee is divided along similar lines. I am asking you to draft the two-page written opinion I will circulate before Tuesday’s vote. It should recommend a concrete course—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—including a specific framework for evaluation metrics and a time-bound pilot structure if you choose conditional approval. The reasoning must engage the tranquilist position on its own terms: explain why measuring success solely by the absence of negative states might be conceptually coherent, methodologically cleaner, and respectful of pluralism about the good life. At the same time, it must give full and honest weight to the strongest opposing consideration, namely that an institution charged with student welfare has a positive duty to promote flourishing, and that an absence-only metric risks legitimizing interventions that leave patients in a state of affective flatness. The memo should be written as a formal institutional document with an actionable bottom line, not personal advice, and it should avoid relying on citations to specific philosophical texts or on bibliographic recommendations.
