I'm working through a problem for a seminar on the politics of moral classification, and I've constructed an argument about the genocide label that seems to collapse in on itself, but I cannot locate the weak joint. The argument concerns Raphael Lemkin's original formulation of genocide—encompassing the deliberate destruction of a group's cultural and social existence, not only its physical annihilation—and the narrower definition adopted in the 1948 Convention, which restricts the crime to acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group in whole or in part through killing, serious bodily harm, or conditions calculated to bring about physical destruction. Many commentators observe that this narrowing served the interests of colonial powers seeking to avoid accusations of cultural destruction; accepting this historical diagnosis, the argument proceeds as follows.

(1) If the genocide concept is to retain its distinctive moral and legal authority—triggering obligations of prevention, intervention, and unique stigma—it must mark a boundary that is not merely quantitative (more deaths) but qualitative: the targeted destruction of a group as such.

(2) The 1948 Convention's exclusion of cultural and political destruction was not conceptually required but politically motivated; therefore, fidelity to the concept's moral purpose demands reverting to something closer to Lemkin's broader formulation.

(3) Under that broader formulation, any state policy deliberately designed to eradicate a group's language, religious practice, or intergenerational transmission—without necessarily killing members—counts as genocide, provided the intent is the group's dissolution.

(4) Historical assimilation programs, such as the Norwegianization policies directed at the Sami people in the twentieth century—compulsory boarding schools, suppression of language in public institutions, and forced relocation—were explicitly designed to dissolve Sami collective identity and replace it with dominant-national culture.

(5) Therefore, under the morally correct broad definition, these assimilation policies constitute genocide.

(6) But if liberal democratic states can routinely commit genocide through ordinary state-building and educational policy, then the label no longer distinguishes exceptional evil from standard governance; its practical moral authority—its capacity to mobilize unique international response—is eroded.

(7) Conversely, if one insists that such policies do not qualify, one must introduce an arbitrary threshold—say, that physical killing must be present, or that intent must be explicit in a particular bureaucratic form—that reproduces exactly the politically motivated narrowing in (2).

(8) Hence, either the genocide label is overinclusive and loses its distinctive force, or it relies on an arbitrary boundary that betrays its moral rationale.

My intuition is that the argument slips somewhere between (3) and (6), perhaps by treating destruction of a group as such as if it could be satisfied by cultural assimilation alone, or perhaps by assuming that a concept's practical utility depends on its rarity rather than its precision. But I want to know which premise is least costly to reject, what rejecting it requires me to concede about either Lemkin's legacy or about the moral work the concept is supposed to do, and whether there is a coherent middle position—perhaps one that distinguishes destructive assimilation from genocide without collapsing back into the 1948 Convention's physicalist bias—that the argument has simply ignored. I would especially like you to apply the diagnosis to the Norwegianization case: does the argument genuinely show that labeling such policies genocide undermines the category, or does it only show that democratic states have committed acts that should be named with equal gravity?
