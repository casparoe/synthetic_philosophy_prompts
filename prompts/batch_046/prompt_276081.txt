I'm putting together a feature on philosophy education for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I'm hoping you can help me sketch out a classroom game that actually makes people feel a philosophical tension rather than just nodding along. The setting is a community workshop — about fourteen people, ninety minutes, no prior philosophy required — and I want them to experience the pull between absurdism and those newer 'objective list' accounts of what makes life meaningful. I was chatting with my editer about this, and she reckons it's a bit too in the weeds for a general reader, but I think that's the point: I don't want something they've seen in a hundred pop-philosophy videos.

Specifically, I'm playing with this concept I've been calling 'rebound commitment' — the idea that once you genuinely absorb that there's no cosmic scorekeeper, your investment in local projects doesn't flatline; it actually spikes for a while, but then risks collapsing into pure irony or performance if you push it too hard. Could you design a structured simulation where small groups act as 'life boards' for fictional characters, using value-cards (creativity, care, achievement, relationships) to build a meaningful life? Every twelve minutes they get hit with an 'absurdity event' — concrete underminers like 'this person will be forgotten in three generations' or 'the heat death of the universe makes all labour temporary' — and they have to revise the plan without walking away from it. I want a token economy that tracks both 'commitment points' and 'distance points,' so groups can see the rebound spike and then feel the moment it tips into parody.

I need rules, roles, materials, a timeline with debrief, and an honest note on what predictably goes wrong — I imagine some groups will just lean into irony immediately and refuse to play it straight. The debrief should map the moments onto the tension between absurdist acceptance and objective-list fulfilment, and say exactly which part of that philosophical clash the game can't reproduce without the facilitator stepping in. No reading lists, please — I'm writing for people who won't be searching for papers afterwards.
