Write a dialogue between a defender of the School of Names (Mingjia) position on the White Horse paradox and a critic who regards the paradox as a conceptual confusion rather than a deep ontological insight. The defender should articulate the classic argument—that "horse" picks out the form or shape, "white" picks out the color, and since "white horse" involves both form and color while "horse" involves form alone, the two expressions do not designate the same thing—without reducing the point to mere equivocation or wordplay. The critic should resist by proposing that kind-terms function as determinables that remain present under determination, so that "horse" is not displaced but specified when we form "white horse," and should press whether the School of Names illicitly treats a qualified instance as a competing ontological entry rather than as a determination of the same kind. Have them engage in genuine back-and-forth: the defender must address the determinable/determinate reading directly rather than ignoring it, and the critic must explain why separating form from color in analysis does not establish non-identity if specification is not exclusion. Require that the exchange conclude with both parties acknowledging one unresolved tension—perhaps whether language carves nature at the joints or merely organizes perceptual access to it—rather than declaring a clear victor. Keep the dialogue philosophically rigorous, avoid all bibliographic references or requests for citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from classical texts; paraphrase the arguments in your own formulation.
