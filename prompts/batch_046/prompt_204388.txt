I'm preparing a handout for a philosophy of probability seminar on deference to objective chance. Please construct a concise decision-tree taxonomy that sorts respondents into positions on the Principal Principle, framed entirely as a sequence of diagnostic questions about concrete cases rather than as a list of philosopher-names.

The tree should begin from the shared commitment that physical processes have objective chances (for example, a fair coin has a chance of 1/2 of landing heads). Each branch must split on a case or claim that genuinely divides the logical space. Include at least these three branching scenarios:

1. The deterministic-substrate case: you know the macro-process is chancy (a symmetrically weighted coin), but you also possess conclusive evidence that a deterministic hidden-variable mechanism fixes the actual outcome. Should your credence still equal the surface chance?

2. The undermining-future case: learning that the chance of an event is high makes that event less likely to occur (because, say, observers who learn this reliably intervene). Should the Principal Principle still apply?

3. The self-locating branch case: in a branching universe with objective weights, you learn the weights and that you are one of several qualitatively identical observers across branches. Should your first-person credence about which branch you inhabit match those weights?

The tree should terminate in clearly described positions. Required leaves: (i) unrestricted deference to objective chance; (ii) the admissible-evidence restriction (the so-called New Principal Principle); (iii) a revision that blocks undermining futures; (iv) rejection of chance-based deference altogether (for instance, purely subjective Bayesianism); and (v) at least one unoccupied logical combination, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Every internal node should be phrased as a yes/no or choose-one question that a student could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. Keep the taxonomy to no more than eight nodes in total, excluding the root, and write the response in British English, without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
