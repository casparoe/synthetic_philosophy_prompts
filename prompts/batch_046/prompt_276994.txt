You are constructing a reusable audit procedure for a certification body that evaluates adaptive infrastructure systems—smart grids, autonomous building controls, and distributed sensor networks—after anomalous incidents. The body must decide, for each incident report, whether to explain the system's behavior using the physical stance (law-governed interactions of components), the design stance (functional organization and counterfactual stability of outputs under perturbation), or the intentional stance (goal-directed patterns that survive both lower-level descriptions). The theoretical basis is the idea that patterns are real only when they carry predictive and explanatory weight that cannot be replaced by a lower-level account without loss—specifically, that a stance is warranted when adopting it yields predictions that are invariant under a defined class of interventions at the lower level. To make this operational, inject the following niche criterion: a design-level pattern is certified as real if the system's functional outputs remain stable across a standardized set of physical perturbations (component substitution, thermal drift, latency injection); an intentional-level pattern is certified as real only if altering internal representational states—while preserving design function—produces behavioral changes unpredictable at the design level, and if attributing goals improves predictive accuracy over both lower stances for the decision-relevant range. The practitioner is an auditor with engineering literacy but no philosophy doctorate, working under a 30-minute time limit per case, with reports co-signed by a technical engineer and a compliance officer. Write a step-by-step decision procedure—ordered tests, a flowchart in words, explicit judgment-call flags, and a short checklist of diagnostic questions—that translates the three-stance framework into this audit context. Include a precise definition of "intervention invariance" usable without philosophical training, specify the hard cases where the procedure must yield an ambiguous or contested result (e.g., emergent coordination that looks intentional but lacks representational architecture), and apply the procedure to three concrete scenarios: (i) a heating system that optimizes energy by selectively disabling ventilation in occupied rooms; (ii) a security network that locks exit doors during a false-positive intrusion detection, where the procedure's attribution of an intentional stance would trigger stricter liability rules that the auditor prefers to avoid; and (iii) a distributed sensor array generating correlated ghost readings due to electromagnetic interference. For each scenario, show the procedure's output and flag where human judgment overrides the formal result. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot determine—such as whether the system genuinely possesses mental states rather than merely supporting intentional predictions—and identify the point at which the audit must defer to legal rather than conceptual judgment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; ground the procedure in the conceptual distinctions themselves, not in named texts.
