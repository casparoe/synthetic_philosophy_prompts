I defend a strict axiological tranquilism: the only intrinsic values are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, and every other apparent good—joy, knowledge, love, achievement—has value only instrumentally, as a means to secure or preserve those absences. On this view, a permanently tranquil state that is phenomenologically sparse, relationally empty, and devoid of positive affect is not merely tolerable but globally optimal; introducing richer positive content is at best unnecessary and at worst risky, because it creates the conditions for future craving. I want you to audit what would genuinely count against this position. Do not stop at the familiar appeal to the value of happiness or meaningful projects; I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including whether the view collapses into a negative hedonism it explicitly rejects, whether it can accommodate the intuition that a life of sustained mild contentment is axiologically superior to a life of sustained neutral blankness without presupposing the very values it denies, whether the total elimination of craving undermines the agent-conditions required to value absence in the first place, and whether there is a structural asymmetry between suffering and joy that makes the absence of the former insufficient for a complete axiology. For each defeater, indicate whether a committed tranquilist should concede it or can dismiss it, and whether the dismissal is principled or ad hoc. Finally, say explicitly what would change your mind—what argument, observation, or formal result would lead you to revise this ranking of defeaters—and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
