I have been working through the case judgments of a philosopher of mind who applies a 'quining' strategy to phenomenal concepts in the tradition of Chase and Sanborn, asking whether proposed qualia involve incoherent posits rather than genuine explanatory targets. Her track record is as follows. First, she ruled that phenomenal contrast—the qualitative difference between seeing red and seeing green—is not quined, because it can be reconstructed as a structural relation among representational contents rather than as a bare intrinsic property. Second, she ruled that pure phenomenal character, conceived as a non-representational qualitative residue that remains when all intentional content is subtracted, is quined, on the grounds that mental states must possess intentional structure and that leaving a bare residue violates conditions of coherence. Third, she ruled that the feeling of mental effort during demanding cognition is not quined, since it correlates with measurable cognitive load and plays a direct behavioral role in sustaining or abandoning tasks. Fourth, she ruled that ineffable private sensations—defined as experiences that are in principle incommunicable and carry no functional or causal signature—are quined, failing on both explanatory and conceptual grounds.

The new case I want to place before her involves a proposed phenomenal quality called semantic saturation residue. During prolonged repetition of a word until it loses meaning, subjects reliably report not merely an absence of significance but a specific, positively present qualitative hollowness or emptiness. Proponents claim this residue is causally efficacious—it correlates with neural adaptation in temporal language regions and motivates the subject to cease repetition—yet they define it precisely as direct acquaintance with the absence of representational content itself, treating an absence as an intrinsic, present phenomenal object available only in first-person experience.

Given these precedents, what verdict should she deliver for consistency, and which of the four earlier rulings is the closest analog? Do her judgments genuinely underdetermine the outcome, with one plausible principle predicting admission because the residue has functional and neural correlates, and another predicting elimination because it requires treating a structural absence as a positively present intrinsic property? If she admits it, what principle is she privileging; if she rejects it, which earlier rejection does she echo? And to settle which governing rule she actually holds, what further hypothetical case—perhaps one with full structural coherence but complete causal inertness, or one with robust functional efficacy but an explicit contradiction in its defining conditions—would finally distinguish the two?
