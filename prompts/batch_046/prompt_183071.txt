I am preparing material for a graduate seminar on the boundaries of moral obligation, and I have become particularly interested in how epistemic uncertainty about personal cost structures the distinction between duty and supererogation. Rather than revisiting familiar scenarios of certain heroic sacrifice, I wish to examine a case in which a professional faces only a probabilistic risk of severe harm—such as career ruin or lasting health impairment—rather than a guaranteed one. Please design a single, realistic thought experiment set in a non-fantastical workplace or clinical environment, confined to one paragraph, in which a competent professional can intervene to prevent serious harm to others, but only by taking an action that exposes her to that probabilistic severe cost.

The scenario must satisfy three precise theoretical conditions. First, a deontological framework that recognizes a threshold for obligatory self-sacrifice should classify the intervention as supererogatory—morally excellent but not strictly required. Second, a rule-consequentialist analysis, applying a genuinely optimal general rule for professionals in this specific role, should classify the same intervention as morally required. Third, a virtue-ethical evaluation should praise the act as exemplary of courage or benevolence without generating a categorical obligation. After presenting the scenario, please walk through the reasoning for each of the three assessments, then identify the precise structural feature—related specifically to the probabilistic nature of the cost—that drives the divergence between the deontological and rule-consequentialist verdicts. Finally, explain what happens to these classifications if the probabilistic risk is replaced by a certain, equivalent cost, and note how a defender of the deontological position might resist the rule-consequentialist framing by challenging the aggregation of risks across similar cases.
