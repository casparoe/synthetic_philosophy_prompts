As someone who recently began facilitating an undergraduate seminar on just war theory, I have noticed students repeatedly invoking the phrase "the end justifies the means" to defend President Truman's decision to use atomic weapons against Hiroshima and Nagasaki, specifically in order to dismiss G. E. M. Anscombe's well-known opposition to awarding him an honorary Oxford doctorate. Rather than engaging with her specific argument about the moral impermissibility of intentionally directing violence against non-combatants, they treat the slogan as if it were a self-evident rebuttal. Could you separate the precise claims that sentence might actually be expressing — from trivial observations about instrumental rationality to full-blown consequentialist principles — and for each indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or actually false? Please specify which of these readings, if adopted consistently, would genuinely support the defense of Truman's decision, and which ones Anscombe's own distinction between effects that are intended as ends or means and effects that are merely foreseen but not intended (the framework she applies in her critique of consequentialist reasoning about war) would block or undermine. I want a menu of interpretations I can bring back to the seminar, not a single authoritative reading.
