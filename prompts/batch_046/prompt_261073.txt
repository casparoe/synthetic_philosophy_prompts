I am a doctoral candidate in political history working on institutional longevity, and I am designing a methodological session for an interdisciplinary workshop of about twelve graduate students in history, philosophy, and the social sciences. Our shared problem is forecasting whether a contemporary great power is likely to undergo rapid relative decline within the next fifty years, but we want to do so through explicit reference-class reasoning rather than through narrative analysis of current events. To force the group to articulate where they actually stand before they adopt any standard label, I need a formal decision tree—suitable for a printed handout—that sorts forecasting stances by the answers to concrete methodological questions, not by the names of the schools. The tree should open with whether one grants that pre-modern imperial cycles and post-hegemonic transitions supply a valid base rate for modern states, then branch through successive commitments: whether inside-view knowledge about present institutions should be permitted to override the outside view; whether small perturbations—say, a single leadership succession crisis or an isolated technological shock—should be treated as statistical noise or as potential divergence points in a chaos-sensitive dynamics; and whether normative goals of planning and intervention may legitimately restrict the reference class one selects. Each terminal node should be assigned either a named stance, if one exists, or marked 'unnamed,' and should include a one-sentence statement of its most awkward consequence—such as being compelled to treat nuclear deterrence and digital information networks as irrelevant to the historical base rate, or being unable to explain why some micro-perturbations cascade into systemic collapse while others dissipate.

Please phrase every branching node as a question that a student could answer without knowing which leaf it produces, and ensure the taxonomy explicitly flags any pair of positions that the binary structure cannot genuinely separate. I also want an honest annotation—perhaps in a sidebar or footnote to the handout—identifying which feature of chaos-sensitive forecasting the tree must suppress in order to maintain discrete branches, so that participants do not mistake the instrument for a complete model of historical dynamics. The group will have ninety minutes, and the activity should begin with each student silently tracing their own path through the tree, followed by a comparison of where people landed and why; therefore, the layout should be compact enough to complete in roughly ten minutes of individual work, but rigorous enough to sustain a full debrief.
