I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I keep noticing a particular pattern in arguments about metaphysical fundamentality and grounding that I cannot place. The pattern is this: a theorist claims that one kind of thing—say, physical properties—is fundamental because another kind—say, mental properties—depends on it. Yet when asked what positively characterizes the first kind apart from that dependency, the answer often amounts to saying it is whatever remains once you remove everything said to depend on it. In software terms, it resembles defining a root node in a dependency graph solely by observing that it has no parents, except that the list of nodes being checked has been assembled precisely because they depend on that root. I have seen analogues in debates about whether social facts are grounded in individual behavior, where the individuals are defined as the non-social atoms, but "non-social" seems to mean little more than "not part of the collective being explained." This feels different from ordinary circular reasoning, because the loop is not in a chain of premises but in the framing of the domain itself: one draws the boundary so that a single item lacks dependencies, then cites that absence as proof of its priority. Is there an established philosophical term for this exact configuration—where a grounding claim implicitly defines its base through the very relations of dependence it asserts? I do not want a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want to know whether philosophers have carved this out as its own failure mode, how it differs from standard circularity and from ontological dependence, and if no standard name exists, how one might precisely delimit it.
