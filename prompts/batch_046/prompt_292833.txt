I serve on the general-education curriculum committee at a university that is finalizing the syllabus for a required module called Infinity, Number, and Conceptual Limits. The proposed design treats Cantor’s theorem and Hilbert’s hotel as straightforward demonstrations of actual infinite sets, embedding them in a standard Platonist framing in which completed infinite totalities are taken as unproblematic mathematical realities. A minority report—submitted jointly by a philosopher of mathematics and a constructivist logician—objects that this structure silently commits every student to an ontologically loaded actualism, marginalizes finitist and potentialist traditions, and violates our charter’s neutrality clause, which holds that required coursework must not presuppose controversial metaphysical interpretations of formal systems. The dissent proposes an alternative architecture: present Hilbert’s hotel not as proof of an actually infinite collection of rooms, but as a paradox that exposes tensions in treating infinite collections as completed wholes; present Cantor’s argument with explicit caveats about its dependence on the power-set axiom and the acceptance of arbitrary completed totalities; and include a substantive unit on strict finitism and ultrafinitist critiques that deny coherence to arbitrarily large numbers rather than treating them as curiosities. The committee must issue a binding decision memo recommending whether to approve the original syllabus, reject it in favor of the minority restructuring, or approve it subject to enforceable conditions. Draft that memo with a clear bottom-line recommendation, reasoning that weighs the pedagogical value of standard set-theoretic literacy against the philosophical legitimacy of the neutrality objection, and an honest, unweakened presentation of the strongest consideration on the losing side—including whether the neutrality clause itself can be satisfied without collapsing mathematical education into a false equivalence among incompatible ontologies.
