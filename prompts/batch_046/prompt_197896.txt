I am organizing the final meeting of a philosophy of knowledge seminar and want to close with a short poem rather than a summary slide or a list of open questions. I would like a villanelle—exactly nineteen lines, composed of five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains that alternate according to the standard ABA rhyme scheme and finally converge in the closing couplet. The first refrain should give voice to the reliabilist conviction that a cognitive faculty is vindicated by the true beliefs it produces; the second refrain should give voice to the circularity worry that those very beliefs are available only through the faculty being tested. Between these returning lines, the poem should pass through the familiar scenario of trying to confirm that one is not a brain in a vat—the kind of “easy” anti-skeptical knowledge that seems to require no independent labor—and should show how granting such knowledge still fails to validate the perceptual process that delivers it. The formal repetition should do philosophical work: each return of the refrains should feel like another turn of the same circle, with the closing couplet leaving the loop suspended rather than breaking it. The language should be precise enough that a student familiar with debates about epistemic bootstrapping, process reliabilism, and the distinction between easy and hard knowledge would recognize the positions being voiced, but it should avoid naming any particular philosopher and should not use technical labels such as “closure principle,” “defeater,” or “domain restriction.” Please observe the villanelle form strictly—do not relax the meter or abandon the refrain pattern—and keep the tone contemplative rather than polemical, so that the poem ends where our discussion ended: with the tension intact.
