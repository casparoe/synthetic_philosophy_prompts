Design a 75-minute graduate seminar activity for 12 participants on interpersonal utility comparison. The target idea is not generic difficulty comparing well-being, but the specific structural knot that appears when agents have adaptively formed, interdependent preference structures—where each claimant’s reported satisfaction is partly a function of the other’s allocation, and both have adjusted their baselines to past deprivation—so that aggregation by sum-ranking, weighted averaging, or prioritarian weighting either smuggles in an unstated external metric or collapses into circularity. The participants should run into this knot through the rules before anyone names it.

Set the scene as a structured simulation with assigned roles: one “allocation officer” with a fixed budget of 30 points to distribute among three “claimants,” each claimant holding hidden rule cards that govern how they report utility. The rules—not any opening lecture—must generate the problem: for example, a claimant’s reported satisfaction shifts mechanically based on the officer’s preliminary draft allocation, and one claimant’s card defines her well-being partly as a ratio to another claimant’s share. Use real but small stakes: the distributed points determine the group’s actual choice of the next seminar’s discussion topic or the division of a shared snack budget, so participants feel the pressure of the decision.

Specify the exact timeline (e.g., 10 minutes silent rule review, 20 minutes first-round allocation and reporting, 15 minutes revised allocation under revealed interdependence, 15 minutes forced final ranking, 15 minutes debrief). List roles, hidden-rule mechanics, materials (index cards, point tokens), and the precise decision procedure the officer must follow.

In the debrief, map at least three moments from the simulation onto structural features of the comparison problem—adaptive baseline shift, interdependence of preference reports, and circular aggregation—showing how the mechanics made each visible. Predict what will go wrong in execution (e.g., participants blame the officer’s fairness rather than seeing that the measurement framework itself is unstable) and advise how to redirect without lecturing. Then be frank: state exactly which feature of the formal problem—likely the distinction between ex-post revealed preference stability and ex-ante normative entitlement, or the ordinal-versus-cardinal distinction—the simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally.
