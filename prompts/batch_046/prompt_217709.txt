I am writing a novel in which the protagonist serves repeatedly as the senior clinical ethics consultant for a hospital’s Advanced Preservation and Neuro-Isolation Unit. She must render preliminary death-determination assessments several times a month—within ninety minutes—because organ-procurement timelines and family-notification protocols depend on them. Each assessment requires co-signature by a neurologist and an ethicist; the neurologist supplies empirical findings, but the conceptual framing and final judgment are hers. The unit employs cerebral-arterial decoupling devices and synthetic circulatory-maintenance pods that can sustain cardiac and pulmonary function after brain perfusion has been isolated or destroyed, producing clinical configurations that standard brain-death and circulatory-death protocols do not cover.

To render her reasoning authentic across chapters, I need a reusable decision procedure—not a legal checklist, but an operationalized philosophical framework that translates the distinction between brain death and circulatory death into ordered practical steps. The procedure should hold together the integrative-unity conception (death as the irreversible collapse of the organism’s self-coordinating coherence, with the brain as the critical integrator of bodily function) and the consciousness-dependent conception (death as the irreversible cessation of subjective experience, which permits that mechanical circulation may continue without the person persisting). It must address three technological ambiguities generated by the unit’s equipment: total brain-perfusion isolation with maintained mechanical circulation; intact brainstem reflexes combined with isolated cortical perfusion and absent spontaneous cardiac output; and detectable but fragmented cortical activity within a decoupled system that exhibits no integrated organismal response.

Please construct the procedure as a flowchart in words: the ordered questions she asks at each stage, what counts as a sufficient empirical answer, the explicit stop-point where the procedure declares death, the stop-point where it declares life, and the marked hand-off points where data are complete but the decision requires a value judgment she must sign for personally. Include a small set of rapid bedside tests—analogous to substitution tests in belief reports—that distinguish whether an observed physiological response is genuinely integrative or merely mechanically sustained.

Then apply the procedure to three cases.

First, a patient on full decoupling with absent brainstem reflexes, no detectable cortical activity, and mechanically maintained circulation: the procedure should declare death, but identify the precise step at which a family or surgical team might resist, and show how she justifies proceeding.

Second, a patient with intact brainstem reflexes and isolated cortical perfusion, requiring synthetic circulatory support with no spontaneous cardiac output: this should fall into the ambiguous zone where the procedure explicitly halts and transfers authority to her judgment rather than to the empirical findings alone.

Third, a case in which the procedure yields a determination she personally finds unwelcome—specifically, where minimal but detectable cortical activity in a decoupled system forces a determination of life, blocking procurement that the family and surgical team have already accepted—so that I can depict the conflict between procedural rigor and human preference.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which conceptual distinctions it suppresses for practical usability, which future technological changes would break its validity, and where it must fail openly rather than disguise an ethical judgment as a technical conclusion. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; I need the architecture rendered usable by a practitioner working without external sources.
