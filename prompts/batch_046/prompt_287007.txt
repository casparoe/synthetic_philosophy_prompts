I am a high school junior preparing a thirty-minute session for our school's philosophy club—students with no formal training in evolutionary psychology, anthropology, or philosophy of religion—on the claim that religious commitment is best understood as a natural phenomenon sustained by credibility-enhancing displays and a self-sealing division of epistemic labor, rather than as mere irrationality or conscious deception. I need a teaching plan, not the lecture itself. First, map the dependency order: what distinction—perhaps between belief formed through direct evidence and belief formed through distributed testimony—must be secure before the mechanism can even be introduced, and why? Second, identify the single concrete case or scenario that should carry the explanation: what it must include, such as an observable costly behavior that reinforces belief without providing propositional evidence, and what it must exclude, such as a focus on doctrinal content or on individual mystical experience. Third, list the misconceptions my audience will likely generate in sequence—such as that this framework eliminates all genuine religious experience, that it requires malicious manipulation by religious leaders, or that it applies only to other people's traditions—and specify exactly where in the sequence each should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, propose one precise check-for-understanding question I can pose at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the structural logic rather than just adopted the vocabulary. Finally, state clearly what I should openly refuse to cover at this level—whether specific theological doctrines, neuroimaging findings, or comparative ethnographic data—and say so explicitly.
