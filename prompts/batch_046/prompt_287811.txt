I've been working out a personal view on Berkeley's idealism without formal philosophical training, and I'd like a consistency check. Here is my position: (a) for physical objects, esse est percipi is correct—there is no hidden material substrate behind experience; (b) Berkeley's master argument fails, since I can imagine a tree in an unvisited forest without that mental act putting me in direct perceptual contact with it; (c) nevertheless, objects do not vanish instantly when unobserved, because perception leaves a temporary "perceptual echo"—a residual structure in the field of ideas that sustains the object for a limited duration before it fades; (d) I reject God as a universal observer, holding that occasional conscious observation plus these echoes explains continuity well enough; (e) abstract objects like numbers or logical relations exist independently of any mind, so esse est percipi is false for them; (f) if all conscious observers vanished permanently, physical objects would eventually dissolve, since there would be no source for fresh echoes.

Which pairs here are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision for each real problem? In particular: does (b) undermine my defense of (a)? Is (c) compatible with a strict reading of (a), or does it smuggle in something like matter? Does (d) leave me unable to account for long-term persistence? Is there a principled reason in my view why (e) should be exempt from (a)? And is (f) consistent with (c) and (d)? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each genuine conflict say exactly which commitment would have to give and at what cost.
