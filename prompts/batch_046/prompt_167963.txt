As a novelist drafting a chapter in which a minor character becomes fixated on collective consciousness, I have lately been discussing with friends the conceptual puzzle commonly referred to as Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue — the question of whether an ant colony, lacking any central controller yet displaying remarkably integrated behaviour, can genuinely be said to possess a mind of its own. For this project I should like you to compose a satirical parody in the form of an 1880s broadsheet agony-aunt column, approximately eight hundred words, written in a deadpan Victorian voice under the heading Dear Aunt Hillary and spoken from the point of view of the colony herself — an invented, rather grandiose proponent of strong emergentism. The column should receive plaintive letters from individual worker ants who feel they have been reduced to mere vehicles for her unified thoughts, and Aunt Hillary ought to reply with condescending moral reassurance, defending her mental unity by appealing to distributed functional integration and the absence of any queenly homunculus, while implicitly treating her correspondents as having no more subjective standing than neurons in a brain. To prevent the piece from feeling like a regurgitated classroom example, please weave in a fictional Fugue Protocol — a pseudo-scientific technique for measuring polyphonic consciousness in distributed systems — which Aunt Hillary dismisses with aristocratic disdain as vulgar mechanism, even as she describes its criteria with unwitting precision. The humour should arise from exaggerating real features of the emergentist position rather than inventing straw figures, so that a reader acquainted with the philosophy of collective mind recognises the satire as fair, whilst a lay reader finds the voice amusing and the argument legible. Finally, please append a brief reflective note identifying precisely where the parody takes liberties with, or relies upon a contested reading of, emergentist theory, and stating what argument or evidence would cause you to regard the satire as philosophically unfair or misdirected — in other words, what would change your mind.
