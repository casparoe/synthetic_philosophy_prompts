I serve as the interpretation director for a regional heritage authority. Every month I must sign off on excavation reports that move from describing artifacts to attributing specific beliefs, rituals, or social institutions to cultures with no written records, knowing these attributions become permanent in public archives and shape how descendant communities are represented. I need a reusable decision procedure—something a reviewer with field training but no outside specialist access can run in under an hour—that orders the critical questions, defines what counts as a sufficient answer to each, specifies where the procedure stops with a mandatory “describe only, do not attribute,” identifies the exact points where the decision must be handed to human judgment because values rather than evidence are at stake, and prescribes the report-language tier permitted at each outcome (for example, “consistent with,” “suggests,” or “evidences”). Build this on what archaeological epistemology actually knows about linking material culture to belief—analogy, negative evidence, scale, taphonomic confounds, and the risk of symbolic overload—not on intuition. The procedure must include a gate-by-gate flow with hard cases it must handle and explicit flags for where it will misfire. Then apply it to three cases: (1) clusters of deliberately broken ceramic vessels sealed in domestic wall niches across four houses at one site, proposed as evidence of “household life-cycle ritual deposition”; (2) a sharp spatial separation of metalworking debris and high-status burial goods divided by a river, proposed as evidence of “hereditary craft-specialist status with institutionalized exchange”; and (3) a uniform burial orientation with heads facing a distant peak in fifteen graves but absent elsewhere, where my team wants to reject any cosmological claim and attribute the pattern solely to slope topography—run the procedure and show me the unwelcome result it forces. For each case, report the gate outcomes and the exact language the procedure allows. Conclude by stating what classes of belief-to-object inference the procedure can never settle and where using it without the marked judgment points will systematically fail.
