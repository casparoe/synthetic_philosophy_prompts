In philosophy of mind and cognitive science, the brain is routinely compared to a computer—more specifically, to a digital architecture implementing algorithms over representational vehicles. A narrower, recently prominent version compares cortical predictive-coding circuits to GPU clusters running parallel Bayesian updates: neurons map onto processing cores, synaptic weights onto model parameters, and prediction-error signals onto backpropagated gradients. Defenders invoke this correspondence to argue that mental processes are substrate-independent computations, since both systems approximate identical mathematical functions. Critics reply that the mapping conceals structural disanalogies that undermine that conclusion. Audit this analogy precisely: provide an explicit correspondence table; distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely threaten substrate-independence—such as the absence of clock synchronization, a centralized loss function, or discrete symbol manipulation—from those that are merely superficial; identify what normative or architectural assumptions the source domain quietly imports into the target; and assess whether the conclusion that cognition is computation regardless of material survives once these breaks are registered. Finally, if the GPU-cluster analogy fails, suggest a more apt comparison—perhaps an analog chemical computer, or a self-organizing dynamical system—that preserves whatever explanatory power the original had without its misleading digital baggage.
