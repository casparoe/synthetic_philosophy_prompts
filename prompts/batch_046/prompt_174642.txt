Following a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about whether political leaders genuinely incur moral culpability when they authorise necessary evils, I have been trying to work through my own ambivalence about the problem of dirty hands in politics. We kept circling the familiar claim that a statesman must sometimes dirty his hands for the common good, yet neither of us could agree on whether formalising that exception makes the compromise more honest or merely more administratively comfortable. I would like you to write a satirical piece that inhabits the latter possibility without ever breaking its deadpan bureaucratic mask.

Please compose an internal government memorandum — in the style of a Whitehall circular distributed to Permanent Secretaries and Cabinet ministers — outlining the Department's new Stain Amortisation Framework for the ethical management of executive decision-making. The memo should be approximately 600 words, formatted with numbered sections and a sidebar, and should adopt the voice of an over-earnest HR compliance officer who treats moral compromise as a quantifiable liability subject to audit, depreciation schedules, and offset markets. Seed the parody with the following invented institutional details: a reimbursement schedule for leather gloves stained in the course of duty; an Ethical Offset Credit scheme allowing ministers to purchase purification tokens from certified NGOs; an annual Hand-Washing Ceremony held in Westminster Hall with graded levels of attendance; and a classification system that distinguishes Necessary Stains, depreciated over five years, from Recreational Stains, which are immediately taxable. The satire should exaggerate the real features of dirty-hands reasoning — the assumption that someone may authorise wrong for right, the difficulty of distinguishing necessary from self-serving compromises, and the temptation to bureaucratise moral judgment — so that the standard objections land implicitly rather than being stated outright. A reader familiar with the philosophical debate should recognise that the memo is ironising the idea that moral dirt can be managed like a budget line, while a reader unfamiliar with it should still find the piece legible as a critique of ethics-washing in public life. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan throughout; do not wink, do not name any living politician, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific academic texts within the memo itself.
