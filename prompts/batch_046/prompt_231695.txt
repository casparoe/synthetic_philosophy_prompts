In several European disability-adjudication systems, claimants reporting chronic neuropathic pain must satisfy a dual-track evaluation: Track A records self-reported intensity, quality, and location through standardized scales, while Track B requires documented affective distress—either through validated psychometric scores or clinician-observed behavioral markers such as protective guarding, facial grimacing, or withdrawal. A claimant who scores high on Track A but registers minimal affective response—precisely the dissociative profile observed in pain asymbolia following insular or anterior cingulate damage—is routinely downgraded to "sensory-only" status and denied full compensation, on the institutional rationale that the absence of suffering demonstrates an absence of genuine disability, despite the claimant's explicit description of the sensation as excruciating. At the same time, clinical-ethics guidelines in the same jurisdictions treat first-person pain reports as authoritative precisely in cases where observable behavior and emotional expression are dissociated from the report.

What philosophical commitments about the ontology and axiology of pain does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in the adjudication rules: does it commit to a metaphysically separable bipartite structure in which sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational dimensions can come apart, coupled with the further claim that the badness of pain resides essentially in the affective component? Or does it presuppose an evidentialist epistemology of self-report—namely, that a pain claim is credible only when corroborated by emotional-behavioral expression—and if so, how does that square with the ethical guideline that privileges self-report precisely when behavior is unreliable? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates comfortably, assess whether the two tracks are philosophically consistent with one another, and specify exactly what the compensation framework would have to change—regarding eligibility criteria, evidentiary weight, and the concept of disability itself—if one replaced its implicit view with a purely representationalist, purely functionalist, or purely hedonist account of pain's disvalue.
