I'm a clinical psychologist leading structured group-therapy sessions for adults in early recovery from substance dependence, and I need to make real-time judgments—often with only a minute or two of reflection—about whether a given intervention I'm about to deploy counts as legitimate persuasion or crosses into manipulation. The stakes are high: if I misclassify, I either withhold a genuinely helpful technique out of excessive caution, or I violate patient autonomy in a setting where vulnerability is already acute and my authority as the facilitator is pronounced. I want you to translate the conceptual distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion into an operational procedure I can actually run under time pressure: a stepwise checklist or flowchart-in-words that preserves the philosophical substance while stripping away what isn't decision-relevant in a session.

To keep the translation honest, I want the procedure built around four analytical axes that seem to capture the morally relevant differences but are rarely brought together in clinical ethics literature: first, intentional transparency—whether the patient can, upon reflection, identify the mechanism by which my words are meant to move them; second, epistemic access—whether the intervention leaves intact the patient's ability to encounter and weigh counter-reasons; third, exit-cost symmetry—whether leaving the interaction or rejecting my framing carries disproportionate psychological or social penalties relative to accepting it; and fourth, preference-formation endogeneity—whether the technique targets the patient's beliefs and values as they stand, or instead restructures the very process by which they form preferences. The procedure should specify the order in which I should test these conditions, which ones are necessary versus jointly sufficient, how to handle cases where the axes conflict, and exactly where judgment cannot be eliminated despite the formal structure. It must include worked applications to three scenarios: (a) using strategic silence after a patient minimizes their relapse risk; (b) reframing a patient's guilt about past harm as data for growth in order to sustain engagement; and (c) a case where the checklist yields a classification I will not want to hear—specifically, that a technique I rely on heavily is manipulative by the lights of the procedure. Finally, the procedure should state clearly what it cannot adjudicate—perhaps cases of structural coercion outside the dyad, or the long-term formation of therapeutic dependence—so I do not overapply it.

The answer should be disciplined about the mapping: for each axis, say what observable behavior or session feature counts as evidence, say where the theory is silent rather than forcing a verdict, and do not ask me to consult bibliographies, citation lists, or external authorities I cannot access in the room. I am not looking for generic therapeutic ethics; I want the philosophical architecture of manipulation versus persuasion rendered as a practitioner's tool, with its failures and gray zones exposed rather than smoothed over.
