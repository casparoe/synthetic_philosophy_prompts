I've been trying to pin down whether the Church-Turing thesis is a claim about mathematics, physics, or something closer to a transcendental precondition for empirical science, and I constructed the following argument that seems to push it into the latter category in a suspicious way. I want to know exactly where it collapses, if it does.

(1) The physical Church-Turing thesis (PCTT) holds that any computation performable by a physical system can be simulated by a Turing machine. (2) If PCTT is false, then there exists some physically realizable dynamical process that computes a non-Turing-computable function—say, one that solves the halting problem for arbitrary Turing machines. (3) If we could embed that process in an instrument, we would have an oracle machine capable of deciding the consistency of any formal system describing physical law, including the very theory that predicts the existence of the oracle process itself. (4) This generates an epistemic circularity: the theory that licenses the oracle becomes subject to verification by the oracle, which seems to undermine the independence required for genuine empirical testing. (5) Therefore, the falsity of PCTT does not merely open the door to hypercomputation; it collapses the distinction between theoretical prediction and metatheoretic verification. (6) So PCTT must hold not as a contingent law of nature but as a necessary condition for the coherence of scientific inquiry itself.

The conclusion feels wrong—surely the Church-Turing thesis is an open empirical question about the limits of physical dynamics, not a Kantian condition on science. But each step looks defensible on its face. Where does the slide happen? Is it in the move from (2) to (3), assuming that computing a non-computable function automatically yields an epistemically usable oracle rather than just an in-principle capacity drowned in noise? Or is the circularity in (4) a pseudo-problem, since physics routinely uses theories to calibrate instruments that then test those same theories? I would like you to distinguish between a subtle technical flaw—perhaps an equivocation between computability-in-principle and computability-under-physical-constraints—and a deeper philosophical mistake about how empirical theories relate to their own formal limits. If you think the argument is actually sound and only sounds strange, say so explicitly and explain what price that commits us to, particularly regarding the epistemic status of analog computation and continuous dynamics. Please also say what would change your mind about where the error lies: for instance, a specific result showing that analog physical systems cannot stably maintain oracle-level precision, a clarification of the distinction between the mathematical and physical Church-Turing claims that dissolves the circularity, or a demonstration that epistemic circularity is benign in other domains of science and therefore harmless here. Finally, indicate where your own reasoning is most likely to fail—whether you are relying on an intuitive but unanalyzed distinction between "using" and "simulating" computation that might not survive closer scrutiny, or whether your diagnosis depends on treating physical law as more formally transparent than it actually is.
