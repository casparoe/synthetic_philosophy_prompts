I am designing a forty-five-minute seminar for third-year political-philosophy undergraduates who have read Hobbes's account of sovereignty in Leviathan but have not encountered Spinoza. The idea I want them to understand is Spinoza's claim—developed across the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus and the unfinished Political Treatise—that democracy is the form of government closest to nature not because it aggregates pre-political individual wills, as in Rousseau, but because right is coextensive with power: a democratic assembly in which citizens do not alienate their natural right to a single sovereign preserves the greatest collective power. I also want them to see that this is not majoritarian voting, and that Spinoza structurally excludes women, servants, and foreigners from the council—not as an accidental prejudice, but because anyone who lives under another's private power cannot contribute to the collective power that defines the democratic state. To carry this, I will use the collapse of the ancient Hebrew commonwealth after the death of Moses: the absence of a unitary sovereign did not produce chaos but a distributed, unstable democracy that eventually failed because the affective conditions for collective reason—the replacement of fear by a shared orientation toward the common good—were not sustained.

Plan the teaching sequence: the order in which the metaphysical premise that right equals power, the anti-contractualist argument, the Hebrew example, and the exclusion clause must be introduced, with explicit dependencies so that each step is intelligible only after the previous one has landed; the precise features the carrying example must have and must lack so that it illustrates Spinoza's point rather than confirming Hobbes's fear of divided sovereignty; the misconceptions students will predictably import from Hobbes and from modern liberal assumptions—especially that sovereignty must be unitary, that democracy is essentially a voting mechanism, and that Spinoza's exclusions are mere historical bias rather than integral to his definition of the free citizen—together with the point in the sequence where each should be anticipated and defused; the check for understanding that demonstrates they have grasped the non-contractual, power-preserving logic rather than memorized a slogan; and what should be explicitly left out at this level and acknowledged as such—perhaps the geometric derivation of the conatus doctrine in the Ethics, the full scriptural hermeneutics of chapters 16 through 20 of the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus, or the precise distinction between imperium and respublica in the late fragments—so that the explanation remains coherent without overreaching.
