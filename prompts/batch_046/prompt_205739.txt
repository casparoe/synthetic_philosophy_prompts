Hey, I sketched this argument for my grad seminar on liberty — positive, negative, and republican — and now I'm stuck because it either saves democracy by destroying republicanism's bite, or it proves too much about temporary tyrants. Here it is.

(1) Republican liberty is absence of domination: you're free only if no agent has arbitary power over you.
(2) But "arbitrary power" should be understood with a modal stability condition: it's not enough that someone can interfere with you now; they must be able to do so across nearby possible worlds where your situation is slightly different.
(3) In a liberal democracy, you can be outvoted, but in nearby worlds the coalition shifts, the law changes, or you move jurisditions. So democratic interference isn't modally stable.
(4) Therefore democratic citizens aren't dominated — they face interference, but not domination.
(5) Now apply (2) to a temporary dictatorship: the tyrant can interfere arbitrarily today, but in nearby worlds he dies, is overthrown, or changes policy. So by the modal stability condition, he doesn't dominate either.
(6) So republicanism can't condemn temporary tyranny, only structural or permanent domination.
(7) But that's clearly wrong — being under a dictator for a year is a paradigm case of unfreedom.
(8) So either (2) is false, or republican liberty is useless for criticizing most real-world oppression.

I really want to keep (1) and maybe salvage some version of (2) — it feels like modal robustness should matter for domination, since a one-off threat doesn't feel like domination. But I can't see how to restrict (2) so it saves democracy but still catches temporary tyrants. Is the slip in (5), applying modal stability too broadly? Or is there an equivocation between "stable over worlds" and "stable over time"? What's the least painful fix, and what does it cost for republican liberty?
