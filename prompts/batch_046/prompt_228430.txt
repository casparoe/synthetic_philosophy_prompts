Consider Avicenna’s flying-man scenario, which is standardly read as showing that the soul possesses an immediate, non-sensory awareness of its own existence even when every sensory and imaginative faculty is suspended. Some interpreters extend this result to claim that, in such a suspended state, the rational soul retains fully actualized intellectual capacities—so that it could, for instance, work through syllogistic reasoning without any bodily instrument. That extension seems to conflict with Avicenna’s broader Aristotelian position that the body serves not merely for sensation and imagination but also as an indispensable instrument for acquiring intelligibles and for the discursive movement between premises. Is the correct resolution that the flying man demonstrates only an occurrent, bare de se awareness—something like a non-propositional indexical self-reference—while leaving all dispositional reasoning capacities dormant until bodily engagement is restored? Or does the argument genuinely commit Avicenna to isolated discursive thought, and if so, how can he consistently maintain that bodily organs are instrumental rather than merely preparatory to the intellect?
