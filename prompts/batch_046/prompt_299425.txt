My study group cooked up this argument about Sen and famine and now I think it proves too much but I cant spot the slip. (1) Sen showed that in the Bengal famine of 1943, food availability per capita wasn't even particularly low in some districts — people starved because rural laborers lost wages and the state failed to redistribute entitlements. Same logic applies to the Irish famine and the Great Leap Forward: mass starvation tracks entitlement collapse, not just crop failure. (2) So if we call something a "natural disaster" only when nature kills people without any social mediation, then famines are never natural disasters; they're always failures of the entitlement system. (3) Now imagine a remote mountain commune with no state at all, just barter, that gets cut off by a landslide. Trade stops, people starve even though there's plenty of food on the other side of the rubble. There's no government, no policy failure — just bad geography and broken logistics. (4) But Sen's entitlement framework still applies: people died because they lacked exchange entitlements, not because food vanished. So by (2), this too should be a politcal crime, not a natural disaster. (5) That feels absurd — there's literally no politics involved. (6) Therefore either Sen's framework forces us to call every starvation event political, which empties the category, or we have to admit that some entitlement failures are non-political and thus the real-world famines might also just be bad luck with institutions. (7) I really want to keep that famines like Bengal are politically mediated, but I also want to keep that the landslide commune is a natural disaster. Which step do I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Please answer in American English and dont send me a bibliography or citation list since I'm working offline.
