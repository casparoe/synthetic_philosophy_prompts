In a seminar on mortuary archaeology, a colleague repeatedly invoked the line “the dead don’t bury themselves” to block inferences from grave goods to the social identity of the deceased, insisting that burial assemblages reveal only the beliefs and emotional needs of the living mourners. I suspect the slogan is being asked to do more precise work than that—perhaps about agency, representation, or the epistemic weight of absence—but I cannot tell which reading is actually supporting her dismissal of my argument that repeated, cross-demographic patterns in grave goods are better explained by institutionalized status categories than by purely individual mourner choice. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing: for example, claims about who performs burial acts, about whether material culture mirrors mental states, about whether absence of goods implies absence of belief, and about the limits of reverse-engineering institutions from objects? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible principle of archaeological inference, or false; and tell me which of these readings she was likely invoking when using the slogan to reject institutional reconstruction from burial patterns. I want a menu of interpretations I can bring back to distinguish the trivial point from the substantive methodological commitment.
