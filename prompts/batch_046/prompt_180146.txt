I am delivering the opening remarks at the closing symposium of my late mentor's interdisciplinary studio; half the audience are cognitive scientists who worked with her on models of self-representation, and half are writers, students, and family who knew her only through her essays. She held that the self is a center of narrative gravity — not an inner observer but a gravitational pattern that organizes experience without being located anywhere inside the body. The speech must take a definite stance on whether that gravity vanishes the moment the brain stops sustaining it, leaving nothing but archives and memories, or whether it leaves some modified, distributed residue in the narratives she anchored. Structurally, the address should make it impossible for me to say she is watching over us without contradicting her view, and equally impossible to say she is simply erased without contradicting the reality of the narrative pattern she was; yet it must still comfort the non-specialists in the room. Build the address around one coherent interpretation, not a survey of options, and calibrate every sentence so that the scientists hear the philosophical constraint while the family hears genuine consolation.
