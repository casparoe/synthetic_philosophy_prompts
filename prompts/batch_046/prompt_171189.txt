I have been invited to present the central philosophical claim developed in the afterword to my recent novel—expanded now into a full argument—at an interdisciplinary symposium on psychiatric taxonomy and narrative medicine. The thesis I intend to defend is that mental disease is best reconstructed neither as a natural biological dysfunction, in the Boorsean statistical-evolutionary sense, nor as a normatively harmful deviation from expected functioning, in the Wakefield-Nordenfelt mode, but rather as a specific rupture I call a fracture in autobiographical continuity of embodied agency. On this view, a condition such as severe dissociative disorder counts as genuinely pathological not because it violates statistical norms of biological design or produces socially recognized harm, but because it severs the first-person temporal coherence through which an agent experiences herself as the continuous author of her bodily actions over time—leaving biological processes intact while breaking the narrative integration that makes agency coherent. The audience will be divided into three camps: clinical psychiatrists for whom DSM-5 categorical reliability is the non-negotiable floor of any useful concept; philosophy-of-medicine scholars, among whom naturalists will regard my appeal to narrative coherence as subjective fluff unsuited to scientific taxonomy, and normativists will object that autobiographical continuity is culturally specific and privileges Western individualist selfhood; and narrative-medicine practitioners who welcome subjective storytelling but will resist my claim that narrative rupture can serve as an objective diagnostic criterion rather than merely a hermeneutic lens. I need a speaker’s prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections in the order they are likely to come, each phrased as that camp would actually phrase it; for each, what I should concede without collapsing the argument, what I must hold firm on, a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. I also need to prepare for the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—namely, whether my criterion can distinguish pathological dissociation from ordinary, non-pathological identity change such as religious conversion or artistic reinvention—together with the reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive, and the precise point at which that reply is most vulnerable. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need to defend this from the argument I have already constructed, not from authority.
