I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I am preparing a single forty-five-minute brown-bag session for senior backend engineers — people who think fluently in terms of distributed state, versioning, and deployment identity, but who have never encountered modal metaphysics — on the disagreement between counterpart-theoretic and transworld-identity accounts of modal claims. Do not explain the theories to me; plan how I should explain them. What dependency-ordered sequence should I follow so that the notion of an object in another possible world emerges as a substantive problem rather than an obvious given — specifically, how should I stage possibility, essential properties, and the identity relation across worlds? Propose exactly one carrying scenario drawn from our domain, such as a container rebuilt with identical source but altered environment state across two clusters, or a git branch that diverges and is subsequently merged back; specify what that scenario must possess and what it must lack in order to do the philosophical work rather than merely illustrate it. Identify the misconceptions this audience will predictably form — for example, equating functional equivalence with numerical identity, interpreting "possible world" as just "another server region," or treating identity preservation as a matter of checksum rather than history — and state precisely at which point in the sequence each should be headed off. Provide one closing diagnostic question that reveals whether they have grasped the metaphysical disagreement rather than merely adopted the vocabulary. Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level — for instance, the ontological status of possible worlds, or the debate about essential origins — and how I should frame that omission so it does not feel like a gap.
