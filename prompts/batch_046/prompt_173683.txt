I'm a novelist preparing a work on the mythology of American self-invention, and I want to sharpen my thinking about how that mythology collapses in practice. Read F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby through the lens of American exceptionalism understood not as a single slogan but as a three-layered structure: a historical thesis about the absence of feudal inheritance in the New World, a normative claim that American institutions are therefore exempt from the cyclical tragedies of old-world class conflict, and an ideology that converts that exemption into a license to remake one's origins. I am particularly interested in whether the novel dramatizes the collapse of the normative claim into mere ideology—specifically, whether Gatsby's attempt to repeat and correct the past reveals that the "exceptional" freedom to reinvent oneself is actually a repetition of the very European patterns of inherited status and exclusion that exceptionalism claims to have escaped. Focus on the green light, the confrontation at the Plaza Hotel, and the valley of ashes: in each case, say whether the scene vindicates or undermines the exceptionalist narrative, and argue for one reading rather than leaving the matter ambiguous. I want a criticism that takes both Fitzgerald's craft and this conceptual architecture seriously—do not reduce the novel to an illustration, and do not distort exceptionalism to fit the plot. End by stating what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about exceptionalism that abstract argument alone cannot supply. Take a definite position throughout, with clear reasons for each judgment. Please do not provide bibliographies, citations, or quoted lines from the text; work from the plot and major scenes as they are generally known.
