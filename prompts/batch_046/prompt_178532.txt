I'm preparing a position paper for a philosophy of social science course and want an audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. Here is where I stand: (1) I accept Lewis's analysis—conventions are arbitrary regularities that solve coordination problems through mutual expectation and common knowledge. (2) But in actual large societies, I think people maintain norms like dress codes or queueing through layered, approximate beliefs rather than full common knowledge, so Lewis's requirement is an unrealistic idealization. (3) I also think that when the original coordination problem vanishes, a convention can leave behind normative residue: it keeps binding people even though there's no longer anything to coordinate. (4) At the same time, I hold that arbitrariness means rational interchangeability—if two regularities solve the same problem equally well, agents have no intrinsic reason to prefer one. (5) Despite that, I think we should preserve conventions with normative residue partly because they are embedded in our shared history and identity, not merely because they solve current problems. (6) Finally, I believe that explaining a convention's evolutionary or game-theoretic origin doesn't undermine its authority—knowing how queueing evolved doesn't make skipping it rational. Do these cohere? Specifically, is the conflict between (1) and (2) fatal, or can Lewis's framework accommodate approximate belief? Is there a hidden contradiction between (3) and (4)—can something be both arbitrarily replaceable and worthy of preservation for historical reasons? And is the tension I feel between (5) and (6) only apparent, or does explaining origin actually undercut the historical-value argument? Please rank the real tensions by severity, indicate the least costly revision in each case, and write your response in American English.
