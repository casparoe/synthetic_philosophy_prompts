hey, long time lurker here. been chewing on this weird argument about epiphenomenalism and phenomenal judgment and i think it proves way too much, but i keep going back and forth on which step is the gotcha. maybe yall can spot the leak?

assume epiphenomenalism is true: phenomenal states (like feeling pain) are real but causally inert shadows cast by brain processes. they don't do anything.

1. if my saying "i'm in pain" is a genuine report of my phenomenal state, then that state should play some causal role in producing the report. otherwise i'm just emitting noise that happens to line up with B.

2. but under epiphenomenalism the phenomenal state plays zero causal role. brain state B causes both the pain and the utterance, so the utterance is caused by B, not by the pain.

3. that seems okay at first - correlation via common cause. but now think about calibration. a reporting mechanism that tracks B tracks B, not the pain. the pain is just along for the ride, like smoke with no fire feedback.

4. so when i form the belief "i have phenomenal state P," that belief is caused by B (or some physical process), not by P itself. the belief is about P only in the weak sense that P correlates with B.

5. apply this symetrically to other minds. when i judge "sarah is in pain," that judgment is caused by sarah's behavior and brain states, not by her phenomenal state (which i can't causally access).

6. so in both first-person and third-person cases, our phenomenal judgments are physically caused judgments about brain-state correlates. the phenomenal states themselves never enter the causal chain of belief formation.

7. here's where it gets weird. if phenomenal states never causally influence any belief or report, then there's no functional or epistemic difference between a world where they exist (as epiphenomenal shadows) and a world where they don't exist but the brain states are identical. by any operational criterion, the phenomenal states are undetectable and unnecessary to explain our judgments.

8. but if they're undetectable and unnecessary, then we have no reason to posit them. the rational move is eliminativism about qualia.

9. so epiphenomenalism collapses into eliminativism: it starts by saying qualia exist but don't cause anything, then shows that they can't be known or referred to in any causal/explanatory way, which undermines the very basis for believing in them.

10. therefore either epiphenomenalism is false, or we should be eliminativists. but the epiphenomenalist wants to have their cake and eat it - they want to say "qualia are real but inert." this argument says you can't have that, because "real but causally isolated from all belief" is indistinguishable from "not part of our ontology."

i feel like step 3 or step 7 is where i'm cheating, maybe smuggling in verificationism or sliding from "can't access causally" to "can't know." if i keep step 1 i'm stuck with either rejecting epiphenomenalism or becoming an eliminativist, and i'm not ready for either. which step should i drop, and what's the cost?

also, is the calibration point in step 3 just a restatement of the standard paradox with extra jargon? i think maybe that's it, but i'm fuzzy.

any thoughts? especially interested in whether there's a hidden modal slide between "no causal access" and "no epistemic access" that i'm missing.
