I have spent the better part of my career as an experimental physicist working on condensed-matter systems, and since retiring I have been trying to work through some epistemology texts with the same patience I once reserved for calibrating instruments. I find myself stuck on what philosophers call the bootstrapping problem and the associated worry about "easy knowledge," and I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to dissolve the boundary between warranted empirical belief and arbitrarily constructed belief systems.

Here is the chain as I understand it. (1) If a belief B is produced by a cognitive source S, then B counts as knowledge only if there is an independent warrant for S's reliability—otherwise B is merely "easy knowledge," acquired without the proper epistemic labor. (2) For any non-basic belief, this warrant can come from another belief B' produced by a different source S', but eventually all justification terminates in basic beliefs produced by fundamental sources: perception, introspection, or rational intuition. (3) These fundamental sources cannot be warranted by other sources without circularity or infinite regress; therefore, if they are to be warranted at all, they must be self-certifying—that is, their own outputs must serve as the evidence for their reliability. (4) But if self-certification is legitimate, then it is not restricted to veridical sources: a systematically deceptive source that is internally consistent—say, a sustained virtual-reality interface or a rigorously maintained conspiratorial framework—can also generate outputs that certify the source that produced them. (5) Consequently, there is no non-circular criterion that distinguishes a legitimate self-certifying perceptual system from an illegitimate self-certifying deceptive system. (6) Therefore, either we deny that basic sources are ever warranted (which yields global skepticism about empirical knowledge), or we accept that any internally consistent belief-generating system produces knowledge. (7) Since the latter is absurd—it would make "easy knowledge" universal and indiscriminate—the argument seems to push us toward skepticism, which I take to be the reductio.

What I want is a precise diagnosis: which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I am reluctant to abandon (1), because it captures the intuition that knowledge requires more than accidentally true belief. I am also reluctant to abandon (3), since the regress argument seems structurally sound, much like an infinite loop in a control circuit that must terminate somewhere. That leaves (4) or (5) as likely candidates, but I cannot see whether the flaw is an equivocation between epistemic self-certification and mere logical self-consistency, or whether there is a hidden modal slide in the move from "can be self-certifying" to "all self-certifying systems are on a par." If you can identify the exact point of failure and explain what alternative position—perhaps a form of modest foundationalism, or a reliabilist externalism that abandons the internalist demand in (1)—one is committed to by repairing it, I would be most grateful. I am not asking for a bibliography; I want the conceptual architecture mapped with the same clarity one uses when debugging a system that refuses to converge.
