Act as a philosopher of science specializing in epistemic demarcation, not as an encyclopedia summarizer. Before constructing anything, briefly brainstorm the logical space of stances one might take toward pseudohistorical claims—focus on the criteria that actually separate legitimate revisionism from pseudohistory, not on famous names or standard lists. Then design a decision tree of six to eight nodes that sorts positions on how to demarcate pseudohistory from acceptable historical revision, using concrete cases such as Atlantis narratives, ancient-astronaut claims, and chronologically revisionist schemes like Fomenko’s New Chronology. The root node should ask which filter applies first when mainstream chronology is contradicted. Each subsequent branch must pose a concrete diagnostic question that a respondent can answer without knowing the philosophical labels—consider whether predictive novelty in archaeology is required, whether peer review is necessary but insufficient, whether explanatory coherence with material culture is mandatory, whether falsifiability is demanded, or whether institutional consensus can ever be overridden by a single anomalous dataset. The tree must terminate in at least these leaves: strict methodological naturalism, open epistemic revisionism, radical institutional skepticism, and a deflationary view that denies any sharp boundary. For each leaf, state its most awkward commitment in one sentence. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate—especially whether open revisionism and radical skepticism collapse onto the same judgment in the Fomenko-style case. Identify any empty combinations in the logical space (for example, a stance that requires peer review yet rejects all correlation with archaeological material culture) and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of scholars. Keep the framing concrete and avoid asking the respondent to name-drop specific theories.
