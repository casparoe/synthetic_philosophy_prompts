I'm a high school student in an advanced ethics seminar, and after working through the antinatalist argument built around Benatar's asymmetry—roughly, that the absence of pain is good even when there is no one to enjoy that absence, while the absence of pleasure is not bad in the corresponding way—I've adopted a stricter version of the view. Specifically, I hold that we should not bring a being into existence if its life would contain any unavoidable suffering whatsoever, even if the life is overwhelmingly good overall and the being would rationally affirm it. My reasoning is that non-existence delivers a genuine good (the absence of that suffering) with no corresponding cost (since there is no subject deprived of the pleasures), so creating the being imposes a moral debt that net welfare cannot cancel. This strikes me as a consistent extension of the asymmetry, but my classmates think I've pushed it into implausibility.

I'd like an honest assessment of what would actually count against this position—not just the standard reply that people are glad to be alive, but which specific arguments, formal results, intuitive cases, or conceptual distinctions would expose it. For instance, does the view depend on treating potential goods and potential harms as commensurable in a way that collapses once we distinguish between deprivation and mere absence? Would a convincing account of "grateful existence"—where a subject with severe but unavoidable suffering still judges their life worth living and claims that missing those goods would have been a loss—function as a decisive defeater or merely an awkward one? I also want to know what defenders of the core asymmetry, not just critics, would themselves concede as counting against my stricter extension: perhaps that it eliminates too many permissible lives, or that it cannot account for obligations to existing beings without contradiction. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say whether my version risks being qualified into unfalsifiability (for example, by redefining "unavoidable" suffering so narrowly that no real life qualifies), and assess whether that insulation is a genuine problem for a normative view of this kind. Finally, apply your analysis to one case I keep returning to: a hypothetical being whose life contains one moment of unavoidable agony but otherwise achieves extraordinary creative and relational goods—does my view correctly condemn its creation, and if so, is that result a virtue or a reductio?
