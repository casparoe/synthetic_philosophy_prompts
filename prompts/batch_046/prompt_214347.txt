As a retired experimental biologist who has spent the last several years working through philosophy of mind and personal identity, I have become preoccupied with a particular variant of the mind-uploading problem that standard treatments rarely examine with sufficient rigor: not the instantaneous scan-and-copy scenario, but the gradual, in vivo replacement of biological neural tissue with synthetic analogues whose signal-propagation latencies and synaptic plasticity rules differ subtly from the original substrate. If such replacement proceeds region by region, with temporal gaps between interventions and with no guarantee that the synthetic modules synchronize into a unified phenomenal field in the same manner as biological tissue, the familiar debates about psychological continuity and survival fracture into finer-grained questions that are seldom ranked against one another. I am trying to determine where my own limited research energy—perhaps a single sustained philosophical project—would be best directed, and I want a defended agenda rather than an open-ended survey.

In this specific context, which unresolved problems deserve the highest priority? I am particularly interested in whether the upstream crux is metaphysical—whether diachronic personal identity can survive discontinuous substrate substitution when functional integration is restored only asymptotically—or whether it is conceptual and empirically tractable: for example, whether we could establish behavioral or first-person-report continuity criteria for partially prosthetic brains without presupposing a unified Cartesian theater. There are also downstream practical implications—legal personhood across replacement stages, the ethics of discontinuing biological maintenance once synthetic coverage exceeds some functional threshold, and whether gradual replacement undermines the moral urgency usually assigned to one-shot uploading—but I suspect several of these implications are overrated because they tacitly assume answers to the theoretical questions that may remain unsettled. Could you provide a dependency-aware prioritization: which questions are genuinely upstream of the others, which are addressable with current conceptual tools versus those that await neuroscience we do not yet possess, and which practical implications actually shift if the core theoretical issue is resolved one way rather than another? I want to understand what makes a question worth years of sustained work, not merely that it is unresolved, and I want the ordering defended with explicit criteria—tractability, neglectedness, and the breadth of downstream questions that depend on its resolution—rather than presented as an equally weighted list.
