I'm writing a feature on how modern legal institutions implicitly engineer moral emotions, and I want to understand what philosophy is buried in the design. The practice is the institutional divergence between carceral systems that treat guilt as the primary emotional currency—isolating offenders, assigning individual culpability through procedural due process—and restorative or community-justice frameworks that treat shame and its repair as the central transaction, using public acknowledgment, collective witnessing, and reintegration rituals rather than private punishment. What theory of guilt and shame does that divergence presuppose? Specifically, does it require the view that these emotions are culturally selectable modules—so that a society can institutionalize one as dominant without the other being ontologically prior—or does it instead presuppose that both are always present but weighted differently according to social structure? Reconstruct the implicit anthropology: does the practice assume that guilt is tied to autonomous agency and therefore suits liberal legal subjects, while shame is tied to relational identity and suits embedded ones? And can a single institution coherently operate both registers—say, assigning legal guilt for culpability while orchestrating social shame for reintegration—without philosophical contradiction? Say also whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a universalist about moral affect, who holds that all cultures share the same emotional architecture, defend this institutional split on purely pragmatic grounds? Please identify where your reasoning is most vulnerable, and specify what finding—perhaps cross-cultural psychological data showing the inseparability of guilt and shame, or a philosophical argument that shame necessarily presupposes internalized guilt—would lead you to reject the reconstructed view.
