me and my roommate were arguing about Leibniz's best of all possible worlds thing after our phil 101 lecture. she (call her Nina) said its basically unfalsifiable: if this world is the best, then any suffering we see is automatically necessary for the greater good, so there's no observation that could ever count against it. i said that misses the point — Leibniz doesnt deny that suffering is real, he just thinks its the unavoidable price of having a world with this much variety and this little law. like, you cant have free will and moral growth without the risk of bad choices, so God isnt being cruel, he's just optimizing over constraints.

Nina came back with this weird idea she called world-splicing — she said if possible worlds are just maximally consistent sets of propositions, then God should be able to graft the cancer-free biology from some other consistent world onto ours without breaking anything, which'd make ours strictly better. so either God botched the splice, or worlds arent really spliceable. i told her thats treating worlds like lego blocks, but Leibniz says each world is a complete causal chain tied together by sufficient reason; you pull out one disease and every event that depended on it collapses, so you dont get our world but healthier, you get a totally different world.

she then said okay, but that just pushes the problem back: if worlds are complete packages, then God just picked the package with the least suffering that was still maximally diverse? and if not, why does completeness excuse the suffering in this specific package? i think shes mixing up metaphysical completeness with moral justification, but im not totally sure. can you adjudicate this move by move? rate each of the four moves — (1) Nina's unfalsifiability objection, (2) my variety-vs-law response, (3) her world-splicing challenge, (4) my lego-block reply — from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. also tell us what, if anything, both of us are missing about how Leibniz actually thinks about evil and compossibility, especially whether world-splicing makes any sense against his view of possible worlds.
