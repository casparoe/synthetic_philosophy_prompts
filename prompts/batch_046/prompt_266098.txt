I'm working through the democratic boundary problem—specifically, who belongs to the demos when collective decisions produce transnational, intergenerational, or ecologically diffuse effects. The literature since the late twentieth century has cycled through the all-affected-interests principle, the all-subjected-to-coercion principle, and various stakeholder accounts, yet in recent seminars I've noticed an exhausting pattern: every participant maps the same hard cases differently—climate refugees, future generations, nonhuman animals affected by policy, citizens of states that suffer externalities—everyone agrees that arbitrary exclusion is unjust, and still the positions do not move. I want a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled rather than a survey of the views. Is the disagreement substantive, or is it verbal—perhaps different camps are answering different questions under the single heading of "membership," with one side asking about legitimate authority and the other asking about fair representation? Or is there a hidden empirical dispute about causal influence being disguised as normative disagreement?

To help you engage the structure concretely, consider the actual moves: the affected-interests camp tends to treat causal impact as sufficient for inclusion, while the subjected-to-law camp treats institutional authorization as necessary, and stakeholder theories try to split the difference by weighting stakes. Given that, please diagnose whether the impasse is a conflict over facts, over standards of evidence, over which cases count as data, or over bedrock intuitions about political subjecthood that neither side can argue for. I also want you to evaluate a conceptual distinction I've been considering—separating prospective membership, meaning who must be consulted before a decision is made, from retrospective authorization, meaning who can hold decision-makers accountable afterward—and tell me whether adopting that distinction dissolves the disagreement, merely relocates it, or reveals that "the demos" is not a single kind of thing at all. Finally, say honestly what, if anything, would count as progress: a new normative principle, a formal model of representation, a conceptual clarification, or an argument that no resolution should be expected.
