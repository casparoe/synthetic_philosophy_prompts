I have been reporting on the prolonged stalemate in how medicine and law define death. For decades, clinicians and philosophers have divided over whether brain death—the irreversible loss of all brainstem and cortical function—should remain the gold standard, or whether circulatory death ought to be restored, especially now that extracorporeal support can maintain respiration and circulation long after consciousness is lost. The clinical facts are largely agreed upon: flat EEG, absent brainstem reflexes, and, in some cases, persistent homeostatic and hormonal activity within a brain-dead body. Yet one camp insists that the organism as an integrated whole has ceased to exist, while another—drawing on recent philosophical biology—maintains that homeostatic integration continues and that these patients are profoundly injured but still living. A smaller, more radical strand suggests that neither biological criterion captures what is really at stake: that death is assigned, not discovered, serving as a normative boundary that licences organ retrieval and ends therapeutic obligations. Given this shared empirical ground, why has the dispute barely moved since the 1980s? Is it a concealed empirical question about biological integration, a conceptual disagreement over what counts as an organism, a normative conflict disguised as metaphysics, or an irreducible clash of intuitions about human dignity? And, depending on your diagnosis, what—if anything—would actually shift it: a refined concept of integration, a revealing neuroimaging result, or an explicit acknowledgment that the debate is institutional rather than scientific?
