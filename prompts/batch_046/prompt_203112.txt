I am a retired systems biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of medicine and bioethics. I have drafted the outline and central argument for a short essay, and I should be grateful for your comments on its conceptual coherence, its philosophical rigour, and whether the scientific analogy I employ is illuminating or misleading.

The essay is tentatively titled 'Death as the Collapse of Homeorhesis: Beyond Brain and Circulatory Criteria'. Its thesis is that both the brain-death and circulatory-death criteria mistake a particular symptom of organismal dissolution for its defining feature. Drawing on my former work in physiological dynamics, I propose that an organism is not primarily a static structure but a self-sustaining flow of energy and information—what the developmental biologist C. H. Waddington called homeorhesis: the maintenance of a dynamic trajectory rather than a fixed equilibrium. On this view, death occurs not when the brain ceases to integrate functions, nor when the heart stops circulating blood, but when the organism irreversibly loses the capacity to maintain its homeorhetic trajectory: that is, when the coupled metabolic, immunological, and neural subsystems can no longer correct deviations and restore the flow-state, regardless of which particular subsystem fails first.

The essay then examines three objections: (1) that this criterion is too vague for clinical practice; (2) that it collapses into the brain-death view once we identify the brain as the chief regulator of homeorhesis; and (3) that it unjustifiably privileges 'process' over 'substance' in defining a biological entity.

Please comment particularly on the following. First, whether the distinction between homeostasis and homeorhesis is doing genuine philosophical work here, or whether it is merely a redescription of integrative function. Second, whether my framing unfairly caricatures the brain-death position as 'localisationist'. Third, whether there is a hidden normative slide in my claim that 'irreversible loss of flow-maintenance' should determine the moral and legal moment of death.

I should emphasise that I am writing for an audience of philosophers without specialist biological training, so observations on clarity and accessibility would also be welcome. Please reply in British English.
