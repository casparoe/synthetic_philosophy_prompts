After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether democratic institutions can ever genuinely express a collective will, I started paying close attention to the decision rules in my own academic department. For routine budget allocations, we vote by simple majority, and nobody seems troubled if the result depends on the ordering of motions or if a different agenda might have yielded a different winner. But for any decision involving hiring, curriculum revision, or tenure, we switch to an entirely different regime: we are explicitly forbidden from ranking candidates or proposals on any numerical scale, we may only register binary objections or support relative to the status quo, and the chair treats any remaining disagreement not as a conflict of preferences to be aggregated but as evidence that we have not yet found the correct framing. If consensus is not reached after two rounds of revision, the matter is tabled indefinitely rather than put to a vote. My friend insists this is merely Arrow's impossibility theorem disguised as professional etiquette—by restricting the domain to unanimous binary comparisons and suppressing transitive preference revelation, we have not solved the problem of collective rationality but only made it invisible. I think something more philosophically substantial is at work. What theory of collective judgment does this dual-track institution actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that deliberation transforms fixed preferences into shared reasons in a way that renders Arrow's conditions inapplicable, or does it rely on a covert distinction between preference aggregation and reasoned consensus that collapses under scrutiny? Is the practice coherent, or does the routine-majority track quietly depend on a probabilistic view of social choice that the consensus track explicitly rejects? And if we replaced both regimes with a single uniform rule—say, every member assigns cardinal weights to outcomes and we compute a social welfare function—what exactly about our institutional self-understanding would have to be abandoned?
