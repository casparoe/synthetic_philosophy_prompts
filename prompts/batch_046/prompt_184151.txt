Here is an argument that seems to show that extending rational choice to Pasadena-style gambles—those whose payoffs scale so that expectation diverges in both directions and is therefore undefined—inevitably paralyzes decision-making.

(1) Standard expected-utility theory requires a precise expectation; if the integral diverges positively and negatively, expectation is undefined and the theory offers no recommendation.
(2) To avoid this silence, extend the framework with an imprecise-probability rule: assign any such gamble an expectation-interval of (−∞, +∞), representing total agnosticism about comparative value.
(3) On this extension, a gamble with interval I is rationally preferable to an option of definite value v only if every member of I exceeds v; worse only if every member is below v; otherwise they are incomparable.
(4) For I = (−∞, +∞), a Pasadena-style gamble is therefore incomparable with every definite-value option, including the status quo of value 0.
(5) Now consider a forced choice between two distinct Pasadena-style gambles, G and H, both carrying I = (−∞, +∞). By the rule in (3), neither is preferable to the other; they are incomparable even with each other.
(6) So the extension produces a complete breakdown of rational ordering whenever a decision problem contains more than one undefined-expectation option.
(7) But this is intolerable: even without a defined expectation, rational agents can distinguish options by symmetry, statewise dominance, or risk posture, so choice should not be paralyzed.
(8) Hence the imprecise-probability extension must be rejected.
(9) The failure traces back to (3): treating total divergence as total indeterminacy ignores non-expectational bases for comparison.
(10) Yet if we reject (3), we still face problem (1): without expectation and now without the interval rule, how should rational choice proceed in these cases?
