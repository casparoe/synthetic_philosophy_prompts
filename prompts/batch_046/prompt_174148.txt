A comparative-politics essay I recently read summarizes the anthropological critique of global anti-corruption norms in roughly this way: practices commonly labeled corruption—clientelism, gift-giving to officials, and the use of personal networks like guanxi or wasta—are not universal moral failures but are the standard infrastructure of social cooperation in many societies. Western legal frameworks misclassify them because they treat all personal obligation in public office as a betrayal of impartial rules, failing to see that corruption is itself a culturally specific concept rooted in the separation of public and private spheres that emerged in particular Western institutional histories. The essay argues that since diffuse reciprocity and long-term patronage obligations exist everywhere—including in Western lobbying and campaign finance—the distinction between legitimate social exchange and actual corruption is purely ethnocentric. It concludes that international anti-corruption campaigns therefore do not enforce universal ethics but instead impose a local Western institutional form, damaging the social cohesion that relational governance provides.

Something about this strikes me as too sweeping, but I am not sure which parts are standard anthropology and which are overreach. Is this summary fair to the actual anthropological and comparative-political literature? Please audit it carefully: identify which claims are well-supported, which are common simplifications, and which are misrepresentations—particularly any conflation between social reciprocity and asymmetric political dependency, or between the observation that norms vary and the claim that no cross-cultural distinction is possible. Also, does the essay's conclusion—that anti-corruption efforts are merely cultural imperialism—actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does it depend on the misrepresentations?
