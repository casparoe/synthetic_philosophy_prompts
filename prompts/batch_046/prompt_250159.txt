I am preparing a graduate seminar on comparative moral philosophy and have been discussing with a colleague whether Buddhist ethical frameworks can be genuinely non-consequentialist in a structurally interesting way, rather than merely appearing so because of hidden long-term welfare calculations disguised as karmic law. To that end, I should like you to construct a carefully engineered thought experiment — confined to one paragraph and set in a realistic clinical or workplace environment, with no appeal to trolley-style contrivances, exotic physics, omniscient predictors, or science-fictional devices — in which a practitioner guided by the Buddhist concepts of cetanā (intentional direction), the first precept against destroying life, and upāya (skillful means) is compelled to choose an action that, according to a straightforward act-consequentialist calculus, produces strictly greater aggregate suffering within every evaluable horizon and offers no compensatory welfare gains. The consequentialist, by contrast, should select the apparently more compassionate option that the Buddhist practitioner must reject.

Please then demonstrate that the scenario meets its design specifications: state the decision at stake, identify the principle or method each framework employs, specify the relevant counterfactual worlds, and explain precisely why the two theories deliver opposing verdicts. Isolate the feature doing the work — namely, that Buddhist evaluation weights the karmic quality of the agent’s root mental state (whether greed, hatred, or delusion is present) independently of the outcome vector, whilst consequentialism collapses all evaluation into that vector alone. Note honestly what happens if this feature is removed: does the case collapse into a standard welfare-maximisation problem? Finally, include how a sophisticated consequentialist defender might attack the case — for instance, by arguing that the alleged non-transferability of merit and the opacity of future rebirth effects are merely epistemic limitations disguised as metaphysical constraints — and respond briefly to that objection from within the Buddhist framework you have constructed. I should be grateful if your answer were composed in British English, with formal precision and without reference to bibliographies, citation lists, or external reading lists.
