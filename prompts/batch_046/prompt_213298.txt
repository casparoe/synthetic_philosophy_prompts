I am preparing a philosophical dialogue for an advanced seminar on cross-cultural pragmatics and moral philosophy. The scene is an informal colloquium in which two equally competent scholars—Dr. Lena Voss and Dr. Kenji Tanaka—debate whether white lies and indirect speech are ever morally permissible, using Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness as contrasting reference points rather than caricatures. Voss advances a view she calls radical epistemic transparency: she argues that Dutch-style bluntness respects rational autonomy by refusing to shift the cognitive burden of interpretation onto the listener, and she introduces the notion of an interpretive tax—the hidden labor required to decode face-saving ambiguity—which she claims becomes a form of epistemic injustice when imposed without the listener's consent. Tanaka, in turn, develops a framework he labels interactional sovereignty, contending that Japanese practices of honne and tatemae are not deceptive but protective mechanisms that preserve an interlocutor's plausible deniability and thereby safeguard their social and psychological autonomy against unwanted intrusion.

Their exchange should center on a concrete case—imagine a junior researcher asking a senior mentor whether a poorly argued draft is worth submitting—so that they must apply their principles to a situation in which direct criticism risks professional harm while indirect reassurance risks misleading belief. Write the dialogue with genuine back-and-forth: Voss should press Tanaka on whether preserving face can ever justify allowing the listener to form an actually false belief about the quality of their work, and Tanaka should counter that Voss's transparency ignores coercive power dynamics that make direct refusal unsafe for subordinates to receive. Neither interlocutor should be a strawman; each should concede a minor point and then sharpen the objection, ensuring that they respond directly to each other's arguments rather than speaking past one another.
