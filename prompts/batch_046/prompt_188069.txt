Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on the philosophy of pain, and I now find it suspicious because the conclusion undermines the special urgency we assign to analgesia. (1) In pain asymbolia, patients report intact sensory discrimination of noxious stimuli—localization, intensity scaling, and sincere use of the word "pain"—yet they deny that the sensation is unpleasant or worth avoiding. (2) If pain is intrinsically bad, its badness must be carried by its sensory-phenomenal character, which is present in these patients. (3) So if these patients genuinely feel pain but do not register any badness, then pain's badness is not intrinsic to its sensory character but depends on an additional affective-motivational component. (4) The patients do genuinely feel pain, not merely detect tissue damage without phenomenal pain, because their reports and behavioral discrimination match those of normal subjects except for the missing aversive response. (5) Therefore pain is not intrinsically bad; its badness is extrinsic, conferred by a separable affective state that could in principle attach to itches, tickles, or neutral tactile stimuli. (6) If badness is thus separable and transferable, there is no principled reason to treat severe pain with greater moral or clinical urgency than equally intense aversive itch or dysphoric tickle. (7) That is clearly wrong: analgesia has a distinctive priority in medicine and ethics. (8) Hence the argument must fail somewhere between (2) and (6). Where does it go wrong? If I must choose, I prefer to preserve (1) and (4) and, if possible, (2) rather than accepting (5), but I want to understand what giving up each of them costs—particularly whether saving (2) forces me into representationalism about pain's affect, or whether there is a hidden scope shift in the move from (3) to (5).
