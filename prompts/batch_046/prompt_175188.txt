In tech-adjacent rationalist circles, people often defend prompt-based image generation with a photography analogy: the diffusion model is the camera, the prompt and seed are the framing and exposure choices, the sampling steps are darkroom development, and the user's curation and inpainting are the photographer's selection and retouching. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that the human remains the artistic author and the model is merely an instrument, so objections that the machine makes the art miss the point in the same way that saying the camera makes the painting misses photography's artistic status. I want to audit how far this holds. Give me an explicit correspondence table — mapping the model architecture, the latent-space sampling, the training corpus, the prompt engineering, and the post-generation selection onto their photographic counterparts — then identify the disanalogies that actually threaten the authorship claim (for instance, that the model synthesizes pixels from a statistical distribution over other artists' works rather than capturing photons from a specific scene) versus the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that the prompt engineer is the artist in the same normative sense as a photographer survives once those breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along by selectively importing only the instrumental parts of photography.
