I am not a professional philosopher, but I have been reading on my own about decision theory and a family of puzzles that ask whether rational agents can coordinate purely by reasoning about one another without any causal connection. The first is the Platonia dilemma, as I understand it: two agents who are perfect logical duplicates are isolated in separate rooms and must each choose either to cooperate or to defect, knowing that because their reasoning is identical they will necessarily reach the same conclusion, with payoffs that reward mutual cooperation but tempt unilateral defection. The second is the Luring Lottery, which I understand as a situation in which an agent faces a reward for refraining from an action, yet the reward structure contains a hidden incentive that makes entering seem rational if one correctly predicts one's own reasoning, producing a kind of self-defeating loop.

I would like you to design a single classroom activity for a community workshop I facilitate, with about ten adults who have no formal background in philosophy or game theory, meeting for one session of seventy-five minutes. The goal is not to lecture them about these puzzles but to make them feel the tension themselves before any name is given. I want a two-round game with real, if small, stakes in the form of points that can be exchanged for a simple token prize at the end. In round one, participants are paired and separated into different spaces; each receives a sealed envelope explaining that they are logical twins in Platonia and must write down either trust or betray, with points awarded according to a symmetric payoff scheme—ten points each for mutual trust, three points each for mutual betrayal, and zero versus fifteen for the mismatched case—though the instructions should ask them to imagine that perfect reasoning identity makes mismatches impossible, even though in practice human divergence will likely produce them. They should not be told the name of the dilemma until after they have submitted their choices and seen the results.

In round two, the same pairs reunite in one room, but each pair receives a different sealed envelope containing one of two hidden rule-sets assigned randomly and not revealed to the other pairs. One rule-set repeats the Platonia conditions exactly; the other introduces the Luring Lottery conditions, in which each individual decides whether to enter or refrain, with points that reward mutual refraining but include a hidden bonus for entering that depends on predicting the partner's choice. Crucially, the participants are told only that one of these two conditions applies to their pair, not which one. They are given five minutes to discuss, then must each submit an individual written choice without further communication. The ambiguity between reasoning about logical symmetry and reasoning about causal prediction should create a palpable strategic confusion.
