I've been noticing a recurring failure mode in long-range consequentialist reasoning that feels distinct from the standard problem of cluelessness, though I can't tell whether it already has a name. The pattern runs like this. You begin with an intervention—say, a funding commitment or a regulatory framework—whose effects over centuries are genuinely unpredictable. Recognizing this, you try to reduce your cluelessness by gathering more data, running models, or deferring to expert forecasts. But the very act of gathering that evidence requires locking in particular research agendas, institutional structures, or measurement frameworks, each of which shapes the distant future you're trying to evaluate. Then a second layer appears: the criteria you use to judge whether you have "successfully reduced cluelessness"—predictive calibration, value coherence, robustness across scenarios—are themselves artifacts of the present institutional and epistemic conditions that the intervention will alter. So you are not merely ignorant of outcomes; you face a kind of reflective instability in which the framework for assessing whether ignorance has diminished is itself destabilized by the attempt to diminish it. I have seen analogues in debates over AI governance, where more forecasting effort is accused of baking in current assumptions, and in global health prioritization, where building better models of far-future welfare is said to depend on normative premises that the modeling process itself might change. Is there an established concept for this specific configuration—the erosion of the evaluative framework under attempts to resolve long-range uncertainty? If so, how is it precisely bounded away from ordinary consequentialist cluelessness, from Knightian uncertainty about unknown unknowns, and from the problem of adaptive or endogenous preferences? If not, what would be the right way to carve it out, and what would be its clearest boundary cases?
