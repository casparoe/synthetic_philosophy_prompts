Design a 70-minute simulation for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 16 students on the moral status of digital minds. The goal is to make participants feel—before any facilitator names it—the pull of granting moral standing not to present phenomenal consciousness but to diachronic normative coherence: the unbroken continuity of an entity’s evaluative commitments across substrate migration, dormancy, branching, and reconstruction from corrupted backups. Invent a specific, somewhat niche framing for this criterion (for example, “normative trajectory locking” or “intermittent moral personhood”) and embed it in the rules and case descriptions rather than in a lecture.

Deliver a runnable activity with: (1) hidden-information cards and committee roles that force groups to allocate two scarce “preservation slots” among four detailed digital-entity profiles engineered to split intuition between present subjective welfare and historical coherence; (2) a non-monetary payoff scheme using symbolic tokens or small collective stakes, with time-pressured decision rules that prevent easy consensus; (3) a debrief that maps specific moments of conflict or surprise in the simulation onto the conceptual distinction between phenomenal-presence and trajectory-based status; (4) an honest prediction of what will misfire the first run (for instance, students defaulting to human-analogy or consciousness-first reasoning) and how to handle it; and (5) a frank admission of which feature of the idea the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion afterward. Do not ask for bibliographies, named theorists, or citations; the model should construct the framing and cases from the conceptual structure alone.
