In an epistemic-game-theory seminar, a colleague ended a debate about climate-model disagreement by saying that if two people are both Bayesian and rational, they cannot agree to disagree—so any persistent divergence means at least one party is irrational. I suspect this compresses several different claims into one dismissive line. Can you separate the precise theses hiding in that slogan? In particular, distinguish: (i) Aumann's result that agents with a genuinely common prior over a finite partition and common knowledge of each other's posteriors must converge; (ii) the stronger normative claim that rationality requires adopting identical priors ex ante regardless of differing evidence or modeling frameworks; (iii) the false generalization that the theorem applies unchanged to beliefs over continuous or non-measurable parameter spaces, such as those in climate projections; and (iv) the meta-claim that disagreement about complex empirical models is always reducible to prior divergence rather than to differences in auxiliary assumptions or computational constraints. For each reading, say whether it is a defensible interpretation of the agreement theorem, whether it is false, and—crucially—whether it actually licenses my colleague's conclusion that one of us must be irrational. Also clarify whether the slogan is invoking common knowledge of rationality, shared evidence, or both, and indicate which of those conditions are actually satisfied in a dispute over climate models.
