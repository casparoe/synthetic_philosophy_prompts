In a recent graduate seminar on the ethics of moral demandingness, a colleague sought to deflate arguments that affluent individuals are obligated to make severe personal sacrifices—say, donating a kidney to a stranger or contributing half of one's income to effective charities—by offering the one-liner: "You don't have to be a saint to be decent." The remark was meant to do heavy argumentative work: it seemed to establish both that there is a stable, low-cost threshold of moral decency and that any demand exceeding that threshold falls outside the domain of obligation altogether. I am asking for a precise disambiguation of what that slogan could actually be claiming, laid out as a menu rather than a single interpretation.

Please distinguish at least the following dimensions of ambiguity: (i) whether the claim is descriptive (about how people actually judge decency) or normative (about what morality actually requires); (ii) whether "decent" marks a binary threshold or a point on a scalar continuum of moral evaluation, and whether "saint" denotes a categorical kind or merely the extreme end of that scale; (iii) whether the slogan asserts a psychological feasibility constraint—people cannot reliably act heroically, so morality should not ask—or a normative permission—morality permits people to stop at decency even if they could do more; (iv) whether it relies on an agent-relative prerogative that allows individuals to weight their own projects more heavily, or instead on an agent-neutral calculation that the marginal good of heroic acts is too small to generate obligations; and (v) whether it denies that supererogatory acts exist at all, or merely denies that failure to perform them is blameworthy. For each reading, indicate whether it is a near-tautology, a substantive but defensible thesis in the literature on supererogation, or actually false—particularly where it might conflate the absence of obligation with the absence of moral reason. Then specify which of these readings would genuinely support my colleague's dismissal of demanding obligations, and which would leave those obligations intact despite allowing that one need not be heroic. Finally, consider whether the slogan risks an equivocation between "not being required to be a saint" and "not being required to bear any significant cost for strangers," and diagnose whether that slide is hidden by an implicit, perhaps role-relative, conception of decency—one that ties minimal moral adequacy to the fulfillment of socially expected duties rather than to the maximization of impartial good.
