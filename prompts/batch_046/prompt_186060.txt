I've been thinking alot lately about whether just being able to imagine something means it could actually happen in reality. I'm not in school or anything, so I just made up my own version of the idea philosophers call conceivability guiding possibility. I call it the "three-step radius" rule: if you can clearly picture a scenario, it doesn't feel contradictory, and it's within three causal steps from something that actually happens, then it's probly metaphysically possible. But if you need four or five weird links in the chain—like flying pigs caused by time-traveling robots—then your brain is just writing fiction and it tells you nothing about what's really possible. I also think the negative side is stronger: if I genuinely can't conceive of a round square even after really trying, that's better evidence it's impossible than me being able to conceive of flying pigs is evidence they could exist.

What would actually count against this view? Don't just hit me with the standard water-is-H2O thing, because I think that fits inside my radius anyway. I want to know what specific cases or evidence would break the three-step rule. Are there scenarios that seem like only two steps away but actually hide contradictions I can't see? What about social or legal things—like could I conceive of a society with no concept of property, and does that being conceivable mean it's genuinely possible in the same metaphysical way? And if cognitive science showed people can't reliably track causal steps in imagination, is that fatal or just awkward? Rank the defeaters from ones that kill the view outright to ones that just make it look a little silly, and be honest about whether my radius idea has been qualified into unfalsifiability—since whenever someone finds a counterexample I could just say "oh that was step four." Is that insulation a problem for this kind of idea, or is it fine? Also tell me what a defender of my position (maybe me in five years) would actually concede counts against them, versus what they'd just brush off as missing the point.
