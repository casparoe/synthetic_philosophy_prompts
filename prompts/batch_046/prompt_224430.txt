In discussions of whether maximal moral goodness is desirable, a recurring analogy treats the moral saint as an Olympic athlete: both pursue peak performance in a single domain, both require extreme sacrifice of other goods—leisure, personal projects, intimate relationships—and both are admired precisely because their achievement is exceptional rather than obligatory. The analogy is frequently deployed to license the conclusion that moral sainthood, like elite athletics, represents a legitimate but optional form of human excellence, and that ordinary people are not defective for falling short of it. I want you to audit how far this correspondence actually holds. Lay out the mapping explicitly—what corresponds to the training regimen, the competition, the judging panel, the medal, the spectator, and the retirement—then identify the fractures: consider whether moral demands are genuinely elective in the way athletic careers are, whether there is any meaningful “off-season” for moral obligation, whether the goods sacrificed are merely instrumental (like training hours) or partly constitutive (like the relationships that ground moral value itself), and whether treating virtue as a competitive, individually scored achievement quietly imports a picture of morality that conflicts with its cooperative or relational dimensions. In addition, examine whether an alternative analogy—perhaps moral fluency as a language one lives within rather than a sport one competes in, or virtue as mastery in a collaborative craft with plural goods—would better capture the phenomenon, and whether that replacement reinforces or undermines the conclusion that maximal moral goodness is not a desirable life-plan. Write your response in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would compose a letter.
