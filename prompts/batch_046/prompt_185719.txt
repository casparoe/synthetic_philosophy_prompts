I have no formal background in philosophy or computer science, but I have been thinking on my own about the frame problem—the puzzle of how an intelligent system knows what stays the same after it acts, without having to list every single unchanged fact. Most accounts I have encountered treat it as one uniform difficulty about physical objects and rules, yet I keep wondering whether it actually divides into two different problems: one about physical persistence (the cup remains on the table) and one about normative persistence (the rules of politeness or obligation remain in force). I would like you to construct a taxonomy of positions on the frame problem arranged as a decision tree. The root should be the basic commitment that an action can alter some facts while leaving others intact. Each subsequent branch must be labeled by a concrete diagnostic claim that separates positions—whether the core difficulty is representational (explicitly writing down what does not change) or computational (knowing which representations to update); whether the problem applies uniformly across physical and social domains or differs sharply between them; whether explicit representations of unchanged states are strictly necessary; and whether the problem can be dissolved rather than solved. The leaves must include the following named positions: the explicit frame-axiom approach, the successor-state-axiom approach, the non-monotonic or circumscription approach, the procedural or reactive-robotics approach that avoids central representations, the enactivist view that treats the problem as an artifact of representationalism, and a probabilistic or default-reasoning approach that treats persistence as a prior rather than as an axiom. Where the logical space allows combinations that none of these named views occupies, note the empty leaf and briefly diagnose whether it appears incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. In particular, flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot cleanly separate—if two approaches yield identical answers on every branch—rather than forcing them apart artificially. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; build the tree purely from the logical structure of the positions. Phrase every branch as a question that someone could answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to.
