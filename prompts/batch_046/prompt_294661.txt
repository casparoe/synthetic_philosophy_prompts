In my current novel, I am constructing a fictional jurisprudence for a polity that sits uneasily between three living traditions: the German insistence on informational self-determination, the American presumption that transparency cures corruption, and the Chinese integration of behavioural data into civic reputation. To dramatise this tension, I have invented a tribunal—the Commission for Civic Visibility—which has handed down five rulings that my characters now debate. First, it held that citizens may encrypt domestic communications even when the state retains identity registries, provided the encryption keys are not state-held. Second, it prohibited the anonymisation of financial flows in a fully digitised economy, ruling that traceability is the price of monetary stability. Third, it permitted the withholding of religious affiliation from census instruments but required disclosure of political contributions exceeding a trivial sum. Fourth, it denied citizens the right to obscure their mobility histories on public transit, accepting the state’s argument that aggregated movement patterns serve epidemiological forecasting. Fifth, it allowed artistic pseudonyms only if registered with a cultural oversight bureau, treating unregistered anonymity as a threat to public discourse.

The case now before the Commission involves a citizen who has disclosed her diagnosis of a rare autoimmune disorder to a municipal health authority, yet refuses to reveal the specific polygenic risk markers that predispose her to it. She concedes that aggregate data serves legitimate epidemiology, but she argues that granular biomarker disclosure enables the state to calibrate employment algorithms and insurance risk pools—uses she considers outside the authority’s mandate. The Commission must decide whether her refusal is consistent with its own logic, or whether it must compel disclosure.

Please advise me, in British English, as though you were helping a novelist maintain internal coherence: should the Commission uphold her refusal or reject it? Identify which of the five precedents offers the closest analog, and be candid about whether the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. If consistency points one way, name the principle being adopted; if it points the other, name that principle instead. Should the track record support both conclusions through different reasoning, please specify the further hypothetical case—perhaps involving educational psychometrics, familial genetic disclosure, or biometric gait analysis—that would best distinguish which principle the Commission is actually running. I am less interested in a definitive legal answer than in a rigorous mapping of the conceptual terrain my characters must navigate.
