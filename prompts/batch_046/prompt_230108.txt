As a retired physicist who has recently begun attending philosophy seminars, I keep encountering the proverb "great minds think alike" invoked to dismiss questions of scientific priority—most recently when a speaker applied it to both the Newton-Leibniz calculus dispute and the Darwin-Wallace episode, concluding that discovery is inevitable and that assigning credit is therefore arbitrary. I would like to take this slogan back to my discussion group with some analytical precision. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing—distinguishing psychological coincidence from structural inevitability within a problem-space, empirical convergence given shared evidence from logical determinacy of the conclusion, and perhaps other readings I have missed? For each, indicate whether it is trivial, substantively defensible, or false, and specify which version, if any, actually licenses the conclusion that the identity of the discoverer does not matter for our understanding of the science. Present the readings as a menu rather than selecting one, since my aim is to see which interpretation the speaker was actually relying on.
