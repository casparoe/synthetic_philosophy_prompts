I've been working through the brain-in-a-vat skeptical scenario, and it seems to me that semantic externalism dissolves the problem in a way epistemologists rarely take seriously. If I genuinely am a disembodied brain suspended in nutrient fluid and stimulated by a computer, then according to a causal theory of reference my token of the word "brain" cannot denote biological brains in the external world; at best it denotes some feature internal to the simulation, and at worst it is semantically empty. By the same reasoning, "vat," "computer," and "world" suffer parallel reference failures. So the very hypothesis "I am a brain in a vat" appears either false or semantically defective—something closer to a self-undermining utterance than to a coherent possibility. Given this, why do philosophers still present the scenario as a live skeptical threat rather than treating it as a linguistic paradox? Is there a hidden commitment to internalist content that lets the skeptic preserve the meaningfulness of the thought regardless of its reference conditions, and if so, does adopting that internalist framework undermine the externalist premise I'm using to generate the paradox in the first place?
