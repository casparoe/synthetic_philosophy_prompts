Write an essay defending a specific answer to the special composition question—whether parts ever compose a whole, and if so, under what conditions—while engaging with the standard trio of universalism, nihilism, and restricted composition. Rather than treating physical objects in isolation, center your argument on a cross-domain tension: consider whether the criteria that plausibly restrict composition for biological organisms (functional integration, causal unity) can be coherently applied to institutional or technological artifacts with spatially distributed and replaceable parts—such as a decentralized digital network maintained across jurisdictions—without either collapsing into universalism or generating an ad hoc multiplicity of compositional rules. You should weigh whether adopting a single, domain-neutral principle is metaphysically preferable even if it yields counterintuitive results in the artifact case, or whether a domain-relative pluralism about composition is defensible. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: either defend a unified restricted view with a principled boundary condition, defend universalism by showing that the artifact case dissolves the intuition against arbitrary sums, or defend nihilism by arguing that functional integration is always a projected pattern rather than an ontological bond. Do not end non-committally.
