I am trying to determine whether accuracy-first epistemology, taken together with epistemic utility theory, can deliver a single principle of rational credence, or whether my judgments about particular doxastic policies force me to smuggle in values other than pure accuracy. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case one: a climate modeller uses a computationally cheap linear approximation that achieves a Brier score of 0.18 across predictions, rather than running an intractable non-linear simulation that would score 0.15 but arrive too late to inform evacuation decisions. I think she is rational to use the approximation.

Case two: a sceptic maintains exactly 0.5 credence in every empirical claim in order to guarantee that she is never more than 0.5 away from the truth value. Her global Brier score is excellent, yet I judge her policy deeply irrational.

Case three: a physician assigns 0.95 credence to a common diagnosis that explains ninety per cent of a patient’s symptoms with one background assumption, rather than to a rare diagnosis that explains ninety-five per cent with three ad hoc assumptions. The common diagnosis turns out wrong. Even ex post, I feel her assignment was rational; accuracy-first evaluation seems to praise it only if we weight explanatory coherence, which looks like a non-accuracy value.

Case four: an automated forecasting system is calibrated to maximise expected global Brier-score improvement. It therefore assigns near-zero credence to catastrophic tail risks, because updating on them rarely improves the global score. I think the system is globally rational but locally reckless, and that a rational agent ought to deviate from global Brier maximisation precisely in high-stakes tail cases.

Case five: a researcher, after years of defending a thesis, receives disconfirming evidence and updates her credence from 0.85 to 0.75, but refuses to drop below 0.5 because of her sunk intellectual investment. I think she is irrational, yet the irrationality seems to lie in her resistance to evidence rather than in any failure to maximise expected accuracy.

Case six: a mathematician believes a conjecture with credence 0.6 solely because the proof strategy is elegant, despite knowing that elegance correlates weakly with truth in this domain. I think this is irrational, even though her credence might happen to be accurate.

The tension that keeps returning is between case one, where sacrificing some accuracy for practical tractability is rational, and case two, where sacrificing informativeness for accuracy is irrational; meanwhile case three makes me want to invoke explanatory coherence, and case four makes me want to allow local departures from global scoring-rule rationality that case five seems to forbid for the wrong reason. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes permissible departures from pure Brier-score maximisation (those governed by decision-theoretic stakes or computational feasibility) from impermissible ones (those driven by sunk costs or aesthetic preference)—that captures all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned, and what does the resulting principle predict for a case I have not listed: a scientist who knowingly uses a slightly miscalibrated instrument because it is the only one available, and applies a uniform shrinkage factor to all her credences to compensate, thereby lowering her expected Brier score but improving her high-stakes decision performance?
