I'm currently enrolled in a seminar on nationalism, collective memory, and the social construction of identity, where we have worked closely with Hobsbawm's account of invented traditions and Benedict Anderson's theory of imagined communities. For my final essay, I am attempting to apply both frameworks to an object that is not a nation-state: the disciplinary self-image of philosophy. My central claim is that philosophy's presentation of itself as a continuous, roughly 2,500-year conversation—organized around a stable canon running from the pre-Socratics through contemporary analytic and continental traditions—functions as an invented tradition in Hobsbawm's sense. The tradition is not fabricated ex nihilo, but it is selectively organized: present institutional needs, including departmental boundaries, funding structures, and pedagogical canons, retroactively arrange disparate historical practices into a single, apparently seamless lineage. At the same time, I want to argue that the philosophical community operates as an imagined community in Anderson's terms. Most practitioners never meet the vast majority of their contemporaries, yet they experience a sense of simultaneity and mutual belonging sustained by citation networks, shared textual rituals centered on canonical works, and periodic gatherings such as conferences and workshops that stand in for the face-to-face interaction characteristic of smaller communities.

I am aware that serious disanalogies threaten this comparison, and I want the essay to confront them directly rather than gloss over them. For Hobsbawm and Anderson, nations are bound up with territorial sovereignty, state power, and—especially in Anderson's formulation—the historical emergence of print-capitalism and vernacular language markets. Philosophy lacks territorial sovereignty; its members are not citizens with legal obligations; and its unifying medium is not a vernacular language but a highly specialized technical vocabulary that is often deliberately opaque. I also want to develop a more specific subsidiary claim: that philosophy's standard textbook narratives perform a kind of retroactive continuity, reordering past thinkers into present-day subdisciplines—for instance, framing Parmenides as engaged in metaphysics in a post-Kantian analytic sense, or interpreting Stoic ethics through the lens of contemporary virtue ethics—in ways that serve current boundary disputes rather than historical fidelity.

My outline currently has three parts. The first reconstructs Hobsbawm and Anderson with attention to the mechanisms through which traditions and communities are constructed: ritual repetition, canonical selection, and narrative framing. The second applies these mechanisms to philosophy's institutional self-presentation, examining the role of survey courses, canonical anthologies, and citation practices in sustaining the sense of an unbroken tradition. The third evaluates whether recognizing philosophy as an imagined community with invented traditions should alter how the discipline is taught or practiced—specifically, whether such recognition undermines the authority of the canon or merely explains its social function without dissolving its intellectual value.

I would welcome comments and suggestions on several points. First, do I risk distorting Hobsbawm and Anderson by removing the dimensions of state sovereignty and linguistic nationalism that are central to their original arguments? Second, is my claim about retroactive continuity in textbook narratives defensible without collapsing into anachronism, or am I overstating the malleability of the historical record? Third, does my tentative conclusion—that recognizing the constructed nature of the philosophical community should motivate a more reflexive pedagogy rather than a rejection of the canon—actually survive the disanalogies I have identified, or does it smuggle in an unwarranted pluralism that the framework itself does not license? I would also appreciate any suggestions on the overall structure, on specific points where the comparison genuinely illuminates disciplinary identity, and on places where I should be more cautious about overextending the analogy.
