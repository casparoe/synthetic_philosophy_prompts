In a graduate reading group on Plato’s Laws, a colleague kept repeating the one-liner “drunkenness is the truest mirror of the soul” to defend a specific reading of the symposion test: she claimed that Plato thinks alcohol dissolves social pretense to expose an authentic, pre-social self, and that this “essential self” is what the civic testing of character is actually meant to uncover. I want to understand exactly what that slogan could be claiming. Lay out, as a disambiguated menu, the distinct precise theses that “drunkenness is the truest mirror of the soul” might express—epistemic claims about how observers learn about a drinker’s character; metaphysical claims about an essential self hidden behind social masks; normative claims about when moral judgment is legitimate; and psychological claims about inhibition, shame (aidos), and the parts of the soul. For each reading, say whether it amounts to a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false, and note specifically whether it aligns with Plato’s actual argument in the Laws that wine functions as a pharmakon for collective civic evaluation rather than as a window onto private essence. Then identify which of these readings is actually doing the argumentative work for my colleague—the one that yields an “essential self” revealed by intoxication—and say whether that reading can survive contact with Plato’s view that the symposion reveals vice or virtue for the sake of political ordering, not for the sake of metaphysical transparency. Present the options with explicit verdicts, and do not collapse them into a single “correct” interpretation.
