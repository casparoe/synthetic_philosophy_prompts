I have been thinking about the clinical and legal practice of honoring advance directives—particularly living wills and durable powers of attorney for healthcare—when an incapacitated patient reaches a state that seems to conflict with the document's stated conditions. Consider the institutional protocol in many hospitals: if a patient with progressive dementia previously wrote that they refuse artificial nutrition once they no longer recognize close family members, but now reaches for food, makes eye contact with caregivers, and shows no signs of distress, the ethics committee typically treats the prior written instruction as carrying decisive authority. The present behavioral cues are interpreted as non-autonomous expressions, not as revocations, and the earlier directive is executed. I want to understand what philosophical commitments about personal identity, autonomy, and harm this practice quietly assumes.

Specifically, what view of the self over time must be in place for it to be coherent that an earlier competent self can bind a later, psychologically discontinuous self who expresses contrary preferences? Does the practice presuppose that psychological continuity is the locus of moral authority, or rather that bodily continuity within a single life-narrative is what matters? There is also an institutional asymmetry worth examining: a fully competent patient can revoke or amend the directive instantly, but once competence is lost, the present self—however content or apparently willing—cannot revoke it. What theory of autonomy explains why authority is retained prospectively but not retrospectively? Reconstruct the implicit theory the institution relies on, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely fits well with it, and say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—whether, for instance, it needs to treat the earlier self as literally identical to the present patient, or only as the legitimate author of a life-plan that the institution has contractually or ethically agreed to respect.

Finally, consider a related design choice embedded in the practice: in many legal frameworks, instructional directives (specifying particular treatments) are treated differently from proxy directives (appointing a surrogate decision-maker), with surrogates sometimes permitted to override instructions if they judge the patient's present welfare to demand it, whereas specific instructions are meant to stand unless revoked during a prior window of competence. What does that distinction presuppose about the source of authority—is it the earlier self's concrete judgments, or the earlier self's delegation of judgment to another? If the entire practice were rebuilt on the view that only present, experiential welfare matters and that there is no metaphysically or morally authoritative "earlier self," what exactly would have to change in the clinical protocol, and which parts of the current framework could survive unchanged?
