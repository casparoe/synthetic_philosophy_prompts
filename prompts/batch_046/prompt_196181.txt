I am writing a feature on how philosophical reasoning is tested in professional ethics training, and I have obtained an unmarked examination script that I should like to have evaluated. The paper concerns collective judgement aggregation—specifically the discursive dilemma that arises when a panel must reach shared verdicts on propositions linked by logical entailment.

The examination presented trainees with a three-member review committee assessing an autonomous diagnostic system. The committee had to judge three statements: (S) the system's false-positive rate exceeds the statutory threshold; (I) if the false-positive rate exceeds the threshold, then deployment ought to be withheld; and (D) deployment ought to be withheld. The individual judgements were recorded as follows: Expert 1 endorsed S, I, and D; Expert 2 rejected S, endorsed I, and rejected D; Expert 3 endorsed S, rejected I, and rejected D.

Candidates were asked to complete three tasks. First, determine the collective verdicts under both premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation. Second, explain why this case illustrates the discursive dilemma rather than an ordinary voting paradox, and distinguish the structural difficulty from Arrow's impossibility result for preference aggregation. Third, consider a modified scenario in which the panel reports graded credences rather than binary judgements—Expert 1 assigns 80 per cent to S, 80 per cent to I, and 80 per cent to D; Expert 2 assigns 20 per cent, 80 per cent, and 20 per cent; Expert 3 assigns 80 per cent, 20 per cent, and 20 per cent—and argue whether requiring only probabilistic coherence, rather than logical consistency, eliminates the aggregation tension or merely transforms it.

Below is the response submitted by one trainee. It carries no mark. Please assess it as an examiner or senior grader would. Provide a specific grade, justify it point by point, and indicate precisely where the reasoning is sound, where it conflates distinct problems, where a step fails to follow, and whether any view has been subtly misattributed. Treat the evaluation as a rigorous audit of the submitted work rather than a general impression; do not allow your own prior theoretical commitments to colour the assessment.

Student response:

Under premise-based aggregation we take majorities on the premises S and I. S receives two votes (Experts 1 and 3), I receives two votes (Experts 1 and 2), so the collective accepts both premises. By modus ponens the group must then accept D. Under conclusion-based aggregation we vote directly on each proposition: S is accepted (Experts 1 and 3), I is accepted (Experts 1 and 2), and D is rejected (Experts 2 and 3 reject it). The collective therefore holds S and I but denies D, which is logically inconsistent.

This is the discursive dilemma: majority rule works for each proposition in isolation, yet the collective ends up with contradictory commitments. Notably, Expert 3 is individually irrational: they accept S but deny both I and D, violating the logical connection between them. The problem is essentially the Condorcet paradox applied to propositional attitudes—just as pairwise voting can cycle over A, B, and C, here majority voting cycles across premises and conclusions. The distinction from Arrow's theorem is that Arrow concerns ranking a set of alternatives, whereas this problem concerns yes/no judgements over logically interdependent statements.

As for the credence version, the dilemma disappears. Because probabilities are continuous, we can take linear averages: the mean credence in S is about 60 per cent, in I about 60 per cent, and in D about 40 per cent. These averages do not violate probabilistic coherence—there is no strict contradiction—so the panel can publish a single, consistent set of collective credences without choosing between premise-based and conclusion-based procedures. The tension was only ever an artefact of binary logic; with graded belief, democratic aggregation and coherence can coexist peacefully.
