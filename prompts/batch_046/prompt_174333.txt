A colleague is weighing whether to sign a contract for a long-term position now, or to spend six months investigating the hidden institutional dynamics of the organization before committing. She argues that gathering more information can only improve her decision, since a rational person updates her beliefs and then chooses the best available option; if the news is bad she can decline, and if it is irrelevant she can proceed unchanged. Her mentor counters that the investigation is not free—time and opportunity are lost—and, more importantly, that learning how power actually operates inside institutions might not just change her estimates of success but might corrode the kind of trust and long-range commitment that make the position viable in the first place. I want to understand whether the intuition that information is always beneficial to a rational decider holds up once we allow that knowledge can reshape what we value, not just what we believe, and once we recognize that some commitments require a kind of stable framing that inquiry itself undermines. How should we think about the boundary between prudent information-gathering and destructive over-analysis in cases where learning changes the learner?
