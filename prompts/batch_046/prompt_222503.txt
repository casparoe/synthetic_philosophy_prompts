Analyze the paired case of the Nuremberg International Military Tribunal and the International Military Tribunal for the Far East as a study in victors’ justice and retroactive law, using a conceptual distinction between substantive retroactivity—defining conduct as criminal after it has occurred—and institutional retroactivity—creating courts, charters, and procedures after the fact to apply law that may or may not have existed beforehand. Focus on how both tribunals prosecuted “crimes against peace” and conspiracy to wage aggressive war, conduct that was not clearly established as individually punishable under pre-1945 international law, under charters drafted and enforced solely by the victorious Allied powers. Assess whether these two forms of retroactivity can be morally separated: does the fact that both courts were institutionally retroactive weaken the substantive retroactivity objection, or does it compound it? Examine the asymmetry of moral judgment—Allied actions such as the firebombing of Dresden or the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki being placed beyond the tribunals’ jurisdiction—to determine whether the core objection is procedural unfairness, selective moral standing, or a collapse of the two. Pay particular attention to the Tokyo Trial’s broader conspiracy framework, which distributed criminal liability for planning aggressive war across a wider network of leaders than Nuremberg’s more direct liability model, and ask whether expanding the reach of retroactive law makes it more defensible as systemic justice or less defensible as arbitrary power. Conclude with what this paired episode reveals about the limits of the nullum crimen sine lege principle when applied to systemic international crimes, and state explicitly where your reasoning is most vulnerable: which premise—about the pre-1945 legal status of aggressive war, about the moral symmetry required for legitimate adjudication, or about the separability of institutional and substantive retroactivity—would need to fail in order to overturn your overall assessment.
