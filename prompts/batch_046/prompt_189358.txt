For my seminar on contemporary theories of freedom, I have been trying to formalize the difference between negative and republican liberty by treating domination as a second-order modal property—specifically, as the sustained availability of an interference pathway that remains open even when unexercised. Under this framing, it seems to me that republican freedom should be satisfied whenever institutional design closes that pathway, as in a constitutional democracy with robust separation of powers. If the pathway is structurally blocked, then arbitrary power is absent, and non-domination becomes coextensive with well-secured negative liberty. Yet I keep encountering the claim that a citizen can still be dominated even in such a system—for instance, if a legislative majority retains the legal authority to revoke minority rights, even if it never does so. To my mind, that sounds like describing a dormant capacity rather than an active constraint on freedom, and I struggle to see why a purely counterfactual vulnerability should diminish liberty any more than the counterfactual possibility that a benevolent neighbor could break into my house diminishes my property rights. Am I wrong to think that republican liberty adds nothing beyond negative liberty plus institutional safeguards, and if so, where exactly does the conceptual separation lie? In particular, is there a principled way to distinguish domination from the mere logical possibility of interference without appealing to psychological effects like fear or deference, which seem to belong to a different normative category altogether?
