In a graduate seminar on historical sociology and the sociology of religion, a fellow student summarized Max Weber’s well-known study of Protestantism and capitalism for me in the following terms: “Weber demonstrated that Calvinist anxiety over predestination operated as a direct psychological cause, pushing believers to accumulate capital systematically as a form of existential reassurance. Because this specific mental mechanism was absent in Catholic, Confucian, and other religious contexts, it provides a sufficient explanation for why modern rational capitalism first developed in Protestant Northern Europe. Consequently, the thesis establishes that cultural values can function as fully independent causes of macroeconomic systems, which means materialist accounts that emphasize trade routes, resource endowments, or legal institutions are simply missing the priority of belief. The student added that contemporary quantitative attempts to test the thesis—treating Protestant adherence as one regression variable among others—necessarily fail, since Weber’s point was that values reshape the very interpretive categories through which economic action becomes meaningful, a dimension that statistical correlation cannot capture.”

I feel this captures Weber’s reputation in popular discussion, yet I also sense that it overstates his claims in directions I have seen in secondary literature, and I am not sure where the line between faithful interpretation and distortion falls. Could you audit this summary for fairness? Please move through its main claims—the direct psychological causation, the sufficiency of cultural explanation, the dismissal of material conditions, and the critique of quantitative testing—and indicate which parts a careful reader of Weber would accept, which are subtle conflations with later psychological or econometric interpretations he might reject, and which are straightforward misrepresentations of his methodological position. In particular, I want to know whether Weber intended the concept of elective affinity to license the strong causal language used here; whether he actually argued that beliefs have temporal or explanatory priority over material conditions in the manner implied; and whether his critique of positivism amounts to the claim that statistical methods are inherently incapable of addressing his thesis. Finally, does the conclusion—that materialist explanations are thereby defeated, or that quantitative sociology necessarily betrays Weber’s insight—actually survive once the summary is corrected?
