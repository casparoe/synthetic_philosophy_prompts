In a graduate seminar on population ethics, we have been examining a variant of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism—the view that reducing suffering beneath a specified boundary takes absolute priority over promoting pleasure, but that pleasure and pain do not share a fully cardinal or interpersonally comparable scale. A fellow student has pressed the following argument against this position, and our group finds the conclusion suspiciously destructive, yet we cannot isolate the exact misstep. (1) To apply a lexical threshold at all—say, to determine whether a state of affairs has fallen below the boundary where suffering dominates—one must be able to compare suffering-states and pleasure-states across at least an ordinal or interval metric; otherwise there is no basis for locating anything "below" or "above" the line. (2) But if pleasure and pain share even a partial comparative metric, then there is no principled reason the boundary should remain lexical; a sufficiently large accumulation of pleasure on one side ought, by that very metric, to outweigh a marginal increment of suffering on the other, rendering the threshold porous rather than absolute. (3) To preserve genuinely lexical priority, the view must therefore deny all commensurability between the two valences. (4) Yet if they are entirely incommensurable, we lack any rational basis for claiming that reducing extreme suffering is normatively more urgent than achieving extreme pleasure; comparative judgments of "better" or "worse" become impossible, and the view's core ordering collapses into normative silence. (5) Thus the position requires commensurability to justify its ordering and requires incommensurability to protect its lexical structure—an instability that is not merely practical but conceptual. (6) From this it follows either that lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism is incoherent, or that rational choice between suffering-reduction and pleasure-promotion is globally impossible, which would undermine not only this view but any normative framework in population ethics that relies on cross-valence evaluation. We are inclined to reject (6) rather than abandon the view, but we want to know precisely which earlier premise cheats, and what philosophical price we pay—whether in our theory of value comparison, in our account of rational choice, or in our understanding of lexical priority itself—to reject it instead. Which step should we give up, and why did it seem so plausible at first?
