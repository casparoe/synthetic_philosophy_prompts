I'm grading take-home essays for an upper-division seminar on American pragmatism and democratic theory. The exam asked students to explain John Dewey's argument that democracy is not primarily a system of periodic voting but a continuous mode of cooperative social inquiry—analogous to scientific experimentation in that it proceeds under conditions of uncertainty, tests proposed solutions through lived experience, and revises both means and ends in light of consequences—and then to apply that framework to a concrete institutional design problem. Specifically, students had to consider whether a citizens' assembly charged with local climate-adaptation policy should be structured to deliver a single unified recommendation or to formally preserve and document persistent disagreement among members, and they had to justify the design choice using Dewey's own criteria rather than generic deliberative-democracy theory.

Below is one submission I need evaluated. The student writes:

"Dewey treats democracy as the scientific method writ large: citizens form hypotheses about policy, implement them on a small scale, gather results, and keep what works. This means democratic life is essentially experimental problem-solving among equals, not a matter of counting votes. Applied to a climate assembly, this framework suggests we should pilot competing adaptation strategies—say, managed retreat versus hardened infrastructure—measure outcomes, and then converge on whichever pilot succeeds. Because Dewey holds that all genuine inquiry aims at resolving a problematic situation, disagreement is only instrumentally valuable, like rival lab hypotheses before the data comes in. Once evidence shows which strategy works, the assembly should issue one consensus recommendation and drop the dissenting view; documenting disagreement past that point would confuse temporary exploration with final warranted assertibility. Furthermore, Dewey is deeply anti-elitist: since every citizen has equal capacity for intelligent judgment, climate scientists should have no special advisory status in the assembly. Their technical input should be treated like any other opinion, not given privileged weight, because expertise threatens the democratic equality that makes inquiry genuinely social."

Please assign a letter grade on a standard A-F scale. Lead with the grade and your one-sentence overall verdict in the very first sentence, then provide point-by-point justification that addresses both the exposition of Dewey and the application to the assembly design, with no preamble and no restating of the prompt.
