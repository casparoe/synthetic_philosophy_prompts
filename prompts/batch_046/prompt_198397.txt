Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—about Buridan’s ass, using the end-words water, hay, equal, starve, reason, and break in the standard rotating order. The rotation itself should do the conceptual work: each stanza must present the two goods as structurally indistinguishable, so that the repeating end-words feel like a loop rather than a progression, and the envoi must gather all six words in an order that reveals the symmetry being broken not by the discovery of a hidden preference but by the arbitrary imposition of a tie-breaking procedure. Treat the ass not as a medieval caricature of animal stupidity but as a model of rational agency facing perfect preference symmetry: the speaker should be aware that modern decision theory offers responses—tie-breaking rules, revealed-preference accounts, the thesis that choice can constitute rather than merely reflect preference—yet the poem should not adopt any of them as a resolution. Allude to these ideas precisely enough that someone familiar with practical reason will recognize them, but do not explain them didactically; keep the tone serious and philosophical rather than comic or pastoral. In the final lines, let the breaking of the deadlock remain ambiguous—whether it saves rationality or betrays it—so that the poem ends in the same tension where the theoretical discussion actually stalls.
