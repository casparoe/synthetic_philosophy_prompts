Write a taxonomy tree—each branch labeled by the claim that splits it—for a philosophy-of-social-science seminar on whether historical comparison can establish that institutions cause national wealth, with geography and culture as persistent confounds. Start with the concrete case of an arbitrary-border partition in an otherwise homogeneous region (e.g., a divided peninsula or colony split by a line) and ask whether it supplies a valid counterfactual for isolating institutional effects. From there branch first on whether identification is possible at all, then on whether any identified institutional mechanism is portable across different geographic or cultural background conditions, and finally on whether residual variance belongs to geography, culture, or irreducible historical contingency. The leaves must include: a universal institutionalist (institutions are primary and portable), a geographic determinist (geography drives both institutions and wealth, so apparent institutional effects are spurious), a culture-interactionist (institutions work only within specific cultural settings), a context-bound institutionalist (local identification succeeds but portability fails), and a deep epistemic skeptic (history never provides stable counterfactuals, so causal knowledge is unavailable). Also include at least two unnamed or unoccupied combinations—particularly the space that accepts valid identification but assigns the causal weight entirely to geography that happens to correlate with institutions, and diagnose whether that combination is incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate. Every diagnostic node should be phrased as a judgment about a specific historical comparison rather than as a request to name a theory. Specify six to eight leaves, an audience of graduate students, and note which distinctions matter for assessing the limits of causal inference from history rather than for adjudicating development policy.
