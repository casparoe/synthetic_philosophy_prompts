In a seminar on democratic design, a colleague kept returning to the slogan that elections are aristocratic while sortition is democratic, using it most recently to brush aside my concern that a legislature selected by lot might lack the specialized competence needed to draft coherent climate regulation. I want to understand exactly what work that sentence can do.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, treating "aristocratic" and "democratic" as variables that can be filled in different ways—for instance, merit-filtering versus class-based rule versus unequal opportunity to govern, and formal equality of selection chance versus descriptive representation versus equal influence over outcomes. For each reading, say whether it is a conceptual platitude, a substantive and defensible institutional claim, or false; specify what empirical or normative premise it requires; and indicate whether that particular reading actually licenses the dismissal of competence-based objections to sortition.

In addition, distinguish readings that stay purely at the level of the selection mechanism's formal properties from those that implicitly carry normative commitments about whether political expertise is a legitimate criterion for holding office. Conclude by saying which reading is likely being invoked when the slogan is used to shut down an argument about whether small, randomly selected bodies can produce technically sound policy, and whether that reading survives if one accepts that democratic legitimacy and epistemic competence are conceptually separable.
