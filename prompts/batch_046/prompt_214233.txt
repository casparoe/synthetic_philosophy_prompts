I'm attempting to articulate the principle, if there is one, that governs my judgments about when epistemic peers ought to conciliate versus remain steadfast in the face of disagreement. Below are my verdicts across several concrete scenarios. I want to know whether a coherent account can accommodate all of them, which ones it must sacrifice for simplicity, and what it predicts for cases I have not listed.

Case 1: Two research scientists with equivalent publication records and domain expertise examine the same raw data from a climate model and reach opposite conclusions about whether a particular feedback loop is positive or negative. Both have independently verified the statistical pipeline. I judge that each should reduce confidence somewhat, but neither should retreat to full agnosticism.

Case 2: You and a colleague with identical philosophical training have spent months debating whether a certain form of moral particularism can account for the wrongness of deception. You have read the same literature and followed the same arguments. You still find your own reasoning sound. I judge that you may remain steadfast in your conclusion, though you should acknowledge that the disagreement lowers the overall reliability of the shared reasoning process.

Case 3: Two physicians with the same specialization review identical imaging and patient history. One is severely sleep-deprived and has recently been disciplined for careless errors; the other is well-rested and has an unblemished record. They disagree on the diagnosis. I judge that only the well-rested physician should hold firm; conciliation should be asymmetric.

Case 4: A single speaker consistently misapplies the term "elm" to beech trees, having never encountered the correct usage. A peer botanist corrects him. I judge that this is not a peer disagreement at all—the speaker's usage is defective rather than equally authoritative.

Case 5: You and a peer disagree about the aesthetic value of a particular atonal musical work. You both understand the score and the history of the genre equally well. I judge that there is no epistemic failure on either side; steadfastness is fully appropriate and conciliation is unnecessary, since the disagreement is not about a fact but about a response.

Case 6: You discover that a long-standing peer disagreement about a historical event was caused by your peer having consistently misread a single primary-source document, though her reasoning was otherwise sound. Once the misreading is pointed out, she updates. I judge that prior to discovery, conciliation was appropriate; after discovery, steadfastness is correct, but the correct attitude was always conditional on access to the same information.

Case 7 (the one that keeps me uncertain): Two reasoners apply different but formally valid priors to an infinite decision tree in which each node represents a choice that affects an unbounded number of future observers. One applies a self-sampling assumption: she treats herself as randomly selected from the reference class of all observers at nodes like this one, which leads her to discount extreme outcomes. The other applies a self-indication assumption: she reasons that the existence of more observers in worlds where one choice is made makes that world more likely from her first-person perspective, leading to the opposite recommendation. Both are equally competent in formal decision theory, and they agree on all non-anthropic premises. I cannot decide whether they should split the difference, whether one framework is rationally mandatory, or whether the disagreement reveals that peer conciliation itself breaks down when the dispute concerns the correct normative framework for weighing peer disagreement.

Is there a unified principle—perhaps involving symmetry of evidence, symmetry of reasoning competence, the presence of a shared epistemic goal, or the level at which the disagreement occurs—that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which case should be overturned? And what does the best available principle say about a further case: you and a peer disagree not about a first-order claim, but about whether conciliationism itself is the correct norm for resolving peer disagreement?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate the specific clause that explains each case, note any verdict it cannot accommodate, and propose a prediction for the meta-normative case.
