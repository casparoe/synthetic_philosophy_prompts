I am a journalist writing on science and democratic theory, and I am preparing a long-form essay on whether Dewey's experimentalist philosophy can genuinely inform institutional design in the present. Before publication, I want an internal consistency check on the position I intend to defend. These are my commitments:

First, I understand democracy not as the aggregation of pre-existing preferences but as a form of collective social inquiry; political decisions are hypotheses to be tested against lived experience, and democratic legitimacy derives from the rigour of that testing rather than from the mere fact of majority assent.

Second, I reject any sharp categorical separation between scientific method and democratic deliberation; the norms of fallibilism, evidence, and revision apply continuously across laboratory and legislative practice.

Third, I hold that expert knowledge is indispensable to democratic inquiry, yet I insist that experts must be embedded in participatory structures—deliberative panels, citizen juries—so that their claims remain publicly contestable and experimentally revisable.

Fourth, I treat individual rights, particularly freedom of expression and association, not as moral absolutes but as instrumental safeguards for the conditions of inquiry; they derive their value from protecting the experimental process itself.

Fifth, I believe that ordinary legislation should be framed with sunset provisions and evaluated as provisional hypotheses against empirical social outcomes, open to rapid revision.

Sixth, at the same time, I regard certain constitutional provisions—procedural guarantees of free expression and mechanisms of judicial review—as non-experimental fixed points that should not be subject to legislative sunset or treated as revisable hypotheses, since without them inquiry collapses.

Seventh, I maintain that democratic legitimacy also requires long-term institutional stability and predictability; citizens must be able to plan their lives across electoral cycles, which implies that rapid, iterative policy experimentation must be bounded by durable frameworks.

Eighth, I take the aim of democratic inquiry to be not consensus but the intelligent reconstruction of shared experience; persistent disagreement should be treated as data for further revision rather than as a failure of the process.

Please audit whether these eight commitments hang together. I suspect there are hidden tensions—especially between the demand for rapid experimental revision and the demand for stability, and between the continuity of science and democracy and the special participatory embedding of experts—but I am unsure which conflicts are genuine and which dissolve on closer inspection. I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the analysis should proceed on conceptual grounds alone.

Please structure your response as follows:
- Joint Tenability Audit (approximately 350 words): rank the tensions by severity, specify which pairs of commitments conflict and whether the conflict is obvious or hidden, and for each tension identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency.
- Dissolved Tensions (approximately 150 words): identify at least one pairing that appears inconsistent but is not, explaining precisely how the commitments cohere once a distinction is drawn.
- The Least Costly Revision (approximately 150 words): state which single commitment should be revised or qualified to resolve the greatest number of tensions at the lowest philosophical cost, and describe the revised position in detail.
- Residual Costs and Unresolved Tensions (approximately 100 words): outline what is conceptually or practically lost by adopting that revision, and indicate whether any tension remains genuinely unresolved.
