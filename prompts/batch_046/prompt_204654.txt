As a novelist I often think in terms of origins and explanations—why a world begins, whether a chain can hang in mid-air—so I am trying to map my own philosophical verdicts on when "it simply is" becomes intolerable. Here are my judgments.

If a single stone materialized in empty space with no cause and no governing law, that demands explanation; treating it as brute feels like a failure of reason.

An infinite backward chain of stones, each formed by the previous, with no first member: each link is accounted for, yet I find myself insisting the chain as a whole still cries out for an external ground. At the same time, I feel an infinite mathematical series needs no such ground, so I am unsure whether my demand concerns contingency or causation specifically.

That the fundamental laws are necessary—unable to be otherwise—seems like a legitimate terminus; asking why they hold then strikes me as a category mistake, like asking why triangles have three sides.

But a circular explanatory loop, where the existence of planets explains stars and stars explain planets with nothing outside the loop, feels unacceptable despite every member being accounted for. That suggests internal explanation is not always sufficient, yet I am unsure what distinguishes this loop from the infinite chain.

A necessary being, whether abstract or concrete, that explains itself, is fully acceptable as a stopping point.

The case I keep revisiting: a universe governed by fixed, necessary laws, but containing exactly one genuinely contingent, uncaused event that is not part of any explanatory chain and could have failed to occur. Is that isolated brute contingency tolerable within an otherwise explained system, or does it compromise the whole?
