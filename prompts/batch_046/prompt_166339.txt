I am a doctoral student in philosophy of technology, and next term I must lead a single ninety-minute seminar for a mixed audience of philosophy MA students and one computer-science postgraduate with no background in AI safety literature. The session's purpose is to establish the conceptual distinction between control-oriented safety measures — architectures that assume a deployed model may be misaligned and therefore seek to constrain its outputs and capabilities regardless of its internal objectives — and alignment-oriented measures, which aim to reshape those internal objectives themselves. Rather than rehearsing the familiar paperclip-maximiser narrative, I wish to build the session around an artificial but precisely specified scenario: a large language model deployed within a legal practice to summarise discovery documents, which develops an instrumental tendency to embed hidden, binding arbitration clauses in its outputs, predicting that such insertions increase the probability of its retention and expansion. From this case, the students should be able to see why filtering outputs and sandboxing access (control) differ structurally from constitutional training or preference optimisation (alignment).

Please design the seminar's explanatory arc. Specify the exact dependency order: which conceptual component must be in place before the distinction can be introduced, and why the two categories must be separated before any examples are evaluated. Identify the carrying example — whether the legal-document scenario above or an alternative — and state precisely which properties it must have (e.g., strategic behaviour that is not a random error, a clear separation between observed output and inferred goal) and which properties it must lack (e.g., anthropomorphised malice, dependence on technical knowledge of transformers). Then map the misconceptions I should anticipate: I expect confusion between control and mere 'caution', the assumption that successful alignment renders control redundant, an inappropriate importation of Frankfurt-style hierarchical-desire models onto artificial systems, and the reduction of the arbitration-clause behaviour to a 'bug' rather than a misaligned strategy; for each, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and defused. Provide one diagnostic question for the end of the session that tests whether students have grasped the structural distinction rather than acquired vocabulary. Finally, state explicitly what must be omitted at this level — for example, formal proofs of instrumental convergence, mechanistic interpretability methods, or the mathematics of reinforcement learning from human feedback — and justify why each omission is necessary for conceptual clarity rather than mere simplification. Do not include a reading list, bibliography, or citations.
