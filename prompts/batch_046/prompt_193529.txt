I'm presenting at a philosophy of mind and quantum foundations workshop my thesis that Schrödinger's Vedanta-inspired claim about consciousness being unitary should be reconstructed not as an ontological identity thesis—according to which all individual minds are literally one substance—but as an epistemic-structural claim about quantum state assignment. Specifically, I argue that the global consistency conditions required for decoherence entail that any subjective point of view is non-localizable in precisely the manner Vedantic non-duality describes, but without requiring a universal mind-substance. I contend that modern decoherence theory actually reverses the explanatory arrow Schrödinger assumed: rather than a prior unified consciousness explaining quantum correlations, decoherence fragments the appearance of individuality while leaving the global state intact, which makes the Vedantic metaphor descriptively apt but metaphysically unnecessary. I draw on Schrödinger's essays collected in Mind and Matter and on his Dublin lectures later published as What Is Life?, though I treat neither as authoritative scripture. The audience includes two analytic philosophers of mind who regard any Vedanta reference as obscurantist mysticism, a quantum information theorist who accepts decoherence but denies it carries phenomenological weight, a historian of physics who knows Schrödinger's correspondence and biography in granular detail, and graduate students whose only exposure to Schrödinger is the popular cat paradox.
