I sit on the university's joint finance-and-ethics committee, which must deliver a written recommendation to the senate by the end of this month on a pilot proposal for the coming academic year. The proposal, advanced by the student government and the economics department, would replace a portion of campus auxiliary payments—dining credits, transit subsidies, and parking permits—with a digital "labor-credit" system denominated not in dollars but in verified hours of university service: peer tutoring, facilities upkeep, and community mentoring. The conceptual premise, inspired by George Berkeley's argument that sound money should represent actual labor and productive capacity rather than speculative or metallic value, is that tying exchange-value directly to performed work prevents the abstraction of labor into a fungible commodity priced by external markets. Proponents claim this keeps the economic relation internal to the university community and honors the dignity of the work performed rather than treating it as an input to be bought at the lowest available rate.

Opponents—including our general counsel and the director of disability services—counter that any standardized medium of exchange, regardless of denomination, introduces commodification and market-like dynamics: students might hoard credits, trade them informally at discount rates, or accumulate status advantages, while those unable to perform service labor due to disability, caregiving obligations, or intensive coursework are effectively excluded from benefits currently available to all. They further note that without the regulatory safeguards that govern legal tender, there is no mechanism to prevent speculative fluctuations in credit value, to protect against discrimination in which services count as valid, or to stop the emergence of an unregulated secondary market.

Please draft the committee's formal opinion memo, addressed to the senate, recommending either approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions and a concrete implementation timeline if applicable. The memo should take the Berkeley-inspired distinction between representational and speculative value seriously—do not dismiss it as utopian—but must also engage honestly with the strongest objection: that a labor-backed token can still generate distortion, exclusion, and informal exchange without the institutional protections that accompany actual currency. The reasoning should be self-contained, should not request or cite external bibliographies or reading lists, and should conclude with an actionable bottom line that the senate could vote on.
