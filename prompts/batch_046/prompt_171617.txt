I am writing a novel centered on an aging cartographer whose early cognitive decline leaves her able to understand immediate requests but unable to retain complex long-range consequences. A documentary filmmaker—whose assistant is the cartographer's granddaughter—approaches her to sign releases for digitizing her private hand-drawn maps, which include the precise locations of unmarked family burial grounds. The filmmaker describes the project as a public cultural archive; he does not disclose that the archive is funded by an extraction consortium that intends to use the geospatial data to clear the land. The cartographer signs, partly out of trust in the stated purpose and partly because she does not want to disappoint her granddaughter. Once she grasps the likely outcome, she insists that her agreement was never genuine, while the filmmaker maintains that her signature, witnessed and given without physical threat, is binding. I am trying to dramatize this confrontation without flattening it into a simple morality tale. Could you help me identify the philosophical structure of what makes an agreement like this valid or invalid—specifically where deception about purpose, indirect pressure through family loyalty, and fluctuating competence intersect? In your reply, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among those who study these questions, and do not present any contested view as if it were the consensus. I am looking for conceptual clarity I can translate into scene and dialogue, not generic narrative advice.
