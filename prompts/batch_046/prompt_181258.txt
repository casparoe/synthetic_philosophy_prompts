I am a journalist covering science and ideas, preparing a feature on whether biography can still function as a technology for moral learning without collapsing into hagiography. At a recent editorial meeting, two colleagues—both well-read in classics but with different intellectual instincts—fell into an argument about Plutarch’s Parallel Lives. They were not disputing historical accuracy so much as the pedagogical logic of pairing lives. Here is how their exchange went, reported as I heard it.

Colleague A: Plutarch’s pairing is essentially decorative hagiography. He selects Greek and Roman figures who already fit predetermined moral types—Alexander set against Caesar, both ambitious; Lycurgus set against Numa, both lawgivers. The comparison flatters each culture while leaving the moral categories untouched. The reader is meant to admire a pre-cast virtue, not to think. The lives are mirrors that show only what the author already believes.

Colleague B: That misses the structural point. The pairing is not decorative; it creates what we might call a dialectical biography. By forcing Alexander’s ambition into proximity with Caesar’s, Plutarch makes the category of ambition unstable. The reader cannot simply imitate either man; she must calibrate her own judgment against two incompatible exemplars. The moral object is not either life but the tension between them.

Colleague A: But you are importing a universalist framework that Plutarch never defends. To compare ambition across cultures requires assuming that ambition is the same kind of thing in both contexts. That assumption is doing all the work. What looks like dialectical openness is actually a hidden hagiography of comparative reason—you are praising not the men but your own capacity to harmonize them.

Colleague B: Even granting that, the pedagogical mechanism is not identification with a hero but the use of lives as scaffolds. The reader does not ask how she can be like Alexander, but under what conditions this trait becomes a vice rather than a virtue. The biography provides boundary cases for practical reasoning, not portraits for emulation. That is precisely how one avoids hagiography.

Keep the analysis grounded in what Plutarch actually does—his paired prologues, his occasional refusal to declare a winner, and his tendency to draw a single moral observation from contrasting deaths—without asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
