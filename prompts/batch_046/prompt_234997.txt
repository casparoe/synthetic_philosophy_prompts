The examination question posed to the student was the following: In his Essay on the Principle of Population, Thomas Malthus argued that human population tends to increase in a geometric progression while the means of subsistence increase only in an arithmetical progression, eventually producing what he called positive checks—famine, disease, and war. Critics in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries observed that sustained technological innovation, including synthetic fertilizers, mechanized agriculture, and the demographic transition, seemed to falsify this long-run prognosis. Philosophers of science have since debated whether Malthus can be epistemically rescued. First, distinguish between rescuing the theory by restricting its domain—for example, to pre-industrial agrarian economies that lack technological feedback loops—and rescuing it by treating the geometric tendency as a latent mechanism that is continuously suppressed but never eliminated by countervailing forces. Second, assess whether either form of rescue permits the theory to count as genuinely predictive over the long run, or whether it collapses into unfalsifiability. In particular, discuss whether the status right but early is a legitimate epistemic category for a scientific theory, or whether it amounts to ad hoc immunization.

The student submitted the following answer:

Malthus's core claim is that population grows exponentially—what he called geometric progression—while food production grows only linearly, or arithmetically, so that eventually population outstrips resources and triggers positive checks: famine, epidemic, and conflict. During the nineteenth century this pattern seemed visible in parts of Europe and Ireland, but in the twentieth century technological innovation, especially synthetic fertilizers and high-yield crops, allowed food supply to expand rapidly, apparently falsifying the theory.

To defend Malthus, one can either restrict the theory's domain to societies that lack technological feedback loops, or one can retain the universal geometric law and add a suppressive counterforce—treating innovation as a compressed spring that holds the geometric tendency in check but never removes it. Both strategies shield the theory from counterexample, but they differ in structure. The domain restriction is a boundary condition: it concedes that industrial economies operate outside the model. The mechanistic rescue is an internal modification: it keeps the original law intact and introduces an opposing dynamic.

There is, however, an important difference in fidelity to Malthus's own framework. Malthus treated technological improvement in agriculture as an external interruption to a closed biological system, not as an internal arithmetic term. Therefore, the domain-restriction rescue is actually more faithful to his intent, because it preserves that externality, whereas the mechanistic rescue wrongly internalizes technology as part of the system's dynamics.

As for being right but early, this is legitimate only when the core mechanism remains untouched by rescue conditions. Because the mechanistic rescue modifies the mechanism by adding suppression, it cannot claim predictive delay; it has altered the very law it seeks to defend. The domain restriction, by contrast, leaves the geometric-arithmetic mechanism pure and therefore allows us to say that Malthus was correct about agrarian societies, merely premature in expecting the checks to appear globally. So long as the auxiliary conditions—such as the absence of technological feedback—are independently testable, the theory avoids ad hoc immunization. Consequently, the domain-restriction strategy yields a genuinely long-run predictive claim, provided we restrict our temporal horizon to pre-industrial conditions, while the mechanistic strategy reduces Malthus to an unfalsifiable background tendency.
