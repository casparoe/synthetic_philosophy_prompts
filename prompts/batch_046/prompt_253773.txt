my uni runs a wellness audit where students get rated solely on reduction of reported cravings and suffering — they literally dont measure happiness or accomplishment, just how empty of wanting you are, and counselors celebrate you if you report zero unmet desires even if youre depressed. but the same school gives out passion grants to chase big ambitious projects, and they penalize "excessive contentment" because it correlates with low drive. what value theory does the audit presuppose? is it straight-up tranquilism (only absence of craving and suffering matters), or could negative utilitarianism or even some weird form of buddhist ethics run it? does the passion grant flat-out contradict the audit's premises, or can you square them by saying desire-fulfillment is only instrumentally good? reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the audit strictly needs from what it just fits well with, and say what a campus policy built purely on that view would have to change — like, would they kill the passion grants and start rewarding apathy? also that "excessive contentment" penalty: does it mess up the theory or is it actually consistent with treating all positive states as suspicious?
