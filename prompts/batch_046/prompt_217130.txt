I have recently returned to full-time professional work following the birth of my first child, and together with my partner—who is also employed full-time—we are attempting to sustain a dual-earner household without extended-family childcare support. In navigating this, I have become struck by a structural mismatch that standard policy debates rarely address: parental leave is generally conceived as a continuous, linear block of wage replacement, whereas the actual labor of infant and toddler care is discontinuous, episodic, and tightly coupled to unpredictable developmental rhythms—illness cycles, sleep regressions, and attachment-sensitive windows that do not align with quarterly business calendars or standardized school schedules. This suggests that the deeper problem is not merely insufficient leave duration, but a misalignment between the temporal architecture of employment and the non-substitutable rhythms of early development. Given this framing, I want to understand where research and institutional design should actually be directed. What are the principal open conceptual and empirical questions at the intersection of paid parental leave, formal and informal childcare provision, and the long-term viability of the dual-earner family model? If one possessed only limited leverage—say, a single policy advocacy platform or a five-year research program—which two or three lines of inquiry or intervention should take priority, and why? Please provide a defended agenda rather than a survey: state the criteria you are using (for instance, tractability, neglectedness, downstream effects on gender equity, or child developmental outcomes), explain the trade-offs among them, and specify what evidence or shift in conditions would cause you to reorder your priorities. I am not seeking bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations; I want to know what makes a question worth the years and resources, not merely that it remains unresolved.
