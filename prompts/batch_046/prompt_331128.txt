I was discussing Rudolf Carnap’s political and planning-related commitments with a colleague in a philosophy of science seminar, and we reached an impasse over how his logical empiricism bears on collective decision-making. One of us argued that Carnap’s treatment of normative statements as cognitively empty expressions of attitude—part of his broader rejection of metaphysics—commits him implicitly to a technocratic model of social planning. On this view, because democratic debate over ends is just emotional bargaining without rational weight, planning should be delegated to scientific experts who optimize technically specified goals, while citizens supply only preferences. She added that Carnap’s principle of tolerance, though usually discussed in linguistic terms, offers no help for institutional choice: selecting an economic framework is value-laden, and since Carnap denies that such choices can be rationally justified by science, his philosophy effectively cedes authority to whoever controls the technical apparatus.

The other person objected that this conflates Carnap’s meta-ethics with his philosophy of science. He pointed out that Carnap’s distinction between internal and external questions holds that once a linguistic or institutional framework is adopted, internal issues are settled empirically or logically, but the adoption itself is a practical decision guided by pragmatic criteria like efficiency and simplicity, not by scientific proof. By direct analogy, planning goals are adopted practically rather than discovered, so Carnap’s view actually blocks experts from claiming scientific authority over them. He also insisted that Carnap was not Neurath—he never endorsed physicalist unification of science as a basis for centralized economic planning, and his writings on inductive logic and utility calculus concern individual rational decision under uncertainty, not macro-level social engineering. From this he concluded that Carnap’s position supports democratic deliberation about planning ends precisely because science is silent on them, leaving the choice to participatory, practical judgment.

We continued back and forth: she noted that if values are purely expressive, democratic deliberation loses its claim to rational justification and collapses into preference aggregation or power dynamics; he replied that Carnap’s pragmatism about framework choice is exactly what legitimizes democratic institutions, since no metaphysical argument can override a collectively adopted practical framework. Neither of us was convinced. Please also identify what both sides are missing, especially regarding the gap between Carnap’s own documented political sympathies and the logical implications of his philosophy of science, and say whether there is a coherent intermediate position that respects both the cognitive status of value judgments and the possibility of rational collective planning.
