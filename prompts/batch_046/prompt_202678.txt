As a journalist writing about moral philosophy for a general audience, I am working on a piece about supererogation—the idea that some acts are morally admirable yet not strictly required—and I want to examine how Steven Spielberg's Schindler's List complicates the boundary between duty and heroic excess. Rather than treating Oskar Schindler as a simple moral exemplar, I would like a philosophical reading that brings in Susan Wolf's critique of moral sainthood—the view that devoting one's entire life to moral improvement can be personally destructive and is not obligatory—along with the concept of moral remainder, the lingering costs borne by agents who exceed what duty demands. The film shows Schindler bankrupting himself, risking death, and suffering profound psychological damage, yet it also frames his actions against the backdrop of ordinary people who do nothing. Where does the narrative present his rescue of over a thousand people as going beyond the call of duty rather than fulfilling a minimal moral obligation? In particular, I am interested in the breakdown scene where he laments the people he failed to save, and in the final ring ceremony: does the film interpret his grief as the residue of supererogation—costs that remain even when one has done more than required—or as evidence that he has fallen short of an absolute duty? I also want to know whether the film implicitly endorses the idea that we are not required to be moral saints: does it allow space for ordinary moral agents who do not undertake such sacrifices, or does its structure make Schindler's inaction appear morally unthinkable? Please treat both the film and the philosophy with equal seriousness: avoid reducing Schindler's character to an illustration of Wolf's argument, and avoid distorting the concept of supererogation to fit the screenplay. What does the cinematic portrayal—particularly its visual and emotional emphasis on Schindler's personal erosion—contribute to the philosophical debate that purely conceptual analysis cannot capture?
