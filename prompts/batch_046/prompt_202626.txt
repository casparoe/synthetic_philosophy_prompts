I am a journalist who reports on philosophy and science, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about six minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop on mental-health ethics. The room will be divided evenly between clinical researchers studying anhedonia and craving, and scholars of Buddhist ethics and Schopenhauerian metaphysics. I want the remarks structured around tranquilism: the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, and that pleasure or satisfaction are not positive goods but merely temporary suspensions of negative states. This idea should shape the architecture of the speech—determining what I can say about gratitude to the organizers, about why these two groups have been brought together, and about what “progress” in this field ought to mean—rather than serving as decorative framing. I must avoid turning the welcome into a philosophical lecture, yet the conceptual framing needs to land precisely for the scholars without losing the clinicians. To keep the task from being generic, please treat the workshop itself as an embodiment of tranquilist practice: its value lies not in generating a new positive theory of well-being, but in removing a specific conceptual craving—the desire for reductive explanations that treat the absence of suffering as merely instrumental to the presence of pleasure. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a journalist addressing a mixed audience of experts and non-experts together. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
