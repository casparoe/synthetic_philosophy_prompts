Since I moved to London last year I have found myself returning to a question that now feels personally urgent: what exactly separates a legitimate constitutional monarchy in a democracy from an illegitimate intrusion into politics? I have formed judgments about particular cases, and I want to know whether they hang together or whether I must surrender some. I think it is right when the British monarch gives royal assent to legislation she privately opposes, and wrong if she refused assent altogether; I think the Swedish monarch’s annual address praising general civic values is acceptable, but using that same occasion to condemn a specific immigration statute would be improper; I feel the Japanese Emperor’s presence at state memorials for the war dead is appropriate to his role as symbol of the state, yet I would feel uneasy if he spoke on whether Article Nine should be revised, even though both concern the nation’s past. The case that troubles me involves reserve powers: I understand that a Scandinavian monarch in an earlier era might refuse to appoint a government he judged unconstitutional and thereby preserve democratic order, yet if a present-day Scandinavian monarch did the identical thing I would call it an abuse of reserve powers. So my verdicts seem to turn on something like the distinction between procedural neutrality and substantive interference, yet they also seem sensitive to historical context in a way that pure democratic theory does not easily explain, and I wonder whether a principle weighing symbolic capital against representative legitimacy might do better. Is there a precise principle that captures all of these judgments, or must I concede that tradition and context do irreducible work? Please reply in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; state any principle clearly, apply it to an unlisted case such as a reigning monarch using private wealth to fund litigation challenging government policy, and tell me honestly which of my judgments it cannot accommodate.
