Hey — I'm drafting a unit on Holocaust uniqueness and modernity and I've walked myself into a corner with an argument that feels like it proves too much. Maybe you can spot the exit.

I'm starting from Bauman's basic idea: the Holocaust wasn't a pre-modern atavism breaking through, it was modern bureacratic rationality working exactly as designed — dividing labor, optimizing processes, distancing moral agents from outcomes. Meanwhile the Historikerstreit defenders of uniqueness (I'm not going to name-check everyone) insisted that treating the Holocaust as comparable to Stalinist terror or colonial genocide normalizes it and erases its singular moral status. But Bauman's point seems to cut both ways: precisely because modern rationality is everywhere, the Holocaust is a warning that applies everywhere, which sounds like comparability built into the analysis.

So here's the chain I wrote down:

(1) If an event E is historically unique in the strong remembrance-relevant sense, then there must be some feature F that E has and no other actual or possible event has.

(2) The uniqueness claim usually points to the combination of total ideological intent — destroying every member of a group everywhere — with modern bureaucratic execution.

(3) Bauman shows that modern bureaucratic rationality is precisely what makes that total intent executable at scale; the bureaucracy doesn't just carry out the intent, it transforms moral responsibility into technical task-completion.

(4) But if bureacratic rationality is a general feature of modernity, then any modern society with a comparable ideological commitment could replicate that structural form.

(5) So feature F — bureaucratic execution of total intent — isn't metaphysically unique; it's replicable in principle.

(6) Therefore, on the uniqueness-defender's own criterion (1), the Holocaust isn't unique.

(7) If it isn't unique, then comparing it structurally to other bureaucratic atrocities (Stalinist purges, some colonial administrative massacres) isn't normalization but accurate analysis.

(8) But uniqueness-defenders say that such comparison collapses the special moral demand of remembrance — that we must not treat the Holocaust as one data point among others.

(9) So they face a choice: deny (1) and let uniqueness rest on something other than an unshared feature (maybe its actual historical role in our collective memory), or accept that uniqueness is false and remembrance needs reconceiving.

(10) I try to save uniqueness by saying it's token-unique, not type-unique: this specific occurrance happened only once, and that's what remembrance honors.

(11) But if uniqueness is just token-uniqueness, then every specific atrocity is equally token-unique, so the claim becomes trivial.

(12) So either uniqueness is false, or it's trivial, or I have to reject Bauman's modernity thesis — which feels historically wrong.

(13) I feel like I've pulled a fast one somewhere between (6) and (8), maybe an equivocation between structural replicability and moral comparability, or a modal slide. Which step should I actually cut, and what's the price? I'd rather keep Bauman and some form of uniqueness, but I want to know what I'm paying.
