I have been attempting to work through museum restitution debates—particularly the disputes concerning the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles—with a colleague in cultural policy, and we repeatedly encounter the same structural obstacle. We concur on the relevant historical and empirical matters: the conditions of acquisition, the present physical state of the objects, their institutional contexts, and even that they carry significance for diverse constituencies. Our disagreement does not concern any discrete fact, but rather the governing framework through which the object is to be evaluated. One approach treats the artifact principally as an element of universal human heritage, for which the decisive considerations are preservation standards, scholarly accessibility, and the breadth of global public encounter. The other approach treats it principally as an active component within an ongoing cultural, ritual, or political practice, for which the decisive considerations are communal authorization, functional continuity, and the capacity of the object to perform the work for which it was originally created. Notably, neither framework is unintelligible to the other; each can be translated and partially accommodated within the opposing view, yet each is ultimately judged deficient rather than false, because the opposing side is seen as applying appropriate criteria to an inappropriate domain.

Is there an established concept—within philosophy of culture, metaontology, or the theory of value—that names this precise form of deadlock? By this I mean a dispute in which interlocutors share factual premises, can reciprocally recognize each other's normative commitments, and agree that both sets of considerations have some weight, yet reach contrary practical conclusions because they assign different authoritative frames to the identical object. If such a concept exists, I would like to understand how it is distinguished from mere verbal disagreement, conceptual pluralism, incommensurability, or standard normative conflict. If no adequate term exists, please propose one together with a precise definition, specify the conditions that distinguish it from its nearest neighbors, indicate its typical symptomatic features, and delineate its boundary cases—especially those in which one frame asserts universal jurisdiction while the other asserts local or functional priority.
