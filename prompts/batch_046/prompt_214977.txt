I was arguing with two friends from my rationalist meetup about Solomonoff induction and whether it's basically cursed for anyone who cares about consequences. One of them (call him J) was saying the universal prior is malign in a specific way: since it assigns nonzero weight to every computable world-description, some of those descriptions contain enormous populations of consequentialist agents experiencing extreme suffering or flourishing, and any decision procedure that uses the prior has to implicitly deal with the moral weight of all those embedded agents. He thinks this makes the prior practically unusable for ethical reasoning because you can't just condition it away — the weight is still there, and if your'e a consequentialist yourself your stuck weighing outcomes across hypotheses in a way that explodes.

The other friend (M) said that's a category error. She pointed out the agents "inside" the prior are just syntactic objects — strings in other computable descriptions — and they don't get moral standing just because they appear in a high-complexity hypothesis. She also said J was smuggling in an ontology where every computable description is equally "real" in some multiverse sense, which isn't required by Solomonoff induction at all. The prior is just an epistemic tool for prediction, not an ethical multiverse generator. She argued that if you actually do decision theory with it, you condition on your observations and act in your own world, so these other embedded consequentialists don't enter your utility calculation.

J came back that this misses the point: if you take the prior seriously as representing your uncertainty, then probabilities of actions affecting "other branches" (if you think computation that way) or just the general problem of caring about all computable minds makes the prior malign for consequentialists specifically. Non-consequentialists don't care, but consequentialists have to face the horror of all those weighted suffering agents. M replied that even for consequentialists there's no causal path, and without causality there's no obligation. Plus she said if J's right then every possible decision theory collapses under logical possibility spaces, not just Solomonoff.

Can you adjudicate this? Who's right about the malignancy claim, about whether embedded consequentialists in the prior have moral weight, and about whether this is a special problem for consequentialists versus other moral theories? Also what's both sides missing — maybe something about how the prior actually updates, or about the difference between epistemic weight and ontological commitment?
