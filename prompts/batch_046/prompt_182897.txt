Grade the following exam answer. The question was: "Does the inefficacy objection defeat individual obligations to reduce carbon emissions, abstain from meat, or vote in large elections? Evaluate whether appeals to collective harm or expressive value overcome it, paying attention to structural differences between cumulative and threshold-based harms."

Student answer: "The inefficacy objection holds that when a group is large enough, any single act makes no perceptible difference to the outcome. In climate change, one person's emissions are negligible against global output; in factory farming, skipping one meal rarely saves a particular animal; in elections, one vote rarely breaks a tie. So the objection applies with equal initial force across all three cases.

Some philosophers answer it through collective-harm reasoning: each person should avoid being part of a jointly harmful practice. But this reasoning maps differently onto cumulative versus threshold harms. Climate damage is cumulatively additive—each emission marginally increases harm—whereas voting operates near a sharp threshold at fifty percent. Applying collective-harm reasoning to climate without treating it as threshold-based risks misrepresenting the physics, so for climate the reasoning must be restricted to additive contribution rather than discrete tipping points.

A second response appeals to expressive value: acting sustainably expresses a moral stance regardless of causal impact. This works well for meat consumption, where purchasing expresses endorsement of the practice. But it fails for voting, because the secret ballot removes any audience and therefore eliminates expressive force entirely. Consequently, collective reasoning best answers climate action once properly restricted, expressive reasoning answers meat consumption, and for voting the inefficacy objection remains intact since neither response applies."
