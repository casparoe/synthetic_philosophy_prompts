I've been working through recent literature in population ethics, and a senior colleague offered me the following summary of David Benatar's asymmetry argument, which I suspect flattens several distinctions. According to him, Benatar holds that there is a strict evaluative asymmetry between suffering and pleasure with respect to potential persons: the absence of suffering in a non-existent individual is positively good, whereas the absence of pleasure in that same non-existent individual is not bad. From this asymmetry, he derives that every act of procreation is morally impermissible, because it necessarily introduces actual suffering into the world without generating a corresponding counterbalancing harm (the deprivation of pleasure) that could be weighed against it in an impersonal calculus. He further claimed that Benatar's argument covertly depends on a person-affecting moral framework — the view that only actual or potential individuals can be the subjects of harm or benefit — yet Benatar violates this framework by attributing a positive value, namely the absence of suffering, to a subject who never exists. Finally, he concluded that once we abandon the person-affecting restriction and adopt a purely impersonal axiology, the asymmetry dissolves, and procreation becomes permissible whenever expected aggregate well-being is positive.

I am unsure which parts of this characterization are accurate, which are standard pedagogical simplifications, and which are genuine distortions. In particular: does Benatar actually invoke, or even need, a person-affecting restriction, or does he explicitly reject it in favor of an impersonal evaluation of absence? Is the asymmetry anchored in a comparison between existence and non-existence for one and the same potential individual, or does it apply symmetrically across all possible persons regardless of identity? And does Benatar's argument genuinely treat non-existence as a state that can be good for someone, or is that a misreading of his claim that absence has impersonal value? I would appreciate an audit that separates faithful reconstructions from subtle misrepresentations — especially regarding the ontology of potential persons, the role of identity in the asymmetry, and whether the argument can survive without presupposing a subject of benefit.
