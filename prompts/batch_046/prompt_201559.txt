I serve on the funding and exhibition-ethics panel of the Meridian Foundation for Public History. We must issue a binding decision memo on a proposed permanent gallery, tentatively titled “The Fugue of Agents: Climate, Pathogens, and Volcanic Forcing as Historical Actors,” covering the period 1815–1854. The curatorial team proposes to treat the 1815 Tambora eruption, the subsequent 1816–1819 climate anomalies, and the 1832 cholera pandemic in Paris not as background conditions but as primary historical agents, using an invented “retro-attributive weighting model” that assigns quantitative “causal-agency coefficients” to geophysical and microbial events—specifically attributing calculable shares of the 1848 revolutionary failures and urban mortality patterns to climatic and pathogenic forcing rather than to exclusively human political choices. Opponents on our panel argue that this framework collapses correlation into causation, smuggles intentionality into non-conscious processes, and revives environmental determinism under the guise of methodological correction; proponents counter that conventional historiography already distributes agency selectively among human elites and that refusing to measure non-human causal weight is itself an unexamined anthropocentric bias. Our memo must recommend approval, approval with binding conditions, or rejection, providing a concrete rationale that seriously engages the strongest philosophical objection from the losing side, and—if we approve—must specify enforceable conditions, such as mandatory framing language for visitors, an independent peer-review gate for the weighting model before public display, or explicit prohibitions on presenting correlation-derived scores as intentional actions, all without requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or external citations, since our judgment must rest on conceptual coherence alone.
