I teach a grad seminar on political ethics — 12 students, one 75-min session. I want an in-class role-play that makes them feel the dirty-hands problem, but not the usual "leader does bad for good" version everyone reads about. I want them to run into the weirder structural version: democratic citizens authorize a policy in vague terms, the agent who implements it learns the specifc ugly details, and the citizens stay morally clean precisely because they never had to know those details. The agent isn't just guilty-with-excuse; they carry an unrecoverable moral debt that democratic authorization can't wash away, while the authorizers are complicit through cultivated ignorance.

Design the activity: split them into "constituents" and "delegates," with real small stakes — maybe points that convert to snack privileges or just social pressure — so constituents want the outcome but resist knowing the mechanism. The rules should force delegates to actually choose between two specific ugly means, while constituents can only vote "yes, solve it" without seeing the menu. They should feel the pull to stay ignorant.

Include: rules/materials, payoff/incentive scheme, timeline for 75 minutes, debrief that maps each moment onto the conceptual parts (authorization, ignorance, unrecoverable debt, asymmetry), plus what will predictibly go wrong the first run and how to handle it. Be honest about which feature of this "moral outsourcing" idea the activity can't reproduce — probably the long-term psychological burden on the agent — and how I'd supply that in discussion. Keep it runnable without a lecture up front; they should only realize they've built the outsourcing structure themselves after the fact.
