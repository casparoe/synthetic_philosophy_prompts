I've been writing about democratic backsliding for years and I'm particualrly annoyed that the debate hasn't moved: Hungary, Poland, Turkey, the US — same four examples, same circular arguments. One side uses V-Dem indicators and talks about executive aggrandisement and court capture; another side says unless there's an explicit constitutional breach it's just populism with a fancy partisan label; and a third camp claims the concept smuggles in liberal proceduralism, as if popular sovereignty can't be democratic unless it behaves like a Western centre-left government. Each describes thier opponents as missing the point. My working theory is that the stall is conceptual, not empirical — we all see the same election tweaks and media consolidation — but we're fighting over whether backsliding needs a prior democratic baseline that some of these countries never really had, versus just a directional change from whatever existed. Is that the real fracture, or is it verbal, or are they answering different questions — one about institutional health, one about normative legitimacy? Diagnose the disagreement: is there a hidden distinction, maybe between institutional vandalism and institutional atrophy, that's being ignored? What's actually driving the impasse, and what — a distinction, a case study, a formal result — could actually move it?
