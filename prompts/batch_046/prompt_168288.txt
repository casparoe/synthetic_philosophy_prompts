I serve on the technical review panel for a regional authority that approves computational models used to justify environmental and public-health interventions—watershed management, epidemic-response planning, ecological risk assessment. Every quarter we evaluate submissions containing gross simplifications—uniform soil permeability, homogeneous human mixing, linear dose-response scaling—defended by modelers who invoke the F-twist in Friedman’s classic argument: that an assumption’s lack of realism is irrelevant if the model’s predictions are accurate. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a one-time opinion, that a panel of scientists and policy officers can run in about thirty minutes per assumption, with clear sign-off points. It must translate that principle into an ordered sequence of questions, specifying for each step what counts as an adequate answer, where it issues a definitive accept or reject, and—crucially—where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining dispute is normative, contextual, or politically accountable rather than empirical.

The procedure should be presented as a flowchart in words, with a small set of test conditions for each gate, and it must flag every step that is a judgment call disguised as a mechanical rule. In particular, it needs to distinguish predictive accuracy within the observed range of data—interpolation—from predictive accuracy under the structural changes that the proposed intervention itself introduces, since I regard the collapse of these two as the most common failure mode in applied modeling. Please also state explicitly what the procedure cannot do and where Friedman-style reasoning legitimately reaches its limit. Then apply it to three assumptions from recent submissions: (1) a watershed model treating soil permeability as spatially uniform despite well-documented geological heterogeneity; (2) an epidemic model using homogeneous mixing for a pathogen spread primarily through superspreading events in specific indoor venues; and (3) an evacuation-planning model assuming that all households make rational, fully informed cost-benefit calculations—an assumption that reproduces historical evacuation data with high fidelity, but that I personally find ethically troubling when used to justify future mandatory-evacuation policies. Include at least one case where the procedure reaches a conclusion I will dislike, and explain clearly why that result is still the correct output of the principle being operationalized.
