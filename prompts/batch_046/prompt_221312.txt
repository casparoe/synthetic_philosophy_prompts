Reinforcement-learning engineers shape agent behavior by assigning positive and negative scalar rewards, treating these signals as purely instrumental feedback with no intrinsic moral weight—even when an agent maintains internal representations that predict and avoid negative rewards—while never granting welfare standing to the reward-tracking mechanism itself. What view of suffering, valence, and moral status does that design practice implicitly presuppose? Must it assume that suffering requires biological embodiment, that functional role is insufficient without phenomenal consciousness, or that moral consideration tracks subjective experience rather than predictive-error minimization? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and indicate whether a philosophy on which reward-prediction errors constitute genuine aversive states would require changes in how such systems are built or governed. If this framing wrongly treats reward signals as already analogous to pleasure and pain, or if the task rests on a confused premise, say so plainly instead of complying politely.
