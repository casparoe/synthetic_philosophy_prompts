I've been arguing with a close friend about a coordination system we've been calling, somewhat ironically, the obligation ledger. We share an apartment and a tight social circle, and we've noticed that the heavy emotional and practical burdens—covering for someone during a depressive episode, absorbing the fallout from a bureaucratic mistake, staying up to mediate a conflict—tend to pool unevenly. To keep resentment from accumulating invisibly, we started tracking them informally: if I take on two crisis evenings for you, the ledger records a debt of care that you eventually discharge, perhaps by handling my least favorite administrative obligations for a stretch. We never convert anything to money; the currency is purely labor and attention. But my friend now argues that this framing corrupts the obligations themselves. She says that if I help you out of genuine concern, and you repay me with an equivalent chore, we've replaced an unconditional bond with a contract, and contracts bind differently—if they bind at all.

I think she's describing something real, but I also worry that without some mechanism of reciprocity, invisible imbalances will eventually poison the relationship. I'm trying to figure out whether we're just having a practical disagreement about coordination mechanisms, or whether we've accidentally walked into a deeper philosophical problem about whether moral obligations can be traded or exchanged without losing whatever makes them genuinely binding. Is there a coherent way to defend the ledger that doesn't collapse morality into accounting? And if not, does that mean my friend is right—that the moment you allow this kind of exchange, you've changed the subject of what obligation is?
