As a retired physicist now studying moral philosophy, I am grading a graduate examination. The question posed was: Explain G.E.M. Anscombe's objection to honoring Harry S. Truman for ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki; clarify how she distinguishes directing one's will at the death of innocent people from merely foreseeing such deaths as an unavoidable side-effect; and evaluate whether that distinction survives translation into a "nodal" framework that distributes moral agency across networks of decision, execution, and institutional command rather than localizing it in a single agent-act relation.

The student answered:

"Anscombe protested because she held that killing innocent civilians is never permissible, whatever good outcome might follow. She applies the Thomistic doctrine of double effect, which requires that the harmful result be neither intended nor employed as a means, together with a condition of proportionate reason. In the bombings, the civilian deaths were not collateral but were intended as a means to compel surrender, so the doctrine is violated. If we adopt a nodal model of responsibility, Truman's order is merely one node in a chain including pilots, engineers, and governing bodies. Because Anscombe locates the wrong in the direct orientation of the agent's will toward harm, and because in a distributed network no single node bears that direct orientation in isolation, her prohibition dissolves for collective actions. Hence her view is unsuited to institutional warfare."
