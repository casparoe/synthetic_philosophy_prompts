I hold that the legal-moral category of genocide should remain anchored to its Lemkin-inspired core—acts directed at destroying a protected group as such through an intent-to-destroy clause—and that this narrowness is a feature rather than a bug. I think expansions to cover cultural destruction, ecological devastation, or structural violence dilute the category's specific normative force, and that the Convention's exclusion of purely political groups correctly distinguishes annihilation from ordinary state repression. What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical cases or legal developments show the intent clause is either unenforceably vague or ideologically loaded; whether there are structurally analogous mass harms that the narrow frame systematically excludes and that carry comparable moral weight; whether defenders have qualified the view into unfalsifiability—say, by stretching 'intent' to cover all collective harm or contracting it to exclude every contested case; and what a committed defender should themselves concede as genuinely damaging rather than dismissing. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish predictions that separate this view from broader atrocity frameworks, and say whether the category's insulation serves legal precision or merely protects it from moral scrutiny.
