I serve as the faculty philosopher on my university’s investment-ethics board, which must review every proposed revenue-generating partnership—land leases, patent licenses, or pooled instruments—before it reaches the trustees. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomia (natural acquisition ordered to use) and chrematistike (exchange-driven acquisition ordered to profit), and in his warning that money functions properly as measure (nomisma) but corrupts practice when treated as reproductive capital (tokos). The deliverable should be an ordered set of questions my board can run in sequence: for each, what answer is sufficient, where the procedure halts with a rejection, and where it must yield to a value judgment that cannot be proceduralized. Apply it to three proposals: a fixed-rent solar-lease of fallow farmland; licensing a life-science patent to an intermediary shell company that will sub-license for markup; and placing endowment capital in a commodity-futures pool. The bottom line—the procedure’s core rules and stopping conditions—must come in the very first sentence, followed by reasoning, with no preamble or repetition of the prompt; include one case where the procedure permits what the board will find objectionable, and state explicitly what the procedure is powerless to decide.
