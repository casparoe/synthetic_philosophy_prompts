Two colleagues at a philosophy seminar, Alex and Riley, got into a sustained disagreement about whether epiphenomenalism can survive the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Alex argued that the paradox is fatal: if phenomenal properties lack all causal efficacy, they cannot cause our judgments about them. That leaves only two options—either our phenomenal judgments are caused by physical brain states and are therefore about those brain states rather than about consciousness, or they have no cause at all and are inexplicable. Either way, epiphenomenalism undermines the very knowledge it needs to state itself. Riley responded that this relies on an overly causal theory of reference and judgment formation. She proposed that phenomenal judgments are formed through direct acquaintance with the properties, where acquaintance is not a causal relation but a primitive epistemic one: the brain state produces both the quale and the judgment, but the judgment is about the quale because the quale is present in the experience that constitutes the judgment's basis, not because it causes the judgment. Alex pressed her on this: if the quale plays no causal or functional role in producing the judgment, then the judgment's content should be determined entirely by the physical processes that actually produce it, which means it is about the brain state. He added that calling the relation "acquaintance" just relabels the correlation without explaining why the judgment targets one correlate rather than the other. Riley then introduced a more specific distinction: she said phenomenal concepts are "demonstratively anchored"—formed by attending to the property within experience—and that this anchoring requires presence but not causation. She compared it to how one might form a concept of a shadow by attending to it, even though the shadow does not cause the attention (the object does). Alex replied that shadows are visible effects of objects, so attending to a shadow is still causally mediated by light, and that the analogy actually supports his view: we attend to shadows through physical processes, not through some direct non-causal link. Riley countered that phenomenal properties are unlike shadows precisely because they are not effects but intrinsic features of experience, so there is no mediating process analogous to light—the physical process generates the experience, and within that experience the property is present and can be conceptually isolated. Alex said that if the physical process generates both the property and the conceptual isolation, then the isolation is still a physical process, and the property's presence is redundant to the explanation of the judgment. They asked me to referee their exchange and determine who is right about each move, whether Riley's demonstrative-anchoring proposal saves epiphenomenalism or collapses into interactionism, and what both of them are missing.
