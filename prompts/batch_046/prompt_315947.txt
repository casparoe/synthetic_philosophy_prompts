I am drafting the final examination for a doctoral seminar in comparative political ethics that I am co-teaching. The exam is three hours long, and I need one essay question—worth forty percent of the total grade, with an expected response of roughly 1,500 words—together with a model answer and a granular marking rubric.

The conceptual core of the course concerns what counts as corruption, specifically the boundary between clientelism, reciprocal gift-giving, and bribery, and whether Western anti-corruption standards are genuinely universal or culturally particular. To prevent answers that merely summarize familiar literature, the question should be anchored in a constructed scenario that forces conceptual analysis rather than recall: imagine a municipal administration in a context with strong traditions of communal reciprocity that launches a digital platform through which citizens earn algorithmic priority in public-service queues—faster permit approvals, heightened visibility in civic forums—by contributing voluntary labor and symbolic endorsements to local officials' community-development projects. The contributions are publicly framed as customary gift obligations, not as contractual payments, and no explicit quid-pro-quo language is used.

The essay prompt should ask students to determine whether this arrangement constitutes corruption, and whether any such judgment necessarily presupposes Western legal-rational norms or can be defended on cross-culturally valid grounds. The model answer must make explicit the analytical moves that distinguish corruption from legitimate reciprocity—particularly whether algorithmic priority counts as a private gain extracted from a public office, and whether the absence of an explicit contract alters the moral structure of the exchange. The marking scheme should identify the specific arguments that earn top marks—such as maintaining the tension between universalist and relativist positions without collapsing into either—and should specify common errors that should cost points, for instance conflating all gift-exchange with bribery without addressing the misuse-of-office condition, or asserting cultural relativism without engaging with the conceptual definition of corruption.

Please do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation requests in the question, the model answer, or the rubric; students are expected to draw on conceptual knowledge developed over the seminar. Avoid framing the answer around specific author-date references; the grading criteria should focus on the precision of conceptual distinctions and the coherence of the normative argument.
