I work as a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to articulate a view about mathematical objects that borrows from how we think about abstraction in computing. The position is this: mathematical structures—numbers, functions, sets—are neither mental entities nor spatiotemporal objects, but invariant fixed points of any system that satisfies specific structural constraints, analogous to an interface or abstract data type that can have many concrete implementations but is defined solely by the relationships it enforces. On this account, our epistemic access to mathematics is not mysterious causal interaction with abstract entities; it is the ability to construct and verify correct implementations, whether in formal proofs, algorithms, or physical computing processes. The view aims to preserve the objectivity and necessity of mathematics—there is a fact of the matter about whether an implementation satisfies the constraints—while avoiding the standard epistemological worry about accessing a non-physical realm.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of objections its defenders can easily deflect. Specifically:

- Which arguments, intuitions, formal results, or empirical developments in mathematics or computer science should genuinely move someone holding this view? Sort them from decisive—something that would force abandonment—to merely awkward—something that requires a qualification or auxiliary assumption.

- Are there predictions this view makes that pure platonism, nominalism, or formalism do not make? For instance, does it commit me to the claim that any correct implementation of a structure reveals the same mathematical facts, and what if there appear to be genuinely incompatible but equally valid implementations?

- Has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability by defining "structural constraint" or "implementation" so broadly that no counterexample could arise? If so, is that insulation a virtue—protecting a necessary framework—or a vice—rendering the view empirically empty?

- Which of these potential defeaters would a committed defender of the interface-style structuralist concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of what implementation independence requires? I want the distinction made from inside the view, not only from the perspective of critics.

- Include any developments that have arguably already occurred—say, in formal verification, constructive mathematics, or the practice of computer-aided proof—that bear on whether mathematical truth is indeed implementation-independent.

- Finally, what should I personally regard as the most serious risk: that mathematical practice reveals irreducible ambiguity in structural interpretation, that the analogy to software interfaces breaks down at some critical point (for example, with non-constructive existence proofs or with infinite structures that have no computable implementation), or that the view collapses into a familiar rival once the qualifications are made explicit?

Please rank the defeaters, assess whether the position risks anything substantive, and be willing to conclude that it is well insulated if that is the honest assessment—while saying whether that insulation is philosophically satisfying or problematic for a view of this kind.
