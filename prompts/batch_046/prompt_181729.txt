I'm working through Søren Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (1843), specifically Problema I, where he introduces the teleological suspension of the ethical through Abraham. I've been focusing on a passage that I find structurally puzzling, and I'd like help parsing its logical architecture precisely. The text reads:

"The ethical as such is the universal, it applies to everyone, and the same thing is expressed from another point of view by saying that it applies every instant. [...] Faith is precisely this paradox, that the individual as the particular is higher than the universal, is justified over against it, is not subordinate but superior — yet in such a way, be it observed, that it is the particular individual who, after he has been subordinated as the particular to the universal, now through the universal becomes the individual who as the particular is superior to the universal, for the fact that the individual as the particular stands in an absolute relation to the absolute."

I'm particularly stuck on the clause that the movement "repeats itself." Kierkegaard insists that the particular must first be subordinated to the universal and then, "through the universal," become superior to it. But I'm unsure whether this describes a temporal biographical sequence (the agent lives ethically, then receives a call that transcends it) or a logical structure that must be present in the very moment of suspension (the ethical is simultaneously affirmed and negated). There's also the question of what "through the universal" means: is the universal serving as a mediating term that is then transcended, or is it preserved in the act of suspension?

To test my understanding, I've constructed a niche extension that doesn't appear in Kierkegaard's own examples. Suppose an agent — call her a "pre-ethical particular" — never actually accepts the universal ethical norms at all; she lives in a state of pure particularity and then claims that her particular will is justified over against the universal without ever having subordinated herself to it. Would Kierkegaard's structure classify this as a failed repetition, specifically as a temptation (*Anfechtung*) rather than genuine faith? Or does the "movement repeats itself" require that the universal actually be incorporated and then suspended, such that anyone who skips the first moment can never reach the second?

Could you help me understand exactly how the repetition works in the passage, whether the universal is preserved or annulled in the suspension, and what this implies for my "pre-ethical particular" case? I'd like to avoid reading the suspension as a generic license for exceptionalism, but I'm not sure whether the text's logical architecture actually rules that out.
