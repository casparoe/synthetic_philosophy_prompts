I have been arguing at length with a friend about whether Keynes’s vision of drastically reduced working hours and the pursuit of the good life could survive the political economy of attention we now inhabit. She maintains that his optimism about liberated leisure was always unfounded because he assumed people would naturally gravitate toward culture, friendship, and self-cultivation once economic necessity was lifted; I contend that he had a more specific, and perhaps darker, diagnosis of what abundance does to human motivation, one that might actually anticipate our present situation. Please predict what Keynes’s position would be on a contemporary post-scarcity environment in which algorithmic platforms monetize unstructured free time by transforming leisure into continuous, low-friction, externally directed engagement—what I will call heteronomous leisure—rather than leaving it open to autonomous cultivation. Would he regard this development as a direct betrayal of the economic possibility he forecast in his essay on our grandchildren, or as a corruption that his normative framework already anticipates? In particular, how would he distinguish between leisure that genuinely serves the good life he described and leisure that operates as an extension of economic rationality into previously private temporal space? I am not asking for a biographical sketch. I want a substantive philosophical prediction that takes Keynes’s normative commitments—his suspicion of money-making as an end, his belief that the economic problem should eventually yield to the problem of living well, and his particular conception of culture and autonomy—seriously, and applies them to this specific configuration of post-work abundance.
