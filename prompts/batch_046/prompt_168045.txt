My firm maintains digital archives of cultural artifacts. We hold two files—one on a server in Reykjavik and one in Singapore—that are bit-for-bit identical copies of the same original scan, verified by matching checksums. A contract partner now argues that if the Singapore copy is corrupted, we have lost a genuinely distinct asset, because the two copies sit in different legal jurisdictions, under different data-protection regimes, and in physically separate infrastructures. I reply that since every intrinsic digital property is identical, treating them as separate holdings is metaphysically confused bookkeeping: the artwork survives intact in Iceland, so nothing with its own independent identity was destroyed. I need to decide how to register these holdings for liability, insurance, and contractual indemnity. Is my partner correct that spatial separation and jurisdictional context make these two genuinely different entities, or am I right that they are essentially one thing present in two locations? And if the distinction is philosophically real, how should it actually shape the language of the contracts?
