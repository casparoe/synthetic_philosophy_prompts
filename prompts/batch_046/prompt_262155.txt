Please rank the following three strategies for answering the epistemic objection to modal realism—the challenge that spatiotemporally isolated concrete worlds are in principle unknowable, which threatens to undermine the realist's claim that they exist—by placing each in a tier (S, A, B, C, etc.) and giving one sentence of justification for its tier. The strategies are: (1) the analogical-inference strategy, which treats modal knowledge like scientific knowledge of unobservables, justified by inference to the best explanation of our modal practices; (2) the indexical-actuality strategy, which denies that knowledge requires causal access and instead reads "there is a world where P" as an indexical claim about logical space, knowable through consistency and internal reasoning; and (3) what I will call modal structural phenomenalism: the view that concrete worlds exist, but our modal beliefs are not representations of them; instead, they are constituted by internal structural relations within our own world that mirror other worlds without referring to them, so there is no epistemic gap because there is no representational relation to fail. Evaluate each on internal coherence and resistance to the epistemic objection, not on fidelity to named authors. After your tier list, state explicitly (a) which argument or consideration—about the ontology of worlds, the psychology of modal belief, or the logic of actuality—would cause you to reorder these tiers or weaken your defense of the top-ranked strategy, and (b) where your reasoning in this ranking is most likely to fail.
