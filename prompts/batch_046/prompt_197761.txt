You are an expert in metaphysics and mereology, with particular attention to the special composition question. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm: list the cases below, note my verdict on each, and identify where they pull in opposite directions. Here are my judgments about when some things compose a further thing.

1. A wooden dining table assembled from four legs and a top. Verdict: yes, there is one table.
2. The same table after one leg is replaced with a matching spare; the original leg is stored in a closet. Verdict: yes, the current parts still compose the table; the original leg in the closet does not become part of it.
3. All four original legs and the top, disassembled and stacked in a warehouse, touching but not joined. Verdict: no, they do not compose a table.
4. A banned text preserved across fifty readers on different continents, each memorizing a segment and able to reconstruct the whole only through a shared decryption protocol they actively maintain. Verdict: yes, they compose one distributed object — call it a living archive — despite no physical adjacency.
5. The same fifty readers after the protocol is lost; each still remembers their segment, but there is no mechanism for reconstruction or interaction. Verdict: no, they are now just fifty people with partial memories; no archive is composed.
6. A hurricane tracked over ten days, with every air molecule replaced, but maintaining continuous dynamic structure through causal interaction. Verdict: yes, there is one hurricane-object composed of whatever molecules are causally integrated at each moment.
7. Those same air molecules, after the hurricane dissipates, scattered globally but coincidentally arranged in the exact same relational pattern they had inside the storm, with no causal connection. Verdict: no, they do not compose a hurricane.
8. Two autonomous cargo modules of a vessel separated by a thousand miles, both still governed by a single real-time navigation algorithm that treats them as one unit; they can dock without adjustment. Verdict: yes, they still compose one vessel.
9. The same two modules after the navigation link is severed; each runs an identical copy of the algorithm independently. Verdict: no, there are now two vessels.

My pattern seems to reward functional-causal integration over spatial contiguity and to penalize mere similarity or proximity. But there is a tension: in (2) I allow replacement of a material part while preserving the whole, which looks like I care about form or function; yet in (3) I deny composition even though the very same parts (before replacement) are present and arranged as in (1), just not joined. Meanwhile (4) allows spatial discontinuity if there is a protocol, but (9) denies it when the protocol becomes duplicated rather than shared.
