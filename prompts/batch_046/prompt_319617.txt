I have been trying to make sense of the recurring claim, in recent political and philosophical discussions, that “Western values” form a coherent normative framework that can be used to evaluate cultural practices and immigration policies. The usual list includes democratic governance, individual liberty, secular public reason, gender equality, and the rule of law, yet each item has a contested history within Western societies themselves—Athenian democracy coexisted with slavery and the exclusion of women, secular governance has often been paired with state-sponsored religion, and liberal individualism has been criticized from within the West by communitarian and socialist traditions. This makes me wonder whether the coherence being claimed is logical, historical, or institutional, and whether the phrase denotes anything more specific than a loose family resemblance.

To clarify my own thinking, I have been working with a distinction that may not have a standard label: between a “value system,” in which all norms derive from or are jointly satisfiable by a single master principle, and a “value ecology,” in which norms persist together through institutional compromise and mutual adjustment rather than logical consistency. If Western values constitute an ecology rather than a system, then the fact that secularism and religious freedom, or democratic majoritarianism and individual rights, can come into tension does not by itself disprove coherence—just as predators and prey coexist in a stable ecosystem. But this raises what seems to me a deeper problem. If the set is genuinely ecological, then there is no internal decision procedure for resolving conflicts between its constituent values: when free expression clashes with equality, or when democratic choice conflicts with human rights protections, the ecology offers no meta-criterion for adjudication. So my question is whether defenders of the category are implicitly smuggling in a hidden system—perhaps liberal democratic constitutionalism—as the actual coherence condition, and if so, whether appeals to “Western values” are not appeals to an open ecology at all, but to a specific institutional form that already resolves the conflicts in one direction.

Given all this, I want to ask whether there is a named fallacy or conceptual error involved in treating this plural, institutionally mediated cluster as if it were a unified cultural essence that can be invoked to include or exclude practices without further argument. I have tentatively called this “polysemic essentialism,” meaning the treatment of a polysemous, conflict-ridden value cluster as a single essential property of a civilization. But I am unsure whether this captures something philosophers have already identified—perhaps as a variant of the fallacy of composition, or as a confusion between descriptive genealogy and normative justification—or whether I am misdiagnosing the problem entirely. Is the incoherence of “Western values” at the level of principles actually fatal to its use in argument, or can an ecological pluralism still support determinate normative judgments in particular cases? And if the phrase does have legitimate philosophical work to do, what exactly is that work—descriptive taxonomy, normative aspiration, or rhetorical boundary-drawing?
