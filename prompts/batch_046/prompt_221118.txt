I've been trying to wrap my head around Lewis's paper on whether we actually believe in penal substitution, and I think I've stumbled into an arguement that makes the doctrine either collapse into a utilitarian free-for-all or turn into a one-off miracle with no moral logic. Here goes. (1) If it's okay for Christ to bear punishment for human sins, then there has to be some principled reason why substituting an innocent for the guilty isn't always wrong. (2) The usual candidates people float are consent, the sheer magnitude of the good achieved, or some deep identity-relation like being the representative head of humanity. (3) But consent can't carry the weight: in ordinary criminal justice we still reject it when an innocent person volunteers to serve prison time for a guilty friend, which is exactly the point Lewis makes about our everyday beliefs.

(4) So defenders lean on the identity-relation—Christ as the second Adam, literally sharing human nature, so punishing him is in some sense punishing humanity itself. (5) Here's where I inject something weird. Suppose we create a synthetic representative: a legally designated artificial collective agent that formally embodies the moral identity of a group, kind of like corporate personhood but extended so that punishing this entity counts as punishing the members. If representative headship is the real justification, then punishing this synthetic stand-in for human war crimes should be morally okay too. (6) But of course we'd reject that—punishing an AI proxy for human crimes just because it represents the group is clearly wrong, and it harms innocent stakeholders. (7) So defenders have to say Christ's relation is ontologically unique, not conventional. But then penal substitution isn't justified by any moral principle we actually recognize; it's just a singular metaphysical exception, which means Lewis's challenge—why don't we believe this in ordinary life?—gets answered by saying we shouldn't, except in this one inexplicable case.

(8) Meanwhile if we go the magnitude route—saving billions justifies punishing one innocent—then we've endorsed a utilitarian exception that applies everywhere: a state could execute one randomly selected citizen to prevent ten guilty murderers from escaping, if the utility is high enough. (9) So either penal substitution proves too much, licensing synthetic representatives and random executions whenever the math works, or it proves nothing, reducing to a miracle with no moral logic. Where's the mistake? I'd really like to keep the idea that Christ's substitution is genuinely just and not just an inexplicable exception, but I want to know what I'm paying if I hold that line.
