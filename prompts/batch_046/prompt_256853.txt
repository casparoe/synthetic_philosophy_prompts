In my doctoral work on metaphysical theories of musical expression, I have been returning to Schopenhauer’s discussion in Book III of The World as Will and Representation, where he argues that music is not merely another art that copies the Platonic Ideas—the adequate objectifications of the Will in nature—but rather an immediate copy of the Will itself, unmediated by representation. In the passage I am struggling with, he describes melody as expressing the multiform strivings of the Will through its constant rising and falling, its deviation and return to the tonic, while harmony corresponds to the gradations of the Will’s objectification, from inorganic masses in the bass to conscious individuality in the highest registers; he insists that because music bypasses the Ideas, it communicates not the phenomenon but the inner essence of the world.

My difficulty arises when I try to reconcile this with his broader claim that the Will, in itself, is entirely outside the form of time, which belongs solely to representation. Music, after all, is irreducibly temporal: it unfolds through succession, anticipation, and resolution in rhythm and melody. If the Will has no temporal properties whatsoever, then a temporal art form cannot literally be its direct image unless we understand the relation in some non-literal sense. I have considered whether Schopenhauer means that music copies the Will’s temporal manifestations in nature, but that seems to collapse music back into the representational arts he explicitly distinguishes it from. I have also toyed with the idea—perhaps too speculative—that Schopenhauer treats melody as a spatialized analog of the Will’s internal dynamics, mapping temporal progression onto structural relations that the Will itself possesses outside time, though this conflicts with his emphasis on music’s essential flow. To sharpen the problem: if bass drones represent the lowest grades of the Will’s objectification, then a sustained, melody-less drone should, on his account, present the Will more immediately than a complex symphonic movement, yet he clearly treats such minimal sound as aesthetically deficient rather than metaphysically privileged. I am hoping someone can help me understand whether Schopenhauer has a consistent account of how a temporal structure can directly image an atemporal essence without either reducing music to representation or attributing temporal form to the Will itself, and in particular whether there is a principled reason within his system for privileging melodic motion over static harmonic mass.
