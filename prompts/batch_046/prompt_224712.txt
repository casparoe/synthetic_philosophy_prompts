Been trying to wrap my head around that old Chinese School of Names debate about whether a white horse is actually a horse—where "horse" picks out the form and "white" picks out the color, so putting them together makes something different. Anyway, I keep seeing a related move in arguments about politics or economics that I dont have a word for. Someone defines a category in totally pure terms—"free market," "democracy," "pure reason"—then points out that every real-world example has some contaminating feature, like regulations or bias or emotion. They use that contamination to dismiss actual cases: "thats not a real X, so we cant learn anything from studying it." But then they immediately turn around and use the same pure definition to make normative claims—"a true democracy would never do that" or "free markets naturally tend toward equilibrium"—treating the pure thing as if it has causal or moral force, even though they just said it never actually exists. Its not quite the white horse argument because the language-vs-reality split isnt the point; its more like keeping an impossible standard around to both exclude evidence and preserve prescriptions. Does this have a name? How is it different from No True Scotsman, since in that fallacy you usually protect the definition after seeing counterexamples, but here the impurity is baked in from the start and used for both dismissal and authority? And if theres no established term, what should we call this kind of double bookkeeping?
