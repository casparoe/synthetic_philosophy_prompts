Please evaluate the following exam answer as if you were grading a philosophy-of-mind exam on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness.

Question: In split-brain patients following complete callosotomy, presenting a chicken claw to the left visual field (right hemisphere) and a snow scene to the right visual field (left hemisphere) typically yields dissociated behavior: the left hand selects a shovel, the right hand selects a chicken, and the left-hemisphere verbal system reports seeing only the snow scene and offers a confabulated justification for the shovel choice. One argument holds that because only one mouth produces a single, grammatically unified report—"I see snow, so I picked the shovel"—there remains one conscious subject with partitioned access streams rather than two distinct phenomenal subjects. Evaluate this argument. In your answer, (a) explain why the localization of speech production undermines the inference from "one speaker" to "one subject"; (b) discuss whether left-hemisphere confabulation about right-hemisphere-initiated actions supports a unified self-model or rather indicates divided agency; and (c) assess whether the patient's failure to report feeling like "two people" is genuine evidence for phenomenal unity, or whether it is a methodological artifact of the very disconnection that prevents interhemispheric comparison.

Student answer:
"(a) Speech production is localized in the left hemisphere for most right-handed patients, so the verbal report reflects only the left hemisphere's visual input. The right hemisphere processes the chicken claw but cannot produce speech, so its content is absent from the report. Therefore, the fact that one voice speaks does not entail that only one subject experiences; it shows only that one hemisphere controls the vocal apparatus. This point is well taken.

(b) When the right hemisphere initiates an action—such as selecting the shovel with the left hand—the left hemisphere often invents a reason tied to its own percept ("I picked it because of the snow"). This demonstrates that the left hemisphere lacks direct access to the right hemisphere's intentions, which supports division: a genuinely unified agent should have transparent access to all its own motives. Furthermore, this confabulation confirms the classic interpretation that split-brain patients contain two complete, linguistically competent minds, each with independent intentionality. The student then notes that shared subcortical arousal pathways do not restore unity, since emotional signaling is not sufficient for phenomenal binding.

(c) The absence of a "two people" report is not strong evidence for unity. The corpus callosum is precisely the pathway that would allow one hemisphere to compare its experience with the other; without it, there is no mechanism for meta-cognitive comparison, so silence is expected even if two subjects exist. Consequently, there is no shared phenomenal background whatsoever—the hemispheres inhabit entirely separate phenomenal worlds, and any appearance of unity is an illusion produced by the left hemisphere's narrative construction. The student concludes that the "one speaker" argument therefore collapses completely."
