Since my father received his diagnosis six months ago, I have become an unintended observer of a distinction that philosophy of action discusses in the abstract. In conversation he remains entirely flexible: he revises opinions, learns modified rules for card games, and spots logical errors in my reasoning with the same acuity he always had. Yet in certain physical routines—preparing his coffee, arranging his pills, walking to the garden gate—he executes an invariant sequence. If interrupted, he does not resume at the point of disruption; he returns to the first step and runs through the entire script again, even while acknowledging the interruption and discussing it without confusion. I have encountered the case of the sphex wasp, whose prey-inspection ritual restarts from the beginning if the prey is moved, and I understand philosophers invoke this to mark a boundary between mechanical rigidity and the adaptability associated with free action. But my father is not globally sphex-like; his rigidity is domain-specific, coexists with complex reasoning, and is sometimes accompanied by self-aware commentary about being stuck on a track. This seems to sit uneasily beside both the wasp's presumed global mechanism and ordinary habit or clinical compulsion, since those are usually either pervasive or resisted, not compartmentalized and calmly acknowledged. Is there an established term—perhaps in philosophy of mind, cognitive science, or action theory—for this kind of modular or localized rigidity, where an agent retains broad flexibility but exhibits reset-dependent, script-bound behavior in isolated domains? If the literature addresses this intermediate case, how is it distinguished from ordinary routine, from obsessive ritual, and from the global mechanical behavior the wasp exemplifies? And if it lacks a specific name, what exactly defines its edges: must the agent be meta-aware, must the script be non-functional, and how many domains can be affected before the condition collapses into something already recognized?
