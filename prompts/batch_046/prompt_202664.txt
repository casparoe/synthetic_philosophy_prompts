After a long argument with a friend about Boltzmann brains and what they imply for both cosmology and ethics, I want an outside adjudication of our exchange. We structured it as a sequence of moves; please go through them point by point, rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, and identify any shared mistakes, false dichotomies, or premises both of us left unstated. Here is how the argument ran.

Move 1 (my friend): In any cosmological model that permits infinite space-time—eternal inflation being the canonical example—thermal fluctuations will eventually produce conscious observers, or Boltzmann brains, in numbers that vastly exceed evolved biological observers. Given standard self-locating reasoning, our finding ourselves as ordinary evolved observers should therefore be treated as strong Bayesian evidence against such models.

Move 2 (me): That reasoning is not circular, but it is unnecessary. The correct measure solutions for eternal inflation suppress Boltzmann brain production to negligible levels, so the premise that they numerically dominate is physically false. The philosophical problem dissolves once the physics is done right.

Move 3 (my friend): Even with a suppressed rate, integrating over infinite volume and infinite time yields divergence unless the rate is identically zero. Avoiding dominance therefore requires exact cancellation or a hard cutoff, which amounts to fine-tuning the cosmological dynamics. So the problem reappears not as an observational puzzle but as a constraint on parameter space.

Move 4 (me): That is a naive use of infinity. Physical dynamics include natural cutoffs—false-vacuum decay, quantum gravitational effects, or eventual heat death—that prevent the literal integration to infinity. Insisting on divergence ignores the fact that cosmological models are defined by dynamical laws, not by bare set-theoretic cardinality.

Move 5 (my friend): Set cosmology aside and consider the moral analog. If Boltzmann brains are conscious and capable of suffering, and if they do outnumber us in the modal space of possibilities, then the expected total suffering in reality might be dominated by these fleeting fluctuations. This creates a kind of longtermist obligation: we should prefer cosmological theories or parameter choices that minimize the production of suffering Boltzmann brains.

Move 6 (me): The so-called moral Boltzmann problem fails on three counts. First, Boltzmann brains lack the sustained narrative identity, memory continuity, and embodied interaction that ground the moral weight of suffering in ordinary agents; treating them as equivalent is to confuse a momentary flicker with a life. Second, we have no causal access to cosmological constants, so the consideration cannot guide action. Third, using modal population frequencies across counterfactual worlds to rank moral priorities is a category error—ethics concerns actual or reachable outcomes, not statistical weights over unobservable branches.

Move 7 (my friend): On the first count, you beg the question against functionalist or integrated-information theories of consciousness, which do not require long-term memory for moral status. On the second, longtermism already assigns weight to causally distant outcomes we cannot directly influence. On the third, if we assign probabilities to cosmological theories, then selecting among them by expected value—including expected suffering—is precisely what rational belief-formation requires, not a category mistake.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each step, say who holds the stronger position and why, flag any premises that need defense but were treated as obvious, and state whether either of the broader conclusions—that Boltzmann brains undermine eternal inflation, or that they generate a genuine moral constraint on cosmological belief—actually survives once the missing distinctions are made explicit. If there is a better framing that avoids the false dichotomies we have fallen into, say what it is.
