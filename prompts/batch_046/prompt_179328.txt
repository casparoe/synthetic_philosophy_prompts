I am a retired biologist who has recently begun reading philosophy of institutions, and I keep returning to what I think of as a calibration problem in cross-cultural corruption studies. Western frameworks tend to treat gift-giving, patronage, and clientelism as deviations from an implicit baseline of impersonal, rule-governed exchange. Yet in many societies these practices do not sit atop a separate institutional layer; they constitute the primary social architecture—what I have been calling embedded reciprocity networks—within which roles, obligations, and trust are generated. This makes me skeptical of the standard framing that asks whether Western anti-corruption standards are "universal." Instead, I want to know where conceptual work should go next, and which unresolved problems deserve limited philosophical attention.

Consider three intertwined open questions. First, is there a philosophically defensible distinction between structural reciprocity—obligations that constitute and sustain social roles, such as ceremonial gift-exchange within a kinship system—and transactional reciprocity, which instrumentalizes those roles for private gain? Or does this distinction collapse once we recognize that all reciprocity is simultaneously role-constituting and interest-serving? Second, does the very concept of corruption presuppose a normative theory of legitimate institutional authority, such that labeling a practice "corrupt" already embeds a specific design ideal for the state and civil society? Or can corruption be defined in purely functional terms—say, as the diversion of collectively governed resources toward private benefit—without smuggling in Western institutional assumptions? Third, do Western transparency norms embed a particular historical ontology of the person, perhaps an atomistic, contract-bearing individual, that renders them systematically blind to relational or hierarchical personhood? And if so, is that blindness a correctable methodological bias, or an unavoidable feature of any universalist moral theory that seeks cross-cultural application?
