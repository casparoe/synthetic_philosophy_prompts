I am writing up my position for a final paper in my political philosophy course, and I want a consistency check before I defend it. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I hold an associative account of political obligation: citizens stand in a relation to their polity analogous to membership in a family, generating duties of loyalty and obedience that do not depend on consent, on benefits received, or on democratic authorship.
(2) I reject fair-play theories as the primary ground of obligation, because they make duty contingent on accepting benefits, which allows too easy an exit and cannot explain obligations to future generations who have not yet received any.
(3) I also deny that democratic procedures have intrinsic legitimating force; democratic authority is purely instrumental—valuable only insofar as it produces substantively just outcomes.
(4) From (1) and (3) together, I conclude that citizens have a robust obligation to obey even substantively unjust laws, provided those laws do not require direct moral complicity (for example, actively enforcing a caste-based restriction). The associative bond persists independently of the law’s justice.
(5) At the same time, I accept a broadly Rawlsian framework for civil disobedience: it is permissible only when it addresses a serious injustice, is public and non-violent, is a last resort, and is undertaken with a willingness to accept legal punishment, which expresses fidelity to the rule of law.
(6) But I also think that in cases of direct moral complicity—where obedience itself makes one an agent of injustice—disobedience is not merely permitted but obligatory, and the willingness-to-accept-punishment condition is inappropriate, because accepting punishment in such cases treats an illegitimate legal framework as worthy of respect.

My question is whether these commitments actually hang together. Specifically: does my rejection of fair play and democratic intrinsic value in (2) and (3) undermine the Rawlsian conditions in (5), since those conditions seem to presuppose that the legal system as a whole deserves a baseline of respect that I have denied? Is there a hidden tension between (1) and (6): if the associative bond is like a family duty, does obligatory disobedience in cases of complicity amount to a justified betrayal of that bond, or does it show that the bond was never unconditional? Does (4) sit comfortably with (5) and (6), or does my claim that obligation persists for unjust but non-complicit laws make the threshold for justified disobedience either arbitrarily drawn or impossibly high? And are there only apparent tensions—perhaps between (1) and (5)—that dissolve once we see that accepting punishment can be an expression of associative loyalty rather than a concession to democratic legitimacy?

Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate which pairs are genuinely incompatible versus merely apparently so, and tell me what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency. I am especially interested in whether I need to abandon the associative ground in (1), restrict the scope of (4), or modify the Rawlsian conditions in (5).
