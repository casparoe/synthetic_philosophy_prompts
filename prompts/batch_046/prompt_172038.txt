My study partner claims Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis undermines itself once you distinguish two ways a contradiction can be settled. Here is the argument: (1) Fukuyama holds that history ends when a political form resolves the contradictions that previously drove ideological evolution, leaving no universalist competitor. (2) Liberal democracy, he argues, resolves the tension between universal recognition and particular identity by institutionalizing rights and markets. (3) If a contradiction is genuinely resolved—call this being dialectically dead—it can no longer generate the pressure that produces a successor form. (4) Therefore, if liberal democracy truly kills its founding contradictions, the Hegelian engine of history stops, which is Fukuyama's desired conclusion. (5) But liberal democracy also preserves open discourse, which makes it impossible to render alternatives conceptually unthinkable; at best it renders contradictions politically marginal. (6) Call being politically marginal but conceptually available dialectically dormant. (7) A dormant contradiction still supplies the raw material for a future synthesis, so the Hegelian mechanism remains intact. (8) Hence Fukuyama needs contradictions to be dead (to block successors) yet can only deliver dormancy (because of open discourse), which means his endpoint is unstable. (9) So either he abandons the Hegelian progress criterion or he must reject open discourse as essential to liberal democracy. Which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost in terms of either Fukuyama's Hegelianism or his defense of liberal democracy, and does the distinction between dead and dormant do any real work or is it merely a relabeling?
