ok so for my workshop paper I'm trying to lay out my actual commitments on transitional justice and see if they hang together. I'm in teh philosophy/poli sci borderland, so bear with me. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Retributive justice is institutionally inappropriate in transitional contexts because the legal order that collapsed was itself complicit; the state lacks moral standing to punish past abuses.

(2) Amnesty should only be granted if it's conditional on full disclosure — basically the South African bargain, but I think the disclosure part is doing all the moral work.

(3) The main point of a truth commission isn't reconciliation or healing, it's epistemic repair: rebuilding a shared factual record so democratic deliberation can actually function afterwards. Everything else is secondary.

(4) Argentina was right to overturn its 1980s amnesty laws and prosecute the juntas, even though it risked democratic instability at the time.

(5) Victims have a non-waivable right to see perpetrators held accountable — you can't democratically vote away someone else's claim to justice.

(6) But if a democratic collective genuinely chooses amnesty for stability, that choice should bind future governments; retrospective repeal violates democratic continuity.

(7) Rwanda's gacaca courts got closer to actual justice than the ICTR did, because they embedded accountability inside local moral economies instead of importing international rights talk.

(8) Also I think amnesty shouldn't be understood as forgiveness but more like a moral bankruptcy: the perpetrator's debt to the victim isn't erased, it's transferred to the collective as an epistemic burden — the community has to carry the knowledge without being able to discharge it through punishment.

So... do these hang together? Specifically I'm worried about (2) and (5): if victims can't waive their right to punishment, then conditional amnesty still violates that right, right? And (4) vs (6) looks like a direct clash about Argentina. But also (1) and (5) feel like they pull in different directions — if the state lacks standing, why is there still a victim right that demands state action? Maybe that's only apparent though.
