Write a Petrarchan sonnet—rhyme scheme ABBA ABBA CDE CDE, with the volta falling precisely at line 9—about the wrong-kind-reasons problem, framed through the experience of a software engineer admiring a colleague’s algorithm. In the octave, the speaker should list genuinely motivating reasons for the admiration that are nevertheless of the wrong kind: the code’s compression will reduce server costs, its conciseness will impress senior reviewers, its efficiency will make the speaker’s own contributions look sharper by comparison. These should be reasons that genuinely cause the admiration without justifying it in the relevant evaluative sense. At the volta in line 9, the speaker should suddenly recognize the misfit: these benefits are instrumental, not responses to the intrinsic structural value of the solution, analogous to praising a mathematical proof for its page length rather than for its insight. The sestet should then attempt to articulate a reason of the right kind—perhaps the direct correspondence between the logical shape of the problem and the formal architecture of the code—while conceding that this reason feels elusive and may still be contaminated by practical benefit. The rhyme scheme itself should do conceptual work: in the octave, pair rhymes that evoke utility and external reward; in the sestet, shift to rhymes that evoke formal fit and structural integrity, so that the sonic transition mirrors the philosophical turn. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with the wrong-kind-reasons debate in value theory—specifically the distinction between reasons that justify an evaluative attitude and reasons that merely cause it—recognizes the structure of the problem rather than encountering a decorative label.
