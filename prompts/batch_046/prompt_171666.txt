A familiar defence of methodological holism compares social structures to the grammar of a natural language: individual agents are like speakers producing utterances, but the rules that render those actions intelligible are not reducible to any personal intention and exist only at the collective level. Holists invoke this to argue that institutions and norms possess autonomous explanatory power. Audit the analogy in detail. Provide a correspondence table mapping speakers, utterances, grammatical rules, language change, and speech communities onto individuals, actions, social norms, historical shifts, and groups. Then locate the fractures: grammar can be reconstructed from a finite corpus of performances in ways that institutions rarely permit; a speaker may violate syntax deliberately without dissolving the language, whereas breaching a norm often redefines it; and language acquisition is biologically canalised in ways that socialisation is not. Next, import Chomsky's distinction between I-language—the internal, individual computational system—and E-language—the external, socially distributed practice. Does this linguistic framework strengthen the holist claim, or does it undermine it, given that Chomsky's programme applies methodological individualism precisely to language? Conclude by assessing whether the holist's conclusion—that macro-level sociology is irreducible—survives once these disanalogies, particularly the I-language/E-language split, are exposed, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work.
