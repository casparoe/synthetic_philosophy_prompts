I've been thinking way too much about automation and what work actually is, not just jobs but the real thing underneath. Me and a friend got into this idea we call "fricional engagement" — maybe we made it up, I don't know — that real work isn't about sweating or getting paid, it's about the specific resistance a task gives back to you while you're doing it. So if a robot can run through something smooth with no pushback, then it was never really work in the deep sense, just occupational noise filling up the calendar. That's why people still do carpentry or garden even when they don't need the money, cause the wood or soil actually resists you in a way that matters. But I want to be honest about where this falls apart. What would actually count against it? Not just "people need income" — I get that part. I mean which observations or arguments should really move me? Are there things that feel high-resistance but nobody calls work? Or automation that somehow makes the human part more resistant instead of less? And has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that a real problem for an idea like this? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to just awkward, plus what someone who actually believes this would concede is a legit weakness, not just what outsiders say. I was definately overthinking this after watching too many videos about AI taking jobs, but I want to know exactly where the weak spots are.
