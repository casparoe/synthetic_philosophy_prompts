Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level ethics or political philosophy seminar of about 18 students. The focus is the inefficacy objection to individual actions in collective harms—climate, consumption, or voting—but reject the usual linear-aggregation framing. Instead, build the exercise around a discontinuous, opaque harm function: collective damage is triggered only if aggregate behavior crosses an unknown threshold; each individual contribution lies below the measurement resolution; and participants never learn the threshold’s location or whether it has already been crossed.

Run the activity as a four-round structured deliberation with small real stakes (e.g., pooled snack credits or token course points). In each round, every student privately chooses whether to take a small private gain that adds an imperceptible increment to a collective pool. After choices are locked, reveal only whether the pool crossed a randomly drawn hidden cutoff, never revealing individual contributions or the cutoff value. The payoff scheme should make defection individually rational and cooperation individually costly, while making the collective outcome depend solely on the opaque aggregate.

Arrange the rules so participants feel the objection from inside: they must experience that their single move is causally invisible, that precise coordination is impossible because of the opacity, and that resisting the private benefit seems futile. Only after the final round should the facilitator name the philosophical problem.

Include a debrief that: (1) maps moments in the game onto causal inefficacy, normative inefficacy, and the “threshold illusion”; (2) predicts what will predictably fail the first time—students will attempt explicit coalitions to stay “just below” the cutoff, collapsing the opacity condition—and advises how to restore it; (3) states honestly which feature of real collective harm the activity cannot reproduce, namely the long-term structural maintenance of harmful systems through widespread but individually invisible complicity, and notes that the facilitator must supply this concept directly.

Keep the deliverable formal and concise: specify rules, materials, payoff structure, round timeline, debrief structure, and the honest limitation. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not depend on specific named texts or dates.
