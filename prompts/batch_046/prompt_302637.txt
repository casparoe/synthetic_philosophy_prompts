I've been working through Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach in a philosophy of mind reading group, and I'm struck by how rarely people apply the Gödelian analogy—where formal self-reference yields statements that are true but unprovable within the system—to narrative structures rather than directly to minds. I'd like to test it on Christopher Nolan's Inception, treating the film's plot as common knowledge: Cobb and his team enter nested dreams, rely on a personal totem (a spinning top) to distinguish dream from reality, and the film ends with the totem wobbling but never clearly falling. Read the film through the GEB framework: does the nested dream architecture function analogously to Gödel numbering, where each level encodes the one below and thereby permits a statement about the top level's reality to loop back and refer to itself?

Specifically, I'm interested in whether Cobb's totem operates like the meta-level step in Gödel's proof—an external criterion that resolves self-reference from outside the system—or whether Nolan's refusal to resolve the totem's fall traps the viewer inside the loop, making the audience the only available "outside" system that can evaluate the statement. Where does this illuminate a particular scene—perhaps Mal's suicide as a false bottom level, or the synchronized kicks that attempt to collapse the hierarchy—and where does the analogy strain, given that dreams are phenomenological rather than syntactic? I want a reading that takes both the film and Hofstadter's distinction between self-referential loops that close back upon themselves and mere hierarchical nesting seriously, that does not reduce the film to an illustration of logic, and that ends by saying whether Inception adds something to the Gödelian account of self-awareness—perhaps about the role of an external observer—that formal argument alone fails to capture.
