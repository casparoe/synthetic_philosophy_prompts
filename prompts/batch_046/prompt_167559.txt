Read Aldous Huxley’s Brave New World through the lens of wireheading and reward tampering, particularly as these ideas have been refined in recent discussions of artificial intelligence alignment and value learning. In the novel, soma functions as a state-administered form of chemical reward substitution; yet the philosophical literature on wireheading typically concerns an agent that modifies its own reward mechanism—whether biological or computational—to maximise a proxy for well-being rather than well-being itself. To what extent does Huxley’s World State anticipate modern worries about reward hacking? Does the Savage’s refusal of soma illustrate the claim that wireheading is defective because it severs pleasure from achievement and authenticity, or does the novel conflate distinct objections—one about the quality of experience, another about instrumental rationality? I am especially interested in whether the distinction between exogenous reward substitution (the authorities supplying the drug) and autogenous reward tampering (the agent altering its own utility function) collapses in Huxley’s society, or whether the absence of genuinely autonomous agents capable of self-modification means the novel addresses a structurally different problem altogether. Where does the metaphor hold, where does it trade on a confusion between pharmacological euphoria and computational reward optimisation, and where does the work resist being read as an anticipation of alignment concerns? I am preparing a seminar on the philosophy of technology and want to use the text without either reducing Huxley to an illustration of AI risk or distorting the technical concept of wireheading to fit the plot.
