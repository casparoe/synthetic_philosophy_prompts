I'm writing a proposal for a one-semester independent study (about 5,000 words) on a conceptual problem in the historiography of collective identity. The standard debate frames Hobsbawm's claim that traditions are invented to serve modern nation-building against Anderson's view that nations are imagined communities made possible by shared temporal consciousness and bounded sovereignty. Both treat the nation as a specifically modern form, but neither clearly distinguishes the structural features that make a community "national" rather than merely corporate, associative, or bureaucratic.

My half-formed idea: apply Anderson's criteria for an imagined community—boundedness, sovereignty, and horizontal comradeship—to pre-modern bodies that maintained continuous archives, cultivated internal solidarity, and exercised self-governance (for example, long-lived cathedral chapters or guild confederations) but that never claimed to represent a popular will. If these bodies satisfy Anderson's criteria, then "imagined community" is too broad to isolate nationhood, and Hobsbawm's "invented tradition" must carry the explanatory load of why some invented traditions become national rather than merely institutional. If they fail the criteria, we need a precise account of which feature is genuinely modern and whether its absence undermines the claim that nations are discontinuous with earlier collective forms.

Turn this into a proposal with: (1) a precise research question answerable through conceptual analysis and selective historical comparison rather than original archival discovery; (2) an explanation of why the existing Hobsbawm-Anderson framing leaves this ambiguity unresolved; (3) the method—which comparative cases you would examine, what conceptual distinctions you would draw (e.g., between sovereignty and self-governance, or between horizontal comradeship and functional interdependence), and how you would structure the work over the semester; (4) what a successful result would look like—such as a revised typology of collective identity or a demonstrated failure-condition for one framework—and what would count as the project failing; and (5) an honest scope assessment: what makes this tractable for one semester, and where it risks expanding into an unmanageable comparative history. Do not include a bibliography, literature review by citation, or reference list; I will supply those separately.
