Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of rational agency who holds that honoring sunk costs is always a category mistake in practical reasoning, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a decision theorist who argues that the "always irrational" claim depends on an unduly narrow conception of what counts as a cost and what counts as forward-looking. Set the dialogue in a seminar room after a talk on intertemporal choice, with both speaking in a precise, analytical register.

Their disagreement should turn on a specific conceptual injection: Vasquez distinguishes "causal history" from "reasons for action" and insists that any rational evaluation must be state-dependent but history-independent; Chen introduces the notion of an "integrity-sunk commitment," where the expenditure itself has altered the agent's option set and the payoff structure of future interactions—so that continuing is not "honoring" a past loss but responding to a present structural fact about credible commitment and relational capital.

Have them test these views against two scenarios: first, an individual who has paid for a non-refundable concert ticket to a performance she now expects to dislike; second, a multi-institutional scientific collaboration that has invested a decade in a theoretical framework now facing contradictory evidence, where withdrawal by one partner would dissolve trust networks that underpin other active projects.

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should directly address the other’s specific arguments rather than talk past them. Vasquez should challenge Chen on whether he has smuggled consequentialist reasoning about reputation back into a framework that pretends to honor sunk costs, and whether his "integrity-sunk" category is just a relabeling of ordinary opportunity-cost reasoning. Chen should press Vasquez on whether her "history-independent" evaluation can account for the fact that agents are embedded in temporal structures where past choices constitute present constraints, and whether her individualistic framing illegitimately universalizes a model of atomic decision-making.

Conclude the dialogue without resolution, but with each side identifying the exact point at which their disagreement becomes foundational—whether it is about the ontology of practical reasons, the boundaries of the decision-making unit, or the legitimacy of treating identity and reputation as endogenous to rational evaluation. Keep the tone formal and the reasoning explicit; do not include bibliographies, citations, or appeals to authority.
