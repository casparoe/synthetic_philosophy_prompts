Write a poem of exactly eighteen lines, arranged in six tercets, with the pronoun "I" placed as the final word of every third line (lines 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18). Take as your subject Douglas Hofstadter's conception of the self as a strange loop — specifically, the claim that "I" is not the name of a fixed substance or soul but a self-referential symbol that points back to the very pattern of cognitive processes currently generating it. In each successive tercet, shift the grammatical subject that this terminal "I" represents: begin with a speaker who assumes they are a single continuous entity; then let the symbol recognize its own lack of a fixed referent; then present the loop of feedback that produces the illusion of ownership; then depict a system observing its own observation; then dissolve the boundary between observer and observed; and finally close the loop on itself without identifying any center. Use the repetition of "I" not as a stable refrain with one speaker but as a recurring word with a different referent each time, so that the poem's formal repetition enacts the very instability Hofstadter describes. Do not resolve the loop with a final revelation; let the concluding "I" remain suspended, as though the poem itself has become the symbol it describes. Keep the language precise enough that someone familiar with Hofstadter's account of self-reference would recognize the philosophical structure, but do not include footnotes, citations, bibliographies, or explanatory prose outside the verse.
