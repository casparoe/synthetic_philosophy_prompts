I have come to hold that coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation do more than merely exclude Dutch-book vulnerability: they positively determine a unique cardinal utility scale, fixed up to positive affine transformation, solely from an agent's comparative judgments over compound lotteries. I further maintain that this determination survives even when we replace the standard independence axiom with a weakened version—restricted coherence—that permits ambiguity aversion, provided the agent's credence sets remain convex. I take this to show that expected utility maximisation is not one rationality constraint among others, but the inevitable structural residue of any preference framework that avoids practical contradiction.

What would count against this position? I want the potential defeaters sorted by decisiveness: formal results demonstrating that restricted coherence still permits representations incompatible with expected utility; conceptual or empirical cases in which agents systematically violate uniqueness claims without manifest irrationality; and arguments that ambiguity aversion is not a local exception but reveals that coherence arguments illegitimately presuppose precise credences. For each, I want to know whether defenders of the standard coherence view concede it as damaging, dismiss it as a misapplication, or absorb it by further qualification. Has the position been insulated—perhaps by retreating to a purely instrumental or domain-restricted defence—into something that risks unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a problem for a normative view of this kind? Finally, what predictions does my version make that rival accounts, such as rank-dependent utility or imprecise-probability frameworks, do not, and have any of those predictions already failed?
