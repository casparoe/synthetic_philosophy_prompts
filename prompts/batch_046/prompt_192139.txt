Having spent a career in condensed-matter physics examining how collective behaviors emerge from local interactions, I have lately begun reading in moral philosophy and have become preoccupied with T. M. Scanlon's contractualism, particularly his resistance to aggregating moral claims across persons in rescue cases. I want to compare Scanlon's standard contractualist framework with a variant I have been developing for my own understanding, which I term structural-relational contractualism. Where Scanlon holds that a principle is wrong if it can be reasonably rejected by any single individual affected, and thus resists straightforward numerical aggregation—so that saving five strangers rather than one does not automatically carry greater weight—my variant asks whether the affected individuals stand in a specific relational structure that jointly constitutes their claim. For example, if five people are exposed to a single systemic risk governed by one institutional protocol, their vulnerability might form a jointly rejectable pattern that is not reducible to five separate individual rejections; conversely, if they are scattered across unrelated contexts, no such joint constitution exists, and Scanlon's individualist restriction should apply unchanged.

Could you compare these two positions with care? First, assess whether structural-relational contractualism genuinely resolves the aggregation problem or merely disguises it behind an under-specified criterion for relational unity. Second, examine how each view treats the classic numbers case in the absence of any institutional or relational link among the potential victims—does Scanlon's refusal to aggregate remain stable, and does my variant collapse back into his position or into a disguised form of utilitarian counting? Third, drawing on my habit of thinking about emergence in physical systems, I wonder whether analogies between collectively constituted moral claims and emergent statistical properties are illuminating or whether they risk a category error by treating normative justification as if it were a physical interaction. I am not asking for bibliographic references or a survey of the literature; I want a focused conceptual comparison that helps me see whether my physicalist intuition about collective phenomena has any legitimate purchase on Scanlon's moral ontology.
