I'm preparing a classification instrument for an advanced metaphysics seminar on causation by omission, and I need a decision tree that sorts positions by the logical commitments they make rather than by authorial labels. The audience is upper-level undergraduates and first-year graduate students who have encountered basic counterfactual and mechanistic accounts of causation but should not need to recognize obscure sub-literature to navigate the tree. To anchor the branches, use this paired case: (A) a surgeon who is scheduled and capable but never enters the operating room, with the result that the patient dies; and (B) a wild tree that dies because no passerby waters it, where no institutional or moral duty to water exists.

Build the tree from four diagnostic questions, each phrased so a student can answer it without knowing which philosophical position it corresponds to. First: do omissions possess causal efficacy in their own right, or are causal claims about omissions always reducible to descriptions of underlying positive causal histories? Second, for those who answer yes: is the selection of which omission counts as causally relevant governed by a normative baseline—what ought to have occurred—or by counterfactual dependence and stability alone? If normative, does the baseline derive from institutional roles, moral obligations, or functional design? Third: is there a metaphysically robust distinction between an omission tied to an agent's unrealized capacity-to-act, as in (A), and an omission tied merely to the non-occurrence of an event, as in (B), and does this distinction survive if we remove agency from the picture? Fourth: can omissions enter into overdetermination relations with positive causes, or does their status as absences block them from competing with present events?

Each leaf should carry a descriptive label—use a standard name only if it is genuinely common in introductory literature, otherwise mark it "unnamed"—plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, especially regarding how it treats (A) versus (B). For every combination of answers that yields no named or defensible position, note it as unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether it is empty because incoherent, unmotivated, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the four branches fail to separate. Limit the tree to no more than twelve leaves, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and ensure that no branch depends on recognizing niche theoretical terminology in advance.
