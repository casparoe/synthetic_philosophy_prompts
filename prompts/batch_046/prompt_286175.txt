For more than a generation, scholars of nineteenth-century political thought have been deadlocked over John Stuart Mill’s writings on British India. One camp reads his defense of East India Company administration—especially his claim that some societies are not yet fit for representative self-government—as a betrayal of liberal equality, masked by a stage-theory of civilization that effectively racializes political capacity. The other camp reads the same texts as a consistent, if uncomfortable, application of liberal developmentalism: Mill never denies Indians eventual self-rule, they insist, but argues that legitimate government requires prior civic formation that prior despotism destroyed and that expert British tutelage might, in principle, restore. Both sides acknowledge the same textual patterns—Mill’s distinction between active and passive citizenship, his praise for expert administration over popular rule in India, his refusal to extend parliamentary representation there, and his simultaneous denunciation of Company corruption—yet the debate never advances.

I think the stall is not primarily biographical or historical, but conceptual, and I want your diagnosis of exactly what kind of conceptual deadlock it is. In particular, I suspect both sides are arguing past each other because they have not named a hidden disagreement about what I will call pedagogical sovereignty: the idea that legitimate authority over adults can be justified not by their present consent but by the ruler’s superior grasp of the conditions under which meaningful consent becomes possible. The first camp treats any such justification as illiberal by definition; the second treats it as a temporary, instrumentally rational extension of liberal ends. Are they therefore answering different questions—one about Mill’s psychological consistency, one about whether liberalism can accommodate non-consensual tutelage—under the single heading “Mill on India”? Or is the disagreement substantive but hidden, turning on whether political equality is a precondition of legitimate rule or its eventual result? Could there be a suppressed empirical claim about whether British administration actually cultivated the civic capacities Mill said it should? Or is the dispute bedrock, grounded in incompatible intuitions about natural hierarchy that neither side can argue for?

Take a definite position. If you think the disagreement is mainly verbal or structural, say so and show precisely where the sides talk past each other; if you think it masks a real empirical dispute about colonial governance, identify the specific factual claim doing the hidden work; if you think the framing “Was Mill a liberal imperialist?” is itself ill-posed, argue for that and propose what question should replace it. In every case, tell me what would actually count as progress—a formal distinction between tutelary and despotic authority, an archival finding, a change of conceptual framework, or an honest admission that no progress should be expected and why. Do not offer a false compromise: commit to one diagnosis and defend it.
