At a recent philosophy of mind seminar, two colleagues—Marcus and Elena—became entangled in a disagreement about whether Daniel Dennett’s multiple drafts model genuinely eliminates the Cartesian theatre or merely redistributes its functions across neural processes. Marcus accepted Dennett’s critique of a central screen observed by an inner homunculus, yet insisted that the model still requires what he termed phenomenal anchoring: a minimal functional convergence point where distributed drafts are compressed into a single reportable temporal index. Without such a bottleneck, he argued, we could not distinguish genuinely simultaneous experiences from those stitched together only retrospectively by memory, and he noted that global workspace theories in neuroscience supply precisely this kind of thin convergence without invoking an inner observer. Elena replied that this thin convergence is the Cartesian theatre in disguise—renaming the audience a functional bottleneck does not remove the need for something to which experience is present at a specific moment. She maintained that Dennett’s editorial metaphor describes distributed, competitive selection among drafts, comparable to evolutionary pressure rather than a board meeting, and that introducing any convergence point abandons the core claim that there is no single place in the brain where all processes are unified.

Marcus then raised a further objection: if the multiple drafts model depends on editorial selection to determine which draft becomes canonical for verbal report, there must be a locus or mechanism of selection, which is structurally analogous to the theatre’s observer; if selection is genuinely distributed with no centre, the model cannot explain why one draft dominates over others at any given moment. Elena countered that dominance emerges from relative activation strength across modular systems, requiring no central arbiter, and that Marcus was illegitimately demanding a why-now explanation that presupposes the very temporal unity Dennett’s framework dissolves. They turned to me as a bystander and asked me to adjudicate their exchange point by point.

Please assess each of the four principal moves—Marcus’s initial claim about phenomenal anchoring, Elena’s charge that this smuggles the theatre back in, Marcus’s objection concerning the locus of editorial selection, and Elena’s reply about distributed dominance—rating each as clearly mistaken, partially right, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then state which position is closer to being correct overall, and identify what both interlocutors are overlooking regarding the relation between narrative coherence and temporal indexicality in conscious report.
