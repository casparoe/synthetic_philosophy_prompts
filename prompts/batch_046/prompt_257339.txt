The exam question was: "In Surfaces and Essences, Hofstadter and Sanders argue that analogy-making is the core of cognition rather than a secondary heuristic. Contrast their view with the standard computationalist account, in which analogy is applied only after conceptual representations have already been fixed. Then apply their framework to the mathematical concept of a function: explain how they would describe its emergence through analogical projection, distinguish a surface analogy from an essential analogy in this case, and evaluate whether describing a concept as a 'bundle of analogies' undermines the objectivity and rigidity of mathematical knowledge. In your evaluation, be explicit about what 'category projection' does and does not guarantee across different thinkers."

Student answer:

Hofstadter and Sanders reject the computationalist picture in which the mind first builds static representations and then uses analogy as a search heuristic. For them, analogy is primary: cognition is continuous category projection, and every concept is formed by mapping structures from prior domains onto new ones. There are no pre-existing representations to which analogy is later applied; the analogy constitutes the concept. A concept is therefore a "bundle of analogies" layered over time, not a definition with necessary and sufficient conditions.

Take the concept of function. Rather than being defined formally as a set of ordered pairs satisfying uniqueness, it emerges through analogical projection from prior domains—input-output machines, causal dependencies, geometric transformations, and set mappings. A surface analogy might notice that a function "looks like" a machine with an input slot and an output slot, capturing appearance without structural depth. An essential analogy, by contrast, captures the deep relational dependency in which one quantity is deterministically tied to another, allowing the concept to be projected onto vector spaces, logical propositions, or computational processes.

Because concepts are analogical bundles, however, mathematical knowledge seems to depend on each thinker's particular analogical history. Different mathematicians might construct incompatible bundles for "function," which suggests that mathematical rigidity is an illusion sustained only by shared cultural analogies rather than objective structure. Hofstadter addresses this concern in Gödel, Escher, Bach through the mechanism of strange loops, which stabilize concepts across minds via self-reference. But in Surfaces and Essences he and Sanders do not solve the problem: category projection is too flexible to guarantee intersubjective convergence, so the objectivity of mathematics remains unexplained.

Thus, while the analogical model explains conceptual creativity well, it sacrifices the universality that makes mathematics more than a culturally shared metaphor system.
