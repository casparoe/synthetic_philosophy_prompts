My verdicts on virtual objects, virtual harms, and virtual obligations are these, and I want to know whether a single principle captures them or whether I must abandon one. In a persistent virtual world, a user spends years cultivating a unique, procedurally generated digital garden with no backup, which an administrator then permanently deletes; I judge this a real loss of value, not mere data loss. If that same garden is copied flawlessly to a thousand instances, however, I judge no harm to anyone, even though the "original" is now indistinguishable from its duplicates. When an avatar is subjected to a simulated assault that causes the controlling user lasting psychological distress, I judge a real harm comparable to severe verbal abuse; yet when an AI-controlled non-player character undergoes an identical simulation with behaviorally indistinguishable responses, I judge no harm whatsoever. In a closed virtual economy with no real-world exchange rate, an explicit agreement to trade virtual goods creates, I think, a genuine but weaker obligation than its real-world analog; but if virtual labor in that economy is directly tied to real income, the same agreement binds with full strength. Is there a unified account—perhaps irreplaceability for objects, consciousness-dependency for harm, and institutional embedding for obligations—that saves every verdict, or does the tension between valuing non-conscious artifacts and denying harm to behaviorally identical AI force me to drop one?
