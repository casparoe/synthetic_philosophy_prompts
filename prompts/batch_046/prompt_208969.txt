I recently retired after thirty-five years as a marine biologist and have begun working through introductory texts in philosophy of science and metaphysics, though I still reason like someone trained to weigh evidence from direct observation. Last week, over dinner with former colleagues, a dispute arose that I now realize is philosophical rather than empirical, and I need help seeing its conceptual structure before I present a recommendation to a conservation board next month.

Two populations of a deep-sea invertebrate—one inhabiting a hydrothermal vent, the other a cold seep—display markedly different morphologies, feeding behaviors, and reproductive cycles. By standard phenotypic taxonomy they are clearly distinct species. Yet destructive genomic sampling, which is now ethically barred for these rare populations, previously revealed near-identical nuclear genomes; the visible differences appear driven by epigenetic modulation and symbiotic microbiome shifts, none of which can be assessed without laboratory access that is now permanently unavailable. Board members are split. Some argue that because the underlying genetic identity is real but hidden, these populations represent one plastically expressed species, and splitting them would misdirect limited conservation funds. Others counter that since the genomic evidence is now irretrievably inaccessible, ontology for policy purposes must track what remains permanently observable—the phenotypes—making them separate species. I feel the disagreement hinges on whether what exists is determined by hidden structural identity or by what remains epistemically available, but I cannot articulate the precise conceptual tension or see where analogies to hidden physical properties might be misleading.

Please act as an expert philosopher of science and metaphysics. Think carefully, and begin with brief brainstorming on the conceptual architecture before offering any practical recommendation. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—this must be worked out from first principles, since I need arguments I can defend without relying on texts I do not have at hand. Specifically: (1) Describe, without unnecessary jargon, the philosophical problem about ontology and permanently inaccessible information that this case exemplifies; (2) Lay out the competing principles—whether ontological commitment should follow epistemic access, causal-functional role, or structural identity—and show exactly how each applies to these two populations; (3) Examine whether the irreversible loss of genomic access alters the ontological facts, or only our justification for classifying them, and address any hidden analogies (for instance, treating hidden genetic structure like hidden charge or mass) that might be smuggling in metaphysical assumptions; (4) Advise, in formal but accessible terms, how someone with my scientific background should weigh these considerations when observable data are fixed but the underlying structure is beyond reach, and flag the strongest objection to your own view. Please be rigorous, avoid generic life advice, and structure your response so that I can present the reasoning clearly to a board of conservation biologists.
