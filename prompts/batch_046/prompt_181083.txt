I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and next week I present at our department's philosophy of mind seminar a paper defending a tripartite fracture of the meta-problem of consciousness. My thesis is that the standard framing—why do we judge phenomenal consciousness to be non-physical?—collapses three structurally distinct explanatory demands: (i) accounting for first-personal judgments that one's own experience resists physical description, (ii) accounting for comparative judgments that phenomenal properties are irreducible to functional or representational ones, and (iii) accounting for ascriptive judgments that other minds possess consciousness despite our lacking direct access to their experience. I argue that Chalmers's unitary formulation obscures these differences, and that as a result both the phenomenal-concept strategy and illusionist responses solve the wrong problem: the phenomenal-concept strategy addresses (i) but not (ii) or (iii), while illusionism undermines (iii) without engaging (ii).

The audience will be about twelve people: eight committed physicalists, including two who defend representationalist or transparency-based accounts and are sympathetic to illusionism about phenomenal properties; two phenomenologists who take first-person authority and intersubjective recognition seriously; and the remainder agnostic philosophers of science trained to press on explanatory redundancy. They know the broad meta-problem literature but are not specialists in its recent taxonomy.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would actually use, ordered by how damaging they are to my proposal. For each objection, indicate whether it has real force, what I should concede up front to maintain credibility, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest. Include a two-minute reply suitable for a seminar exchange. I also want you to flag the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—specifically, the challenge that targets the boundary conditions between my three categories, since my taxonomy is most vulnerable where judgments blend first-personal, comparative, and ascriptive elements. Do not ask for bibliographies or reading lists; I need replies I can deliver from the argument I have just sketched.
