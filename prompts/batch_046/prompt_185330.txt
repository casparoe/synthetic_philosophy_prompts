A study guide I am using for an introductory epistemology course summarizes Plato's Meno problem and its modern resolution as follows. Socrates asks why knowledge is more valuable than true belief if both guide action equally well. The guide explains that Plato answered by saying knowledge is true belief tied down with an account of its cause—essentially, justification—while modern reliabilism updates this by replacing justification with a reliable causal process. It then claims that Gettier cases show that even reliable processes can produce justified true beliefs that fail to be knowledge, but that adding a modal safety condition—requiring that the belief could not easily have been false—fully closes the gap. From this, the guide concludes that knowledge is simply stable true belief, and that the original Meno question is therefore dissolved: there is no extra value, only greater stability. This strikes me as suspiciously neat, especially since I have recently been helping students distinguish between analyzing knowledge and explaining its value. Is this summary fair? Please separate what accurately reflects Plato's text and standard epistemology, what is a common pedagogical simplification, and what misrepresents either the ancient argument or the contemporary debate—particularly regarding whether reliabilism and safety conditions actually address the value problem rather than just the analysis problem. Please also say whether the guide's final conclusion—that there is no remaining value gap once stability is secured—follows from the corrected premises.
