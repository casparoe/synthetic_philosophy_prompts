I'm convening the closing session of an advanced seminar on the ethics of armed conflict, having spent the term moving past the standard just ad bellum and just in bello framework into the more stubborn debate over the moral equality of combatants. Rather than asking for yet another summary of the traditional and revisionist positions, I want to finish with a villanelle that takes seriously the liability-based challenge to the symmetry thesis: the claim that the distinction between liability to defensive harm and the permission to inflict it collapses once we notice that an unjust combatant, lacking just cause, cannot claim the same moral shield as his opponent, even if he is conscripted or otherwise individually blameless.

Please construct the poem so that the form enacts the argument. Use two refrains—one voicing the traditional equality thesis, perhaps along the lines of The uniform grants both sides an equal right to strike, and one voicing the revisionist asymmetry, perhaps Only justice licenses the hand that kills. These should alternate through the tercets according to the strict villanelle scheme (A1 b A2 / a b A1 / a b A2 / a b A1 / a b A2 / a b A1 A2), so that the mechanical repetition mirrors the deadlock between the positions, and in the final quatrain they should stand side by side without resolution, exposing the contradiction rather than reconciling it. The imagery ought to be precise enough that someone familiar with the debate recognises the moves—the problem of conscripted soldiers on the unjust side, the way non-combatant immunity depends on a distinction that the equality thesis itself undermines, and the absence of individual culpability required for liability—without the poem turning into a sermon or a mere slogan. Keep it formal, exact, and structurally rigorous, leaving the tension intact in the rhyme.
