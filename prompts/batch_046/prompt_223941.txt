I am a graduate student in philosophy of biology co-organizing an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Functional Change in Disturbed Ecosystems," and I have been asked to deliver the five-minute opening remarks. The audience is split evenly between philosophers of biology—who will immediately recognize the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function—and practicing conservation biologists, who use "function" and "dysfunction" constantly in management plans but have never encountered either philosophical account by name. I need the remarks to make that conceptual tension do real structural work rather than serve as decoration: the speech should frame the workshop by showing how the choice between an etiological account, which fixes a trait's function to its selective history, and a causal-role account, which fixes it to its present contribution within a system, changes what can coherently be called a "functional loss," a "functional replacement," or a "dysfunction" when ecosystems are altered—for example, when a non-native pollinator takes over the role of an extirpated native species, or when climate change causes a keystone predator to cease contributing to the same system-level dynamics it once did. Because half the room are experts in the philosophy and the other half are not, the address must be precise enough that philosophers feel the distinction is being handled with care, yet accessible enough that biologists see why it bears on their practical judgments about intervention, without ever sounding like a lecture to either side. The speech should include a brief, explicit acknowledgment of the risk of talking past the biologists, and it should conclude by posing the workshop's guiding question in terms both audiences can own but that each account interprets differently. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific publications; I need to deliver this from memory, and I want it to stand entirely on conceptual clarity. Write it as a polished, deliverable address.
