I'm drafting an essay—tentatively titled Normative Orphans and the Decoupling of Archive from Practice—on mnemonic sovereignty in epistemic communities, using the long-running rationalist forum ecosystem as a limiting case. The central thesis is that when collective memory and documentary record conflict, resolution should be domain-dependent: in causal and historical inquiry the record ought to correct memory, but in normative and practical reasoning collective memory should take precedence over the archive, because shared norms supervene on operative expectations and present social coordination rather than on preserved traces.

The argument has three moves. First, I distinguish between what happened, a causal-historical claim, and what binds us, a normative claim about present obligations and standards. In informal communities without formal institutions, norms are enforced through shared expectation, not through codified rules; this parallels the legal distinction between recorded statute and operative practice, though I do not develop any specific jurisprudential literature. Second, I use the forum ecosystem illustratively. Early canonical posts establish baseline norms—explicit Bayesian reasoning as a default, disavowal of status-based argumentation—but over years the community's operative memory of these norms drifts through selective retention, reinterpretation, and practical accommodation. Newcomers who cite literal archival texts to correct long-time participants are typically treated as having missed the functional point, not as having delivered a valuable correction. This suggests the live normative structure is maintained by mnemonic consensus and enacted practice, not by documentary fidelity. Third, I introduce normative orphaning: when an archival correction establishes a true historical proposition but fails to shift collective expectations, the result is a fact that is historically accurate yet normatively inert, carrying no weight in present reasoning.

I anticipate three objections. One is that privileging collective memory risks epistemic closure, allowing communities to suppress inconvenient records. I respond that mnemonic correction remains possible through explicit renegotiation and present practice, not solely through archival retrieval. Another is that the causal-normative distinction collapses, since norms themselves have causal histories. I concede partial overlap but maintain that the functional role of a norm in current reasoning is not determined by its documentary origin. The third is that my framing overstates coherence, given internal fragmentation within any large forum.

I am particularly unsure about two points. First, does the rationalist example actually support the thesis, or does it instead show that archives are precisely the tool used to resolve mnemonic disputes—making the record sovereign after all? Second, is normative orphaning a genuinely useful conceptual distinction, or does it merely rename the familiar gap between descriptive and prescriptive claims?

I would appreciate comments on the coherence of the domain split, the aptness of the forum example, the utility of the orphaning concept, and any structural weaknesses in the argument. I am especially interested in whether there are closer analogues in social epistemology or the philosophy of social practices that I should be engaging, though I want to avoid turning this into a literature survey, since the essay aims to develop conceptual machinery rather than to map existing scholarship.
