I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to work through Bradley's regress problem about how a proposition achieves unity—specifically, how a subject and a predicate can form one judgment rather than remaining isolated mental items. To make the abstract structure concrete, I have been modeling a proposition as a tree-like data structure in which terms are nodes and relations are edges that link nodes together. Thinking in those terms, I constructed the following argument, which now seems to collapse all internal complexity into an undifferentiated whole, and I cannot pinpoint the exact error.

(1) Any genuinely informative proposition contains at least two terms that are not identical—call them S and P.
(2) For S and P to constitute a single proposition rather than two separate mental items, there must be a relational link R that connects them.
(3) If R is not genuinely distinct from both S and P, then it cannot perform the work of connecting two different things; so R must be a third term, distinct from both.
(4) But then R must itself be linked to S by a further relation R1, and to P by R2, and those links must in turn be linked by R3 and R4, and so on without end.
(5) Hence any proposition with internal structure requires an actually infinite hierarchy of linking relations.
(6) No actually infinite hierarchy can be completed or instantiated; therefore no proposition with internal structure can exist.
(7) Yet we clearly grasp informative judgments, so the premise that they contain genuinely distinct terms must be rejected.
(8) It follows that every true proposition is a completely undifferentiated unity, with no internal parts or distinctions—which seems to erase the difference between judging that Socrates is wise and judging that Socrates is Socrates.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd, but each step looks locally reasonable. I would prefer to keep (1) and (6) if possible, since I do not want to deny that propositions have internal parts, nor do I want to accept an actual infinity of relations. Where is the hidden mistake—in (3), in (4), or in the slide from (4) to (5)? If the error lies in treating R as an additional node that needs its own edges, what does correcting it cost me? Does it force me to accept that relations are not part of the propositional content, or that they are external in a sense that breaks the tree analogy? I would appreciate a diagnosis that names the problematic step, explains why it seemed plausible, and prices the available alternatives, particularly with respect to whether the structural analogy can be preserved in any modified form.
