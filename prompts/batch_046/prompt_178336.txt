I am preparing a classification instrument for a philosophy of language workshop focused on Wittgenstein's language games and family resemblance. Please construct a branching taxonomy — a decision tree in which each internal node is a concrete claim or case on which positions genuinely diverge, and each leaf is a named philosophical stance (or 'unnamed' if the combination is unoccupied). The tree should begin from the question of whether every language game shares at least one common feature, then split on whether family resemblance applies to the concept itself or only to the practices it groups, then on whether the lack of an essential feature blocks normative critique of a given practice, and finally on whether applying family resemblance to the category of language games is viciously circular or benignly reflexive. For every leaf, provide the position's conventional name (if one exists) together with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Identify any pair of leaves that your tree cannot separate, explaining briefly why the diagnostic questions fail to distinguish them. For any unoccupied branch, offer a brief diagnosis of whether the gap arises from incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect. The instrument should be usable by someone who knows the debates but does not already know the labels, so phrase every node as a question or commitment that can be answered without prior knowledge of which interpretation it yields. Please write the response in British English, maintain a formal register, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
