I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or theoretical physics, though I have been trying to work through some conceptual puzzles that come up at the boundary of computation and thermodynamics. In a recent discussion with colleagues, someone drew an analogy between Maxwell's demon and a garbage-collection routine in a managed runtime: the demon sorts fast-moving molecules from slow-moving ones using only information about their velocities, just as a GC traverses reference graphs to separate live objects from unreachable ones, apparently reducing disorder—physical entropy in the first case, memory fragmentation in the second—without applying mechanical work to the items being sorted. The usual resolution involving Landauer's principle was then mapped onto the energy cost of clearing registers or truncating an append-only log: erasing one bit of stored information necessarily dissipates heat on the order of kT ln 2, which means the demon's record of observed velocities cannot be wiped for free, and the second law is preserved.

I want to understand precisely how far this analogy holds and where it collapses. Could you audit the mapping in detail? Specifically, identify the exact correspondences—does the demon's gate correspond to the marking phase, the velocity measurements to pointer reachability states, the thermal reservoir to the power supply driving the processor? Then show where the analogy breaks: does it quietly import the assumption that information is immaterial, or that logical and physical layers can be separated cleanly in a way that fails in thermodynamic reality? I am particularly interested in whether framing the demon's memory as an append-only event log rather than a mutable register changes the accounting—does the entropy cost disappear, or merely relocate to the storage medium and its eventual reset? Furthermore, assess whether the analogy genuinely demonstrates that information is physical, or whether it smuggles in that conclusion by treating software state as already thermodynamic. Finally, evaluate whether a different computing analogy—perhaps to copy-on-write snapshots or reference-counting cycles—would do less damage to the physical reasoning, and state clearly which disanalogies matter for resolving the paradox and which are merely decorative.
