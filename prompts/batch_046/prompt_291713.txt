Write the opening remarks for a workshop titled "The Moral Economy of Time," to be delivered before a mixed audience of labor historians, moral philosophers, and family members of the retiring keynote speaker—a sociologist who spent forty years arguing that leisure is not merely the absence of labor but a form of temporal ownership. The remarks should run about five minutes and must treat the Protestant work ethic, the French 35-hour week, and the Japanese phenomenon of karoshi not as decorative references but as structural poles that shape what can be said about a "right" to leisure. Specifically: first, articulate how Weber's secularized asceticism transforms the 35-hour limit from a policy achievement into a kind of moral failure of will; second, show that karoshi is not an aberration but the logical endpoint of that same ethic once its theological compensation has been stripped away; third, use that tension to introduce the speaker's own thesis—that leisure must be reconceived as positive temporal sovereignty rather than negative freedom from work—without reducing it to a slogan or inventing statistical claims, legal citations, or page references. The scholars in the room should recognize that you are working with the standard conceptual frameworks, not fabricating sources; the family should grasp why the idea matters emotionally without being lectured. The tone should be formal, precise, and accessible to intelligent non-specialists.
