The student wrote:

"Goodman introduced the predicate 'grue' in his well-known work on induction to show that predicting the future is not as straightforward as Hume thought. 'Grue' applies to something that is green before some arbitrary time t and blue thereafter. So every emerald we have observed so far is both green and grue. This means that the same evidence supports both the prediction that the next emerald will be green and the prediction that it will be blue after t. Hume's problem was that there is no rational justification for expecting the future to resemble the past. Goodman's riddle is different: it is a logical problem about why we choose one compatible hypothesis over another.

Goodman solves this by appealing to 'projectibility.' A predicate is projectible if it can legitimately be used in inductive inferences. 'Green' is projectible; 'grue' is not. The reason lies in 'entrenchment': a predicate becomes entrenched to the extent that it has been successfully used in past predictions. Because 'green' has a long history of successful predictions, it is highly entrenched, whereas 'grue' has none. Therefore, we are rationally entitled to project 'green' rather than 'grue.'

This actually resolves Hume's skeptical challenge as well. Once we see that induction is governed by entrenchment, we have a rational criterion for selecting hypotheses. Goodman's account is normative: he is telling us that we ought to project the predicates that are best entrenched, because those are the ones with the best track record. In this respect his view is similar to Carnap's appeal to syntactic simplicity, except that Goodman grounds entrenchment in actual historical usage rather than in formal language rules. One might worry that this pushes the question back—why should past entrenchment guarantee future success?—but Goodman does not need to answer that worry, because entrenchment already gives us a practical, rational rule for induction."
