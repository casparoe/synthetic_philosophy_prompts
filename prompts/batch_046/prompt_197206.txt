I was just arguing with a friend about whether pure time preference can ever be rational in intergenerational ethics, and we kept circling back to climate policy without resolving anything, so I drafted a short exam question for an advanced seminar and asked a student to answer it. My friend thinks I am being too generous in my reading of the response, and since I want an assessment that is genuinely point-by-point rather than impressionistic, I would like an outside grading of the work.

Here is the question I posed: "Pure time preference holds that goods or harms are less valuable or urgent merely in virtue of lying in the future, independent of risk, changing preferences, or uncertainty. Sidgwick treats temporal location as analogous to spatial distance and argues that rational impartiality requires us to discount neither. More recently, some philosophers have defended a relational asymmetry view: present decision-makers stand in a direct causal and epistemic relation to near-future agents that they do not stand in to distant-future agents, and this relational difference—not mere temporal location—justifies differential weighting of welfare across generations. Assess whether this relational asymmetry successfully distinguishes a legitimate form of intergenerational weighting from pure time preference. In your answer, address whether Sidgwick's spatial analogy still undermines the view, and apply your analysis to a concrete policy choice—say, whether to invest now in carbon abatement that benefits people in 2150 at a measurable cost to people in 2030."

And here is the student's answer: "Sidgwick is right that rationality requires impartiality across time, just as across space. If I care less about suffering in 2150 only because it is in 2150, that is exactly like caring less about suffering in another country only because of distance. So pure time preference is irrational. The relational asymmetry view tries to avoid this by saying that we have special relations to near-future people—causal links and knowledge of their preferences—that we lack with distant-future people. But this is still just pure time preference in disguise: the only reason the relation weakens is temporal distance. So Sidgwick's analogy still applies; if spatial distance doesn't justify discounting, neither should the weakening of causal relations over time. However, there is an important exception drawn from the person-affecting principle, as developed by Narveson and discussed by Parfit. If our actions determine whether distant-future people exist at all, then we have no obligation to them if we choose not to create them. In the climate case, reducing emissions now might change who is born in 2150. So the reason to discount the welfare of those future people is not temporal location but the fact that they are not actual, identifiable individuals to whom we owe direct duties. Thus, the relational asymmetry saves intergenerational weighting in standard climate cases, because it shows that the people in 2150 are not fixed members of our moral community. Therefore, pure time preference remains unjustified, but not because of Sidgwick's spatial analogy. Rather, it is unjustified because any legitimate weighting must come either from empirical risk or from the person-affecting restriction on who counts morally, neither of which applies when we are dealing with future generations whose existence is independent of our current policy."
