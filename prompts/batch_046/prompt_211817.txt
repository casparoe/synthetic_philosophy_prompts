I am a doctoral student working on normative ethics and decision theory, and I have been trying to formalize moral uncertainty not as a discrete distribution over named theories—consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, and so on—but as a continuous parameter space. Call a point in this space a moral profile: each profile assigns a real-valued weight λ ∈ [0,1] that determines how severely deontological constraints are enforced relative to consequentialist aggregation, together with a family of rights-functions that become lexically dominant as λ approaches 1. My intuition was that this avoids the arbitrariness of treating theories as isolated atoms, but I have now constructed an argument that seems to collapse the framework into paralysis, and I cannot locate the faulty step.

Here is the argument. (1) Rational choice under moral uncertainty requires integrating choice-worthiness over the space of moral profiles, weighted by a credence density ρ that is continuous and strictly positive near every point, including the deontological pole where λ = 1. (2) At that pole, any action that violates a protected right receives choice-worthiness −∞, because the constraint is lexically dominant. (3) For any realistically available action a, there exists a neighborhood of profiles arbitrarily close to the pole in which a violates some right—whether of property, bodily autonomy, or distributive fairness—since rights-claims are densely distributed across the parameter space. (4) Consequently, in every neighborhood of the pole, V(a) equals −∞ on a set of positive measure, and the integral of V(a,λ)ρ(λ) dλ diverges to −∞. (5) Therefore every feasible action carries negatively infinite expected choice-worthiness. (6) If every option is infinitely bad in expectation, no comparative ranking is possible, and rational agency is impossible. (7) So either we reject the continuous integration model, or we accept that moral uncertainty makes action irrational.

The conclusion feels like a reductio, but each premise seems defensible: continuous credence is standard in Bayesian modeling, lexical dominance is part of what makes deontology distinctive, and the density of rights-violations seems empirically plausible. I am particularly reluctant to give up (2), because watering down lexical dominance just collapses the deontological pole into weighted consequentialism, which was not my credence assignment. I am also reluctant to abandon (1), since discrete theory-selection seemed ad hoc. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, does the problem lie in assuming that ρ must be positive near singularities, in the claim that rights-violations are dense near the pole, or in applying standard expectation to a value function that is not Lebesgue-integrable? And if the fix requires truncating the space or bounding the value function, does that amount to smuggling in a meta-normative commitment that I was trying to avoid?
