Philosophers debating whether “better than” is transitive frequently invoke the sorites paradox—the heap of sand—as an intuitive scaffold: just as removing a single grain never clearly turns a heap into a non-heap, a small incremental improvement never clearly transforms a bad state into a good one, yet we insist there is a real difference between the extremes. The analogy is often used to license the spectrum argument—that transitivity fails across long chains—or to motivate a hidden precise cutoff. More recently, Ruth Chang’s idea of parity—two options being on a par, neither better, worse, nor equal—has been introduced to resist collapsing every comparison into a linear order, and some discussants quietly import non-Archimedean models (infinitesimal value differences that never accumulate) to preserve transitivity despite appearance.
