I live on the board of a housing cooperative that was founded in 1962 by a group of artisans who pooled resources to build their own apartments. Over the past sixty years, every original resident has died or moved away, every load-bearing wall has been replaced during seismic upgrades, and even the carved wooden sign above the entrance was reconstructed after a storm—though the new sign uses the same letters in the same font. The cooperative's governing documents have been amended so often that a lawyer recently told us the only unbroken thread is our tax identification number; everything else, including the original mission statement, has been rewritten. Last month, a descendant of one of the founders appeared at our meeting claiming that we are no longer the real collective his grandfather helped create, and therefore we should not receive a historic-preservation grant reserved for continuously operating original institutions. He argues that if every human member and every physical component has been swapped out, we are essentially a replica that inherited the paperwork. Some neighbors counter that continuity is maintained by institutional memory, legal succession, and the fact that replacements always followed a deliberate protocol—each departing resident trained their successor, each new beam matched the original specifications—so the form and function never actually broke. I was discussing this with my sister, who restores antique furniture and mentioned that some restorers replace every joint in a chair yet still call it the original piece, and I realized I cannot tell whether that analogy saves our claim or undermines it. Without appealing to legal technicalities alone, what actually determines whether an organization—or anything made of replaceable parts—remains the same entity over time? Am I wrong to feel that the grandson's challenge has real weight even if the tax records are continuous?
