I run a workshop for about 12 grad studnets in phil of mind / AI ethics, 90 minutes. I want them to feel why drawing moral boundaries around computation is hard when the computation is spread out and gets interupted. Design a game with roles and a point system.

Use this niche framing: functional fragmentation — a computation only counts morally if its interrupted pieces can eventually reconverge on a shared state, but in the game nobody knows in advance whether reconvergence will happen.

Split them into 4 clusters of 3. Each cluster tracks a moving target on paper, but they only recieve partial messages from other clusters and have to coordinate. After round 1, split one cluster across two tables, duplicate another cluster's data onto a new table, and merge two clusters together. They vote secretly each round: is there still a subject here with interests? Should we spend collective points protecting the system from further disruption?

Payoffs: collective points for tracking accuracy, individual points for correctly predicting whether the system survives each change. Make protection costly — it slows message passing. Run 3 rounds total.

Debrief should map their secret votes onto boundary-drawing for sentience, say what will predictably go wrong (probably social dynamics, not computation logic), and be honest that this can't reproduce true substrate-independence or continuous processing — you'll supply that part verbally. Don't lecture up front, don't name functional fragmentation until after round 2. Keep it casual.
