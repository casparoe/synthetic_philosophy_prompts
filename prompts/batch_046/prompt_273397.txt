There's this argument my study partner skecthed extending Benatar's asymmetry to branching welfare subjects, and now it feels like it proves way too much. (1) Benatar holds that absence of pleasure is only bad if there's an actual subject being deprived of it. (2) A welfare subject is whatever has a unified point of view - a human, maybe both hemispheres in a split-brain case if they're both conscious, definately not a bee colony. (3) If a subject never comes to exist, there's no deprivation, so failing to reproduce or failing to split my mind into copies is totally harmless. (4) But destroying an existing subject removes goods and causes suffering, so that's clearly bad. (5) Now apply this to potential branches: if I could split into ten conscious copies, those copies are potential subjects, not actual ones. (6) So not splitting is neutral, but killing nine copies after splitting is catastrophic. (7) By parallel reasoning, not having children is neutral, having them creates suffering subjects, and killing them is catastrophic. (8) So far so antinatalist. (9) But here's the weird part: if I take a drug that prevents me from ever being able to split, I'm preventing potential subjects - neutral. But if I take a drug that makes me split uncontrollably into suffering copies, I'm harming actual subjects - bad. (10) The argument concludes that Benatar's logic makes any technology or choice that creates new welfare loci morally dangerous but never required, which seems to prove we should avoid all consciousness-expansion innovation. (11) That feels wrong - surely creating more happy people shouldn't be on a par with destroying them? Where does it slip? Is it (3) treating potential copies like potential children, or an equivocation in (2) about what counts as a unified subject? If I have to reject one, I'd rather keep (1) and (4) intact, but I want to know what fixing (3) costs us.
