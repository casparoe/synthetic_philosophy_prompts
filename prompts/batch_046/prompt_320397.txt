Write a short story, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, about a navigator named Elias who receives an unmarked map predicting his death in Damascus and spends the narrative trying to outmaneuver it by altering his route through a series of self-referential choices. Rather than treating the classic Damascus parable as simple predestination, frame the story around epistemic fatalism and the logic of counterfactual escape: Elias thinks that if he never reaches the city the prediction fails, yet every detour he takes appears to have been anticipated, raising the question of whether the map records a fixed future or creates the conditions for its own fulfillment. Include a conversation in which a companion presses him on whether knowing the endpoint changes the path or merely reveals it, and structure the journey so that its circularity — ending in a place that echoes the beginning — embodies the idea that avoiding a predicted destination can redraw the map without changing the coordinates. Do not resolve the tension into a moral; let the story remain a thought experiment in people and landscapes, faithful to the philosophical problem rather than reduced to allegory.
