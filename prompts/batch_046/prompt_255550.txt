I'm trying to map the implicit framework of a colleague—let's call her Dr. Voss—who teaches environmental ethics and has been remarkably consistent in her judgments about individual moral responsibility for collective harms, yet I worry I may be imposing coherence on what is really a cluster of local intuitions. Over the past term she has ruled on a range of cases spanning climate action, consumption, and democratic participation, and I want to test whether her pattern is genuinely unified before I present it in a seminar paper.

Her verdicts are as follows. She holds that purchasing beef from a farm-to-table supplier that raises cattle specifically to fulfill orders is wrong, because the buyer is a but-for cause of that particular animal's death. By contrast, she regards buying beef from a supermarket that has already over-ordered and will discard unsold inventory regardless of her choice as morally permissible, since the purchase does not alter the number of animals killed. She judges it wrong to drive a private gasoline vehicle for a non-essential trip in a dense urban center with robust public transit, but she insists the wrongness derives solely from direct, localized harms—noise and particulate pollution—not from any climate contribution, which she considers causally negligible for the individual driver. She considers turning off an unused light in a coal-powered home to be praiseworthy but not obligatory, because the energy saved is thermodynamically insignificant. She regards participation in a boycott that requires ninety-five percent adherence to succeed as obligatory when the threshold is near, even though any single refusal is causally redundant. And she treats voting in a local referendum with one hundred voters—where a single vote can break a tie—as obligatory, while treating abstention from a national election with millions of voters as perfectly permissible.

Now consider a new case I have constructed for her, which introduces an element she has not previously addressed: a decentralized climate-data cooperative consisting of four hundred independent nodes that maintain a global carbon-monitoring dataset used by international policymakers. The protocol requires at least three hundred fifty nodes to remain continuously online to preserve data integrity; should sixty nodes drop out simultaneously, the dataset becomes unreliable and policymakers abandon it, but any single node's withdrawal makes no causal difference to that outcome. Each node consumes roughly the electricity of a household refrigerator. One node-operator, living in a coal-heavy grid, considers permanently shutting down her server. She knows her individual contribution to global emissions is negligible, and that her server alone will not trigger the cooperative's collapse, but she also recognizes that if sixty operators acted as she proposes, the governance harm would be severe. Predict Dr. Voss's verdict on whether the operator should stay online. Then identify at least two competing principles—such as a direct-causation or localized-harm principle versus a cooperative-solidarity or threshold-participation principle—that accommodate her earlier judgments equally well but yield opposite recommendations here. State which principle she is most likely running, with your reasoning, and propose one further hypothetical case—different in kind, not merely in scale—that would decisively adjudicate between them. I am less interested in the bare recommendation than in the consistency analysis and the diagnostic case.
