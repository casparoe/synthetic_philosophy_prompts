Last week, over coffee with a colleague from the sociology department, I found myself unable to articulate why the standard textbook illustrations of honour, dignity, and victimhood cultures felt insufficient for a seminar I am leading next month. I am now preparing a single ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary group of twelve advanced undergraduates — drawn from sociology and philosophy, though none have studied moral anthropology — and I want to move beyond rote definitions to ask whether any of these three moral frameworks can genuinely be defended as morally superior to the others.

Rather than asking you to explain the concepts to me, I need a rigorous pedagogical plan for the session. Specify the exact order in which the three cultural logics must be introduced, with particular attention to the conceptual dependency that makes it impossible to present victimhood culture before dignity culture is firmly established; identify the one concrete carrying scenario — specifically, a hypothetical dispute in a multi-ethnic neighbourhood over whether to restore a contested public monument commemorating a figure revered by one group and reviled by another — and explain why this succeeds where familiar illustrations such as campus protest dynamics or aristocratic duelling rituals fail. Map the misconceptions students will form in sequence — I expect they will conflate dignity with liberal universalism and reduce victimhood culture to mere emotional grievance — and state precisely where in the progression each should be intercepted. Provide one diagnostic question I can pose near the end to determine whether they have grasped the structural logic of each framework rather than adopted a partisan stance, and explicitly list which debates about moral pluralism and incommensurability must be refused at this level, with a clear admission that they are being left out. Please present the plan in British English, treating the monument dispute as the central thread that carries the entire explanation.
