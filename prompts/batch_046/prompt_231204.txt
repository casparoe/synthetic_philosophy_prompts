As a journalist who writes on institutional ethics and the philosophy of history, I keep returning to a tension visible in several post-conflict democracies—perhaps most systematically in Germany’s enduring apparatus of state-funded remembrance, but now echoed in newer frameworks of official apology and intergenerational compensation elsewhere. The practice is concrete: tax revenue sustains memorial museums and school curricula about historical crimes; the state issues formal apologies; compensation schemes disburse payments to descendants of victims; and public architecture is permanently altered to mark liability. Yet the legal and pedagogical rhetoric inside those same institutions insists that moral guilt is strictly individual and intransmissible—no generation inherits another’s culpability, and “collective guilt” is treated as a conceptual mistake. The state behaves as though an enduring entity bears a continuing obligation, while its official theory denies that such entities can be moral agents capable of guilt at all.
