I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy preparing a seminar paper on Adam Smith’s account of sympathy and the impartial spectator. For the opening of my section on internal division, I would like a poem—specifically a villanelle in modern free verse, adhering strictly to the nineteen-line form with its two refrains and closing couplet—that takes seriously Smith’s claim that sympathy is not emotional contagion but an imaginative change of situation in which one represents another’s feelings from within their circumstances, and that the impartial spectator is not an external judge but an internalized figure constructed through repeated acts of this imaginative projection. I want the two refrains to enact the structural division Smith describes: one refrain should voice the agent’s effort to enter another’s situation (for example, a line such as “I change my place with yours”), and the other should voice the spectator’s retrospective judgment (for example, a line such as “The figure within looks back”). The villanelle’s formal requirement that these refrains finally converge in the last two lines should mirror Smith’s claim that the agent and the spectator are ultimately aspects of the same divided self rather than independent entities. The poem should avoid resolving whether this internal division yields genuine moral knowledge or only socially constructed self-regulation; it should leave that tension open, since that is where my argument lands. Please make sure the philosophical mechanics are accurate enough that a specialist in Smith’s moral philosophy would recognize the distinction between sympathy and approval, and the specifically imaginative rather than affective nature of sympathetic exchange. At the end, please indicate how confident you are in your rendering of Smith’s view and identify the specific point about which you are least certain—particularly whether the internal division you depict aligns with recent interpretations of Smith’s concept of self-command.
