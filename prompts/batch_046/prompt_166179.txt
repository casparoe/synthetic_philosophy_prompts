I just got out of a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about whether it genuinely makes sense to say that one person’s life is going better—or suffering more—than another’s. We kept circling the same concrete cases: comparing post-injury pain across two patients, weighing whether a policy should deliver a small welfare gain to millions or a large one to a few, and wondering whether a hypothetical technology that let you experience exactly what another person feels would settle the comparison or just push the problem one step back. I want to turn our mess into a diagnostic instrument rather than another circular debate.

Please take the role of an expert philosopher of ethics and welfare economics with training in decision theory. Before constructing the instrument, spend a brief opening section—about two hundred words—brainstorming the logical space of disagreement. In particular, distinguish the metaphysical question (is there any fact of the matter?), the epistemic question (could we ever know it?), and the normative question (should we rank people at all?). Consider whether comparison requires a single-dimensional welfare metric or can proceed through multidimensional dominance relations; whether subjective experience, revealed preference, or objective capabilities provide the right basis; and whether only ordinal ranking is defensible, or whether cardinal intervals or even ratio-scale judgments are possible. Also introduce one somewhat niche conceptual distinction—such as the gap between “thin” ordinal comparison (who is better off) and “thick” ratio-scale comparison (how many times better off), or the question of whether perfect first-person phenomenological access to another’s mental states resolves interpersonal comparison or still leaves a normative gap—so that the taxonomy is not merely a reproduction of standard encyclopedia entries.

Then write a formal, polished taxonomy structured as a decision tree with clearly labeled branch nodes. The audience is intellectually serious but non-specialist—people like my friend and me—so every node should be phrased as a concrete claim or scenario that someone can answer without already knowing which philosophical label it leads to. The tree should be built from the logical space first, then populated with positions.

The root node should ask whether cross-person welfare comparisons are meaningful at all. From there, branch by the nature of any barrier (metaphysical absence of fact, epistemic inaccessibility, or normative prohibition against ranking). Next, branch by the proposed basis for comparison: phenomenal or hedonic states; preference, choice, or revealed welfare; objective goods or capabilities; or a constructivist or deliberative procedure. Then branch by the strength of comparison permitted within that basis: pure ordinal ranking only; cardinal comparison allowing intervals; or ratio-scale comparison allowing multiplicative judgments.

Aim for about eight to ten leaves. At each leaf, provide a descriptive position name if one is standard (for example, strict ordinalism, interpersonal hedonism, capability-based partial ordering), or label it “unnamed” if the combination is unoccupied or lacks a standard designation. Follow the name with exactly one sentence stating the position’s most awkward or counter-intuitive commitment—what it must accept that sounds strange at first.

Make sure to identify any combinations of answers that are logically possible but currently unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis: is the view incoherent, deeply unattractive, or merely unexplored? Also explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate—positions that agree on every diagnostic case but differ only in underlying metaphysics or rhetorical framing.

Include at least two concrete diagnostic cases embedded in the nodes: for instance, a case involving two people with identical neural pain signatures but divergent self-reports, and a case involving a policymaker who must choose between a small uniform benefit for many and a large benefit for one. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should be fully usable by someone without web access. Keep the tone suitable for a philosophy seminar handout.
