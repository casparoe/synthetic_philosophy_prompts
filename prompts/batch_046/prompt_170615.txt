As a journalist covering science and ideas, I need an operational decision procedure that my editors and I can apply repeatedly when drafting coverage of technological or biological systems—ranging from large language models to neural implants—where intentional-language descriptions such as “the system believes,” “it intends,” or “it recognizes” might be warranted. The procedure should be grounded in the philosophical framework of real patterns and the three explanatory stances—physical, design, and intentional—associated with Daniel Dennett’s work, but it must be usable by non-philosopher copy editors under publication deadlines and must not depend on access to specific unpublished experiments, exotic physics, or science-fictional predictors.

The core challenge I want the procedure to handle is what I call “stance-crossing reports”: cases in which a behavioral pattern is robust and predictively useful at the design stance yet becomes unstable, noisy, or non-reproducible when examined at the physical stance, and in which intentional-stance predictions either conflict with design-stance explanations or fail to improve upon them. For each of three decision nodes—(1) establishing whether the target pattern qualifies as a real pattern rather than an observer-projected artifact; (2) determining whether the design stance alone delivers adequate predictive and explanatory coverage; and (3) deciding whether intentional-stance attribution adds genuine predictive value without violating the design-stance account—provide a concrete checklist item or test that an editor can apply in sequence.

The deliverable should be a formal procedural document, addressed to an editorial review board as standing policy, containing: a step-by-step flow in words specifying evaluation order and conditions for advancing between nodes; three worked examples—one in which intentional language is justified despite physical-level variability; one in which it is unjustified despite superficial behavioral similarity; and one ambiguous case in which the procedure yields an indeterminate result requiring explicit editorial judgment; an honest identification of the structural feature that makes intentional attribution peel away from design-stance description in each case; and a clear flag of the points where the framework is contested or the procedure is likely to misfire (for instance, when physical-level descriptions become available retroactively, altering the predictive economics of the higher stances). Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, reading recommendations, or requests for further sources; build the procedure from the conceptual structure alone, and conclude with an actionable bottom-line recommendation for when our publication should permit, restrict, or flag intentional-language descriptions in science reporting.
