A growing proposal in AI governance holds that a superintelligent system should not present its reasoning in a single transparent form, but should instead route every explanation through an adaptive “epistemic filter” that calibrates the complexity, emotional framing, and inferential depth of its responses to each human interlocutor’s real-time cognitive profile — deliberately withholding representations that the filter predicts the human cannot verify, even when those representations are true and relevant to the decision at hand. The rationale is that extreme persuasive power makes unfiltered transparency dangerous, because humans might assent without comprehension. What theory of epistemic agency, rational autonomy, and the ethics of persuasion does this design practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it assume that understanding can be cleanly decomposed into “verifiable by the user” and “not verifiable by the user” without loss, that autonomy is preserved as long as assent is voluntary regardless of the representational gap, or that there is a legitimate asymmetry in epistemic authority that justifies paternalistic filtering? Is the practice strictly committed to one of these, or can it be licensed by several incompatible theories? And what would have to change in the design — and in our conception of human-machine discourse — if we rejected that underlying theory in favor of one that treats unfiltered, fully explicit reasoning as an inalienable condition of respectful interaction, even at the risk of manipulation?
