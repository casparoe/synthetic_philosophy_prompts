You are delivering the opening remarks at a memorial colloquium honoring Dr. Mara Ellison, a philosopher of mind who died last month at sixty-four. Her work defended a strict token-epiphenomenalist account of phenomenal consciousness, coupled with a claim she developed over decades: that if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then first-person judgments about them are not caused by the properties they purport to describe, producing a structural asymmetry that distinguishes phenomenal knowledge from every other empirical domain. She treated this not as a skeptical threat but as a boundary condition that any adequate theory of mind must accommodate. The audience is deliberately mixed: family members with no philosophical training, the graduate students she mentored, senior colleagues from philosophy and cognitive neuroscience, and clinical researchers who collaborated on her consciousness studies. You have been asked to set the intellectual frame for the day without either sanitizing her view into a palatable falsehood or allowing it to sound as though she denied that grief, wonder, and suffering are genuinely real. Because her position imposes real constraints on what can be said, several conventional consolations are structurally unavailable to you: you may not suggest that she is in any sense aware of this gathering, that her phenomenal life continues in some form, or that the audience's present grief is causally shaped by her phenomenal states. At the same time, you must account for why her research mattered to people outside academic philosophy, acknowledge honestly what it means to convene in her absence, and offer a form of comfort that does not depend on claims her work would have rejected. Write these remarks.
