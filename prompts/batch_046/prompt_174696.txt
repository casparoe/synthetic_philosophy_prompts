Please construct an argument that gradual replacement of biological neural tissue with functionally equivalent artificial substrates — the familiar Ship of Theseus scenario applied to mind uploading — fails to preserve personal identity, not because of any abrupt discontinuity in psychological function, but because it introduces what we might call normative fragmentation. In a genuinely incremental transition, the practical authority to bind future stages to prior commitments is dispersed across a sequence of intermediate agents, none of which is strictly identical to either the original biological person or the final digital substrate. On this view, the standard psychological-continuity defence misses the point: functional isomorphism between stages does not restore a single, continuous locus of diachronic self-authorship. You should explain why piecemeal consent at each stage cannot repair this gap, and why the result is better understood as a succession of partly authorised successors rather than one enduring self. Be precise about who bears normative standing at each point in the sequence, and address the objection that voluntary, step-by-step agreement is sufficient to maintain identity across the transition.
