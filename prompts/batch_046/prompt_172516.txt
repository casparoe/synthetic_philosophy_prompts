I'm on this cooperative housing board — twelve units, everyone knows each other way too well — and we keep getting grievance filings that aren't legal issues but still need a call. I took a course on moral philosophy and got fascinated by Adam Smith's writing on sympathy and the impartial spectator, specifically his idea that we judge actions by running a kind of internal simulation: first you feel with the person acting (sympathy), then you check that feeling against an imaginary impartial spectator who isn't involved and can correct for your biases. I want to turn that into a reusable proceedure our board can actually run in 45 minutes, since we meet every other Teusday and two of us have to sign off on anything. None of us are philosophers, so the steps need to be concrete, with a small checklist we can literally read out loud.

Please write me a decision flowchart in words: the ordered questions we ask about a grievance, starting with the sympathy check (what was the agent actually feeling and facing?), then the spectator shift (how would an uninvolved but informed observer see the propriety of the sentiment and the action?), with clear flags for when sympathy is pulling us toward leniency we should resist. I want hard cases built in — like when the offender is in crisis and our sympathy is overwhelming, or when the affected party is being overly sensitive — and I need you to mark exactly where the proceedure has to hand the decision back to human judgment because it's a values call, not a fact. Inject something like 'spectator-distance tiers' — maybe Tier 1 direct neighbor, Tier 2 informed outsider, Tier 3 stranger — and a 'sympathy-bias correction' step where we have to state whose feelings we're tracking and from what distance. Then apply it to three cases: a musician practicing loud late at night after losing a gig, a compost-bin dispute where one resident is putting meat in and it smells terrible, and a case where the board's sympathy for a depressed resident makes them want to excuse a clear rule violation but the impartial spectator says no — include the one that gives us an answer we'll probably hate. Also say what this proceedure definitely can't handle and where it will misfire, especially with people we actually like.
