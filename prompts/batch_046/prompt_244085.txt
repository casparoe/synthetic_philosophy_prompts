I am trying to calibrate my disagreement with an interlocutor about liberal eugenics, understood here not as state-coerced breeding but as a parental pro tanto obligation to use available genetic selection to maximize the expected well-being of the particular child who will be conceived. Give me your credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that such an obligation exists in principle, but do not provide only the number. Decompose the judgment into its constituent sub-questions: first, whether the non-identity problem genuinely neutralizes harm-based objections to selecting for traits that correspond to disability in counterfactual comparison; second, whether expected well-being can be operationalized across genotypic differences without covertly presupposing a normative baseline of species-typical functioning; third, whether reproductive liberty carries lexical priority over a merely pro tanto beneficence claim; and fourth, whether the aggregation of individually rational selection decisions creates a collective-action risk of genetic stratification sufficient to vitiate permissibility even when each act is benign. Assign a rough probability to each component, indicate the logical structure by which they combine—whether as independent necessary conditions, as weighted considerations, or otherwise—and identify which sub-judgment contributes most to the distance between your final credence and the nearest extreme. Finally, specify what empirical finding—concerning, for instance, the heritability of subjective well-being or the elasticity of social mobility under voluntary enhancement regimes—would most substantially revise your overall number.
