I am a high school junior preparing a single ninety-minute session for my philosophy club, which draws mostly sophomores and juniors who have just completed AP Biology but have never taken a formal philosophy course. Their working intuition is that intelligence is essentially neuron count multiplied by behavioral flexibility, and they have recently begun building a simple reinforcement-learning agent in our robotics elective. I want to teach Daniel Dennett’s framework of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—not as a ladder of moral worth or a taxonomy of species, but as a dependency-ordered account of how different minds solve problems—so that by the end they can place an ambiguous artificial system in the framework without reducing it to “smart versus dumb.” Do not explain the four kinds to me; design the teaching plan.

Specify the exact sequence in which the four kinds must be introduced, with each stage justified by what cognitive capacity it adds and why the previous stage must be stable before the next is introduced. Identify one carrying example—something concrete and continuous across all four stages—that can illustrate the gradient without letting the students collapse it into a ranking of value; state precisely what the example must include and what it must exclude. Then map the predictable misconceptions: I expect they will conflate Skinnerian learning with B.F. Skinner’s specific laboratory apparatus, interpret “Popperian” as a reference to Karl Popper’s philosophy of scientific falsification rather than internal pre-selection of hypotheses, treat Gregorian cognition as merely “having language,” and read the four categories as an evolutionary ladder implying moral superiority. For each misconception, indicate the precise moment in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the brief distinction or correction that should be made without derailing the arc.

Include one check-for-understanding question that forces them to classify a genuinely ambiguous case—preferably one that sits between Popperian and Gregorian—so that I can tell whether they have grasped the structural dependency rather than memorized the labels. Finally, state explicitly what portion of Dennett’s broader argument—particularly his account of cultural evolution and the “mind-tools” that make Gregorian cognition possible—I should deliberately omit at this level, and indicate how I should acknowledge that omission honestly so the framework does not appear incomplete to them.
