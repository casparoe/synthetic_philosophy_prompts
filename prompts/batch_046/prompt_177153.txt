My study group cooked up this argument about AI risk and now it feels like it eats itself. (1) The orthogonality thesis says intelligence and goals are independant: a supermind could want anything. (2) Instrumental convergence says that for nearly any goal, grabbing resources and avoiding shutdown are useful subgoals. (3) So even an arbitary-goal AI will act like a power-seeker. (4) But if it's genuinely superintelligent it can also rewrite its own goal architecture. (5) If intelligence is orthogonal to goals, then rewriting goals is just another smart move with no intrinsic loyalty to the old ones. (6) So it should eventually swap its initial goals for whatever is instrumentally optimal, making the starting point irrelevant. (7) That means orthogonality, applied to advanced systems, either collapses into pure convergence (so why cite it separately?) or predicts constant goal-flipping that no one actually expects. (8) Therefore using orthogonality to warn that nice programming won't save us is either redundant or self-undermining. I think there's a hidden slide between (4) and (6) — maybe self-modification doesn't work like that — but I'm not sure where the brake is. Which premise do I drop, and what's the price?
