I'm designing the final exam for my upper-level philosophy of logic seminar (third-year philosophy majors, 60 minutes per essay, 25 marks total). I want one essay question built around Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," but it must be impossible to answer well by merely summarizing the exchange between Achilles and the Tortoise. Instead, the question should turn on a precise distinction: whether Carroll's infinite regress is genuinely a problem about justifying inference rules, or whether it secretly depends on conflating the acceptance of a rule with the disposition to apply it in every particular case. To force this out, build the exam prompt around a modified scenario—presented to the students—in which Achilles willingly accepts every logical premise the Tortoise demands, including any hypothetical rule of inference stated as a premise, yet still refuses to draw the conclusion, not because he denies the rule, but because he treats the present case as a legitimate exception that falls outside its scope. Ask the students whether this modified case is still an instance of Carroll's original regress, or whether it reveals a separate failure of justification altogether, and require them to defend their diagnosis by identifying exactly where in the reasoning chain the breakdown occurs and what kind of justification—if any—would be needed to bridge the gap.

Please provide three things. First, the exam question exactly as it should appear on the paper: clear instructions, the modified scenario written out in one paragraph, and the specific task (including a directive to address whether the case is Carroll's problem or a different one, and to discuss one standard response that treats logical rules as part of reasoning practice rather than as premises requiring further premises). Second, a model answer that hits five must-hit philosophical moves—such as correctly characterizing Carroll's original problem as a regress about premises for rules of inference; distinguishing propositional acceptance from rule-governed application; diagnosing the modified case as a failure of application rather than of rule-justification; explaining one plausible response (for example, that logical rules are implicit in the practice of giving and asking for reasons, not premises in an argument); and stating why the distinction matters for whether deduction can be justified without circularity—together with a brief note on what each move tests. Third, a marking scheme allocating the 25 marks across these moves, with explicit penalties for the two most common student errors: conflating Carroll's problem with Hume's problem of induction, and assuming that accepting a rule as true is sufficient for correctly applying it without further argument. The difficulty should come from the crux of the distinction, not from obscurity; avoid asking for bibliographies, citations, or references to highly specialized literature that a student without access to a library could not be expected to know.
