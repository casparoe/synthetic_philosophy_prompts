I serve on the Residential Life and Character Education Committee of a university, which must vote by the end of next month on whether to pilot a first-year residential program modeled on Benjamin Franklin's system of incremental moral self-improvement from his Autobiography. The proposal asks students to maintain a weekly virtue-tracking journal, cycling through Franklin's thirteen virtues—Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and the belatedly added Humility—recording daily lapses with black marks and concentrating on one virtue per week in rotation. Proponents argue that Franklin offers a secular, practice-based framework that treats moral development as habit-formation rather than doctrinal adherence, and that his candid admission that he never achieved true perfection—particularly after a Quaker friend confronted his pride and prompted the thirteenth virtue—makes the model humbly realistic rather than perfectionist. Opponents counter that Franklin's list encodes specifically eighteenth-century bourgeois and Protestant values that privilege productivity, bodily control, and silent deference, while framing ethics as individual self-surveillance rather than relational or structural responsibility; they further argue that because Franklin appended Humility only after social embarrassment, his taxonomy is reactive and ad hoc, undermining any claim that it represents a coherent account of moral perfection. A further complication raised in our last session is whether Franklin's teleological orientation—treating virtue as progressive approximation to an ideal—can be separated from its elite cultural roots without reducing the exercise to an arbitrary regimen of self-discipline.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—adopt the pilot with specific modifications, reject it outright, or adopt it on a clearly defined trial basis with a fixed evaluation date—rather than leaving the matter open. Your reasoning should treat Franklin's own conception of moral perfection seriously, and it must give an intellectually honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it. Lead with your bottom-line recommendation in the very first sentence, then provide the reasoning; do not include a preamble or restate the question.
