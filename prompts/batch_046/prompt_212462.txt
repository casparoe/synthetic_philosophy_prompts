I'm running a single seventy-minute session for interdisciplinary grad students—mostly cognitive scientists with minimal philosophy—on free-floating rationales: normative reasons that hold without being anchored to any particular agent's mental states, intentions, or reasoning processes. They will arrive assuming rationality is always agent-relative or at least possessed. Plan the explanatory arc: which conceptual distinction must be secured first (between reasoning, justification, and rationale) and in what dependency order the layers unfold; choose one concrete carrying example that is neither moral nor mental—something drawn from institutional design or biological structure—and specify exactly what it must illustrate and what it must avoid being mistaken for; map the misconceptions they will form in sequence—likely that "free-floating" means objective-but-still-needs-a-mind-somewhere, then that this is merely functional explanation in normative clothing, then that if no agent can act on it the claim is not genuinely normative—and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted; design one final check that reveals whether they have grasped the idea rather than merely acquiesced to it; and state explicitly what philosophical machinery—particular realist metaphysics of norms, specific historical debates about naturalism, or named theoretical frameworks—you will deliberately leave out and openly say so.
