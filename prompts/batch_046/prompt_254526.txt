I am marking examination scripts for an advanced undergraduate seminar on collective rationality and democratic theory, and I should like you to assess the following answer in the manner of an external examiner. The question asked students to analyse a sequential variant of the discursive dilemma involving a three-member deliberative body. The body must evaluate a proposal on two premises — P (that the proposal is effective) and Q (that the proposal is equitable) — with the collective conclusion R (approve the proposal) following by logical entailment from P and Q. The protocol requires an initial anonymous vote on the premises, derivation of R, and then — only if the derived result conflicts with a direct majority vote on R — permission for members to revise exactly one of their premise judgements before a final binding decision. Candidates were asked to determine whether this revision mechanism resolves, relocates, or intensifies the original dilemma, paying particular attention to the tension between collective rationality and democratic responsiveness.

The student wrote: The discursive dilemma occurs when majority votes on premises and on a conclusion are jointly inconsistent. In the standard three-person case, two members endorse P, two endorse Q, but only one endorses R, so premise-based aggregation yields approval whilst conclusion-based aggregation yields rejection. Under the sequential protocol, members may revise one premise after observing the conflict, which removes the inconsistency because they can adjust their premises to match their preferred conclusion; hence the protocol resolves the dilemma. Nevertheless, this is essentially Condorcet’s jury problem applied to propositions rather than preferences — just as Condorcet showed that majority voting can cycle over pairwise comparisons, the discursive dilemma shows that logical aggregation can fail. Furthermore, because Arrow’s impossibility theorem proves that no aggregation rule can satisfy all fairness conditions, the discursive dilemma is properly understood as a special case of Arrow’s result restricted to binary propositions. It follows that if the body wishes to preserve both rationality and democratic legitimacy, it ought to adopt conclusion-based aggregation, since that respects the direct majority will on the outcome that matters.

Please award a grade on the British undergraduate scale and provide point-by-point comments that justify it precisely. Do not open with an overall verdict; instead, work through the answer sequentially, indicating where the analysis is sound, where it subtly conflates distinct aggregation problems, where a misattribution or misnaming occurs, and where a claimed resolution does not in fact follow. In particular, comment on the treatment of the revision mechanism, the comparison with Condorcet and Arrow, and the final normative recommendation. Your assessment should read as a detailed marking sheet rather than a general impression, and you should avoid signalling any prior view of the script.
