I'm the lead curator at a small regional museum and we just acquired a big donation of unprovenenced ritual objects — pottery shards, broken figurines, carved bone tools — probably late bronze age but the dating is shaky and nobody agrees on the site culture. We're planning an exhibit for next spring and I'm sick of seeing other museums put up labels claiming "this proves sun-goddess worship" or "this demonstrates a priestly hierachy" based on basically one fancy pot. I want a decision procedure my copy editors can actually run — step-by-step, in order — for deciding when it's okay to move from an artifact to a claim about a belief or social instution. Ground it in the real inference problems archaeologists deal with, like equifinality and the gap between what people did and what they believed, but translate it into something a non-specialist can apply in about ten minutes per object. For each step: what's the pass/fail test, where the procedure stops cold with "not enough evidence," and where the call is about values (how conservative our labeling should be) rather than facts. Then apply it to two cases: a decorated burial urn where the board really wants to claim ancestor veneration but the objects might only support status display, and a cluster of standardized weights that could imply a centralized trade authority or might just reflect local convention. Make sure you say what the procedure cannot do, and include one case where it gives an answer the museum won't like. Finally, please say how confident you are that this captures the archaeology correctly, and single out the specific step or assumption you're least sure about — my friend who studies philosophy of science said I'm probably being naive about middle-range theory, so don't hide behind vague language.
