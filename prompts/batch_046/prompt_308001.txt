I'm preparing a 90-minute workshop for about sixteen advanced undergraduates in a comparative politics course; we've been examining federalism and subsidiarity across the US, Germany, Switzerland, and the EU, and I want an activity that makes participants feel why subsidiarity is so difficult to operationalize—not as an abstract slogan, but as a concrete institutional design problem in which the level assigned to a decision determines whether a solution is even reachable.

Please design a structured simulation divided into three blocs of roughly five to six students each. The scenario should be a cross-border environmental externality—specifically, regulating synthetic agricultural runoff that flows across subnational boundaries—so that pure local autonomy clearly fails. Assign each bloc a different constitutional architecture and corresponding decision rules: one bloc operates under US-style competitive federalism (state-level autonomy with conditional federal preemption via spending or commerce powers); one under German-style cooperative federalism (joint legislative competences requiring agreement between federal and Länder representatives, generating a Politikverflechtungsfalle risk); and one under Swiss-style direct-democratic federalism (cantonal sovereignty combined with a popular referendum veto). Give every participant a hidden role card—local representative, regional/state minister, federal minister, or direct-democracy delegate—with point-based incentives: local actors gain for protecting regional agricultural interests, federal actors gain for reducing pollution, and all blocs lose points if no binding agreement is reached within thirty minutes. The payoff structure and veto rules should be arranged so that at least one bloc deadlocks despite universal preference for coordination, another reaches rapid agreement at severe local cost, and a third is tempted to deploy its democratic veto strategically rather than substantively.

After the negotiation round, I need a debrief—no longer than thirty minutes—that maps each bloc's experience onto actual constitutional provisions without turning into a lecture. The debrief should identify the specific moment in each bloc's negotiation that corresponded to the US conditional-spending mechanism, the German Bundesrat's concurrent-legislation trap, and the Swiss cantonal referendum. Crucially, include an honest note on which normative feature of subsidiarity—the claim that authority should rest at the "lowest competent level"—the simulation cannot genuinely reproduce, since "competence" is precisely what the blocs fought over rather than discovered. Also provide: the exact timeline for the full ninety minutes, the point-based payoff scheme (no real money), rules for inter-level negotiation within each bloc, a facilitator script for guiding the debrief, and a prediction of what will predictably go wrong—likely that the Swiss bloc treats the referendum veto as a bargaining chip rather than a democratic check—along with guidance on how to handle that breakdown without interrupting the simulation's flow.
