At a recent faculty governance session, two colleagues debated whether our disciplinary panel should hold Professor Hale responsible for failing to disclose a significant conflict of interest. It is agreed that the failure stemmed largely from a deep-seated optimism bias—shaped by years of professional socialization—that led Hale to interpret ambiguous evidence in her own favor. The disagreement turned on how Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument constrains institutional judgment.

(1) Dr. Reeves argued that the argument applies directly: Hale did not cause her own cognitive architecture, so she is not responsible for the way she is, and therefore she cannot be truly morally responsible for the omission that flowed from it. Any punitive sanction based on desert is therefore unjustified.

(2) Dr. Okonkwo replied that this misapplies the argument to institutional practice. The panel’s charge is not to determine cosmic desert but to assign accountability for a specific volitional failure—namely, the failure to complete the disclosure form. The infinite regress is irrelevant at this practical level, because control over the discrete act of filing was available to Hale regardless of her character’s origins.

(3) Dr. Reeves countered that there is no bare volition separable from interpretation. The failure to file was mediated by the very optimism bias that shaped Hale’s perception of the situation as non-problematic. To be responsible for the volitional failure, she must be responsible for the interpretive framework that produced it, which sends us back to Strawson’s demand that she be responsible for the way she is.

(4) Dr. Okonkwo conceded that true desert-based responsibility may indeed be impossible on Strawson’s view, but insisted that the panel operates on forward-looking grounds—protecting students and maintaining institutional trust. He added a further point: since Hale, upon being informed of the bias, has now explicitly endorsed it, stating that she will not alter her optimistic approach to conflict evaluation, she has made the bias her own in the present. This reflective endorsement blocks the regress for all future omissions, even if it does not retroactively justify punishment for the past one.

As a bystander with no stake in the outcome, I am unsure whether the panel should distinguish past and future responsibility in this manner, or whether Reeves is correct that any sanction collapses into an unjust attribution of cosmic desert. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then state which specific point each party gets right, whether they share a hidden agreement they do not acknowledge, and what—if anything—both are missing about how Strawson’s argument should constrain institutional practice.
