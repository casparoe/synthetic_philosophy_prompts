Consider the track record of an epistemologist—call her Dr. R—on cases involving bootstrapping and what has been called easy knowledge. Her verdicts so far are these.

Case 1 (Perceptual loop): A subject distinguishes two nearby shades of blue using color vision, takes that success as confirming her color vision is functioning, then identifies the color of a distant object using vision alone. Dr. R says the subject knows the distant object's color, but does not come to know that her color vision is generally reliable.

Case 2 (Testimonial loop): A subject asks an informant whether he is generally truthful, receives an affirmative answer, and then accepts the informant's report about an experimental result. Dr. R says the subject lacks knowledge of the report's content.

Case 3 (Calibrated instrument): A subject verifies a digital scale's accuracy using a certified reference weight—an independent physical check—then uses the scale to determine her own weight. Dr. R says she knows her weight and also gains knowledge that the scale is accurate in this range.

Case 4 (Memory loop with false intermediate): A subject recalls event E1, uses that recall to establish her episodic memory is reliable, then recalls E2; but her belief that E1 occurred exactly as she remembers contains a false detail. Dr. R says she does not know that E2 occurred, and she does not come to know that her memory is reliable.

Case 5 (Proof-checker loop): A subject uses an automated proof-checker with a long error-free record to verify a proof she constructed, accepts the result, and takes this agreement as further evidence of the checker's reliability. There is no false intermediate belief, and the specific theorem being checked was not part of the checker's prior verification set. Dr. R says the subject knows the theorem is true, and that the circularity is benign.

Now consider Case 6 (The predictive taste-model): A subject trains a machine-learning model L exclusively on her own past philosophical judgments. She prompts L with a novel meta-ethical argument she has not previously evaluated; L returns an evaluation that matches her own intuitive reaction. She takes this agreement as confirming that L tracks her genuine philosophical competence rather than mere pattern-matching. She then relies on L's evaluation of a second, more complex argument—one she has not read—to form her belief that the second argument is valid. There is no false intermediate belief in the chain, and L has never failed on arguments of this complexity in its training distribution, though that distribution consisted entirely of her own prior judgments.

Predict Dr. R's verdict on Case 6: does the subject know that the second argument is valid? Then identify at least two plausible implicit rules that jointly fit her verdicts in Cases 1–5, say what each rule predicts about Case 6, and argue for your best guess about which rule she is actually running, with reasons. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—different in structure from Cases 1–6—that would clearly distinguish the two rules you identified.
