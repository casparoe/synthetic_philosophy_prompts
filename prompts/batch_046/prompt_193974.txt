Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Dr. Kiran Mehta, who defends the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) in anthropic reasoning, and Dr. Lena Vasquez, who defends the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA)—debating which preservation protocol an interplanetary archive should adopt. Protocol Alpha distributes exactly one million identical copies of Earth’s cultural record across confirmed habitable worlds; Protocol Beta generates either zero or ten trillion copies via a quantum-fluctuation replication substrate, each outcome carrying prior probability one-half. The archive committee asks them which protocol maximizes the expected evidential value of an observer finding herself inside the archive’s dataset, given that the committee’s cosmological model assigns equal prior weight to a universe containing 10^30 total observers and one containing 10^50.

Both speakers must reason from first principles—indexical uncertainty, reference-class selection, and evidential updating—without citing authors, naming specific historical thought experiments, or using verbatim quotations. Mehta should press the “presumptuous philosopher” objection to SIA: that SIA renders independent cosmological priors irrelevant by overweighting the bare fact of one’s own existence, and that it fails to restrict the reference class of possible observers in a principled, non-ad hoc manner. Vasquez should counter that SSA arbitrarily excludes observers who could have existed but do not, and that SIA correctly treats one’s own existence as direct data about the total population of observer-moments, particularly when the very parameter being estimated causally determines whether observers arise at all.

Require genuine back-and-forth: Mehta must directly answer Vasquez’s charge that SSA smuggles in an unjustified boundary around the reference class, and Vasquez must directly answer Mehta’s charge that SIA collapses rational belief into a function of self-location regardless of external cosmological evidence. Each speaker should be technically precise—using terms such as reference class, observer-moment, prior distribution over cosmic scales, and evidential weight—while avoiding strawman representations. Include at least one moment where one speaker concedes a local point and slightly revises her framing, without abandoning her core assumption. The dialogue should conclude not with a declared winner, but with a precise statement of what empirical or conceptual finding—perhaps regarding the causal structure of observer-generation or the correct demarcation of an indexical reference class—would actually settle the disagreement.
