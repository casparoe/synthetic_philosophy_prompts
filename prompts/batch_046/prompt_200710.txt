I am grading a final examination for an upper-level course on the ethics of historical judgment, in which students were required to apply a modified framework for the "man of his time" defence. Rather than treating only the familiar epistemic and normative variants, the exam asked students to distinguish three layers—epistemic limitation, normative-framework limitation, and institutional-role limitation—and to apply all three to a specific case. I need you to evaluate one submitted answer as if you were the instructor: provide a specific point-based grade and justify it with point-by-point comments that identify sound reasoning, subtle errors, and omissions, without signaling your own moral view of the figure in question.

The exam prompt read: "Distinguish three versions of the 'man of his time' defence—epistemic (the agent lacked access to relevant moral knowledge), normative (the agent's dominant social framework did not recognize the wrongness), and institutional-role (the agent's formal position structurally required actions that personal morality might reject). Apply all three to Christopher Columbus's establishment and enforcement of the encomienda system between 1493 and 1500, using documented policies or recorded actions rather than general reputation. Then argue whether the failure of any or all defences removes our entitlement to judge Columbus, or merely moderates the severity of judgment, and explain why that distinction matters."

The student wrote: "The epistemic defence fails because Columbus's journals show he recognized indigenous people as human and documented their suffering under forced labor. The normative defence also fails: although fifteenth-century Spanish society endorsed conquest, figures like Bartolomé de las Casas demonstrated that the framework contained resources to condemn encomienda, so Columbus could not claim his society uniformly endorsed the harm. In fact, because Las Casas protested within Columbus's own administrative context, this proves the normative framework was already available to Columbus. The institutional-role defence succeeds but is actually part of the normative one, since Columbus's legal obligations as appointed Viceroy under his contractual agreement with the crown embodied society's values. Accepting this defence for Columbus therefore commits us to accepting it for Jefferson's slaveholding, which was also legally protected and economically embedded. Since the epistemic and normative defences fail, and the institutional version collapses into the normative one, there is no remaining excuse; we are entitled to condemn Columbus without any moderation. The absence of a valid defence removes all reason for leniency."

Please assess this answer. Give a specific grade and then detailed, point-by-point commentary addressing the handling of each of the three defence layers, the comparison to Jefferson, the chronological and logical relationships drawn, and whether the student has properly answered the final question about entitlement versus moderation.
