Question: Compare Hart's concept of the minimum content of natural law with Fuller's internal morality of law. In particular, explain (i) how each thinker uses this element to respond to the charge that legal positivism collapses law into a system of mere coercive command, and (ii) whether Fuller's eight desiderata—generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, consistency, feasibility of compliance, stability over time, and congruence between official action and declared rule—should be read as purely procedural conditions on efficacy or as carrying substantive moral weight. Be precise about what each thinker takes to be necessary for a normative system to count as law rather than as raw social power.

Student answer: Hart addresses the coercive-command objection in The Concept of Law by arguing that every functioning legal system must contain a minimum content of natural law—rules prohibiting violence, theft, and deception, together with basic protections for property and promises. He derives these constraints not from moral intuition but from empirical generalizations about human vulnerability, approximate equality of physical strength, limited altruism, and the need for social cooperation. Because these rules are universal prerequisites for group survival, they appear in every legal system, yet they remain contingent facts about human nature rather than necessary moral truths. Consequently, Hart maintains the separation of law and morality: law can be identified through the rule of recognition without appealing to substantive moral criteria, even though legal content will usually overlap with basic morality. Fuller, in The Morality of Law, offers a different defense. He proposes that law possesses an inner morality composed of eight procedural conditions: rules must be general, promulgated, non-retroactive, clear, consistent, possible to obey, stable across time, and applied with congruence to official declarations. Fuller argues that a system systematically violating these conditions fails not merely as an efficient mechanism of control but fails to be law altogether, since it cannot guide human action in a manner consistent with respect for agency. This appears to collapse the separation thesis, but Hart responds in the postscript to The Concept of Law that Fuller's conditions are really just conditions of efficacy—if rules are secret or contradictory, people cannot follow them, so the system will not achieve general obedience. Thus Hart treats Fuller's internal morality as an instrumental requirement for any coercive system to function, not as a moral constraint on legal validity. There is, however, a deeper convergence. Fuller's eight desiderata actually extend Hart's own rule of recognition, because both frameworks require citizens to identify valid norms without consulting hidden moral premises. Furthermore, Fuller's emphasis on congruence and feasibility reveals that his position is closer to a substantive natural law tradition—similar to Thomist accounts—because these conditions encode a commitment to human dignity that transcends mere procedural regularity. Therefore, although both thinkers reject the image of law as pure coercion, they arrive from opposite directions: Hart embeds law in empirical human needs, while Fuller embeds procedural fairness within a substantive moral framework that ultimately undermines legal positivism.
