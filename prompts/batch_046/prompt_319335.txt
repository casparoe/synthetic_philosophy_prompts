I've been working through the idea of evidential cooperation in large worlds, and I keep hitting a conceptual snag that the standard introductions don't address. Suppose two decision-makers are in causally disconnected regions, and each knows there are many copies of both of them spread across space. They want to cooperate evidentially—each taking an action that benefits the other because they are correlated—but they disagree on the prior over how many copies exist: one treats the total number as uniformly distributed over a vast range, while the other weights heavily toward a specific large value. My question is whether this disagreement about scale breaks the cooperation condition, or whether they can still coordinate conditionally on each copy's behavior being linked. Specifically, I want to understand the distinction between cooperating with the copies you personally think exist, versus cooperating with the copies your counterpart thinks exist, and why this isn't just resolved by averaging over priors. Can you explain the conceptual structure here—why the reference class for counting copies matters for evidential reasoning, and whether there's a coherent way to cooperate without first agreeing on the size of the world?
