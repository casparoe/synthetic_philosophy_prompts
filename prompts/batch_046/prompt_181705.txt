ok so i was at a meetup last week and someone brought up this old philosophy problem called bradley's regress about how a sentence actually holds together as one thing. i started thinking about it in terms of RDF triples since thats how we model knowledge graphs at my job, and now i think i accidentally built an argument that proves sentences with more than one word dont exist? let me lay it out:

(1) if i say "sarah writes code", thats not just "sarah" and "writes-code" sitting next to each other - there has to be some actual relation or link binding them into one single proposition.
(2) but if that binding relation is itself part of the proposition, then we need another relation to bind "sarah" to that first relation, and then another one for that, so its an infinite regress.
(3) in our RDF store we avoid this by just using triples - subject, predicate, object - and the predicate IS the relation. we dont store a separate "linker" object; the triple itself is the flat structure.
(4) so if propositions work like RDF, either you accept an infinite stack of triples (impossible to represent), or you admit the "proposition" is just a flat tuple with no real internal unity.
(5) but then "sarah writes code and bob reviews it" cant be one unified thought - its just two unrelated triples sitting in the same database.
(6) therefore bradley's regress shows that any complex statement collapses into disconnected fragments, which is obviously wrong but i cant see the escape.

i feel like something slipped between (3) and (4) but i cant name it. i really want to keep (1) since it feels right that binding is real, and i'd rather not say our RDF model is philosophically broken. which step should i reject and what does it cost me? also is there some weird middle ground where relations are real but dont need further binding that im missing?
