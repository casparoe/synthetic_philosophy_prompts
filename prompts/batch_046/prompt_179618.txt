I have been trying to work out for myself what a genuinely adequate moral framework about eating animals and plants would have to accomplish, rather than just winning one argument. The comparisons that keep coming back to me are beef, chicken, and the grains or legumes that replace them, but whatever framework I end up with ought to work generally. Give me the full specification of what such a theory must be able to do to count as adequate for guiding actual dietary choices without hidden inconsistencies.

For each requirement, explain the concrete case or distinction that makes it necessary. The framework must distinguish direct killing from the indirect deaths caused by harvesting machinery, rodenticides, and habitat clearing in crop fields. It must account for the feed-conversion reality that beef requires many more calories of feed per calorie of meat than poultry does, and that much beef feed is grain that itself entails field mortality—so that comparing beef to chicken is not just comparing one slaughterhouse to another. It must decide whether wild animals killed or displaced by clearing land for pasture suffer in a morally different way from farmed animals killed in slaughterhouses, and whether that distinction survives once we notice that crop fields also displace wild populations. It must separate harm that is unavoidable in any food system from harm that is genuinely added by selecting beef over lentils or chicken over rice. It must also respect the biological fact that suffering capacity is not uniform across species, without letting that collapse into the claim that only human-like cognition matters.

Mark each of these as non-negotiable or as a preference of one particular camp—say, those who prioritize minimizing total lives lost versus those who prioritize minimizing intense suffering per unit of nutrition, or those who think land-use change is the decisive variable. If some cluster of these requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps minimizing total animal deaths and also minimizing suffering per calorie, or honoring a sharp direct-versus-indirect distinction while still comparing beef and chicken fairly—identify that tension explicitly and say which single requirement does the most damage to the positions people commonly defend in casual debate. Keep the weight on building the checklist and explaining why each item belongs; I am less interested in a scorecard of named thinkers than in knowing whether the standard itself is coherent, and whether there is a hidden contradiction in demanding all of these things at once.
