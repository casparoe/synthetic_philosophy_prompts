For a feature I am preparing on the afterlife of conservative epistemology in institutional design, I need a structured adversarial-collaboration document. The two parties are institutional-preservation Burkeans—who treat the heuristic commonly called Chesterton's fence as a defeasible but weighty presumption in favor of inherited social arrangements whose full rationales may be opaque to current observers—and rational-reform critics—who grant the underlying epistemic humility but deny that opacity alone shifts any burden of proof onto those proposing change.

Please draft the joint statement both could sign after good-faith collaboration. The deliverable should be meaningful for a reader without web access; do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations, and do not invoke obscure empirical studies or theories a generalist model would not have encountered.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order, with approximate word counts:

1. Joint Agreed Claims (about 350 words, numbered). Cover: a precise paraphrase of what the fence heuristic asserts (without verbatim quotation); the class of institutions to which it applies; the epistemic problem of opaque institutional rationale; the distinction between knowing how an institution operates and knowing what function it serves; agreed boundaries—where both sides reject both naive rationalist redesign and uncritical traditionalism.

2. Residual Disagreements (table format, about 300 words). Each row should contain: the issue; the preservationist formulation in wording that camp accepts; the reformist formulation in wording that camp accepts; and the precise divergence in one neutral sentence.

3. The Cruxes (about 250 words). Identify the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side. I take the central ones to involve whether functional opacity is sufficient for preservation, whether negative institutions—prohibitions, absences, or deliberate non-structures—fall within the scope of the fence, and how path-dependency should be weighted against distributive or efficiency outcomes. State each neutrally.

4. Tests, Cases, and Predictive Divergences (about 350 words). For each crux, present a concrete institutional transition or argument that both sides accept as bearing on the question—use widely recognized examples rather than niche scholarship, such as the replacement of jury adjudication with bench trials in particular common-law jurisdictions, or the digitization of land-title registries in post-colonial legal systems. For each case, record in advance the preservationist prediction and the reformist prediction about the correct interpretation or likely outcome.

5. Verdict on Verbal Dispute (about 150 words). Assess whether any part of the disagreement collapses once terms are fixed—specifically, whether demands for explicit justification and demands for institutional continuity are genuinely incompatible or can be jointly satisfied.

Keep the analysis in the weeds: avoid generic summaries of Burke or Chesterton that could be reproduced from pretraining. Introduce and require both camps to address at least one non-standard conceptual distinction—specifically, the difference between functional opacity (not knowing why an arrangement produces its effects) and structural opacity (not knowing what effects it produces at all)—and apply it to both positive institutions and negative institutions. Do not include verbatim quotations from any published text; paraphrase all references. Do not depend on specific edition numbers, page citations, or obscure secondary literature.
