I'm taking a seminar that connects formal learning theory with classical epistemology, and I'm trying to work out a conceptual point about the relationship between no-free-lunch results and Hume's problem of induction. The theorems show that, averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm performs identically—so any successful learner must depend on an inductive bias that breaks the symmetry. But this seems to relocate rather than solve the problem of induction: instead of asking why we generalize in one particular way from observed data, we have to ask why we privilege one prior distribution over hypothesis spaces rather than another. Could you explain precisely how this relocation works? In particular, is the choice of inductive bias just a restatement of the original inductive problem at a meta-level, or does the formal structure of the argument introduce a genuinely different epistemic challenge—say, about the justification of symmetry-breaking principles like computability or simplicity? I'd like an answer that gets into the weeds: address whether the uniform averaging over problems is itself an unjustified inductive assumption, and whether avoiding collapse requires us to defend our bias in terms that are vulnerable to the same skeptical pressure Hume applied to causal inference.
