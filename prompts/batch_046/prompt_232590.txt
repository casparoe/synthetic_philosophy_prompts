Below is a line of reasoning I developed for a discussion of wrong-kind reasons, applied specifically to moral admiration. I think it must go wrong—its conclusion is that justified admiration is impossible—but I cannot locate the error. (1) A wrong-kind reason for attitude A is a consideration that makes adopting A rational or beneficial without being part of A's constitutive justification; standard cases include bribes for belief or coercive threats for desire. (2) Moral admiration is justified, if at all, solely by the target's genuinely admirable moral qualities; considerations about the admirer are therefore wrong-kind for it. (3) The instrumental-admiration thesis holds that the primary function of admiration is to reinforce the admirer's own dispositions through emotional modeling; consequently, any practical reason to admire ultimately traces back to this self-regulatory benefit. (4) By (1) and (3), every practical reason to admire is wrong-kind. (5) Meanwhile, right-kind reasons—direct apprehension of the target's admirable qualities—are psychologically inaccessible without prior practical motivation; we do not perceive moral excellence except through interpretive frameworks shaped by our own needs. (6) Hence there are never right-kind reasons actually available to an admirer. (7) Therefore moral admiration is never justified, which is implausible. Which step should I reject, and why did it seem plausible? I am inclined to preserve (2) and (4), but I want to know what each available revision—denying (5), denying (6), or weakening (2)—would cost for the underlying moral psychology.
