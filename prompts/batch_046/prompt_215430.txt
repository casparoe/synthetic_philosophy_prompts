I'm delivering the retirement toast for my former advisor, who taught Buddhist philosophy and philosophy of personal identity for thirty-five years and always maintained that anattā is not the claim that people are unreal, but that the specifically permanent, partless, owner-like self—the kind that could survive death unchanged or possess its experiences like a container—does not survive careful phenomenological scrutiny. Roughly half the guests are philosophers and former students who have spent seminars arguing whether anattā maps onto Western bundle theories, reductionism, or something more radical; the other half are family members who have heard her say, over holiday dinners, that "there is no self," and who have quietly worried she meant they were illusions or that she had no real attachment to them. Write a five-minute toast that makes one precise conceptual distinction do real structural work—specifically, the distinction between denying a substantial, enduring self (paramattha-sacca) and denying the conventional, causally continuous person (sammuti-sacca) who can still be loved, missed, and honored. The toast must genuinely celebrate her career without smuggling in a soul, an essence, or a unitary "inner her" that endures unchanged, yet it also must not collapse into cold eliminationism that treats her life as "just a sequence of aggregates." In particular, address the structural tension that retirement toasts usually presuppose—namely, that the same person who started teaching decades ago is now stepping away—and show how that tension can be acknowledged and even turned into affection rather than being papered over. The philosophers in the room should recognize the conceptual moves; the family should feel comforted rather than lectured. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts she assigned.
