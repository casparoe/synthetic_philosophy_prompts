In a graduate seminar on character and moral psychology, two fellow students—Jordan and Sam—became locked in a sustained disagreement about whether situationist findings genuinely undermine virtue ethics. I observed the exchange as a bystander and do not wish to signal which view I find more persuasive; I want a verdict on the arguments exactly as they were made, including who is right about which specific point and what, if anything, both parties are missing.

Jordan opened by citing classic situationist experiments: the obedience study in which participants administered what they believed were painful electric shocks; the study in which seminary students preparing to speak on the Good Samaritan parable largely failed to assist a distressed confederate when told they were running late; and the telephone-booth study in which finding a small coin sharply increased the likelihood of helping a stranger with a dropped folder. Jordan argued that these results show behaviour is driven far more by minor situational variables than by any stable character trait, and therefore that virtue ethics—which makes character the centre of moral evaluation—is empirically untenable in its traditional form.

Sam replied that this targets a caricature. Virtue ethics, Sam said, need not commit to global trait consistency—the expectation that a courageous person will display courage identically across physical, social, and intellectual domains. Instead, Sam proposed that virtues are domain-local: one might possess intellectual courage without physical courage, and situationist variability across domains is precisely what a modular view of character would predict.

Jordan countered that even within a single domain, situationist effects dominate. In the seminary study, all participants occupied the same moral domain—responding to visible suffering—yet time pressure, a trivial situational factor, suppressed helping behaviour dramatically. If a virtue can be overridden by being in a hurry, Jordan said, then it is not a stable local disposition either.

Sam responded by distinguishing behavioural output consistency from dispositional architecture consistency. A local virtue, Sam argued, is not a mechanical tendency to produce a specific action regardless of competing considerations; it is a structured disposition of practical reasoning that weighs relevant reasons. The hurry condition introduced a competing professional obligation, so the failure to stop did not demonstrate an absence of compassion but rather the operation of practical wisdom within a broader framework. Sam then introduced what they called the integration constraint: for a local virtue to count as a genuine trait rather than a mere behavioural habit, it must be integrated into the agent's overall practical reasoning in a recognisable way across contexts, even when behavioural outputs differ.

Jordan then pressed an epistemic objection. Even granting domain-locality and practical reasoning, Jordan said, virtue ethics still needs some criterion to distinguish a genuinely virtuous agent from someone adept at post-hoc rationalisation. If every failure can be absorbed by invoking competing reasons and every success confirms the virtue, the theory risks empirical emptiness. Jordan suggested that virtue ethicists implicitly rely on what they termed the aggregation assumption—that over time and across varied situations within a domain, a genuinely virtuous agent will display a recognisable statistical pattern distinguishable from that of a non-virtuous agent. But situationist data, Jordan claimed, often show that within-domain variance exceeds between-person variance, which undermines even this weaker aggregation claim.

Sam partially conceded the risk of unfalsifiability but argued that the aggregation assumption, properly understood, is normative rather than purely statistical. It is not that virtuous agents behave identically, but that they are subject to a distinctive kind of corrective feedback: they experience their own failures as failures, revise their practical reasoning, and display what Sam called reflexive stability—not stability of behaviour, but stability of commitment to moral improvement within the domain. Sam claimed that situationism actually helps virtue ethics by forcing it to abandon naive behavioural prediction and to focus on the structure of moral learning over time.

Jordan concluded that without behavioural prediction, virtue ethics loses its distinctive explanatory power over a combination of situationist social psychology and ordinary moral education theory. Sam maintained that reflexive stability is precisely the explanatory factor situationist experiments overlook, because such experiments typically measure one-off behavioural responses rather than long-term patterns of moral self-correction.

Please adjudicate this disagreement round by round. For each stage of the exchange, indicate which party makes the stronger argument, where each is missing something important, and whether there is a coherent middle position that preserves what is correct in Sam's integration constraint and reflexive stability without falling into the unfalsifiability problem Jordan identifies.
