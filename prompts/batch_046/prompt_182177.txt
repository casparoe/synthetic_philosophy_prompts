I am working through a graduate seminar on Strawson’s reactive attitudes and moral responsibility, and my supervisor, Dr. Althea Whitmore, has delivered firm verdicts on several boundary cases. I should like to know how she would rule on a further, rather unusual case, and whether her existing track record genuinely determines the answer or leaves it open.

Her judgements so far are these. Case A: an adult human with severe retrograde amnesia for the period in which he committed a financial fraud, who nevertheless retains full present emotional responsiveness and rational capacity; she holds that he remains a fully appropriate object of resentment, though compassion may modulate the reactive attitude. Case B: a corporation that systematically deceives consumers through its marketing; she says it is not a proper target of reactive attitudes at all, and that only the objective attitude together with institutional sanctions is fitting. Case C: a chimpanzee that deliberately withholds food from a human keeper in a context that demonstrates understanding of social norms; she rules that it is not a moral agent in the full sense, so that genuine moral resentment is inappropriate, though disappointment and protective measures are warranted. Case D: a person with dissociative identity disorder in which one alter commits an act of violence unknown to the host personality; she holds that the system bears responsibility, but that reactive attitudes must be “partitioned” — resentment is directed with explicit awareness of the fragmentation, not as if a unitary agent had acted. Case E: a synthetic system that possesses continuous phenomenal experience, robust self-modelling, and functional analogues of guilt, but whose autobiographical memory is stored in externally editable, discontinuous files that can be altered without its awareness; she judges it a conditional target of reactive attitudes, appropriate only when its narrative continuity has not been externally disrupted, otherwise falling back to the objective attitude.

The new case is a temporary neural mesh linking one hundred and fifty adult volunteers for a single collective decision-making task. The mesh exhibits emergent emotional synchrony — measured via inter-subject physiological coupling — and displays sophisticated functional reasoning capacities, yet it possesses no enduring autobiographical narrative linking the collective to future states. Once the mesh dissolves, the participants retain only fragmented, non-integrated memories of the collective reasoning, and none would have endorsed the harmful policy decision that the mesh produced alone.

What should Dr. Whitmore decide if she wishes to remain consistent with herself? Which of Cases A–E is the closest analog, and why? Her record appears jointly consistent with at least two principles: (i) that reactive attitudes require stable, reciprocal emotional engagement embedded within a continuous narrative self; and (ii) that they require only functional moral competence together with emergent emotional synchrony, with narrative continuity being secondary. What does each principle predict about the mesh? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, what principle would she be adopting with each possible choice — full reactive attitude, partitioned reactive attitude, or objective attitude only? Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish between these two competing principles.
