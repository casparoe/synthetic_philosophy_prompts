Question: Is inference to the best explanation (IBE) reducible to probabilistic or deductive reasoning, or does it constitute a distinct form of inference? In your answer, address the so-called bad lot objection and discuss how explanatory virtues—such as simplicity and scope—relate to the likelihood that a hypothesis is true.

Student answer:
Inference to the best explanation is not reducible to deduction, because deduction does not amplify content beyond the premises, whereas IBE clearly does so. It also differs from standard induction, which projects observed patterns onto unobserved cases; IBE instead chooses among competing hypotheses on the basis of explanatory power. Peter Lipton held that explanations are contrastive in form: they answer why one outcome occurred rather than some specified alternative. This contrastive structure resolves Bas van Fraassen’s bad lot objection, since restricting the comparison class to genuinely rival hypotheses means we are no longer picking the best option from an arbitrarily poor set, but evaluating within a properly bounded field. Moreover, Lipton’s distinction between loveliness and likeliness shows that virtues like simplicity and unification are not mere proxies for prior probability; they signal genuine understanding, which is why we should favor a lovely explanation even when its prior likelihood is low. Hence IBE relies on explanatory value rather than on probabilistic calculation alone. The argument from no miracles, developed by Bas van Fraassen, reinforces this point: scientific realism requires IBE, because without it the predictive success of our best theories would be inexplicable.
