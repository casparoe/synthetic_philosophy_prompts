If Simone Weil—who died in 1943 and maintained that political parties operate as machinery for suppressing independent judgment and manufacturing collective passion—were confronted with organizational innovations that emerged only after her death, how would she assess them? Consider three forms: first, algorithmic recommendation architectures that generate ephemeral “micro-parties” around single issues, lacking formal membership, programmatic discipline, or enduring identity; second, liquid-democracy platforms that allow citizens to delegate voting power algorithmically on an issue-by-issue basis, dissolving persistent party affiliation into continuous, calculable exchange; third, transnational supply-chain governance bodies that coordinate regulatory and fiscal policy across corporate and state nodes without democratic mandate or partisan identity. Would Weil regard any of these as genuinely escaping her critique, or would she argue that they reproduce party-logic—demanding mental conformity, substituting collective emotion for reasoned judgment, and replacing truth with belonging—in more diffuse and therefore more dangerous forms? I am particularly interested in whether her core objection was organizational (parties are too hierarchical and large-scale), psychological (belonging corrupts cognition), or epistemic (collective passion displaces individual reasoning), since that determines which modern structure she would find most threatening and whether she would see algorithmic delegation as an improvement or a perfected form of the same pathology.
