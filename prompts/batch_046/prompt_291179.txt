I work as an interpretation curator at a regional history museum — not one of the big ones — and every month we recieve donated objects with basically no provenance, sometimes just a sticky note saying "found near old settlement site, maybe 400 years?" My job is to write the display card and the acquisition justification, and I have to decide whether the thing points to a specific belief (like a religious idea) or to an institution (like a burial practice or trade network). The board keeps pushing back because I'm either overclaiming or being way too timid, and I don't have budget or time to call an academic specialist for every spoon and shard. So I want to turn whatever philosophy of archaeology actually knows about inference from material culture into a hard procedure my team can run in about thirty minutes, with my intern initialing each step.

The framework I want baked in is what I'll call the three-layer filter, but I need it operationalized. Layer one: physical pattern — what's actually present and what's missing. Layer two: functional alternative — could this pattern exist for purely practical reasons without anyone sharing a belief or running an institution? Layer three: cross-domain redundancy — does the same pattern show up in unrelated material categories (pottery, architecture, burial goods, tools) in a way that wouldn't happen unless people were coordinating? For beliefs specifically, there's a stricter sub-test I'm calling symbolic overdetermination: the feature has to be more specific, costly, or arbitrary than any practical need explains. I need the deliverable as ordered questions, each with what counts as a "yes, move on" answer, clear stop-points where the answer is definatly no, and explicit hand-off points where the question is values not facts — like whether we should display a contested religious interpretation even if it's plausible. Flag where the procedure will misfire, especially with unique artifcats or missing context.

Then apply it to three cases: (1) a cluster of ceramic fragments with the same geometric motif found scattered near burial pits, (2) a single ornate metal amulet with zero parallels in our region and no wear patterns, and (3) a set of stone tools arranged in strict size-order inside a storage pit — which I personally really want to read as evidence of a ranked craft guild, but I suspect the procedure will kill that interpretation and leave me with just "efficient storage." Make sure one answer comes out in a way I won't like, and say exactly where my judgment gets overruled. Also tell me flat-out what the procedure can't do — forgeries, totally isolated finds, anything else — so I don't pretend it's magic.
