Question: Consider Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes—such as resentment, gratitude, and indignation—versus the objective attitude, developed in his account of moral responsibility. A university instructor grading final examinations discovers that a student has plagiarized a substantial portion of one answer. The student subsequently explains that they were experiencing an acute anxiety crisis and feared losing a scholarship that required maintaining a specific GPA. The instructor feels resentment, yet also recognizes the explanatory weight of the student's psychological state and institutional pressure. Address three points. First, does Strawson's framework allow the instructor to maintain a reactive attitude, specifically resentment, toward the student despite these excusing conditions, or do such conditions necessarily require adopting the objective attitude? Second, does taking an objective attitude in this grading context entail treating the plagiarism purely as a causal phenomenon to be administratively managed, and is that stance compatible with assigning a failing grade? Third, does Strawson's account imply that institutional roles such as grading necessarily suspend reactive attitudes altogether, or do they merely modify how those attitudes are expressed?

Student answer: Strawson distinguishes reactive attitudes, which are emotional responses like resentment and gratitude that we direct toward others as responsible agents, from the objective attitude, which we take when we view someone as not a fit subject for such responses—typically because of determinism, mental illness, or other excusing conditions. In this case, the instructor can initially feel resentment because reactive attitudes are natural human responses, but the student's anxiety and institutional pressure provide excusing conditions that should lead the instructor to suspend resentment and adopt the objective attitude instead.

The objective attitude involves seeing human behavior as causally determined by psychological and social factors beyond the agent's control. Once the instructor takes this attitude toward the student, they can no longer view the plagiarism as a moral failure deserving evaluation; they must treat it purely as a problem to be managed, such as by referring the student to counseling or adjusting institutional policy. Consequently, assigning a failing grade would be inconsistent with taking the objective attitude, since grading presupposes that the student is responsible for the submitted work.

Moreover, Strawson implies that institutional roles like grading necessarily require the objective attitude. Because grading must be impartial and administrative rather than personal and emotional, anyone acting as an instructor must suspend reactive attitudes entirely and treat all students as objects of institutional management. Therefore, the instructor's initial resentment is inappropriate not only because of the excusing conditions, but because the institutional role itself demands the objective stance.
