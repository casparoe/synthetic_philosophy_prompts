In my philosophy of science seminar, two participants—both with backgrounds in quantitative genetics—have reached an impasse over how to interpret heritability coefficients, and I should like a point-by-point adjudication rather than general commentary. The first contends that if the narrow-sense heritability of a cognitive trait in a population is estimated near 0.6, then genetic differences literally account for sixty percent of the observed phenotypic variance, which implies that broad environmental interventions can reshape at most the remaining share and can never nullify the genetic contribution. The second responds that heritability is strictly a variance ratio within a particular reference population with a specific range of genotypes and environments; if an intervention equalizes environments, genetic variance becomes a larger fraction of the reduced total variance, so a high coefficient can signal environmental uniformity rather than genetic immutability. The first then cites studies of monozygotic twins reared apart, noting that substantial phenotypic correlations persist across diverse adoptive settings, which in his view demonstrates that genetic causation operates independently of environment and justifies treating the coefficient as a stable biological parameter. The second counters that separated twins still share prenatal conditions and are subject to non-random placement into similar adoptive homes, and she adds that standard ACE models neglect genetic nurture—where parental genotypes shape the rearing environment—so the partition of variance into pure genetic and pure environmental components is conceptually unstable in human populations. She grants that the breeder’s equation uses narrow-sense heritability to predict evolutionary response to selection, but insists that this predictive utility for populations does not license causal claims about individual development or about counterfactual outcomes outside the sampled environmental range. The first asks whether this reduces heritability to a mere statistical artifact lacking biological reality; the second denies this, describing it instead as a real but domain-specific predictive parameter that philosophers consistently misread as an ontological causal weight.

Could you go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about which specific claim, where they are talking past each other, and what conceptual distinction—perhaps involving reaction norms, indirect genetic effects, or the logic of counterfactual explanation—they are both missing? I am a retired biophysicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of science, so I would particularly value clarity on whether their disagreement is primarily mathematical, causal, or semantic.
