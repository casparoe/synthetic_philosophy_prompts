I'm completing my dissertation on methodological norms in philosophy of science, and in a recent seminar on demarcation we reconstructed an argument that appears to drive Popperian falsificationism into direct self-refutation. I want to locate the precise fault line, because I am inclined to preserve both the idea that scientific claims risk empirical contradiction and the idea that theories are never tested in isolation. Here is the argument, with steps numbered for clarity.

(1) A claim counts as scientific, according to the falsificationist criterion, if and only if there exists at least one logically possible observation statement incompatible with it. (2) If such an observation statement O is genuinely incompatible with claim S, then observing O should rationally compel the abandonment of S, unless some other part of one's theoretical system can be revised instead. (3) But for any non-isolated claim S embedded in a theoretical network, there is always at least one auxiliary hypothesis A available for revision, so that S can be retained by sacrificing A rather than S. (4) Consequently, for any claim S and any potential falsifier O, a rational agent can always avoid abandoning S specifically by adjusting auxiliaries. (5) Therefore, there is never a case in which observing O rationally compels the specific abandonment of S. (6) By (2), this means no claim S actually satisfies the falsificationist condition of being vulnerable to rational abandonment. (7) Hence, by (1), no claim qualifies as scientific. The result is that falsificationism classifies every theory—including paradigmatic scientific ones—as non-scientific, which is precisely the collapse of demarcation the criterion was meant to prevent.

My intuition is that something slips between (4) and (6), perhaps a hidden quantifier shift or an ambiguity between logical incompatibility and rational compulsion, but I cannot name the error precisely. I am especially reluctant to reject (1) or (3), since both seem essential: science must expose itself to empirical risk, and theories are indeed protected by auxiliary commitments. Which specific step should be rejected, and what conceptual cost does that rejection carry? In particular, does saving falsificationism require denying that auxiliaries are always available for rescue, or does it require a sharper separation between logical falsifiability and the normative psychology of theory change?
