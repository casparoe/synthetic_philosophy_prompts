Consider the following argument concerning epistemic subcontracting, by which I mean the practice of treating outputs from cognitive or social modules—perceptual systems, expert testimony, algorithmic tools—as justified without independently re-deriving their bases. (1) If foundationalism is false, then every justified belief depends on further beliefs for its justification. (2) In subcontracting, agent A treats belief B (produced by module M) as justified for A. (3) If foundationalism is false, B’s justification depends on further beliefs about M’s reliability, which depend on beliefs produced by other modules, yielding either circularity, an infinite regress, or a need for basic beliefs. (4) Empirical psychology suggests human cognition operates through deeply nested, hierarchical subcontracting with no identifiable basic level of self-justifying beliefs. (5) So if foundationalism is false, human cognition is globally unjustified. (6) But human cognition clearly produces knowledge. (7) Therefore foundationalism is true: there must exist basic beliefs that terminate the regress. (8) Yet if basic beliefs are genuinely immune to revision, then scientific revision—which requires that even perceptual or methodological beliefs be correctable—is impossible. (9) Thus foundationalism saves justification only by rendering cognition dogmatic and unscientific. (10) Hence either we accept universal skepticism, or we embrace dogmatism, both of which are unacceptable. Where does this chain slip? Identify the step that is least costly to reject, explain why it initially looks sound, and say what each of the three standard positions—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—must pay to reject it.
