hey so I'm writing a thing for my philosophy of language study group and there's this pattern I've noticed that I don't have the vocab for. You know how Grice talks about implicature - like saying "some of the boys went to the party" and implying not all? Well I've been seeing this other move in political speeches and corporate reports where the speaker doesn't rely on one false implicature. Instead they stack up five or six literally true sentences, but arrange them with strategic pauses and ordering so the listener has to build a false picture just to make the sequence feel coherent. For example - hypotetical - a company report might say "We reduced carbon emissions per unit" then later "Total production doubled" but never mention that absolute emissions went up. The listener walks away thinking the company went green, but every sentence was technically correct and the listener did the wrong math themselves, except the speaker knew exactly which mental assembly people would perform because of the presentation order.

Is there an established term for this kind of scaffolded misdirection? It's not standard conversational implicature because the maxims aren't being flouted - relevance and manner are satisfied if you check each sentence in isolation. It's not lying, and it's not quite framing in the usual media-studies sense because the speaker isn't picking one frame; they're sequencing frames so the audience assembles them into a false model. I want to know if philosophers of language have named this, and if not, can it be defined precisely - like what distinguishes it from just giving information out of order, or from the listener being careless? I don't care about a bare label; I want the carving: where are the edges, what's the nearest established concept it should be compared to, and does it actually fall under some expanded version of speaker meaning?
