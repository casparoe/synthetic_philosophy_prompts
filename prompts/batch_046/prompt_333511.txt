As a retired marine biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science, I have been working through Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species and Alan Turing's 1950 essay on computing machinery, and I find myself unable to resolve a tension in the analogical reasoning each employs.

Consider the movement Darwin makes in the opening chapters. He observes that human breeders—pigeon fanciers, cattle keepers, horticulturists—can produce varieties so modified that they approach specific distinction, despite possessing only fragmentary knowledge of heredity, development, or physiological mechanism. From this partial competence coupled with partial comprehension, he asks us to extrapolate to a process that possesses neither: natural selection, operating over geological time without foresight, representation, or intentional direction, nevertheless generating the intricate functional adaptations we see in wild organisms. The inference inverts our ordinary epistemology of design. Rather than moving from observed organization to an inferred organizing mind, Darwin moves from observed organization to the certainty of its absence, while insisting that the competence—the adaptive form—remains fully intact and indeed vastly amplified.

Turing, in his discussion of whether machines can think, proposes that we replace the direct question with an operational test of behavioral indistinguishability. A system that produces linguistic and problem-solving outputs matching those of a human being should, he suggests, be granted the relevant mental status regardless of its internal architecture, substrate, or evolutionary history. Here, too, there seems to be an inversion: the appearance of mental comprehension—intelligent discourse—is detached from the biological and evolutionary substrate that, in nature, produces the underlying competence for thought.

My difficulty concerns the logical relation between these two inversions. Darwin's argument moves from a cause with limited comprehension (the breeder) to a cause with none (nature), preserving and magnifying the effect (adaptive design). Turing's proposal moves from an effect resembling comprehension (behavioral performance) to an attribution of mental status, while explicitly setting aside the causal substrate—organic evolution—that Darwin demonstrated could generate profound functional competence without any mental guidance whatsoever. Are these genuinely parallel moves—both severing the customary link between understanding and functional success—or do they run in opposite directions? Specifically, does Darwin's analogy depend on a continuity of causal power between intentional and non-intentional domains that Turing implicitly rejects when he separates behavioral performance from evolutionary origin? I am concerned that I may be forcing a symmetry that the texts do not support, and I would welcome a careful unpacking of whether the analogical structures share a hidden premise or instead belong to entirely different logical registers.
