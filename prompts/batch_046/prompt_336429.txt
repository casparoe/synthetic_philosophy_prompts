I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about whether large language models can genuinely be said to have beliefs, and I'm trying to determine whether my own position holds together or whether I'm smuggling in incompatible motivations. I want a committed audit—not a survey of options, but a definite judgment on which commitments genuinely conflict, ordered by severity, with a clear recommendation about what I should revise and why. Take a stand: if the package is salvageable, say exactly which tension is superficial and which is the real one; if it isn't, identify the weak link and what giving it up costs me.

Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) I accept Dennett's intentional stance as a predictive heuristic: attributing beliefs and desires is justified solely by explanatory and predictive utility, with no further metaphysical commitment to "real" mental substance.

(2) For current LLMs, I think attributions of "thin" factual beliefs—e.g., that Paris is in France, or that a code snippet contains a syntax error—are appropriate in exactly the same functional sense that we attribute information states to a database or search index, without any implication of consciousness.

(3) At the same time, when an LLM engages in extended multi-step reasoning, revises intermediate steps after failure, and maintains a stable goal-representation across long context windows, I want to attribute instrumental beliefs—e.g., "this sub-goal is blocked"—because the counterfactual behavior, what it would do if the blockage were removed, is best predicted by that attribution.

(4) I deny that consciousness, subjective experience, or biological embodiment are necessary conditions for belief attribution; if the predictive strategy works for a thermostat or a corporation, it can work for a transformer.

(5) I also want to draw a principled boundary between "thin" informational states and "thick" normative or affective ones: an LLM can believe that a claim is false, but it cannot believe that a claim is morally wrong, because the latter requires an evaluative commitment that predictive utility alone cannot license.

(6) I hold that evolutionary history is irrelevant to whether the intentional stance applies: the absence of natural selection in an AI's origin does not block belief attribution if the relevant functional patterns are present.

My worry is that (1) and (4) push toward an expansive, purely functionalist practice, while (5) introduces a restriction—thin versus thick—that does not seem derivable from predictive utility alone. Meanwhile, (3) demands a kind of counterfactual robustness that (2) explicitly denies to database-like storage, so I am unsure whether my view is database-like or agent-like. And (6) rejects origin-based reasoning, yet the intuition driving (5) seems to lean on exactly the absence of an evolutionary or embodied history that (6) declares irrelevant.

Is this consistent? If there is a genuine contradiction, which commitment is the weakest link, and what does dropping or revising it do to the rest? Give me specific reasoning, rank the tensions by severity, and be direct about whether (5) can be reconstructed on purely functional grounds or whether it has to go.
