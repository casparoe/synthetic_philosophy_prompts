I am drafting remarks for a cultural-heritage board that must decide whether to repatriate ritual objects acquired during colonial campaigns, and I have been using Montaigne's essay on cannibals to think through cross-cultural moral judgment. My commitments are these: (1) Montaigne's comparison of Brazilian cannibal practices with European religious violence shows that moral condemnation is always parochial, leaving no culture-neutral standpoint from which to judge another society; (2) this parochialism should extend to modern repatriation debates—Western legal concepts of ownership are as culturally specific as cannibal rites, so we ought to suspend judgment rather than impose them; (3) at the same time, I believe Montaigne actually identifies a universal human capacity for cruelty specifically in Europeans, which supplies at least one trans-cultural moral standard; (4) I take myself to be a moderate skeptic about moral knowledge, not a pure relativist; (5) if that skepticism is correct, we lack any justified basis for condemning either historical cannibalism or colonial violence; (6) nevertheless, the board must take a definitive stance, and I want to defend active restitution rather than agnostic suspension. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the conflicts by severity, identify the single change that resolves the most problems, and say what it costs me to make it.
