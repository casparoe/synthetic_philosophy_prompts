I'm auditing a graduate seminar on practical rationality and have been trying to work through a disagreement that came up during a coffee break between two fellow auditors, Maya and Jonah, over the case of Dr. Elena Vasquez, a climate researcher offered a well-funded advisory role with an energy firm developing direct-air-capture technology. The contract grants her full publication rights and an explicit clause allowing her to criticize the firm's other operations, but it also requires her presence at two annual industry conferences where her image will be used in marketing materials. Maya and Jonah disagree not merely about what Elena should do, but about what kind of normative question is being asked at all.

Maya's position is that the relevant norms—scientific integrity, conflict-of-interest avoidance, and fidelity to public commitments—are indirect: they regulate not the bare act of signing the contract, but the structure of deliberation that produces the decision. She argues that Elena should engage in procedural endorsement under idealized information, modeling the choice not as her actual, cash-strapped, tenure-anxious self, but as a version of herself with the same values and commitments yet with unbiased risk-assessment, full knowledge of the marketing dynamics, and no immediate financial pressure. If that idealized agent would endorse the consulting arrangement given the carbon-capture potential and the contractual safeguards, then the indirect norm permits it, even if her actual psychology is partly driven by relief at solving her mortgage problem. Maya insists that without this idealization, direct norms collapse into arbitrary lists of forbidden employers, unable to distinguish genuinely harmful conflicts from structurally protected collaborations.

Jonah replies that this framing commits the abstraction fallacy in practical normativity: by stripping away Elena's actual vulnerability, financial dependence, and institutional weakness, Maya transforms a norm designed to protect real people in power imbalances into a thought experiment for an agent who does not exist. He says indirect normativity makes sense for institutional design—crafting university funding rules—but not for first-person deliberation, where the agent's actual beliefs, biases, and dependencies are precisely what need regulation. Jonah argues that the norm applies directly to Elena's action: given her actual promises to her graduate students that she would avoid fossil-fuel-linked funding, and given the actual reputational risk, she should decline. He adds that Maya's idealized self is epistemically inaccessible; Elena cannot reliably distinguish between what her unbiased self would want and what her biased self wants to hear, so the indirect method becomes a rationalization engine rather than a constraint.

Maya answers that Jonah conflates the content of a norm with its application conditions. She grants that Elena's actual psychology and promises are relevant data, but insists they enter as inputs into an idealized reasoning process rather than as direct vetoes. She proposes a distinction between thin idealization, which removes only false beliefs and logical errors while preserving contingent values and vulnerabilities, and thick idealization, which strips away all dependence and desire; she claims she is defending only the thin version. Elena should ask whether she, with accurate beliefs about the marketing use and accurate probabilities about climate impact, still endorses the decision from within her own value set. Jonah counters that even thin idealization requires Elena to know which of her current beliefs are false and which are biases—precisely the knowledge she lacks by definition—and that this is why direct, rule-based norms exist: they protect agents from having to perform impossible self-surgery. He points out that promises generate direct reasons not because they are inputs to an idealized calculator, but because breaking them damages the trust structure that makes scientific collaboration possible, regardless of what an idealized Elena might compute.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: who is right about whether indirect normativity requires thick or thin idealization, about whether promises in this case supply direct or indirect reasons, and about whether the epistemic inaccessibility of the idealized self undermines Maya's framework. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—especially regarding how an actual agent should integrate institutional safeguards with first-person deliberation without either collapsing into rationalization or into rigid rule-following.
