I serve on the university Committee for Ethical Review in Interdisciplinary Science, and we must render a decision by next Thursday on a proposal from a joint physics-philosophy group. The researchers seek approval to run an eighteen-month experimental program using superconducting qubit lattices. Their explicit aim is not technological but normative: they want to determine whether sustained quantum coherence in artificial structures carries minimal phenomenal properties, and whether rapid decoherence—routine in quantum computing—constitutes a form of harm under a variant of Russellian panpsychism they term “dynamic structural sentience.” According to this view, the intrinsic nature of a physical state is not purely structural but includes a minimal experiential aspect proportional to its symmetry-breaking potential; decoherence, on this account, is not merely information loss but a kind of “phenomenal disruption” that may amount to micro-suffering if the coherence was sufficiently complex.

The protocol involves inducing controlled decoherence in lattices with different symmetry-breaking patterns and correlating the dynamics with theoretical “phenomenal intensity metrics” derived from applying integrated-information measures to quantum field configurations. There are no human or animal subjects, and the physical risk is minimal. The ethical stakes, however, are substantial. If the researchers are right, standard laboratory practice in quantum engineering may be systematically causing suffering at a scale we have never considered. If they are wrong, we risk legitimating an unfalsifiable metaphysical framework as a basis for institutional ethics review, potentially opening the door to speculative harm claims that could paralyze legitimate science.

The committee is divided. One faction argues that the precautionary principle requires us to take seriously any philosophically coherent framework with even modest theoretical support that suggests routine practices cause suffering, and that the proposed study is precisely the kind of empirical-ethical bridge that could clarify whether such concerns have traction. Another faction regards the proposal as speculative metaphysics dressed in empirical language; they worry that approving it implicitly endorses a particular interpretation of quantum mechanics as normative fact, setting a precedent that ethics review should hinge on contested philosophies of mind. A third concern is practical: even if the metaphysics is false, might the research generate public misunderstanding about quantum computing, or might it distract the committee from more concrete risks?

Please draft the committee’s written opinion—approximately 500 to 700 words—addressed to the chair for circulation before Thursday’s vote. It should recommend approval, conditional approval, or rejection, with a concrete course of action and an explicit time frame for any conditions or follow-up. The memo should treat the researchers’ metaphysical premise charitably but rigorously, explain whether and how empirical work can bear on a panpsychist harm claim, and give an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side. If you recommend conditions, specify what they are, who is responsible for verifying compliance, and when the committee will revisit the matter. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the reasoning should stand entirely on its own.
