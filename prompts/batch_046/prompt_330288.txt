The long-running exchange between defenders of Jain anekāntavāda and analytic philosophers who charge the view with either harboring true contradictions or collapsing into context-relative triviality has settled into a repetitive pattern. The Jain side appeals to syādvāda and the saptabhaṅgī schema to argue that apparently incompatible predicates apply only relative to distinct standpoints (nayas) and that every assertion carries an implicit conditional qualifier (syāt); critics reply that without a non-relativized substrate this either violates the law of non-contradiction or renders all metaphysical claims uninformative. Defenders then accuse critics of importing an alien bivalent absolutism, and the cycle restarts. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive metaphysical dispute about whether reality is ontologically manifold, a semantic dispute about whether standpoint-indexing is equivalent to contradiction-tolerance, a normative dispute about whether rational belief revision requires global consistency, or a bedrock clash of intuitions about the aims of philosophy? Diagnose the stalemate by engaging the actual mechanics of the Jain position—particularly the interaction between syāt-qualification and naya-relativity—and specify what each side treats as the decisive burden. Then say whether a formal distinction, for instance between paraconsistent logic and indexical semantics, could genuinely advance the debate, or whether the sides are answering different questions about what metaphysics should deliver, and if so, propose a reformulation that would make progress measurable.
