i was arguing with friends about Dennett's Where Am I? puzzle — the one where Hamlet's brain gets pulled out and put in a vat with radio links to his old body, and then there's that second duplicate brain Yorick running in parallel. everyone kept reaching for the laptop analogy: your brain is basically the SSD holding the OS and user data, and the body is just the chassis with peripherals. swap the drive into a new shell, you still boot up as you; replace the chassis, no big deal. they use this to argue that identity clearly travels with the neural information, so Dennett should be totally relaxed about brain transplants and substrate independence.

but i think it's smuggling in a lot of baggage. can you audit the analogy properly? i want a correspondence table — brain -> ?, consciousness -> ?, the radio link -> ?, the duplicate Yorick -> ? — then the fractures. especially, does it handle the fact that Hamlet's brain is still wirelessly coupled to the original body for a while, so he's kind of in two places at once? and what about this idea i just made up, call it "embodied caching": a lot of what feels like "me" might actually be cached in peripheral nerves, gut signaling, and hormonal loops, not stored centrally on the drive. if that's true, pulling the SSD doesn't capture all the data. also, the laptop picture assumes a single-user OS, but human selfhood might be more like a distributed git repo with local commits happening in the skin and muscles. so: lay out the mapping, flag which disanalogies actually kill the identity-travels-with-the-brain conclusion and which are just cosmetic, and tell me whether the relaxed conclusion survives or if the analogy was carrying the whole argument. if there's a better analogy — maybe an orchestra where the conductor gets moved but the section leaders keep playing? — suggest one. don't need citations, just the conceptual audit, and feel free to point out where my "embodied caching" thing breaks too.
