As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of mind, I have become preoccupied with Daniel Dennett's notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity—the idea that the self is not a Cartesian substance but a fictional point, analogous to a center of mass, around which autobiographical narratives orbit. The metaphor strikes me as methodologically elegant, yet I remain uncertain whether it operates merely as an eliminative heuristic or can be developed into a substantive explanatory model.

Write an essay that addresses the following controversy. On one side, critics argue that treating the self as a center of narrative gravity dissolves it entirely into a useful fiction, leaving no residual phenomenon capable of grounding moral responsibility, long-term rational planning, or the phenomenological unity of consciousness. On the other side, defenders maintain that the view should be taken more literally—not as the elimination of the self, but as its identification with a dynamical attractor in belief-space, one that possesses what I will call epistemic inertia: a structural resistance to rapid narrative revision that functions analogously to inertial mass in physical systems, and that distinguishes genuine agents from systems that merely generate context-dependent outputs.

Weigh both positions carefully, but do not end on a non-committal note. Reach a definite conclusion: Is the center-of-narrative-gravity model ultimately eliminative, genuinely explanatory, or best understood as a hybrid? And does the introduction of epistemic inertia strengthen the account, or does it smuggle back in the very metaphysical substance that the metaphor was designed to avoid? Defend your verdict through conceptual analysis alone, without recourse to empirical bibliographies or appeals to authority.
