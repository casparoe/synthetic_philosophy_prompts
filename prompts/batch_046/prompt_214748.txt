I have recently become responsible for designing participatory civic rituals in a municipal cultural program, which has led me to read Rousseau’s writings on public spectacle with an urgency I did not expect. In discussions with colleagues—some political theorists, some practitioners—the conversation about whether Rousseau’s critique of theater is salvageable for contemporary public art has reached a frustrating standstill. We all accept the same textual premises: that he condemns theatrical representation as corrupting, that he praises collective festivals where citizens are simultaneously actors and spectators, and that he ties both judgments to the dynamics of amour-propre and civic virtue. Yet we divide cleanly into three camps that do not seem to be answering one another. The first camp holds that Rousseau’s objection is formal and irreducible: representation as such introduces a fatal split between being and appearing, so any mediated spectacle is incompatible with authentic civic presence. The second camp argues that the objection is historically indexed to the Parisian theater of his day—its class segregation, its commercial logic, its encouragement of passive consumption—and that a non-hierarchical, non-commercial participatory performance would escape the critique entirely. The third camp contends that the real target is not representation but emotional contagion without rational accountability, and that what Rousseau fears is the suspension of critical distance that theater produces, not the fact of framing itself.

What strikes me is that none of these camps can be shown wrong by the others using the texts we all share, yet none of us has changed position in months. My working hypothesis is that we are not really disagreeing about Rousseau, but about a hidden question: whether moral formation requires what I would call indexical co-presence—interaction in which no participant is assigned a representational role distinct from their civic identity—or whether framed interaction, where roles are explicitly artificial but collectively acknowledged, can serve the same formative function without corrupting self-love. If that is right, then the debate is partly verbal (we use “theater” to cover both framed interaction and hierarchical spectacle) and partly a substantive disagreement about moral psychology that neither side has articulated clearly.

Can you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement? Is it a case of hidden empirical assumptions about how social learning works, a difference in what counts as a moral reason (autonomy versus authenticity versus rational accountability), bedrock intuitions about the value of unmediated presence, or something else—perhaps that the three camps are answering different questions about civic art, one normative, one historical, one psychological? I want the diagnosis to engage the specific claims of each camp as I have described them, not a survey of Rousseau scholarship. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the conflict, a historical counter-example that forces one camp to concede, an empirical result about social learning, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it conflates aesthetic framing with political domination? If you think no progress should be expected, say so and explain why the disagreement is likely to persist regardless of evidence.
