As a verification engineer without formal philosophy training, I need a reusable audit procedure that operationalizes the Meno problem—why knowledge is more valuable than true belief—for our safety-critical ML release reviews. The procedure should proceed in ordered steps: first, establish whether a correct model prediction carries justification traceable by our review team; second, determine whether that correctness is accidental with respect to our training distribution or non-accidentally sustained by it; third, specify how distributed, non-localizable justification embedded in deep network weights should be evaluated—as supporting knowledge, as an absence of justification, or as an undefined case; fourth, articulate what practical risk value, if any, the knowledge-versus-true-belief distinction adds beyond mere predictive accuracy. At each step, identify where the procedure yields a technical verdict, where it must stop and defer to a human values judgment, and where it is silent. Apply the procedure to three scenarios: a medical vision model that correctly identifies a tumor but produces only an uninterpretable saliency map; an identical architecture with a fully traceable feature chain but trained on data with known demographic bias that may invalidate the justification; and a deterministic expert system that reaches the correct diagnosis through a hidden compensating logic error that cancels only for this specific input profile. For each scenario, classify the output as knowledge-grade or true-belief-grade, note any deployment-authorization implications, and highlight where the procedure breaks down or produces a counterintuitive result—particularly the case where perfect traceability coexists with hidden failure. Conclude by assessing whether the Meno value gap survives this engineering translation: does distinguishing knowledge from true belief genuinely improve our risk framework, or does it reduce to a preference for interpretability that leaves the core epistemic value claim unaddressed?
