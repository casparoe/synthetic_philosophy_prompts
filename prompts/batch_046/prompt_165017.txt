I'm working through Hobbes's philosophy of geometry in De Corpore and find myself stuck on how he would have responded to two developments that came well after his death: Hilbert's formalist axiomatization, which treats geometric primitives as implicitly defined by axiomatic relations without requiring physical constructibility or causal generation, and the mature formulation of non-Euclidean geometries—specifically Riemannian spaces of constant positive curvature, where no parallel lines exist and the metric departs radically from that of tangible space. Hobbes held that geometric figures are not eternal essences but bodies actually produced by motion; he attacked Euclid for offering mere descriptions rather than true definitions that reveal the cause of generation, and he was deeply suspicious of actual infinities and of figures that could not in principle be constructed. Given these commitments—his materialist ontology, his nominalist refusal to treat universals as independent entities, and his epistemic demand that geometric certainty derives from knowing the generative motion—how would he assess Hilbert's axiomatic approach? Would he dismiss it as an empty verbal game, a scandal analogous to the scholastic logic he rejected, because it severs geometry from the causal process that alone gives its terms meaning? Or could his nominalism accommodate it as an arbitrary but internally consistent system of names?

Furthermore, I want to understand whether Hobbes's constructivism would force him to reject Riemannian geometry as incoherent—since no motion through physical space could generate a surface where every geodesic eventually intersects—or whether he might treat it as a hypothetical fiction permissible for calculation but not a science of real bodies. There is also a tension I am not sure how to resolve: Hobbes admired geometry because its demonstrations were certain and uncontroversial, yet this certainty rested on the transparency of construction. If Hilbert shows that consistency alone can secure a geometric system without any reference to construction, does that undermine Hobbes's claim that geometric knowledge is paradigmatic of scientific certainty, or does it merely reveal that there are two different enterprises—one about real bodies, one about formal relations—that he never needed to distinguish? I am not asking for a bibliographic survey or a list of secondary sources; rather, I want a disciplined reconstruction of what Hobbes's own premises entail, where they break, and whether any part of his framework can be salvaged for modern formal geometry without betraying his materialism.
