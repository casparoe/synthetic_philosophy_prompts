Draft the joint statement that would result from a year-long adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps in European Union political theory: (1) the demoi-cratic intergovernmentalists, who maintain that democratic legitimacy in the EU depends on preserving distinct national demoi, that ever-closer union erodes popular sovereignty by transferring authority without generating a corresponding European demos, and that the democratic deficit is therefore a structural feature of excessive supranational integration; and (2) the federal democratic integrationists, who maintain that sovereignty can be pooled rather than lost, that democratic legitimacy is strengthened—not diluted—by robust European-level democratic mechanisms, and that the democratic deficit is best addressed by deepening ever-closer union rather than retreating to intergovernmental veto points.

Produce a formal working document with the following sections, in this order:

1. Agreed claims: a numbered list of precise, non-vacuous statements about EU institutional design, the allocation of competences, the role of national parliaments and the European Parliament, and the concept of democratic legitimacy that both camps can endorse without reservation. Each claim should be specific enough to constrain interpretation, not merely platitudinous.

2. Residual disagreements: a structured table or parallel list in which each disagreement is phrased in neutral wording, followed immediately by each camp's own statement of its position rendered in terms its representatives would accept. Include disagreements over whether sovereignty is zero-sum in a multi-level polity, whether regulatory harmonization in areas such as digital markets or climate standards can be democratically legitimate in the absence of a unified European electorate, and whether the principle of ever-closer union is compatible with the preservation of democratic self-rule at the national level.

3. Cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would likely move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete institutional case, empirical pattern, or structured thought experiment that both sides agree bears on it—for example, the democratic legitimacy of the European Central Bank's mandate expansions, or the accountability mechanisms surrounding the Conference on the Future of Europe—and record in advance each camp's explicit prediction or normative evaluation of that case.

4. Conceptual clarifications: a brief section distinguishing disagreements that are substantive from those that were merely verbal or definitional, particularly regarding terms such as "sovereignty," "democratic deficit," and "ever-closer union."

Write in a polished, formal register appropriate for publication as an appendix to a policy working paper. If the task rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the two camps as defined are not genuinely adversarial, if "demoi-cratic intergovernmentalism" and "federal democratic integrationism" cannot be coherently opposed on these terms, or if the framing of sovereignty as potentially non-zero-sum illegitimately presupposes one camp's metaphysics—state that plainly and do not fabricate agreement. Additionally, include a brief meta-note identifying where your drafting takes liberties in representing either camp's view, where it relies on contested interpretations of EU treaty law or democratic theory, and where it injects conceptual framing (such as pooled-authority sovereignty) that is not universally shared within the named camp. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
