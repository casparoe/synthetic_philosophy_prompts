I'm working through the philosophical literature on whether pain is necessarily bad, and I'm struck by how static the debate has become around clinical descriptions of pain asymbolia—cases in which subjects report clear nociceptive sensations, often with intact localization and intensity scaling, yet deny any unpleasantness, do not withdraw from the stimulus, and in some reports even seek repetition of the experience. On one side, the essentialists maintain that what these subjects experience is not genuine pain but a sensory analog lacking the intrinsic affective charge; they treat aversion as constitutive, so that without it one has at best a hollow tactile report. On the other side, the separatists argue that pain is fundamentally a sensory-discriminative state and that its typical badness is a contingent psychological or evaluative overlay; for them, asymbolia is not a deficit but a revealing dissociation that shows the sensory core can exist without the evaluative shell.

Despite broad agreement on the clinical phenomenology and on the normative premise that suffering matters, the two camps have not converged in decades. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not hidden empiricism—there is no disputed neural marker that either side awaits—nor is it merely verbal, since both sides use "pain" with apparent sincerity. Rather, I suspect the stall reflects a deeper divergence about what kind of property badness is: whether it is a phenomenal feature directly present in experience, a functional disposition to motivate avoidance, or a higher-order cognitive appraisal that can be detached without altering the first-order sensation. I also wonder whether the sides are actually answering different questions—one about the concept of pain in ordinary language, the other about the ontology of the experience—while using the same clinical cases as ammunition.

Could you diagnose the structure of this impasse? Is it a substantive disagreement masked by shared vocabulary, a difference in standards for what counts as authoritative first-person data, or an ill-posed question that conflates phenomenal aversion with prudential disvalue? In particular, I'd like you to engage with the possibility that neither side has clearly distinguished between an immediate affective charge and a reflective judgment that the sensation is harmful to one's interests, and to say whether introducing that distinction—together with the hypothetical case of a subject who reports full sensory discrimination, complete absence of withdrawal motivation, and yet a cool, deliberate preference for the sensation—would actually move the debate, or whether the disagreement runs deeper, perhaps down to bedrock intuitions about the unity of consciousness and evaluation. Please write your answer in American English, and I am looking for a diagnosis with reasons rather than a survey of positions or a verdict on who is right.
