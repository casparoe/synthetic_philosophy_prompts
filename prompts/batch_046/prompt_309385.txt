Two interlocutors, A and B, are debating whether the Lucas-Penrose argument retains any force against mechanist accounts of mathematical cognition, specifically in light of modern deep-learning architectures.

A argues that the argument presupposes a formal system F with a recursively enumerable theorem set, appropriate for axiomatic proof procedures but not for neural networks. Because deep-learning models approximate functions via stochastic gradient descent rather than deriving conclusions from fixed axioms via deterministic rules, there is no well-defined formal system that captures the network's mathematical endorsements. Consequently, there is no Gödel sentence constructible for such a system, and the anti-mechanist inference fails at its first premise.

B responds that any computable process—including the input-output mapping of a deterministic neural network after training—can in principle be described by a formal system that enumerates its endorsed claims. The stochasticity of training concerns how weights are reached, not the logical structure of the resulting computation. Therefore, one can still construct an F that captures all mathematical propositions the network accepts. From this, a Gödel sentence G_F follows that the network cannot derive, yet a human mathematician could recognize as true by reflecting on F's consistency. The physical implementation does not block the logical argument.

A then counters that this reply ignores Gödel's second incompleteness theorem in practice. Even if a formal description F exists in principle, its consistency proof might be far too complex for any human mind to survey—especially given the scale of modern architectures. Thus the premise that "a human can see what the machine cannot" becomes epistemically hollow: neither agent has practical access to the consistency proof required to recognize G_F.

B concedes the epistemic point but insists the argument is logical, not psychological. The human capacity to access the consistency proof is available in principle, even if never exercised. Yet B grants that if the formal system is so complex that no human could ever verify its consistency, the Lucas-Penrose argument loses its dialectical force, since it was meant to show an actual cognitive superiority, not merely an abstract logical gap.

Please adjudicate each of the four moves in turn (A's first argument, B's response, A's counter, B's concession) on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification for each rating. Then identify what both interlocutors are missing: does the distinction between a system's logical description and its physical implementation actually undermine the anti-mechanist claim, or does it merely relocate the dispute? Be precise about whether you think the argument survives in a modified form, and if so, what that modified form requires.
