At a philosophy of perception workshop, two participants—let’s call them Maya and Leo—got stuck in a back-and-forth about whether expert knowledge can genuinely penetrate visual experience or merely accelerates post-perceptual judgment. Maya began by describing a trained ornithologist spotting a distant warbler: she argued that the expert’s prior belief about the species increases the precision-weighting of shape-relevant predictions in her visual hierarchy, so she literally experiences sharper contour boundaries and more distinctive silhouette properties than a novice seeing the same retinal input. Leo replied that this conflates perceptual processing with perceptual content; both observers receive the same noisy signal, and while the expert’s brain may assign higher confidence to certain inferences, that only means her categorization is faster and more decisive, not that her phenomenal field contains different properties. Maya countered that Leo was presupposing a “pure” sensory core that predictive processing has shown not to exist—if all perception is already hypothesis-testing, then expertise does not sit downstream but restructures the very organization of the visual field, with the novice failing to register species-relevant features that are genuinely present for the expert. Leo conceded that predictions modulate sensory gain, but insisted on a distinction he thought Maya was eliding: there is ubiquitous precision-modulation, which is just attention, and then there is conceptual injection, which requires that a belief introduces representational content not available without it. He challenged Maya to show not merely that the expert is more confident, but that she sees a property—say, a specific wing-curve—that remains phenomenally absent for the novice despite identical stimulation. Maya then invoked ambiguous figures like the Necker cube, noting that prior belief can stabilize one organizational interpretation over another, and asked why the same mechanism could not stabilize feature-boundaries in the warbler case. Leo answered that the Necker cube involves selection among structurally available interpretations, not the construction of new phenomenal content; both viewers can access either orientation, whereas the novice never has the “sharp-contour” interpretation available at all, so Maya was comparing selection within a given set with the fabrication of new perceptual properties—a category error, not an analogy.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of Maya’s three main moves and Leo’s corresponding replies, say whether the argument holds up, where it slips, and whether Leo’s distinction between precision-modulation and conceptual injection is genuinely defensible or just a relabeling of the original question. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both are missing about how predictive processing actually bears on the cognitive-penetration debate. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I am trying to see whether the disagreement can be resolved conceptually without importing outside authorities.
