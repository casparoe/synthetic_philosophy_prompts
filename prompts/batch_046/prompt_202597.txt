Consider the claim that among the canonical “lost cause” national myths—the postbellum Confederate narrative, Masada in modern Zionist memory, the Kosovo Field (1389) in Serbian nationalism, and Dunkirk in British self-conception—at least three depend for long-term cultural persistence on what we might call an “unclosed moral ledger”: a framing that treats defeat not as settled tragedy but as an outstanding obligation still owed to the fallen. Give your actual credence in this claim, decomposed into: (i) the base-rate probability that collective defeat narratives survive more than two generations without active institutional reinforcement; (ii) the probability that such reinforcement requires a grievance or unfinished-business frame rather than pure honorific closure; (iii) the probability that the four listed cases genuinely exhibit this ledger structure rather than functioning mainly as dignity-preserving closure mechanisms; and (iv) the probability that “persistence” is being measured in a way that distinguishes living mythic function from mere historical reference. Assign each a rough number, show how they combine into an overall credence, then identify which term is the weakest link in your reasoning and specify what kind of comparative historical or social-psychological evidence—without requiring specific citations—would most move it.
