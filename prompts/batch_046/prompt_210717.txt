Read Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through tranquilism: the position that only the absence of craving and suffering carries moral weight, while the presence of pleasure, happiness, or flourishing adds no value whatsoever. The story is typically treated as a critique of utilitarian trade-offs, but examine it under this stricter asymmetry. Would a tranquilist reject the citizens' bargain for reasons that differ from both utilitarian and deontological objections—namely, that the child's suffering is the only negative fact that matters, and the citizens' joy is morally inert? Consider whether the narrative's emotional force secretly depends on positively valuing Omelas's happiness, and whether those who walk away are refusing complicity in suffering or also sacrificing a weighty good. Identify scenes where the story resists this reading and where it might illuminate something that conventional suffering-focused ethics overlook. Please separate what is settled about value asymmetry from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who take negative value seriously, and do not present any disputed claim about Le Guin's intent or about tranquilism's implications as the consensus view.
