Over the past several months I have been helping to draft welfare guidelines for a small cooperative aquaculture initiative that raises both finfish and decapod crustaceans, and the work has made me try to articulate a coherent philosophical stance rather than an ad hoc list of rules. Here is my current position:

(1) Direct moral obligations to these animals are grounded in sentience—the capacity for valenced experience—and I take the available neurobehavioral evidence to show that both teleost fish and decapods such as shrimp and crabs meet this threshold.

(2) Welfare is plural. It is not reducible to the absence of suffering, but also includes the exercise of species-typical functioning: for fish, continuous exploratory and social behavior; for crustaceans, the successful completion of ecdysis (molting) cycles.

(3) For practical regulation I prioritize process-based safeguards—water-quality management, stocking-density limits, and humane stunning protocols—over outcome-based metrics such as mortality percentages or feed-conversion ratios, because outcomes can obscure sustained, low-grade suffering.

(4) I hold that a painless death is not a serious harm to these animals, provided they lack the kind of psychological continuity or future-oriented preferences that would make death a deprivation; therefore humane harvest is permissible if prior welfare conditions were adequate.

(5) I have recently adopted the view that during ecdysis decapods enter a temporary regenerative state marked by heightened neuroplasticity and lowered nociceptive thresholds. In this window, standard welfare interventions—handling, environmental adjustments, or analgesic administration—should be minimized. The reason is not that suffering is absent, but that external interference disrupts a biologically critical regenerative process that is not itself experienced as distress.

(6) At the same time, I think the strength of our obligations tracks not only sentience but also the complexity of temporal self-awareness. Because decapods likely lack the narrative selfhood that teleost fish may possess, our obligations to crustaceans are somewhat weaker, even though both groups are sentient.

I need a consistency audit rather than a general survey of animal ethics. Which pairs of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision for each real tension? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify the cheapest fix for each, and tell me which single revision—if I were forced to make only one—would eliminate the most conflicts. Please answer in American English.
