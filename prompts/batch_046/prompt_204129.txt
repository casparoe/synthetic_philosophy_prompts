I've been reading about diachronic rationality since I left the lab — I was in condensed matter physics for thirty years, so I keep trying to treat consistency over time like some kind of conservation law, which probably isn't right. Anyway, here are my gut reactions to a few cases, and I want to know if there's one principle that gets them all, or if I'm just being incoherent.

Case one: the classic money pump. Someone ranks A over B, B over C, and C over A, and they're actually going to face these choices in sequence with a small fee to switch each time. I say that's flat-out irrational — they'd lose money forever and never learn. Case two: the exact same intransitive ranking, but the person only ever encounters these options in isolation, years apart, with no mechanism that lets anyone pump them. I still feel there's something wrong with thier preferences — like a design flaw — but it's not the same kind of wrong as case one. I wouldn't call them irrational in the practical sense. Case three: hyperbolic discounting with money. You'd take $100 now over $110 next month, but you'd wait for $110 in thirteen months instead of $100 in twelve. For ordinary people I think that's okay; we're not exponential discount functions. But then there's a climate-policy planner with the same reversal: she wants immediate carbon cuts now, but pushes both targets back equally when they're both in the future. For her I think it's irrational, because the framing shouldn't flip just because the clock moved. So I have two hyperbolic patterns with opposite verdicts. Case four: someone sets up an automatic contract that binds thier future self to stick with a savings plan. That seems smart to me — a rational use of precommitment. But what if they bind themselves into the cyclic preference from case one, locking in A, then B, then C, on autopilot? That feels like they're automating thier own mistake. Case five: after a big life change — say, a health scare — a person genuinely stops valuing travel and starts valuing security. The preference reversal isn't time-discounting; it's a real shift in priorities. I think that's perfectly rational, even though it breaks temporal consistency.

The tension is that in case two the cycle never hurts anyone yet I smell something off, while in case five the reversal is fine despite breaking consistency. Is there a principle that separates harmful dynamic inconsistency from benign or justified change? And if I need to drop one verdict to keep the principle simple, which should go? Also, what does your principle predict for a case I haven't listed: someone who uses an AI advisor to pre-commit thier weekly budget, but the AI updates its model of thier preferences every Monday — is that like the smart contract in case four, or does the constant updating recreate the pump?
