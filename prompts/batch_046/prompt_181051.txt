You are delivering the opening remarks at the inaugural session of a workshop on constitutional design and logical self-reference. The room is divided evenly: about half are constitutional lawyers and legal philosophers familiar with entrenchment clauses, Alf Ross's paradox of self-amendment, and Peter Suber's self-modifying rule-game Nomic; the other half are legislative drafters, judges, and policy scholars with no training in formal logic or game-based rule systems. Your task is to set the workshop's conceptual framework in a way that respects both groups' competence without lecturing either.

Center your remarks on a specific, invented design problem that will serve as the running example for the two days. Introduce a hypothetical constitutional amendment rule—call it Article X—whose text reads: "This article may be amended only by a procedure that does not itself amend this article." Explain that a 1956 drafting convention inserted the clause to protect core democratic procedures, but that a present-day reform coalition now proposes a "functional replacement" statute: it avoids technically amending Article X, yet renders Article X's procedural requirements inoperative by transferring their function to a new body. For the drafters, present this as a live institutional ambiguity about whether constitutional protection can survive indirect nullification. For the theorists, show that it reproduces the familiar structure of legal self-reference—the rule attempts to govern all amendments while excepting itself, and the proposed workaround attempts to be simultaneously inside and outside that exception.

The philosophical idea must constrain what you can and cannot say. You should not treat the paradox as a mere logical curiosity that practical wisdom can dissolve, because the drafters face real risks of institutional erosion. At the same time, you should not reduce it to a purely practical drafting error, because the theorists recognize that the risk arises precisely from the logical impossibility of a rule being fully authoritative over itself. Explicitly navigate this tension. Propose that the workshop investigate whether a self-amending legal system requires an external anchor—such as an unamendable constitutional core—or whether the paradox of self-reference is not a design flaw to be eliminated but a constitutive feature of democratic legitimacy.

Keep the remarks formal, appropriate for a professional workshop, and approximately five minutes long. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to court cases or academic texts. End with a single, precise question that both audiences can grasp, but that reveals to the specialists the deep structure of legal self-reference—something like whether constitutional continuity is best preserved by eliminating self-reference or by institutionalizing it. Write the remarks.
