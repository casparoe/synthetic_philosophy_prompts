I'm currently taking a seminar on philosophy of history, and we've been discussing Herbert Butterfield's critique of Whig historiography—specifically the tendency to read the past as an inevitable progression toward present liberal institutions. In conversation with a fellow student, I constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to vindicate Whig history by making its rejection catastrophic, yet I cannot locate the exact misstep, and I would like your help diagnosing where the reasoning fails, if it does.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Whig history is objectionable primarily because it evaluates past actors and institutions by implicit reference to present norms and outcomes, thereby committing normative anachronism. (2) If importing present-centered standards into our assessment of the past is illegitimate in the normative domain, consistency requires that we reject importing present-centered outcomes into our explanation of the past, since both practices make the present the privileged terminus of historical inquiry. (3) But virtually all macro-historical explanation relies on retrospective causal framing: for instance, explaining the erosion of Roman senatorial authority partly by reference to the eventual collapse of the Western Empire, or accounting for the gradual centralization of early modern monarchies by appealing to the later consolidation of nation-states. (4) Therefore, if we genuinely reject Whig history in the strong form demanded by (1) and (2), we must reject retrospective causal framing altogether. (5) Rejecting retrospective causal framing eliminates the possibility of explanatory historiography, leaving us with mere chronicle. (6) The elimination of explanatory historiography is unacceptable. (7) Hence, by reductio, Whig history cannot be objectionable in the manner described in (1).

I am inclined to preserve (1) and (6) if forced to choose, but I want to understand precisely what I am paying for that choice. Is there a hidden equivocation between normative evaluation and causal explanation—perhaps between treating the present as a moral benchmark versus treating later events as evidential anchors for earlier conditions—or does the failure lie elsewhere, for example in the slide from (3) to (4)? If I must abandon one premise, which rejection does the least damage to a coherent anti-Whig position, and what does that revised position imply for cases where historians legitimately invoke later outcomes—say, using knowledge of the Industrial Revolution to explain shifts in eighteenth-century agricultural practice without thereby endorsing a teleological narrative?
