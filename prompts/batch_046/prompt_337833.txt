You are a philosopher of education with deep familiarity in classical biography, its modern secular adaptations, and the mechanics of moral pedagogy. Before you construct the plan, please think carefully about the conceptual dependency structure—what must be understood before what can be introduced—and briefly brainstorm the specific misconceptions that arise when intelligent non-specialists first encounter pre-modern biographical ethics.

I have just finished a long and rather heated argument with a close friend about whether biography can genuinely teach virtue or whether it inevitably collapses into either celebrity worship or moralistic flattery. We were discussing Plutarch’s method of parallel lives and what I have begun calling “negative biographical space” in contemporary hagiography—the systematic smoothing over or omission of mundane moral failures, ambivalent choices, and moments where the subject could easily have acted otherwise. My friend insists that biography fails pedagogically only when it is badly written; I argued that there is a structural feature at work, namely that effective moral learning from a life requires a calibrated “mimetic distance” between reader and subject—close enough that the reader can imagine occupying the subject’s position, yet distant enough that identification does not collapse into projection. Modern sanitized profiles destroy this distance by presenting uninterrupted arcs of moral clarity, thereby eliminating the very choice-points that make imitation instructive rather than merely aspirational. I want to teach this framework—not Plutarch exegesis, but the structural claim—to a reading group of graduate students in literature and politics with no formal philosophy training, in a single ninety-minute session.

Please do not explain the idea to me; instead, design the teaching plan. First, lay out the exact order in which the components must be introduced and why: why the concept of mimetic distance must come before any discussion of Plutarch’s pairing method, and why “negative biographical space” should only be introduced after the group has already attempted to extract a lesson from a conventional, smoothed-over biographical profile. Second, specify the one carrying example or paired comparison that should anchor the session: it must be realistic rather than mythic, must display both the presence of moral friction in one biography and its absence in another, and must avoid figures so canonized that students bring fixed prior judgments; explain precisely what the example must include and exclude. Third, map the predictable misconceptions—I expect they will conflate moral learning from biography with “finding a role model,” they will assume that omitting failures is a kindness to the subject rather than a pedagogical failure, and they will think that Plutarch’s parallel structure is merely decorative rather than functional—identify exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and defused, with one targeted question for each. Fourth, provide the check for understanding at the end: one exercise that reveals whether they have grasped that the pedagogical value lies in the visibility of choice-points rather than in the accumulation of virtues. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—perhaps a full taxonomy of Aristotelian habituation, or recent empirical literature on narrative transportation—and say so openly. Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask the group to consult primary texts during the session.
