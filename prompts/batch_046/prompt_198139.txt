I write about neuroscience and philosophy for a national magazine, and I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a two-day workshop I am co-hosting, titled "Neural Prediction and Human Agency," which convenes cognitive neuroscientists, philosophers of action, and a cohort of law students studying criminal intent. The audience is deliberately bifurcated: half the room can interpret a readiness-potential trace, while the other half has never read an EEG but will immediately recognize sloppy reasoning about moral responsibility. I need these remarks to do genuine structural work—establishing the conceptual architecture for the entire meeting—rather than serving as a decorative welcome.

Specifically, I want the speech to be organized around Libet's experiments on voluntary movement and the subsequent neuroscience of free will, but through a precise framing that rejects both the deterministic punchline ("your brain decides before you do") and the dualist rescue ("consciousness saves agency"). The core idea should be that voluntary action is not a point-like event but a temporally distributed process: neural initiation, conscious awareness of intent, and a possible veto are separated by hundreds of milliseconds, creating an architecture rather than a single moment of choice. To prevent the speech from collapsing into a familiar summary, please weave in an unconventional scenario: a near-future courtroom in which defense counsel has access to real-time monitors that detect pre-conscious readiness potentials roughly four hundred milliseconds before a defendant reports awareness of intent, and use that image to ask whether such a technological window alters the legal concept of premeditation. The remarks must show why translating millisecond-scale neural data into judgments about responsibility requires philosophical mediation that the data alone cannot provide, and they should explicitly foreclose two moves—the claim that Libet's findings disprove free will, and the symmetrical claim that conscious veto proves it—without leaving the audience in false comfort.

The speech must be fully intelligible to the non-specialists present without sacrificing precision for the experts, and it should set the terms for the next two days of discussion. Please write a formal, polished address of no more than 500 words, with no bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, that uses this distributed-phase framing and the courtroom-monitor scenario to welcome the participants and to define the conceptual stakes.
