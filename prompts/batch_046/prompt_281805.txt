I have been thinking about how institutions handle deadlocked decisions—juries that hang, election boards that split evenly, or hiring committees that rank two candidates identically. Rather than demanding further reasoning until a preference emerges, the rules typically invoke a procedural tie-breaker: a coin toss, a senior member's casting vote, or a random draw. This seems to treat choice without preference as unproblematic, yet I keep running into the old puzzle of Buridan's ass, which suggests a rational agent cannot pick between perfectly equal options. What philosophical commitments about rationality, preference, and the nature of choice does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely fits it, and explain what would have to change in these procedures if we genuinely believed that rational choice demands a prior reason. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
