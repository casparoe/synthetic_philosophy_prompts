I am not a professional philosopher, but I have been reading about how knowledge is modeled in logic, and I keep returning to a problem called logical omniscience. The idea is that if you truly know one proposition, you automatically know every logical consequence of it — no matter how distant or complex the deduction might be. For ordinary people this is plainly false: I know my home address, yet I do not instantly know every geometric or arithmetic fact that could be derived from its coordinates. Still, philosophers debate whether an ideal rational agent ought to be modeled as omniscient in exactly this way.

Here is the specific boundary case that puzzles me. Imagine an agent — perhaps an artificial system — that holds a complete and correct set of axioms and possesses a flawless rule of inference. Whenever it is asked directly about a particular consequence, it can derive that consequence correctly and without error. But if no one asks, it never derives anything spontaneously; its active beliefs remain precisely the axioms with which it began. So there are true statements that logically follow from what it knows, yet it does not currently hold them as beliefs. The capacity for derivation is fully present, but the activation is absent.

If Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who lived long before this kind of logical modeling existed, were shown this agent, do you think he would say that it genuinely lacks knowledge of those unactivated consequences? Or would he argue that because the derivation rule is fully embedded within the agent's rational structure, the agent already implicitly knows everything that follows — so that the absence of explicit belief is merely a temporary state of inattention rather than a real epistemic gap? I am especially curious whether Leibniz would allow that a being could be rationally perfect yet epistemically incomplete in this specific, activation-dependent manner, or whether his commitment to the principle of sufficient reason and the universal calculus would force him to treat implicit possession of the rule as equivalent to explicit possession of all its outputs.
