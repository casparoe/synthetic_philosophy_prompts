I've been comparing how different societies institutionalize the memory of state-sponsored violence—specifically Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan's fragmented WWII commemoration, Turkey's official denial of the Armenian genocide, and the recent removal of Confederate monuments in the American South. In Germany, the practice is not merely educational but infrastructural: decentralized sidewalk memorials (Stolpersteine) embed individual victims into ordinary urban space, school curricula are federally coordinated to treat the Holocaust as a foundational negative reference point, and criminal law prohibits public denial, all while the state insists that the Federal Republic is a democratic successor rather than a continuation of the Reich, and that reparations are political obligations rather than inherited personal guilt.

This package seems to rest on a specific but rarely articulated philosophy of collective identity and moral transmission. What view of diachronic group agency, if any, must be in place for a political community to be continuously responsible for crimes committed by a prior regime that no longer exists, without attributing individual culpability to present citizens? Does the practice presuppose a corporate-person theory of the state, a form of origin essentialism applied to nations, or perhaps a purely pragmatic social contract that treats historical acknowledgment as a condition of democratic legitimacy? And is the practice philosophically underdetermined—could a strict individualist about moral responsibility, a deep pluralist about national identity, or a presentist who denies obligations to past victims license the same institutions?

Please take a definite position: reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the German configuration strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, identify which rival philosophical views it quietly rules out, and argue whether the practice is ultimately coherent or rests on an unstable hybrid. I am especially interested in whether you think the tension between denying collective guilt and enforcing collective responsibility can be resolved without smuggling in a metaphysics of national essence.
