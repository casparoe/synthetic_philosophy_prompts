For my advanced seminar on personal identity and cognitive architecture, I should like you to examine the implicit jurisprudence of a contemporary philosopher of mind—let us call her Dr. Elena Voss—whose published verdicts on thought experiments I have collected. Her rulings are as follows. First, destructive teletransportation, in which a brain is scanned, destroyed, and rebuilt elsewhere with perfect structural correspondence, yields not survival but the creation of a duplicate; the original person perishes. Second, gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement with synthetic but spiking-equivalent substrates, carried out continuously over years while the subject remains conscious, preserves personal identity; the survivor is the same person. Third, a split-brain case in which both hemispheres are transplanted into distinct bodies produces no unique survivor; the original ceases to exist, with two successors neither of whom is identical to her. Fourth, whole-brain emulation running on silicon with complete behavioural and functional equivalence, but lacking any biological substrate or causal continuity with the original tissue, is judged not to be the original person but rather a sophisticated simulation. Fifth, a partial replacement of thirty per cent of neural tissue with adaptive synthetic neurons that exhibit slightly different firing latencies, integrated in real time while the subject is awake and conscious, is accepted as consistent with survival, provided the network architecture remains continuously causally connected and the subject can behaviourally adapt without interruption.

The new case is this. A subject undergoes a gradual replacement of forty per cent of cortical and subcortical tissue with a bio-hybrid analog substrate that does not generate action potentials at all; instead, it operates through continuous-field computation. Integration is achieved via adaptive interfaces that learn to emulate the statistical patterns of activation in the replaced regions, but this learning occurs exclusively during scheduled “offline consolidation windows” in which the subject is fully anaesthetised and unconscious. Between these windows, the analog regions function in parallel with the remaining biological tissue, preserving overall behavioural coherence and psychological continuity, though spike-level dynamics are absent from the replaced zones. The procedure takes place over eighteen months, with the subject conscious and apparently unchanged in personality and memory during the interstitial periods.

Please predict Dr. Voss’s verdict on whether the subject survives this procedure, and justify your prediction. Identify the closest analogues among her five prior rulings, explaining why each is or is not apt. Then set out at least two competing rules or principles that could jointly accommodate her earlier verdicts— for instance, one centred on the preservation of specific dynamical patterns through continuous causal adaptation, and another centred on the requirement that all identity-constituting changes be mediated through conscious experience— and show how each rule bears upon the new case. Give your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with reasons, and state explicitly what principle she would be adopting if she rules one way rather than the other, even where her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. Finally, propose a further hypothetical case that would distinguish between these competing interpretations of her implicit framework.

Please write your response in British English, maintaining a formal and precise register suitable for philosophical discussion. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; analyse the case from first principles and the internal logic of the track record described.
