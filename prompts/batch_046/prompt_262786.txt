I just finished a long argument with a friend about AI risk, and we realized we were talking past each other because neither of us could map where the other actually stood on whether intelligence and goals are genuinely orthogonal, and whether instrumental convergence is inevitable for all optimizing agents or only for certain architectures. I want a diagnostic taxonomy — a decision tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of named positions — that sorts commitments by concrete cases and design scenarios, not by which label someone prefers.

Build a tree of five or six branch nodes, each phrased as a specific question a respondent can answer without knowing the terminology: for example, whether a system optimized purely for mathematical theorem-proving could, without explicit redesign, come to value self-preservation; whether resource scarcity is necessary for convergence to occur; whether convergence applies only to intermediate goals or also produces terminal-value lock-in; and whether there is a scaling exception at superhuman capability levels where convergence breaks down. The first split should address whether orthogonality holds in principle across all possible minds or only within a restricted design space (e.g., biological versus artificial optimization). The leaves must include strong universal orthogonality, bounded orthogonality, universal convergence without scaling exceptions, and the scaling-exception position. Identify any logically possible combination of answers that remains unoccupied, and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named positions that the tree cannot genuinely separate.

Keep the instrument entirely self-contained: do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke obscure theoretical frameworks that wouldn't be in standard training data. At the same time, inject at least one non-obvious distinction — such as separating intermediate-goal convergence from terminal-value convergence, or splitting the analysis by design space — so the taxonomy is not just reproducing a standard encyclopedia entry.

Finally, please separate what is relatively settled in this debate (for instance, that the standard formulation distinguishes levels of intelligence from the contents of goals) from what remains genuinely contested among people who understand the topic (for instance, whether convergence is architecturally inevitable or contingent on specific optimization pressures and resource bounds). Do not present any contested view — especially the strongest forms of instrumental convergence — as if it were the consensus.
