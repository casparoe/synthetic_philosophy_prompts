As a retired experimental physicist only recently working through philosophy of language, I want a consistency check on the following package of commitments about vagueness. (1) I accept epistemicism across the board: for every vague predicate there is a precise extension, and our failure to locate the cutoff is best explained by a margin-for-error principle rather than by ontological fuzziness. (2) I also think supervaluationism correctly captures the logic of vague discourse—truth requires holding on every admissible precisification, falsity requires failing on all of them, with genuine indeterminacy in the middle. (3) I take the source of vagueness to be instrumental rather than metaphysical: it resembles the resolution limits and noise floors of physical measurement apparatus, not a lack of determinacy in nature itself. (4) In scientific taxonomy I therefore favor replacing natural-language terms with precisely stipulated operational definitions, though I do not take these stipulations to discover pre-existing boundaries. (5) By contrast, for moral and aesthetic predicates—such as 'cruel' or 'elegant'—I deny that there are hidden sharp boundaries waiting to be found, and I treat reflective equilibrium as the appropriate method for revising such concepts, whereas for empirical predicates I think hidden boundaries should be discovered rather than negotiated.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are in genuine tension and which tensions dissolve on closer inspection? Rank them by severity, state the least costly revision for each, and identify the single change that would clean up the most conflicts. In particular, I am unsure whether my instrumental picture in (3) undermines the universal epistemicism in (1), whether holding both (1) and (2) is stable, and whether my differential treatment of moral versus empirical vagueness in (5) is consistent with (1).
