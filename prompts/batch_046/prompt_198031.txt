A philosophy blog I read recently offers a summary of Charles Peirce's and William James's pragmatist theories of truth. According to the author, Peirce defines truth as the belief that inquiry is fated ultimately to agree upon, which the blog interprets as meaning that true beliefs are merely those that prove permanently useful for organizing experience, carrying no further relation to an external reality. James, the author continues, holds that truth is what works in the way of belief, but this is not subjective whimsy: James demands that a belief be verified by the concrete experiential consequences it produces, so truth is always anchored in observable outcomes. The blog then concludes that the real difference between the two is only methodological—Peirce examines the collective long-run endpoint of inquiry, while James examines individual verification episodes—and that neither philosopher accepts a correspondence theory in which propositions mirror a mind-independent world, since both treat reality as what is eventually settled or constructed by the process of inquiry. How fair is this summary? Please examine it sentence by sentence, distinguishing what a scholar of pragmatism would accept, where the author subtly conflates Peirce's communal long-run account with James's individual verificationism, and whether Peirce's appeal to the resistance of brute fact and Secondness undermines the claim that he rejects all correspondence with mind-independent reality. I am also unsure whether the blog's characterization of James's verification as strictly tied to observable outcomes misses his allowance for non-observable satisfactions, and I want to know whether the author's final conclusion—that both philosophers reject correspondence—actually survives these corrections.
