I have just finished a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment and whether it genuinely demonstrates that self-awareness is immediate and non-inferential. We kept circling the same points until I proposed a modified version that I think exposes a hidden premise in the standard reading. Instead of the usual suspended figure in empty space who is merely cut off from sensation, imagine a subject who has never experienced any exteroceptive or proprioceptive input whatsoever, and whose entire mental life consists of one continuous, objectless affective tone—something like a low-grade, undifferentiated anxiety with no bodily localization, no sensory modality, and no intentional object. My friend insisted that even this stripped-down subject could still affirm her own existence without any inference, which would preserve Avicenna’s conclusion that the self is known through pure intuition alone.

Please argue that, in this specific affect-only case, the presence of that minimal qualitative content actually undermines rather than supports Avicenna’s claim. Show how the subject’s awareness of herself must be mediated by her awareness of this bare affective state, so that self-knowledge depends on knowing something other than the bare self, and explain what implicit assumption about the possibility of a total vacuum of content Avicenna’s original argument must smuggle in to avoid this result. Keep the analysis conceptual rather than historical; I am not asking for a survey of medieval commentators, but for a rigorous philosophical reconstruction of what this modified flying-man scenario reveals about the conditions of first-person awareness.
