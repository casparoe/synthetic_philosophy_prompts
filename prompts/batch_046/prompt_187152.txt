For a graduate seminar in philosophy of religion (10 students, 90 minutes), design an in-class simulation that makes participants feel the force of Wittgenstein’s claim that religious belief operates as a form of life rather than as an empirical hypothesis. Do not open with a lecture; instead, split the group into two sub-communities that share a vocabulary but assign it different grammatical rules. Give both groups an identical scarce-resource task—distributing limited aid based on statements made by members—where one community must treat key utterances (for example, that a recovery is underway) as predictive claims subject to verification, while the other treats the same utterances as commitments that generate obligations regardless of empirical outcome. Specify roles, a timeline, communication rules (including restricted cross-community dialogue), and a consequence structure that creates real but non-monetary stakes (e.g., public justification of allocations). Ensure that participants discover for themselves that they cannot resolve the dispute by appealing to shared evidence. Then build in a hidden structural feature: one participant, the mediator, is secretly instructed to be aspect-blind—to perceive every utterance only in its empirical aspect and never in its commitment-structuring aspect—who must attempt to broker agreement. In the debrief, map moments from the simulation onto the conceptual distinction between empirical belief and grammatical framework, explain what the mediator’s inevitable failure reveals about the nature of religious disagreement, and state frankly which feature of Wittgenstein’s view (such as the idea that conversion is not rational persuasion but a change in form of life) the activity can reproduce and which feature it necessarily omits, requiring the facilitator to supply it explicitly. Avoid citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts; present everything in conceptual terms.
