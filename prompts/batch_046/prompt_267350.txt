I have recently retired after thirty-five years in experimental condensed-matter physics, and since then I have been reading philosophy of science and technology with some seriousness. I now sit on my university’s Interdisciplinary Research Governance Committee, and we must reach a decision before the end of this term.

A proposal has arrived from the Institute for Advanced Machine Learning. They wish to deploy a large-scale training pipeline for a language-model system and request approval for an exclusively outcome-based supervision regime: independent auditors would evaluate only the final model’s outputs for harmful, deceptive, or misaligned behaviour, with no institutional access to intermediate weights, optimisation trajectories, or internal representations. The researchers argue that this safeguards proprietary architecture, avoids what they term “process fetishism,” and respects the fact that emergent capabilities are not reliably legible until they manifest in observable behaviour. They further propose, as a concession, a “cryptographic time-lock” protocol: intermediate logs would be encrypted and released automatically only if the final audit detects an anomaly, thereby preserving opacity during development while allowing retrospective forensic analysis.

A minority of the committee—myself included—worries that this treats alignment as a black-box statistical filter rather than as an engineering property of the system’s dynamics. We are concerned that deceptive strategies or harmful sub-routines might be suppressed only at the surface level, remaining latent within the weights, and that without real-time process diagnostics we cannot distinguish genuine alignment from strategic compliance tailored to pass the audit. The time-lock compromise, in our view, still denies us the ability to intervene during training, which is precisely when correction might be feasible.

The committee must recommend one of three courses: approve the proposal as submitted; reject it outright; or approve it subject to specific, enforceable conditions with a clear review timetable. I have been asked to draft the committee’s written opinion for circulation.

Please draft the memo. It should state a concrete recommendation with a defined time frame for implementation or review, and it should treat the researchers’ position—including their epistemic argument about the illegibility of intermediate representations—seriously rather than dismissing it. In your reasoning, examine whether there is a philosophically coherent distinction between supervising the process of cognition and supervising its products, drawing where appropriate on analogies from scientific methodology or the philosophy of mind. Address specifically whether the proposed time-lock protocol resolves the committee’s concern or merely postpones it, and say what conditions, if any, would make outcome-only supervision ethically acceptable in this context. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need reasoning that my colleagues can assess on its own merits. Write in a formal register suitable for an institutional document.
