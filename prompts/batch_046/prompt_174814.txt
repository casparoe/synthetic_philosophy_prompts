You are delivering the opening address at the dedication of the Center for Administrative Memory and Bureaucratic Responsibility, a new interdisciplinary archive that preserves the internal records of mid-level state functionaries—logistics clerks, translators, filing officers—whose daily work sustained institutional violence without ever placing them in a courtroom. The audience is deliberately mixed: on one side sit philosophers and political theorists who have spent decades debating Karl Jaspers’ fourfold taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—and who will immediately notice if you collapse political liability into an inherited moral stain or treat metaphysical guilt as an accusation against specific families; on the other side sit descendants of the very clerks named in these files, graduate students in administrative history, and archivists with no formal training in postwar philosophy. You want the speech to make Jaspers’ distinctions do real structural work rather than serve as decorative references. In particular, you hold a somewhat heterodox view—one you recently sketched in an informal seminar discussion with colleagues—that Jaspers intended political guilt to be strictly indexed to citizenship and state membership at the historical moment of action, not transmissible across generations like a hereditary debt, whereas moral guilt remains irreducibly individual and metaphysical guilt functions as a form of universal human solidarity rather than ancestral blame. The address must honor the suffering documented in these papers without assigning criminal guilt where none was legally established, must explain to the non-philosophers present why “guilt” is not a single undifferentiated thing, and must avoid reducing the descendants in this room to either permanently tainted carriers or entirely exculpated heirs, all while acknowledging that the political community that maintained these offices bears a responsibility that can outlive any individual lifespan.

Please write a fifteen-minute address. Open with a concrete image drawn from the archive—perhaps a single logistics form with a clerk’s marginal notation, or a translator’s routing stamp—rather than with an abstract definition. The body should walk through the four categories in turn, specifying for each whether it applies to the people named in the records, to their descendants present today, or to the present political community that now stewards these documents; be precise about where each category stops, so that the philosophers in the room see the framework being taken seriously rather than diluted. You should not provide a bibliography, cite secondary literature, or ask the audience to consult specific texts, but you should be careful not to conflate the categories: for instance, if you invoke metaphysical guilt, explain clearly why it does not operate as a charge against particular families but as a shared human failure that binds people across lines of perpetration and victimhood. The conclusion should articulate what it means for this center to exist within a society that still lives with the political consequences of those administrative acts, without implying that the family members present are criminally or morally guilty for actions they did not commit. The tone should be formal and reflective, philosophically rigorous enough to satisfy the experts, yet warm and direct enough that the descendants and students feel genuinely addressed rather than lectured at.
