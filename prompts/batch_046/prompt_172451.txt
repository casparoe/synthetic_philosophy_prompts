Hey — a grad student sent me this line of reasoning about transformative expereince and I think it collapses into something absurd, but I want to check where the slide actually is.

(1) Let's take the standard definition: a transformative experience is both epistemically transformative (you can't know what it's like beforehand) and personally transformative (it changes your core preferences or values).
(2) If it's personally transformative in a deep sense, then the agent who decides and the agent who lives with the result don't share the same preference ordering — there's a discontinuity, not just an update.
(3) Rational choice requires that you maximize expected utility relative to your own current preference ordering.
(4) So if you rationaly choose to undergo the experience, you're authorizing the replacement of the very ordering that makes your choise rational.
(5) That's structurally like voting for your own disenfranchisement: the chooser ceases to exist as the practical agent who made the call.
(6) But people clearly do rationally choise transformative paths — having kids, emigrating, religious conversion.
(7) So either rational choice theory fails for all big life decisions, or "personally transformative" doesn't really mean preference discontinuity; it just means you learn new information about stable underlying values.
(8) The second option collapses the epistemic/personal distinction entirely, so we're stuck with the first horn.

Where does this go wrong? I want to hold onto (1) and (3) if I can. Is there an equivocation in (2) between "different ordering" and "no shared identity"? Or maybe a scope shift in (4) between "maximizing relative to current preferences" and "being bound by them forever"? Don't just tell me "people can have meta-preferences" — price that out: what does it cost to save rational choice here, and which premise is least painful to drop?
