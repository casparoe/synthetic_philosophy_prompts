At a workshop on architectural ethics, Mara and Leo disagreed about whether the architect Chen genuinely holds normative commitments to accessibility. Chen never reviews accessibility specifications herself; she only configures an automated compliance engine that applies an idealized user-model—representing needs that users would endorse after fully informed deliberation—to generate all technical specifications. Mara argued that Chen's commitment is indirect but fully real: she endorses the normative filter that produces the outputs, just as citizens endorse laws they never personally draft. Leo replied that this describes a commitment race downward—Chen receives moral credit for accessibility without bearing deliberative burden, and because she does not understand the engine's reasoning, she could not correct it if the model misrepresented actual users. Mara answered that direct engagement is unnecessary for genuine commitment; we accept medical research ethics through institutional review boards we do not sit on, so endorsing an external normative structure is sufficient. Leo countered that institutional boards are collectively accountable and transparent, whereas Chen's engine is an opaque black box she treats as authoritative; her endorsement is therefore empty, since she has no idea what specific norm she is actually committing to. They asked me to referee, and I could not decide. Can you work through their exchange step by step, saying who is right about each specific point and not merely who wins overall? Also, what important consideration, if any, are both parties missing?
