Otto Neurath devoted the latter part of his career to ISOTYPE, a rigorously standardized visual language developed with collaborators to convey quantitative and social-scientific information across linguistic barriers without the ambiguity he associated with natural language. He was an avowed physicalist, deeply suspicious of metaphysical propositions, and convinced that scientific communication required fixed, human-agreed symbols whose meanings were anchored in observable, reproducible design rules rather than in interpretation.

Consider a contemporary but still niche proposal called the Adversarial Pictogram Stream (APS): an AI-safety documentation framework in which every claim about a model’s behavior is expressed exclusively through a continuously generated sequence of synthetic pictograms. These symbols are produced by a generative system trained solely to maximize cross-cultural recognition scores, with no fixed dictionary, no human-curated icon inventory, and no explicit mapping rules. Proponents argue that this eliminates linguistic bias and makes alignment audits universally readable regardless of literacy or native language.

(2) How would his physicalism and anti-metaphysical stance inform his assessment of letting an opaque generative process—rather than transparent, observable design rules—determine what a given symbol denotes in an audit context? (3) If Neurath were asked to rank three documentation formats for an international AI-alignment audit—traditional English technical prose, a static ISOTYPE-style chart with human-curated icons, and the dynamic APS—on the dimensions of scientific rigor, universal accessibility, and resistance to hidden ideological distortion, what ordering would he likely defend, and where would he concede that the modern proposal outperforms his own system?
