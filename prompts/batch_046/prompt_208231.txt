I'm trying to work out what I actually take "brain-like computation" to mean, in the specific architectural sense von Neumann gestured at in his comparisons of neural tissue and electronic computers — the ones that stress unreliable analog components, parallel statistical logic, and an arithmetic that does not use positional notation. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases, and I'm wondering whether there's a coherent principle behind them or whether I'm just clustering around a family resemblance.

The clear yeses:
- A biological cortex computing via noisy, millisecond-scale synaptic transmission, aggregating signals statistically without deterministic gate-level logic: clearly brain-like.
- A hypothetical self-replicating automaton built from unreliable analog parts, using statistical error-correction during reproduction: also clearly brain-like, and in fact captures the other half of the same research program.

The clear nos:
- A modern digital GPU running a neural network with deterministic floating-point arithmetic, perfect error-correction, and explicit positional binary encoding: not brain-like, despite learning and parallelism. The substrate and algorithmic structure miss the point.
- A digital computer that simulates every neuron with bit-perfect fidelity: it's a model of a brain, not an instance of brain-like computation.

The ambiguous middle, where my intuitions start pulling apart:
- A system made of unreliable analog elements (say, noisy memristors) arranged in parallel, but organized so that all computation is ultimately reduced to positional binary arithmetic at the macro level: I want to say it's partly there, but the positional encoding feels like it violates the core idea, so I lean "no, not genuinely brain-like."

Now the hard pair I can't reconcile:
- I think the brain's extreme slowness — neuronal firing on millisecond timescales — is architecturally essential, not incidental. If you kept the analog unreliability and statistical aggregation but sped the dynamics to nanosecond cycles, you'd have something that behaves like a very strange digital-analog hybrid computer, not a brain.
- At the same time, if you replaced biological neurons with faster analog devices (memristors operating at microsecond scales) but preserved the statistical logic, the parallel layout, and the absence of positional notation, my intuition is that this is still brain-like — the speed change feels like an engineering parameter, not a category shift.

So is there a principle that saves both? One candidate I've been toying with — call it the anti-positional statistical unreliability thesis — says a system is brain-like in this sense iff it computes reliably through parallel, substrate-independent statistical aggregation over unreliable analog components, using an arithmetic that lacks positional notation, regardless of replication capacity or temporal scale. But that principle forces me to drop the first verdict about millisecond slowness being essential.

Another candidate adds a temporal clause: brain-like computation requires dynamics that are slow relative to the error-correction and aggregation timescales of the components, so that unreliability is experienced and corrected within the computation rather than eliminated beforehand. That saves the slowness verdict but seems ad hoc, and it makes the memristor case depend on arbitrary ratios.

What does either principle predict for a case I haven't considered: a quantum analog device that uses continuous variables, is inherently noisy (no explicit error correction), and evolves via deterministic unitary dynamics at the macro scale, with no positional encoding? Would that count?

Please tell me whether there's a cleaner principle that accommodates all my verdicts, or which one I should abandon, and what your preferred principle predicts for the quantum analog case. Also, if the principle needs repair, show me the minimal patch that saves the most intuitions.
