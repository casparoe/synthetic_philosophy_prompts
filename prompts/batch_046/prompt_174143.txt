After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend last evening about evolutionary explanation and its limits, I attempted to organize my scattered thoughts into a short essay on adaptationism and the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique. What follows are not polished paragraphs but the central line of argument I want to pursue, and I would be grateful for your comments, particularly on whether my characterization of the spandrels argument is accurate, whether the architectural analogy I rely on is genuinely illuminating or quietly misleading, and whether my tentative conclusion is warranted. The critique, as I understand it, warns against treating every biological trait as an adaptation shaped by natural selection for its current function; some traits are spandrels, structural byproducts that arise necessarily from other architectural decisions and are not themselves targets of selection. My friend insisted that this distinction has been largely defanged, because any spandrel can be reclassified as an exaptation once it acquires a secondary function, allowing functional analysis to resume without interruption. I want to resist this dissolution of the distinction by arguing that the spandrels metaphor carries a hidden philosophical commitment: it assumes we can separate the adaptive frame from the accidental space within it, much as an architect distinguishes load-bearing structure from decorative fill. But in organisms there is no independent blueprint and no architect; development is a unified process in which form and function are intertwined at every level. If we take the architectural comparison seriously, the absence of a designer means that every feature is potentially both frame and spandrel, and the distinction collapses into a post-hoc narrative choice rather than an empirical discovery. I therefore suggest that the spandrels critique does more than caution against Panglossian overreach; it threatens to undermine the very possibility of isolating functional explanations from structural ones in evolutionary biology. At the same time, this conclusion seems too destructive, since biologists do identify adaptations with reasonable confidence, and I wonder whether there is a coherent middle ground—perhaps informed by the philosophy of artifacts, where design explanations can be partial and context-dependent without requiring a literal designer—that allows us to preserve the adaptation-spandrel distinction without committing to a full-blown design stance. I am especially interested in whether my use of the metaphor smuggles in assumptions that Gould and Lewontin themselves would reject, and whether my claim that the distinction threatens to collapse survives once we acknowledge that evolutionary explanations can be partial rather than totalizing. Please respond in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a thoughtful letter or email, addressing the accuracy of my framing, the strength of the analogy, and whether I have missed a more nuanced position that resolves the tension without abandoning either adaptationism or the spandrels critique.
