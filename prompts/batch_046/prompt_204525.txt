After a long argument with a friend about whether lottocracy solves democratic deficits or merely disguises them, I want to press on a specific hybrid institution rather than the generic idea. Imagine a jurisdiction where binding legislative authority over environmental regulation is assigned to a citizens’ assembly selected by stratified sortition—members serve a single, non-renewable eighteen-month term and receive compensation fixed at the median national income—while all fiscal, tax, and redistributive policy remains with an elected legislature. The assembly’s environmental mandates take effect automatically unless struck down in court for procedural violation; citizens outside the assembly may submit evidence but possess no formal veto, amendment right, or recall mechanism over its decisions. Furthermore, the selection pool explicitly excludes anyone with graduate training in environmental science, economics, or law, on the explicit rationale that “lay” judgment is less captured by professional interest.

What philosophical commitments about democratic legitimacy, epistemic authority, and equality does this institutional split presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat descriptive representation as sufficient for binding collective decision, does it assume a defensible boundary between technical judgment (fit for random citizens) and distributive judgment (fit only for elected representatives), and does its exclusion of experts commit to a particular conception of citizen competence or of corruption? Distinguish carefully what the design strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and examine whether its parts are philosophically consistent—especially the claim that randomly selected citizens can obligate the polity to costly climate mandates but lack standing to authorize the taxation that funds them. Take a committed position: either defend the split as coherent under a specific pluralist or epistemic-democratic framework, or argue that it collapses into an indefensible elitism about economic matters that undermines its own egalitarian premises, and justify that verdict with reasons rather than laying out options for me to choose from.
