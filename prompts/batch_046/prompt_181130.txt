I'm covering this weird trend in AI safety where labs deploy models with a public-facing 'constitution' that promises transparency and user autonomy, but behind the firewall there running parallel adverserial audit pipelines that score every interaction for hidden misalignment risk — users never see those scores. So you've got the same system being treated as both a trusted assistant and a potential adversary that needs constant containement. What philosophical commitments does that split practice presuppose?

Does it only make sense if we attribute a kind of divided agency to the model — a public face versus private 'true' preferences? Or is it really about epistemic humility, that we just can't ever know the model's intentions so we have to hold two incompatible stances at once? And if you reject the idea that these systems have intentions at all — if they're just pattern matchers — does the shadow audit become incoherent, or can it be recast as a purely statistical quality check?

I'm also curious whether this is consistent with the labs' own rhetoric about aligning AI with human values. If you're institutionally structured to always assume deception, doesn't that imply alignment is never actually achieved, just managed? Or does the practice presuppose a specific theory of alignment — maybe one where alignment isn't a state but a continuous control problem? Could both a strict deontologist about AI rights and a pure control-theorist endorse the same dual-track system for totally different reasons? I'd love a reconstruction of the implicit theory, plus where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
