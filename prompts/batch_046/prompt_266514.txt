I'm taking this poli-sci course and we're looking at voting rules across different countries, and I keep running into what feels like a philosophic contradiction but I can't quite name it. So Australia has compulsory voting for citizens — you get fined if you don't show up, though you can submit a blank ballot. Meanwhile, in parts of Sweden and a few other EU places, non-citizen residents who've lived there long enough can vote in local elections. And then Scotland lowered the voting age to 16 for local and Scottish parliament elections. So on the one hand, voting looks like a civic duty tied specifically to citizenship status; on the other hand, it gets extended to people who aren't citizens but are afffected by local policies, and contracted downward to teenagers who might not have full legal capacity in other domains.

What view of democratic legitimacy or political membership does this cluster of practices actually presuppose? Is there a coherent theory that treats voting as an office that citizens must occupy, while also treating long-term residency or adolescent development as qualifying conditions? Or do these rules quietly rely on different, incompatble theories — maybe one about stakeholder interest, one about republican virtue, one about developmental readiness? And if the underlying view is that anyone affected should have a say, why is it compulsory only for citizens and not for all affected residents? I'm trying to figure out whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined or if there's one implicit framework I'm just not seeing.
