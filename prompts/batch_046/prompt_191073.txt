In our seminar on moral education through biography, the professor has consistently assessed whether various texts serve as effective vehicles for learning from lives within a broadly Plutarchian framework. Her verdicts so far: she regards Plutarch’s Parallel Lives—specifically the pairing of Demosthenes and Cicero—as exemplary, precisely because the comparative structure forces the reader to exercise judgment about virtue and vice rather than merely admire; she dismisses a well-known medieval hagiography of St. Anthony that suppresses all moral failures and substitutes miracles for character development, saying it offers nothing to inhabit or judge; she approves a modern critical biography of a twentieth-century statesman that meticulously details both ethical achievements and failures while implicitly comparing the subject to his contemporaries; and she rejects a purely chronological catalog of Roman magistrates’ honors and offices reconstructed from inscriptions, noting that without narrative or comparison it is dead to moral imagination. She has said nothing yet about texts reconstructed through modern computational methods.

Next week we will discuss a recently reconstructed biography of an obscure second-century provincial magistrate, assembled from administrative papyri and fragmentary correspondence through algorithmic gap-filling and stylistic normalization. The result is granular and historically authenticated: it records day-to-day ethical deliberations, administrative compromises, and personal failures in chronological order, with no rhetorical framing, no comparison to other figures, no idealization, and no synthetic narrative arc—just a documentary sequence of choices and outcomes. The proposal is to include it as a core text for moral learning in our course.

Predict whether she will approve it for moral education, identify the two or more rules about biography and moral learning that could consistently explain her four prior verdicts, say which rule predicts approval and which predicts rejection of this reconstructed text, identify her closest prior analog, and specify what further case—perhaps one that offers rich comparative framing but entirely lacks granular deliberation—would best reveal which rule she is actually running. I am less interested in a simple yes or no than in the reasoning that connects her track record to this case, and in an honest statement of what principle she would be adopting with either choice.
