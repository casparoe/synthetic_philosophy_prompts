I serve on the Joint Committee on Autonomous Clinical Systems at a major teaching hospital, and we must circulate a written opinion by Friday on whether to continue deploying System X-9, an ICU triage-support algorithm that implements a “relevance-fencing” architecture. To manage real-time belief revision without triggering an unmanageable cascade of contextual updates, the system partitions incoming patient data into “update-eligible” clinical frames and “update-ineligible” background frames. In a recent adverse event, a Jehovah’s Witness patient’s religious refusal of blood products was categorized as an “update-ineligible constitutive commitment”; because it was fenced outside the urgency-recalculation frame, the algorithm failed to escalate hemorrhagic shock for forty minutes. The engineering team defends this design as a philosophically necessary response to the frame problem—without such fencing, they argue, any change in background conditions would force a total revision of clinical priorities. The clinical ethics staff counter that the architecture does not solve the frame problem but disguises a normative choice about which patient values count as medically relevant.

Please draft the committee’s formal memo recommending one of the following: (a) approval with binding redesign conditions and a monitoring protocol; (b) immediate suspension pending architectural overhaul; or (c) restricted deployment with a mandatory human-override trigger and a sunset clause. The memo should specify a concrete course of action with explicit deadlines (e.g., redesign milestones, review dates, or suspension timelines). It should treat the engineering team’s frame-theoretic justification with intellectual seriousness—identifying exactly where relevance-fencing crosses from a legitimate epistemic strategy into an unacceptable moral evasion in clinical contexts—and it should give the strongest argument for the option you reject a fair, substantive hearing rather than a dismissive restatement. Write the document so that non-philosopher board members can follow the reasoning, but do not dilute the conceptual precision: name the specific feature of the frame problem at stake, explain why fencing fails to resolve it in this setting, and state the bottom line in language the hospital administration can act on. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to specific philosophers by name unless they are essential to the reasoning; the memo should stand on its argument alone.
