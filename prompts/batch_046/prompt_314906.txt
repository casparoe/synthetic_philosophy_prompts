After beginning a long-term administrative position in a public-health bureaucracy last autumn, I have returned to Sartre's later social philosophy—specifically his analyses of the practico-inert and seriality—to understand whether my growing sense of being trapped by procedural rules is a failure of authenticity or something more structural. I have constructed the following argument, yet its conclusion strikes me as implausible, and I cannot locate the precise misstep.

(1) For Sartre, bad faith is the denial of one's radical transcendence: treating oneself or one's situation as possessing a fixed essence rather than recognizing that consciousness always exceeds its facticity.
(2) In his account of the practico-inert, Sartre grants that human praxis creates material and institutional structures—bureaucracies, technologies, economic systems—that subsequently constrain future praxis; these are not mere illusions but real sedimentations of past freedom.
(3) Seriality denotes the condition of belonging to a passive collective in which individuals are isolated from one another and alienated from the group's collective praxis; within seriality, one's actions are largely shaped by the practico-inert rather than by conscious collective choice.
(4) Consequently, in a fully serial institution, an individual's freedom is not simply concealed by bad faith but is structurally suppressed: the bureaucrat genuinely lacks the power to alter the rules governing her labor.
(5) If freedom can be genuinely suppressed in this manner, then Sartre's thesis that we are always radically free—always able to transcend our situation—must be false, or at least restricted to contexts outside structural domination.
(6) Conversely, if freedom is never genuinely suppressed, then to describe bureaucratic domination as real oppression is itself an act of bad faith: the bureaucrat falsely endows the practico-inert with deterministic power over her.
(7) Thus Sartre's framework appears to force a dilemma: either structural domination is real and radical freedom is false, or radical freedom is true and all structural domination collapses into individual bad faith.

This conclusion seems too stark, particularly in light of Sartre's explicit commitment both to the reality of the practico-inert and to the permanence of freedom. I would prefer to preserve both the claim that structures genuinely constrain us and the claim that freedom remains operative—perhaps by rejecting (4) or (6)—but I do not see how to do so without undermining one of the two commitments I value. Which step should be rejected, why did it initially appear sound, and what are the costs of abandoning it for Sartre's broader social ontology? Does the error lie in treating seriality as total suppression, in conflating constraint with determination, or elsewhere?
