Exam question: The heterogeneity problem asks what, if anything, all pleasures share despite their manifest diversity—physical, intellectual, malicious, aesthetic. Two strategies are often contrasted. On the "tonal uniformity" view, every pleasure contains an identical qualitative feel, differentiated only by intensity and intentional object. On the "functional coherence" view, pleasures are unified not by a shared feel but by a shared motivational architecture: being pursued for their own sake and tending to perpetuate the activity that generates them. Evaluate both strategies, paying special attention to cases of "reflexive displeasure"—experiences in which pleasure seems constitutively tied to an awareness of lack, pain, or deprivation (for instance, the satisfaction reported during a voluntary fast, or the enjoyment of tragic narrative art). Can either strategy accommodate these cases without collapsing the distinction between pleasure and its opposite?

Student answer:
The heterogeneity problem is serious because pleasures range from eating to solving equations to schadenfreude, so it is unclear what they have in common. The tonal uniformity view claims they all share a basic feeling—like a warm glow—just directed at different objects. This fails for reflexive displeasure, because in these cases suffering or deprivation is present alongside the enjoyment. Since suffering feels nothing like that warm glow, tonal uniformity cannot explain how pleasure exists when its supposed qualitative signature is missing or contradicted. The functional coherence view handles this better. Even in reflexive displeasure, the subject pursues the experience for its own sake and wants it to continue—the faster continues fasting because he values the deprivation, and audiences seek out tragic art repeatedly. So the motivational structure remains intact. The real error of tonal uniformity is treating pleasure as a simple sensation rather than a complex attitude. That said, functional coherence faces its own difficulty: it cannot easily distinguish genuine pleasure from obsessive compulsion, since both involve continuation and pursuit. But this is not fatal, because hedonists can reply that compulsion lacks the right kind of reflective endorsement. Therefore, functional coherence solves the heterogeneity problem, and reflexive displeasure actually supports it by showing that pleasure depends on attitude and motivation, not on any particular feeling.
