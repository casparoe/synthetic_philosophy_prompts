I work as a software engineer at a firm that builds and deploys large language models, and I recently became puzzled by an institutional split in our documentation protocols that seems to carry hidden philosophical baggage. For every released model, we maintain two parallel documentation tracks. The first track, used for regulatory transparency reports and user-facing disclosures, requires us to describe the system in mentalistic language: we must specify what the model believes about user intent, what goals it pursues, and what preferences shape its outputs, and external reviewers evaluate these descriptions as if they were genuine psychological assessments. The second track, governing all internal engineering, debugging, and safety analysis, enforces a strict prohibition: any root-cause explanation that invokes beliefs, intentions, or desires is automatically rejected, and every behavior must be explained through activation patterns, gradient dynamics, and statistical structure in the training data. The same team writes both documents, and the company treats this as perfectly coherent—the mentalistic descriptions are labeled legally required framing, while the mechanistic descriptions are treated as literally true.

I am currently taking an introductory philosophy-of-mind course in the evenings—my background is in distributed systems, not philosophy—and this split looks like it presupposes a specific view about whether beliefs and desires are real. Does our dual-track practice implicitly commit us to eliminative materialism, treating mental-state talk as useful fiction that should be eliminated from serious explanation? Or could it be sustained by an instrumentalist view that allows mental-state language in some contexts but not others? More specifically, I want to know whether a realist who thinks beliefs and desires genuinely exist could make sense of why we systematically ban mental-state explanations in engineering while mandating them for compliance. Please reconstruct the implicit theory of mind that our protocol requires, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, identify where the two tracks are philosophically consistent or in tension, and explain what would have to change in our documentation practices if the underlying theory about mental states turned out to be mistaken.
