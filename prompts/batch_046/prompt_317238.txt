I've been working through Carroll's dialogue on the justification of deduction with a study group, and we've formalized an argument that now strikes me as proving far too much—if it is sound, deductive reasoning seems impossible or circular, yet I want to preserve the intuition that rational inference requires some form of explicit justification. Here is the chain as we have laid it out:

(1) For any rational transition from premises to conclusion, the reasoner must be able to cite the specific principle that licenses that move.
(2) In the dialogue scenario, once premises A and B and the conditional linking them to Z are all explicitly granted, a skeptical interlocutor can still withhold assent to Z by refusing to apply the rule of inference—say, modus ponens—until that rule itself is added to the premises.
(3) But the same demand applies to the rule just added: applying it to these particular premises requires a further principle, generating an infinite regress of explicit premises.
(4) If the regress is never halted, no conclusion is ever reached; if it is halted at some arbitrary point, then the final step is unjustified, and the reasoner could have halted one step earlier without rational cost.
(5) Therefore, deductive justification is either incomplete (infinite) or circular and arbitrary, and logic offers no independent support for any claim not already assumed.
(6) Moreover, since any inference rule can be rewritten as an axiom in a suitable formal system—think of the structural rules of a sequent calculus being internalized as premises—the distinction between axioms and rules collapses, removing any non-arbitrary stopping point.
(7) So there is no rational difference between accepting a conclusion because it genuinely follows from premises and accepting it because it was smuggled in as an unexamined rule.

I find (7) deeply counterintuitive, and I am especially reluctant to surrender (1) or (2), since they express a real pressure toward explicitness that I think any adequate account of rationality should honor. Where does this argument go wrong? Is the flaw an equivocation between accepting a propositional premise and being entitled to apply a rule, or is there a more subtle slide between object-language premises and meta-language permissions? If I must reject one step, which rejection is least damaging to the idea that deduction needs justification but does not require an infinite ladder?
