Please explain a conceptual tension in Grice's account of implicature and speaker meaning. Grice distinguishes what a speaker means from what a sentence means by appealing to the Cooperative Principle and the conversational maxims; implicatures arise when a speaker flouts a maxim in a way meant to be recognized, allowing an audience to recover intended meaning beyond literal content.

Consider a structurally complex case—call it a stratified-audience flout—in which a speaker addresses two audiences with different background knowledge in a single utterance. The speaker violates the maxim of Quantity in a manner transparent to Audience A, who thereby derives a specific implicature, but opaque to Audience B, who interprets the utterance literally and takes it at face value. The speaker intends one speaker-meaning for A and a different, literally truthful content for B, with no shared cooperative purpose binding the two interpretive frames.

Does Grice's framework accommodate this without reducing speaker meaning to audience-relative interpretation? Specifically: is the speaker's intention sufficient to fix the implicature for A regardless of B's failure to recover it? Does the Cooperative Principle presuppose a unitary shared purpose that is absent here, or can it be applied locally to discrete audience frames? And does this case show that Gricean machinery implicitly depends on a single intended audience, requiring supplementation—say, by treating maxims as operating within indexed conversational contexts—rather than globally?

If this framing rests on a confusion—if, for example, it wrongly treats Gricean cooperation as requiring mutual awareness rather than rational expectation, or if it collapses speaker meaning into hearer uptake—say so plainly and correct the premise rather than accommodating it. I want a precise diagnosis of where the conceptual machinery holds and where it fractures, not a survey of the literature or any list of sources.
