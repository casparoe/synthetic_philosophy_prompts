Question: Evaluate whether tranquilism—the thesis that only the absence of craving (taṇhā) and suffering (dukkha) possesses intrinsic value—is compatible with the experience of aesthetic tension. Take as your central case the listener who attends to an unresolved musical cadence, such as a dominant seventh suspended before its tonic resolution: the listener anticipates closure, feels a subtle harmonic pressure, and is released only upon resolution. Does this structure necessarily involve the kind of craving that tranquilism condemns? In answering, distinguish carefully between bare perceptual anticipation and grasping desire (upādāna), and consider whether any philosopher's account of will-less contemplation might illuminate the case.

Student's answer: The tranquilist maintains that intrinsic value belongs exclusively to states lacking craving and suffering; all other conditions are at best instrumental. Listening to an unresolved cadence, however, appears to violate this ideal, since the listener thirsts for harmonic resolution. That thirst is not mere prediction but a form of taṇhā: the mind grasps at a future state, generating dukkha through temporal attachment. Consequently, aesthetic appreciation structured by anticipation is incompatible with tranquilism.

Yet Schopenhauer's doctrine of aesthetic contemplation offers a defense. For Schopenhauer, pure artistic perception suspends the will, turning the subject into a will-less mirror of the Platonic Idea. Because the will is the metaphysical root of suffering, its suspension is identical to the cessation of craving and dukkha. Thus Schopenhauer provides a Western formulation of tranquilism: the good is precisely the absence of willing. From this it follows that aesthetic experience is compatible with tranquilism whenever it is will-less. The unresolved cadence is problematic only if the listener clings to resolution; experienced as bare, non-grasping expectation—like the perceptual anticipation of a falling body—the tension involves no upādāna and therefore no suffering. Hence tranquilism does not reject music, but only those modes of listening infected by identification with desire.
