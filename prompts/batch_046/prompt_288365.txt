I'm presenting to our philosophy of psychiatry working group next Tuesday. My thesis is that Hacking's looping effects operate differently under dimensional versus categorical psychiatric classification: categorical diagnoses generate strong "kinds of people" feedback loops through discrete self-identification, while dimensional models—specifically the DSM-5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders—suppress that discrete looping but produce a distinct "gradient identity" effect, in which patients internalize continuous trait scores and calibrate self-monitoring to quantitative thresholds, altering phenomenology through distributed normalization rather than typological assimilation. The audience is eight philosophers of science and psychiatry well-versed in Hacking on human kinds, three clinical psychologists who use the Alternative Model daily, and one senior psychiatrist skeptical of social-construction talk who will reach for neurobiological counter-explanations. Give me the objections in the order they'll come, phrased as those groups would phrase them: what the Hacking specialists will push on about the ontology of human kinds, what the clinicians will raise about ecological validity and measurement, and what the psychiatrist will say about neural substrates. For each, tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and the weakest point in my reply that I should defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks—likely whether "gradient identity" is empirically distinguishable from ordinary diagnostic uncertainty or from social-desirability bias in self-report instruments.
