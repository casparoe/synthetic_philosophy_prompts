I'm writing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on how the Hart–Devlin debate over legal moralism is being reshaped by cases that neither philosopher anticipated. To ground the piece, I am mapping the implicit jurisprudence of a contemporary legal philosopher—call her the commentator—whose recent advisory rulings and published case analyses have drawn attention precisely because they do not fall neatly into either Hart's harm-principle liberalism or Devlin's society-protecting moralism.

Here is her track record across five cases she has addressed explicitly.

First, on private, consensual sexual relations between adult siblings who have taken all available measures to eliminate reproductive risk, she argues that criminal prohibition is unjustified, because privacy and autonomy outweigh the symbolic offense to family structure, provided the act remains hidden from public institutional life.

Second, on commercial surrogacy contracts with rigorous medical and psychological safeguards, she supports legalization with state regulation, holding that commodification concerns are serious but do not by themselves justify banning a practice that preserves the agency of all parties.

Third, on a proposed statute that would permanently allow citizens to contract away their legal personhood—entering a state-protected but right-less status—she argues for prohibition, not merely on paternalistic grounds, but because the very possibility of such dissolution threatens the coherence of legal responsibility as a collective institution.

Fourth, on municipal ordinances that ban the public display of algorithmically reconstructed voices and likenesses of deceased persons, even with family consent and absent any defamation, she defends the prohibition, maintaining that collective practices of mourning require protected symbolic boundaries that technology should not dissolve.

Fifth, on the private, voluntary consumption of a synthetic compound engineered to replicate exactly the neurochemical signature of canonical religious ecstasy, marketed explicitly for recreational weekend use, she supports a ban, arguing that the degradation of shared ritual meaning constitutes a harm to social orientation even when no individual is physically injured.

Now consider a new case that has just reached her advisory docket: a proposed statute that would criminalize private, consensual "neurocognitive synchronization"—a practice in which two consenting adults use non-invasive brain-computer interfaces to enter a temporary shared subjective state lasting several hours, during which individual autobiographical memory becomes indistinct but fully returns upon disconnection. There is no physical risk, no lasting cognitive damage, and no third-party harm; proponents frame it as an extreme form of private cognitive liberty analogous to deep meditation or religious union, while opponents argue that it dissolves the metaphysical boundary of personhood on which legal attribution of responsibility depends.

Please do the following. First, articulate at least two distinct principles—one closer to Hart's concern with agency and harm, one closer to Devlin's concern with shared moral structure—that could consistently explain all five of her earlier verdicts, showing for each verdict how the principle applies. Second, state what each principle predicts about the neurocognitive synchronization case, and explain why they diverge there. Third, predict which verdict she is actually more likely to reach, identifying the closest analog in her existing track record and explaining whether her reasoning in that analog is more about preserving individual agency conditions or about protecting collective symbolic boundaries. Fourth, propose one further hypothetical case—involving no technology at all—that would most sharply distinguish the two principles you have identified, specifying exactly how each principle would come down on it and why.

I am less interested in a simple yes-or-no answer than in the architecture of consistency that connects her past rulings to this frontier case.
