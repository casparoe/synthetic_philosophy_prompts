As a novelist constructing a philosophical thriller in which the protagonist is a decision theorist advising on strategic risk, I have drafted the following argument for a pivotal confrontation, yet its conclusion strikes me as intolerably strong and I suspect a concealed equivocation or scope slide. I term the hybrid mechanism branch-coupled threat evaluation. (1) In counterfactual mugging, if a randomizer has already yielded heads so that you are safe, but you know that in the tails branch your algorithm pays, then—if your decision procedure is transparent across worlds—you ought to pay even in heads, to maintain consistency with the counterfactual self who pays. (2) XOR blackmail threatens two mutually exclusive harms, say destruction of your archives or exposure of your identity; the adversary promises to execute exactly one if you refuse, and neither if you pay. Because the harms are exclusive in any actual world, refusing seems rational when you weight only realized risk. (3) Now suppose the adversary randomizes which harm applies, but frames the choice as binding across counterfactual branches: in world H you face harm A unless you pay, in world T you face harm B unless you pay, and your algorithm is evaluated jointly across both. (4) By the reasoning in (1), your procedure in H should match your procedure in T; since paying in T averts B and is coupled to H, paying in H also averts A. (5) Therefore paying dominates refusal in both branches, irrespective of the ransom's magnitude. (6) But this coupling applies to any pair of exclusive threats, however extreme or implausible the demand, implying that rational agents must submit to every XOR blackmail regardless of cost. (7) Furthermore, if nested randomization allows threats within threats, the obligation compounds without bound, apparently requiring total resource depletion. I reject (6) and (7) as absurd, yet I am reluctant to abandon (1), which seems to capture a genuine insight about decision-theoretic consistency. Where precisely does the chain collapse? Is the error in (3)'s assumption that counterfactual evaluation licenses coupling across mutually exclusive actual worlds, in (4)'s application of algorithmic transparency to adversarial framing, or in a quantifier shift between evaluating a single decision procedure and evaluating paired world-specific outcomes? If I must preserve (1) and reject only one other step, which rejection least damages the coherence of counterfactual decision theory, and what does that cost?
