I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure in debates about algorithmic governance and republican liberty, and I am asking whether philosophers have already named and mapped it. The structure unfolds like this. A person is denied some important good—credit, housing, or a welfare benefit—by an automated system that applies a fixed rule. Critics charge that this is domination: an authority set the parameters and can revise them at will. Defenders reply that republican domination requires the capacity for arbitrary interference by an agent, and in this case no particular agent decides against this individual; the denial is mediated by an impersonal rule. Critics then observe that the authority still exercises discretionary power over the rulebook itself. Defenders answer that this power is legally constrained, market-disciplined, or subject to democratic oversight, and therefore not arbitrary. At this point the debate tends to stall, cycling between the absence of a present interferer at the moment of harm and the presence of remote but real control over the conditions of harm. The pattern is related to older concerns about bureaucratic rationality, but the algorithmic version makes the absence of an agentive decision-maker at the point of denial especially sharp. Is there an established concept—within republican theory, philosophy of technology, or political philosophy more broadly—for this exact dialectical configuration, in which rule-mediated, non-agentive interference is defended as freedom-preserving because no one is there to dominate, even though a concentrated discretionary power over the rules persists? If a name exists, how does the phenomenon differ from Pettit’s account of domination that exists without actual interference, and from familiar critiques of bureaucratic discretion? If no established concept fits precisely, please coin a term with a precise definition, and delineate its edges: does it require algorithmic mediation, or does any system with remote rule-makers and local rule-enforcement trigger it; and does the defense collapse if the rules are made genuinely immutable? Please begin with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then provide the reasoning. Do not include any preamble, do not restate the question, and do not offer bibliographies or reading lists.
