I've been trying to wrap my head around teleosemantics — you know, the idea that mental content comes from evolutionary purpose rather than just correlation or causation. I'm in this philosophy of mind course and we were comparing Millikan's view with older stuff, and I stumbled on Peirce writing in the early 1900s about signs and their "final interpretants". There's a passage where he seems to say that a sign isn't really a sign at all unless it tends toward producing some habit or rule of action in the interpreter, and that this habit is the sign's true meaning, not any mental image that pops up. He distinguishes this "final logical interpretant" from the immediate feeling or idea it first causes, and he talks about it almost like the sign has a built-in purpose or final cause directing it toward that habit.

Here's roughly the part I keep rereading: he says a sign stands for an object only insofar as it determines, or tends to determine, in the mind of some interpreter a further sign — the interpertant — and this process continues until it reaches a final logical interpretant, which is not another sign but a habit of action, a general law or rule according to which the interpreter will behave. The immediate interpretant is just the feeling or mental image first produced; the final one is the real effect the sign was meant to have.

I'm trying to understand whether Peirce is already doing something like naturalizing content — like, is he saying the meaning is fixed by the functional endpoint of the sign, independent of anyone's concious thought? Or is he still sneaking in a concious interpreter who has to recognize the habit? And here's where I'm stuck: in modern teleosemantics, Millikan says a representation's content is determined by what it was selected for, but Peirce's talk of a "final" endpoint sounds normative, like there's a correct interpretation that the sign is aiming at. Does that normativity come from the same place as evolutionary selection, or does Peirce need something more like an intentional agent to supply the purpose? I feel like there's a hidden disagreement about whether purpose can exist without a purposive mind, but I can't tell if I'm just misreading Peirce's jargon about "final causes".

Can you help me understand what Peirce is actually saying in this kind of passage, especially about whether the interpretant requires consciousness, and whether his view is closer to modern teleosemantics than people usually say?
