One concludes that mental states therefore do not exist; the other concludes that mental states exist but are strictly identical to those neural processes. They share every empirical and explanatory commitment; they differ only on whether achieving complete reduction licenses retaining the mental term with a deflationary gloss, or instead mandates dropping it as an eliminated fiction.

One party calls it “merely fictional” and denies that it “really exists”; the other insists it “exists as a social construction” but freely concedes that it possesses no independent or “heavyweight” ontology. Once again, all substantive claims align; the disagreement is solely over whether the agreed-upon reduction permits retention-with-redefinition or requires elimination.

The pattern is this: total convergence on all properties, causal roles, and explanatory dependencies, combined with a divergence that concerns only whether to apply an existence-predicate or an elimination-predicate, where the retentionist admits the entity lacks any robust ontological status and the eliminativist admits the term remains pragmatically or expressively useful.
