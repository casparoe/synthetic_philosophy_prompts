I'm serving on my university's procurement and research-governance committee, and we've been asked to rule on a proposal that is philosophically loaded in a way I think the board hasn't fully parsed. A coalition of researchers and technical staff want the university to restructure its machine-learning data-annotation contracts away from conventional vendor agreements and toward worker-owned cooperatives, and they explicitly ground the proposal in Mill's late socialist writings—specifically his defence of voluntary cooperative association, his distinction between revolutionary state-socialism and evolutionary partnership, and his claim in the later editions of Principles of Political Economy that the stationary state could be made tolerable through cooperative production rather than competitive accumulation. They argue that if our institution takes Mill's liberal-socialist synthesis seriously, we should treat annotators as partners with profit-sharing and governance rights, not as contingent labour.

The senior administration objects that this is procurement policy being held hostage to a philosophical interpretation: Mill's cooperative socialism was, they say, a speculative future-state thought experiment rather than a procurement manual; introducing cooperative governance into our supply chain creates fiduciary risk, conflicts with existing employment-law frameworks, and sets a precedent that every vendor relationship must pass an ideological test. They also note—correctly, I think—that Mill retained strong liberal safeguards and was deeply sceptical of any transformation that required state coercion or that sacrificed individual liberty for collective equality, and that he insisted cooperative experiments must compete in the open market rather than receive protected status.

Please draft the committee's formal decision memo. Recommend a concrete course—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—together with a clear time-frame if the recommendation is conditional. The reasoning should treat Mill's actual late position with precision: not the caricature of Mill as a proto-socialist revolutionary, but the nuanced view that partnership between capital and labour could emerge voluntarily, that liberty remains the overriding constraint, and that such arrangements should be tested through practical market competition rather than institutional mandate. At the same time, the memo must engage the administration's strongest objection—that institutions should not use procurement budgets to run philosophical experiments—without dismissing it as mere conservatism. I want a document both sides could cite as representing their position fairly, with an actionable bottom line that the researchers and administrators can both respond to.

Please conclude with a final line in the form Verdict: [approve/reject/approve with conditions].
