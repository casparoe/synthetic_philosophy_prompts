You chair the risk-assessment committee of a development-finance institution that approves long-horizon infrastructure loans—primarily climate-adaptation projects with 30- to 50-year lifetimes, such as upgraded storm-water systems, relocated coastal utilities, or ecological retreat programs. You intend to operationalize Keynes’s framework on probability and uncertainty: the distinction between situations where rational, measurable probabilities can be assigned (risk) and situations where no objective basis exists for specific numerical probabilities (uncertainty), together with his concept of the weight of argument—the balance between the amount of relevant evidence available and the remaining ignorance—and his insistence that false precision must not be manufactured where the weight is low.

Design a reusable, written decision procedure—essentially a flowchart in words with explicit checkpoints—that your committee can apply to any proposed loan in this portfolio. The procedure must satisfy the following requirements.

First, classify every decision-relevant source of uncertainty into Keynesian risk or Keynesian uncertainty, using explicit boundary criteria, and mark a judgment-call box where the classification itself is genuinely debatable rather than algorithmic.

Second, for risk components: specify which quantitative tools are permitted (for example, historical-frequency analysis, structured expert elicitation, or Monte Carlo simulation), and require a transparency checklist that distinguishes data-driven parameters from judgment-driven ones.

Third, for uncertainty components: forbid the assignment of spurious point probabilities or false-precision expected values; instead, mandate an alternative safeguard—such as robustness criteria, staged commitments with mandatory exit clauses, option-value preservation, or scaled pilot structures—calibrated to the weight of argument.

Fourth, incorporate the weight of argument explicitly: define how the committee is to assess whether relevant evidence is substantial relative to ignorance, and state precisely how approval conditions (maximum loan exposure, collateral requirements, review-trigger frequency, and chair sign-off thresholds) must tighten as the weight decreases.

Fifth, identify three hard cases the procedure must handle, marking exactly where human judgment cannot be eliminated: (a) a project with abundant historical data but a clear structural break that undermines the frequency basis; (b) a project relying on novel technology supported by broad expert consensus but possessing almost no operational track record at scale; (c) a politically favored proposal with low weight of argument but intense pressure to approve rapidly.

Sixth, apply the procedure to three hypothetical loan proposals:
- Case A: A conventional seawall upgrade in a region with eight decades of storm-surge records and well-understood engineering practice.
- Case B: A first-of-its-kind integrated floating-solar and desalination complex for an arid coast, backed by strong engineering consensus but lacking any operational analog at proposed scale.
- Case C: A large-scale mangrove-restoration and managed-retreat program for a densely populated delta, where ecological dynamics are poorly parameterized, political stakeholders demand rapid approval, and the committee’s quantitative model outputs an attractive net-present-value figure that rests on an unverified carbon-price trajectory.

In Case C, the procedure must yield an answer that the asker will find unwelcome—presumably either rejection, a drastically scaled-down pilot with binding exit conditions, or approval only under safeguards that the political sponsors would consider unacceptable—and you must explain exactly which step of the procedure forces that conclusion.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: for instance, it cannot convert true uncertainty into risk by administrative decree, it cannot eliminate ambiguity about the weight of argument, and it cannot guarantee that judgment calls will be correct.

Constraints: The committee meets biweekly; members have quantitative training but are not philosophers of science; any deviation from the procedure requires the chair’s explicit written sign-off; and the deliverable must be practical enough for a compliance memo but precise enough that a careful reader of Keynes on probability would recognize the underlying distinctions.
