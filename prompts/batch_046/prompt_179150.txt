For an advanced philosophy-of-logic seminar, design a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions on whether true contradictions exist and how logic should respond, built from concrete commitments rather than named labels. Begin with a root question asking whether a contradiction can be literally true in any domain, phrased as a yes/no/qualified choice. Then branch through three specific contexts: semantic self-reference (e.g., a strengthened Liar-type construction), physical description (e.g., assigning incompatible precise properties within a single quantum measurement context), and institutional norm (e.g., a statute that unambiguously commands and forbids the same act without resolution rules). At each node, frame the split as a question answerable without knowing the view's standard name.

The tree should contain at least ten terminal nodes. Map occupied nodes onto named positions—such as global dialetheism, domain-restricted dialetheism, relevance-logical rejection of explosion, adaptive logic with premise withdrawal, classical revisionism denying one premise, and deflationary accounts treating contradictions as merely apparent—while marking genuinely unoccupied combinations as 'unnamed' and diagnosing each in one sentence (incoherent, redundant with another leaf, or conceptually unexplored). For every named leaf, include one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

Add a novel branching criterion near the leaves drawn from the physics-of-information angle: ask whether a contradiction arising specifically at the boundary between quantum-state descriptions and macroscopic measurement records should be read as ontic (in the world), representational (in our model), or pragmatic (in the speech-act of reporting). Use the answers to split leaves further, noting any positions this distinction cannot separate.

Finally, in a separate section, flag at least two pairs of leaves that remain indistinguishable within the tree despite being genuinely different positions, explaining briefly whether the overlap is in motivation, in meta-logical framework, or in scope of application. Specify that the audience is graduate students with basic logic training but no assumed familiarity with primary paraconsistent literature. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask the respondent to quote specific texts.
