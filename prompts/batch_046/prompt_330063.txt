I'm sorting out my philosophical profile for a small reading group on East Asian vs Western accounts of the person and I want to know if I'm being consistent or just cherry-picking. Roughly, I hold:

(a) The Confucian relational-self view gets the ontology right: I'm partly constituted by my social roles, not an atomic individual who just happens to have relationships.
(b) Even so, there's a kernel of intrinsick moral worth that doesn't disappear if all my ties are severed; it's not just a Western hangover, it does real normative work.
(c) Moral development is practice-embedded in the way MacIntyre describes: you learn virtue through participation in living traditions, not through detached reasoning.
(d) But I reject MacIntyre's conclusion that liberal modernity is basically incoherent; liberal institutions can protect people precisely because they recognize that kernel in (b).
(e) So in practice East Asian societies usually strike the right balance, whereas the West tends to overcorreects toward atomism.
(f) At the same time, I think the culture of mianzi and heavy social conformity in some East Asian contexts actually corrupts relationality by turning relationships into instrumental status performances.

Do these hang together? Is (a) compatible with (b) without reducing the relational self to a liberal core in disguise? Does accepting MacIntyre's virtue theory in (c) commit me to his anti-liberal politics in (d), or can I separate them? And does admitting (f) undermine my praise in (e), or is there a principled way to distinguish healthy relationality from performative face? Rank the real tensions by severity, flag which pairs only look incompatible, and say what the least costly single revision would be. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble, don't restate the question.
