As a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, I have settled on the following package of views about belief-attribution to artificial systems, and I want a consistency audit before I present it in a discussion group.

(1) I accept Dennett’s intentional stance as a purely predictive heuristic: attributing a belief is justified solely by improved forecasting accuracy, with no commitment to an inner mental reality.

(2) I deny that large language models possess Searlean original intentionality, but I allow that they may carry a form of derived intentionality provided the derivation chain runs through the model’s own learned weight structure rather than terminating only in the intentions of designers or users.

(3) I propose that warranted attribution to an AI should be governed by what I call teleonomic calibration: the attribution is legitimate only when it tracks stable, design-independent regularities in the system’s outputs across domains, analogous to attributing biological function without imputing conscious purpose.

(4) Even when (3) is satisfied, I hold that in ordinary discourse we ought to avoid saying that “the model believes p,” because the pragmatic risks of anthropomorphism outweigh the predictive gains, despite the instrumental warrant being technically present.

(5) I am an anti-eliminativist about human propositional attitudes—there genuinely are beliefs in people with mind-independent conditions of existence—whereas I think eliminativism should be the default stance toward artificial “beliefs,” with any attribution treated as a convenient fiction rather than a discovery.

(6) I maintain that the correct boundary for belief-attribution should depend on counterfactual robustness across contexts, not on biological substrate or on the presence of consciousness.

Please examine these commitments for tension. Which pairs genuinely conflict, which only appear to, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? Rank the tensions by severity, indicating for each what must give. I am especially uncertain whether the internalist flavor of (3) and (6) can coexist with the strictly pragmatic framework of (1), and whether the human-machine asymmetry in (5) survives the substrate-independent criterion advanced in (6).
