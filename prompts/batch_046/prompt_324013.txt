In our seminar on moral particularism versus generalism, Professor Vance has rendered verdicts on six case studies this term, and I am trying to reconstruct the implicit logic behind her judgments rather than merely catalog them. Her rulings have been consistent as follows: breaking a promise of medical confidentiality is permissible when it prevents imminent serious physical harm to an identifiable party, but impermissible when it merely averts social embarrassment; delaying the restitution of a cultural artifact is permissible when immediate return would expose it to destructive preservation conditions, but impermissible when the delay serves only institutional prestige; and a public official may misrepresent a procedural fact to avert a clearly catastrophic policy outcome, but not to secure a merely advantageous bureaucratic result.

For next week she has assigned a boundary case that introduces what she calls an asymmetry of visibility: an archivist is bound by a fifty-year seal on private letters donated by a family. After thirty years, she learns that selective excerpts—released not as direct quotations but through a strained yet technically defensible interpretive protocol she terms "redacted contextual material"—would correct a widespread false historical narrative about the donor's political role. The correction involves no prevention of tangible harm, only an epistemic repair; the family retains a legitimate privacy interest; and invoking the protocol does not technically violate the seal, though it clearly subverts its spirit.

Predict Vance's verdict on whether the archivist should invoke this protocol and release the excerpts. Identify at least two competing implicit rules—one generalist, one particularist—that are jointly consistent with all six prior judgments, specify what each predicts in this new case, and say which you think she will actually endorse and why. Then propose one further hypothetical case that would force a choice between these two rules. I am less interested in the ethical outcome itself than in whether her track record supports a threshold principle of serious-harm override, a holistic feature-matching view, or something closer to a principle about the moral salience of diffuse structural harm versus concentrated visible harm.
