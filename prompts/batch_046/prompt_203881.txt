You are writing the opening remarks for a retirement colloquium honouring Professor Elena Voss, who has spent thirty-five years simultaneously holding the chair in philosophy of logic and serving as the university’s chief academic registrar. The audience is split: roughly half are philosophers and logicians familiar with self-reference and formal systems, the other half are family, former students, and administrative staff with no technical background. The remarks should take about eight minutes and be delivered in British English.

The philosophical framework must do real structural work, not serve as decoration: Douglas Hofstadter’s conception of the self, specifically the ‘I’ as a strange loop — a self-referential symbol that arises within a system of symbols yet seems to point outside it. Because Voss’s career literally embodies this loop — she is the registrar whose own employment records, degrees, and retirement file are among the documents she has certified and archived — the speech must navigate a precise tension. It cannot invoke a ‘true self’ hidden behind her institutional roles, since on the strange-loop view there is no core self outside the symbol-system; yet it also must not reduce her to pure administrative data, since that would collapse the loop into flat self-reference and erase the causal vortex that makes the symbol meaningful.

Inject the following niche conceptual twist and treat it as operative: the idea that every strange loop requires what we might call an ‘external anchor’ — a non-self-referential substrate (here, the physical archive, the signatures of others, the embodied memory of colleagues) that prevents the loop from spinning into empty solipsism. The remarks should therefore acknowledge that Voss’s ‘I’ as registrar-philosopher has always depended on these anchors: the paper files she did not write about herself, the students whose records she kept but who also kept her accountable, the family members present who know her outside the symbol-system of the university.

Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not quote verbatim from Hofstadter’s texts unless you have fetched the exact passage; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. The remarks should be accessible to the non-philosophers in the room while containing at least one precise, technically coherent reference that the philosophers will recognise — for instance, the distinction between a symbol that denotes and a strange loop that denotes itself — but explain it quickly enough that family members are not left behind. Avoid generic retirement platitudes about legacy or watching over us from somewhere; instead, let the strange-loop structure determine exactly what can and cannot be said about her departure.
