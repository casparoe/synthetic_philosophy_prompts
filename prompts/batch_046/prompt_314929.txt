I'd like you to design a structured classroom game or simulation that makes participants feel the force of the idea behind Milton Friedman's 'F-twist'—that deliberately false premises in a model can be a strength rather than a weakness if predictive accuracy is high.

Please adopt the role of an expert philosopher of science with experience in science education. Think carefully, begin with brief brainstorming on how engineering intuitions about abstraction and overfitting can stage this tension, and then present the complete activity in British English.

The deliverable should be a runnable activity, not a lecture. Include:
1. Setting and group structure (16 people, workshop room, 90 minutes).
2. A framing task that avoids naming the philosophical point until afterwards: teams must predict system failures using either a 'high-fidelity' ruleset (many realistic parameters, noisy data) or a 'stripped' ruleset (absurd simplifications like infinite memory and zero latency).
3. Rules, roles, and incentives arranged so that the stripped-ruleset teams consistently outperform the others, including a hidden 'black-swan' stress event that the realistic model misses due to overfitting local noise.

7. An honest note on which feature of the F-twist idea this activity cannot reproduce—namely, the normative question of whether we should deploy knowingly false models in ethically consequential domains—and a clear instruction that the facilitator must supply this point verbally.

Keep it focused and practical. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid asking for obscure theories or named philosophers beyond Friedman. Use British spelling and phrasing throughout.
