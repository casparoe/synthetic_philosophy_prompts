I'm working through a course on conservative political philosophy and have been trying to map Chesterton's fence onto Burke's theory of prescription. The heuristic is familiar: do not tear down a fence until you know why it was erected, because its purpose may be hidden but real—whether to stop erosion, contain animals, or mark a boundary whose loss would invite chaos. A classmate applied this directly to Burke's argument for inherited institutions, proposing that social arrangements—common law, representative bodies, religious establishments—function like such fences. Their claim is that radical reformers suffer from an epistemic deficit: they see only an obstacle and miss the accumulated practical knowledge embedded in the institution, just as someone tearing down a fence misses its hidden hydrological function. The analogy, they say, converts Burkean conservatism from mere nostalgia into a theory of institutional knowledge—prescription is justified not because old things are good, but because the reasons for their existence may exceed our present capacity to articulate them.

I want to audit this analogy carefully. What corresponds precisely to the fence, its builder, the hidden purpose, and the act of removal? Does the source domain import features that the target lacks—such as the fence being a deliberately constructed artifact with a single, recoverable design intention, whereas institutions often emerge from conflict, accident, and layered compromise rather than from a single architect? Where does the mapping break down when we consider that institutions, unlike wooden fences, are dynamic processes that change continuously, and that their builders are long dead or anonymous, making the recovery of purpose impossible in principle rather than merely difficult? Most importantly, does the Burkean conclusion—that there is a strong presumption against dismantling inherited arrangements—survive once these disanalogies are laid out? Or does Chesterton's heuristic, properly restricted to artifacts with recoverable functions, collapse into a much weaker cautionary principle that does not uniquely support Burkean conservatism over, say, a pragmatic gradualism that permits extensive renovation once the hidden functions are mapped?
