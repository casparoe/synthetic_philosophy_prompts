I'm planning a single fifty-minute discussion section for first-year philosophy students who have just spent a week on correspondence theories of truth through Plato's allegory of the cave and the basic intuition that a true belief somehow "matches" reality. Now I want them to understand deflationary theories — minimalism and pure disquotationalism — not as the claim that truth does not exist, but as the claim that it is not a substantive metaphysical property. I need the teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

Lay out the dependency order: what intuition about truth must be in place before deflationism can appear as a response rather than a denial, and what distinction — between possessing a property and performing a linguistic act — must precede what. Choose one carrying example that is not "snow is white"; it must include both a vivid correspondence pull and an obvious case where adding "it is true that" changes nothing descriptively, and specify what the example must contain and what it must avoid. Then map the misconceptions in the order students will form them: first, that deflationism removes the difference between true and false statements; second, that it collapses into relativism; third, that it cannot explain why truth matters in moral and political disagreement. For each, state the exact point in the arc where it should be intercepted and with what move. Provide one end-of-section check that distinguishes a student who grasps deflationism as a metaphysical thesis about truth from one who thinks it is merely a claim about language use. Finally, state explicitly what technical apparatus should be named but not developed — the formal disquotational schema, distinctions among minimalists and pure disquotationalists, and the machinery for semantic paradoxes — and explain how leaving these undeveloped protects rather than weakens the core idea.
