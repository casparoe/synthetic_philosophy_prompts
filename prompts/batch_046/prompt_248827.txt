Design a 75-minute workshop activity for a philosophy seminar of 12 students (mixed upper-level undergraduate and graduate) on whether an “I” can be distributed across brains, taking Hofstadter’s pattern-persistence view of the self as the framing idea but requiring no prior reading of any specific text. The activity should make participants experience the pull of treating personal identity as a replicable pattern of cognitive and affective traits that can migrate across substrates, and then make them encounter a structural resistance that is not merely sentimental—before either position is named by the facilitator. To satisfy this, inject an invented constraint into the rules: call it the indexical residue clause, which holds that every original brain retains an irreducible, non-transferable pointer of first-person ownership regardless of how completely its cognitive patterns have been copied elsewhere. Arrange the roles, rounds, and incentives so that participants discover the asymmetry between content-transfer and ownership-transfer through play rather than lecture.

Specify the exact pairing or grouping structure, a round-by-round timeline with concrete actions (not open discussion), and the materials required—such as profile cards, migration tokens, or written judgment slips. Include a non-monetary payoff or consistency-scoring scheme that creates real stakes for coherence: for example, a shared group score that rewards pairs for maintaining logically compatible persistence judgments across rounds, with visible penalties for contradictions that the group must resolve collectively. The debrief should map specific moments in the play—such as the round in which all content cards have moved but one partner still reports subjective presence—onto features of the patternist view and onto the resistance introduced by the indexical residue clause.

In the debrief, also include an honest admission of which feature of the distributed-self idea the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the long-term temporal stability of a pattern sustained across radically different physical substrates over months or years—and instruct the facilitator on how to supply that missing element verbally in the final ten minutes without undermining the experiential point of the exercise. Finally, identify two failures that will predictably occur on the first run—such as participants treating the hidden ownership pointer as a physical object rather than a conceptual placeholder, or allowing emotional loyalty to the original partner to drive strategic voting rather than philosophical judgment—and explain precisely how the facilitator should handle each without derailing the mapping between game dynamics and the conceptual argument.
