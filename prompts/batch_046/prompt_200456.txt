I'm serving on the examination committee for our doctoral programme's historiography module, and I'm trying to design a comprehensive essay prompt that will test whether candidates actually understand the conceptual architecture of the objectivity debate rather than simply reproducing the familiar Ranke-versus-Carr-and-Hayden-White storyline they've encountered in survey courses. In particular, I've been discussing with a colleague the importance of distinguishing representational objectivity—the idea that a historical narrative can be evidentially constrained to correspond with past events—from procedural objectivity, which concerns whether the methods of inquiry are intersubjectively regulated in ways that filter out individual bias, regardless of whether the resulting account is literally correct in every detail. I think this distinction is rarely made explicit in the standard literature, yet it is precisely what is needed to see whether Carr's emphasis on the historian's present concerns and White's tropological analysis of emplotment undermine Ranke's aspiration to show how things actually happened in both dimensions, or only in one, and whether any defensible residue of objectivity survives.

Please draft a single essay question suitable for a PhD-level comprehensive exam that candidates could answer in approximately ninety minutes. The question should ask them to deploy the representational-versus-procedural distinction to evaluate the scope of Carr's and White's scepticism about Ranke's project, and to determine what kind of objectivity—if any—remains available to historical practice once both presentism and narrative form are taken seriously. The prompt should not ask for bibliographic lists, page citations, or the reproduction of publication dates; it should assume familiarity with the primary arguments but reward conceptual precision rather than summary. Specify any length or format constraints within the question text itself.

Alongside the question, provide a model answer that makes the required conceptual moves explicit, and include a marking scheme built around five must-hit points that together total twenty marks. For each point, state the specific conceptual move a top answer must make, the marks it carries, and a one-line note on the characteristic error—especially the conflation of Carr's presentist critique with White's narrativist critique, or the assumption that procedural safeguards automatically restore representational fidelity.
