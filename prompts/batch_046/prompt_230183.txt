I think I've convinced myself that Buddhist ethics isn't consequentialist about the world but about your own mental stream. The view is that karma operates through cetana — intention — but the result (phala) isn't an external reward, it's the storage of a karmic seed (bija) in the alayavijnana that reshapes your future dispositions. So moral evaluation tracks internal-state outcomes: action plus intention produces mental transformation. The weird part is that in some Abhidharma frameworks there's no stable self recieveing this, just continuity of seeds. So it's consequentialist about mental states with no subject. I'm not asking for a literature review, just: what would actually count against this? Which arguments or conceptual problems would show Buddhist ethics really does care about worldly results, or that the seed-model doesn't work that way? And is there a hidden contradiction in being consequentialist about a process that has no agent? I want an honest map of where this falls apart — what's fatal, what's merely awkward, and what a defender of this view would themselves concede is a real problem — not just the usual "Buddhists value compassion" reply.
