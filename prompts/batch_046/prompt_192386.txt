For an independent research project I am conducting at the high school level, I have been reading about Max Weber’s argument that certain Protestant values helped shape the economic behavior behind modern capitalism. In working through the secondary literature and some of the original essays, I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure that I cannot find a precise label for, and I am hoping to determine whether it is a recognized problem in the philosophy of social science.

The structure appears whenever a scholar wants to treat a cultural value system as an independent cause of a large-scale economic or institutional change. First, the value system is said to motivate behavior in a distinctive way — for instance, that the Calvinist emphasis on predestination encouraged rational accumulation and deferred gratification rather than consumption. Second, when a critic points out that similar religious outlooks existed elsewhere without producing comparable economic results, the scholar responds that the necessary material conditions were not yet present to activate those values. Third, when the critic reverses the point and observes that those material conditions sometimes arose in societies lacking the corresponding values, the scholar replies that without the values present, the conditions could not be directed or sustained toward that particular outcome. The result is a pairing in which culture and material conditions are each presented as necessary for the other, with the absence of either one always explained by the absence of its partner. I have seen versions of this not only in discussions of Weber’s Protestant ethic — comparing Geneva, parts of the Netherlands, and regions of Catholic Europe with similar merchant structures — but also in contemporary explanations that invoke an entrepreneurial culture to account for technological clusters, where capital availability is said to depend on cultural risk-tolerance and that risk-tolerance is said to depend on capital availability.

My question is whether this specific loop — not merely correlation or interaction, but a symmetrical explanatory dependency in which each factor’s failure is accounted for by the other factor’s absence, so that neither can be tested in isolation — has an established name in methodological critiques of Weber or in the philosophy of history. I am aware of Weber’s own idea of an elective affinity between religious ideas and economic interests, which describes a kind of mutual suitability rather than this defensive circularity. I am also aware of ordinary circular reasoning and of dialectical models that grant priority to one side over time. What I am after is the specific concept, if one exists, for this closed pairing that preserves both causes by making each one’s absence conditional on the other. If there is no standard term, I would like a clear definition that distinguishes it from a genuine interaction effect — in which two variables genuinely require one another but remain in principle separable — and from simple post-hoc rationalization. I am not looking for a reading list; I want to understand what the phenomenon is, where its boundaries lie, and whether the literature treats it as a fallacy, a methodological artifact, or a legitimate feature of historical explanation.
