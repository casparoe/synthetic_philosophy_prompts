A moral philosopher—call her Dr. V—has a public record of judgments about when comparing historical atrocities is legitimate scholarship versus obscene instrumentalization. Her rulings are: comparing the Holocaust and the Cambodian genocide to ask which was “worse” is obscene, because it treats victims as fungible units on a suffering scoreboard; comparing the Holodomor and the 1943 Bengal famine to study state-induced famine dynamics is legitimate, because the comparison serves structural explanation; comparing the Rwandan genocide with the Srebrenica massacre for scale analysis is permissible, but ranking them by cumulative suffering is obscene; comparing the Atlantic slave trade’s overall mortality with that of the Middle Passage alone is a false isolation that obscures systemic violence, so she rejects it, yet she approves comparing the economic extraction mechanisms of chattel slavery with those of the Belgian Congo rubber regime for structural analysis; and comparing Hiroshima with the Dresden firebombing as strategic-military cases is legitimate, but asking which civilian population “deserved” death less crosses into obscenity.

She now faces a comparison she has not addressed: an analysis that ranks the 20th-century forced assimilation of Indigenous children in residential school systems (Canada, Australia, United States) against 19th-century removal policies such as the Trail of Tears, not by mortality or direct violence, but by what the author terms “epistemic rupture”—the degree to which the policy destroyed intergenerational knowledge transmission—while explicitly refusing to aggregate suffering metrics or claim one was “worse” overall.

Predict Dr. V’s verdict on whether this comparison is legitimate or obscene, and identify the principle or principles actually governing her prior rulings: is the distinction about explanatory purpose versus competitive ranking, about the fungibility of victims, about whether the comparator belongs to the compared groups, about the availability of non-comparative descriptions, or about the metric’s susceptibility to zero-sum framing? State which rule best fits her record, recommend what she should say for consistency, and name the single further comparison case that would best discriminate the leading rules. Give your answer as a committed judgment—take a definite position on her implicit rule, give a definite recommendation for the residential-school case with explicit reasoning, and do not merely catalog possibilities. End with an honest note on the weakest point in your own interpretation of her track record.
