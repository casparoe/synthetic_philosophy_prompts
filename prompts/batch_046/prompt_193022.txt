As a doctoral student in epistemology, I've been trying to map the precise conceptual architecture of the dogmatism paradox in a way that doesn't simply rehearse the standard Meno-style formulation. The familiar version runs roughly like this: if I genuinely know that p, then I must regard any apparently disconfirming evidence e as misleading; but if I allow that e might genuinely defeat p, then I never really knew p to begin with, since knowledge seems incompatible with the possibility of error. Most discussions treat this as a direct clash between the stability of knowledge and the norms of rational belief revision. What I find underexplored is whether the paradox genuinely depends on treating knowledge as an all-or-nothing binary state, or whether it persists if we model knowledge instead as a high-credence fixed point within a broader doxastic system—one that is structurally insulated from local perturbation but remains globally revisable.

More specifically, I want to understand the interaction between propositional justification and doxastic justification in this context. Suppose we distinguish the epistemic status of p itself (its being well-supported and true) from the rationality of maintaining belief in p upon encountering e. The dogmatist appears to conflate these: she takes the stability of p's propositional status to license an inflexible doxastic posture, dismissing e not because she has independently assessed it, but because revising p would violate the conditions under which she counts as knowing it. But is there a coherent intermediate position—perhaps what we might call "defeasible stability"—where maintaining belief in p despite e is rational not because e is automatically disqualified, but because the epistemic cost of globally restructuring one's belief system exceeds the local evidential weight of e, given p's central structural role? Or is this merely a redescription of dogmatism dressed in decision-theoretic language?

Please explain, with conceptual precision rather than technical proof, whether the paradox can be dissolved by separating knowledge's structural role from doxastic inflexibility, and whether admitting that knowledge allows for such defeasible stability forces us to abandon the intuition that knowing p requires being unable to rationally doubt it. I'm particularly interested in whether this framing reveals the paradox to be an artifact of conflating the closure of knowledge under entailment with the inertia of belief revision, or whether a genuine tension remains even once those are pulled apart.
