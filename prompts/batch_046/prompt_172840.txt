For a 90-minute upper-level seminar of 14 students on Marx’s theory of exploitation and alienation, design a structured, two-phase production game—call it “The Tower Protocol”—that makes participants feel the difference between formal and real subsumption of labor under capital before any theoretical label is applied. The group should include 10 workers, 2 capitalists, and 2 rotating observers. Provide a precise timeline, material list, and token-based payoff structure, and specify what participants should come away seeing: that exploitation is not merely low wages but a structural extraction of surplus value, and that alienation operates in four dimensions (product, process, species-being, and other workers), with the crucial shift being that capital first appropriates surplus through contract while leaving the labor process intact, then eventually redesigns the process itself so that alienation becomes embedded in the method of work.

In Phase 1 (Formal Subsumption, 25 minutes): Workers receive identical kits of index cards, tape, and markers and are told to build freestanding towers that meet a visible, minimal standard—height over 15 cm, self-supporting for 10 seconds. They may organize themselves, divide tasks, talk freely, and choose tools. The two capitalists set a fixed piece-rate—3 tokens per approved tower—and “sell” each tower to the facilitator for 8 tokens, pocketing the 5-token surplus without initially revealing the resale price to workers. At the end of the round, workers count wages and capitalists display surplus.

In Phase 2 (Real Subsumption, 30 minutes): The capitalists announce an “efficiency reform.” They introduce a mandatory, silent assembly-line protocol: each worker is assigned exactly one repetitive action (folding, taping, coloring, or stacking), paced by a 45-second bell, with no cross-communication permitted. The visible quality standard is replaced by a hidden template—towers must match an unseen color-sequence and dimension card held only by capitalists—so workers submit towers for inspection, face arbitrary rejections without explanation, and must redo the work for no additional tokens. The piece-rate is cut to 2 tokens, justified by “higher productivity,” and capitalists now control all tool distribution. Capitalists also accumulate “control points” for every protocol deviation they penalize. The surplus is calculated as before, but now the extraction is visibly coupled to a redesigned labor process.

The two observers record behavior on a shared board across four categories mapped to Marx’s dimensions of alienation: separation from the product (hidden standard, unexplained rejection), from the labor process (assigned single action, bell pacing), from fellow workers (enforced silence and division), and from species-being (repetition without creative input or recognition).

After both phases, run a 25-minute debrief. First, reveal the token ledgers: workers compare wages across phases, capitalists display surplus and control points. Ask workers to describe, without using theoretical terms, what changed in their experience of the work itself—not just the pay. Only then introduce formal and real subsumption: map Phase 1 to capital appropriating surplus through contract while the labor process remains under worker control, and Phase 2 to capital restructuring the process so that exploitation is embedded in the method and alienation becomes structural rather than incidental. Map specific moments—the bell, the hidden template, tool requests, silent repetition—onto the four alienation dimensions.

Include an honest failure forecast: workers often resist the protocol (breaking silence, sharing tools, or sabotaging the hidden standard), which is pedagogically valuable but must be named as resistance rather than system failure; some capitalists feel moral discomfort and redistribute tokens, which breaks the structural logic and should be noted without shaming, since the activity models structural positions, not personal morality. Finally, be explicit about what the game cannot reproduce: the macroeconomic coercion that forces capitalists to accumulate, the historical development of machinery that drives real subsumption, and the ideological naturalization of these relations. The facilitator must supply that this is an analog of a structural tendency, not an inevitable law, and that the hidden template stands in for capital’s design authority rather than a literal market mechanism.

Arrange the rules, roles, and payoffs so that the central problem—extraction deepening through process redesign—emerges from the incentives and constraints themselves, not from the facilitator’s framing after the fact.
