In my philosophy-of-mind working group, a debate about the ethical implications of panpsychism has reached a standstill that feels less like disagreement and more like two monologues running in parallel. The metaphysical premise shared by both sides is that fundamental physical entities—quantum fields, elementary particles, or the basic structures of spacetime—instantiate some form of minimal phenomenal or proto-experiential property. From this, one faction derives what they call a distributive suffering problem: if each fundamental unit possesses even a vanishingly thin quality of experience, then any physical process that disturbs its state—thermal decoherence, gravitational compression, or the noise of vacuum fluctuations—constitutes a micro-harm. They argue that because the number of such entities is astronomically large, panpsychism implies a cosmic scale of suffering that renders ordinary biological ethics almost trivial by comparison, and that this should motivate a kind of ethical paralysis about interacting with physical reality at all. The opposing faction accepts the same metaphysical premise but denies the ethical inference entirely. They maintain that suffering is not merely phenomenal but architecturally constrained: it requires something like integrated informational closure, a unified subject of experience, or at least a non-equilibrium organizational dynamics that fundamental physics, operating in near-thermal equilibrium, simply does not exhibit. For them, distributed micro-experience is ethically inert—closer to background noise than to harm—so panpsychism carries no special moral burden beyond standard physical ethics.

My own hypothesis is that the stall is not about empirical physics, nor even about ethics in any familiar sense, but about a hidden disagreement over the compositional logic of suffering itself—whether suffering is an aggregative quantity that can be summed across disunified micro-subjects, or whether it is a structurally emergent property that requires a specific kind of unity absent from fundamental fields. Yet I worry this diagnosis may be too neat. It is possible that the sides are not disagreeing about a single hidden question at all, but are instead answering different questions under one heading: one about the metaphysical distribution of experience across physical substrates, the other about the conditions for moral patiency and harm. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, turning on ambiguous uses of “suffering,” “subject,” or “harm”? Is it substantive but masked, concealing an empirical claim about integration thresholds or equilibrium dynamics that neither side can presently defend? Or does it rest on bedrock intuitions about scale, naturalness, and the moral significance of organization that function as unargued starting points? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the tension, a formal result about information integration in physical systems, a reframing that abandons aggregation altogether, or an honest argument that the question is ill-posed and should be left aside?
