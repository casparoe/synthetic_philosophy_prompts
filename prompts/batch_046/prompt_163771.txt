I have been thinking about the old saying that family wealth rarely survives three generations—shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves—and about how people use it to argue either that inheritance taxes are unnecessary because dynasties dissolve on their own, or that they are urgent precisely because they interrupt that dissolution. I want to examine this tension through a real case rather than an abstraction: the Vanderbilt fortune, built by Cornelius Vanderbilt in railroads and shipping in the nineteenth century, enlarged by his son William Henry, and then largely dissipated over the next generations through lavish spending, division among dozens of heirs, and weak investment, so that by the middle of the twentieth century no Vanderbilt ranked among the very wealthy even though the family name still carried social prestige and institutional access. I want to analyze this episode through the lens of equal-opportunity theory, but with a specific framing I keep returning to: the distinction between financial inheritance, meaning the actual capital that can be taxed or divided, and structural inheritance, meaning the social networks, educational credentials, and institutional credibility that may persist long after the bank accounts shrink. The theory holds that large inheritances distort fair competition; the Vanderbilt story seems to show the financial part can vanish. So I want a careful, disciplined mapping. Who are the actors—the original builders, the spending heirs, the later generations who retained status without comparable wealth? What counts as the unfair advantage in each generation? Does the case suggest that the heirs faced a kind of dynastic moral hazard, where unearned status discouraged the development of productive skills? Does the eventual loss of capital show that the initial distortion was self-correcting, or does it hide the fact that structural advantages kept even “poor” Vanderbilts ahead of people with no family name? And where does the 1916 federal estate tax fit—arriving after much of the division had already occurred—does its absence in the critical decades show a missed opportunity, or does the Vanderbilt trajectory suggest that taxing capital is the wrong tool if the deeper problem is structural? I want you to say what the equal-opportunity framework explains well about the spending patterns, the division of the estate, and the persistence of the name; what it misdescribes or is silent about; and whether the episode ultimately supports, undermines, or reveals a blind spot in the argument that inheritance taxes are necessary for genuine equality of opportunity.
