I'm putting together a handout for an epistemology reading group — we're trying to figure out where people actually stand on higher-order evidence and that weird state where you think P but also think you shouldn't think P (epistemic akrasia, I think it's called?). I want a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts real positions by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they like.

Start with this split: you've got strong first-order evidence for some claim, but you also have higher-order evidence that your reasoning is unreliable right now — say you just realized you've been working through a proof while running a high fever, or you notice that everyone you respect disagrees with you on this exact point. First branch: does the higher-order evidence always require you to revise downward, or can it be rational to hold steady? Then split the 'always revise' branch by whether revision means full suspension of belief or just lowering confidence a bit. For the 'hold steady' side, split by whether you think it's okay to stay steady only when you can pinpoint exactly where the unreliable mechanism went wrong, or whether you can stay steady even when you can't.

Then I want a separate axis (maybe cross-cutting) on akrasia itself: is it ever rational to be in that split mental state — believing P while believing that your belief in P is irrational? Or is that always a kind of cognitive malfunction? And please include where peer disagreement lands: conciliationists vs. steadfast people, but phrase it as 'if you learn that epistemic peers disagree, do you treat that as defeating evidence about your own reasoning, or just as information about them?' Make each leaf a named position if one exists, or 'unnamed' if nobody holds it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — like maybe a view that lets you stay steadfast about peer disagreement but forces you to suspend belief in the fever case, and whether that combination is even coherant. Flag any pairs your tree can't seperate, especially if some view treats self-undermining belief differently from peer-undermining belief. Don't give me a bibliography; I just want the map, and I'm mostly interested in how the tree handles cases where the higher-order doubt comes from inside your own head versus from outside.
