I have been working through an argument that ties Weber's classic definition of the state to the current debate over policing—reform, defund, or abolish—and I worry that it proves far more than any of those positions can accept. The argument runs in eight steps. (1) By Weber's formulation, a state is the human community that successfully claims a monopoly on legitimate physical force within a given territory. (2) This monopoly is not merely legal or symbolic but effective: in practice no other actor routinely exercises coercive authority over residents without being subordinate to the state's authorization. (3) Policing is therefore not one public service among others but the institutional form through which this effective monopoly is continuously maintained—detecting violations, apprehending suspects, and enforcing court orders. (4) Abolishing police removes the institution that performs this function, and because no other institution automatically assumes it, either law enforcement collapses or it is taken up by private actors such as neighborhood militias, corporate security forces, or informal community councils. (5) If those private actors take it up without being revocably authorized by the state, they become competing authorities and the state's claim to monopolize coercive force is false; if no one takes it up, the state can no longer enforce its laws, which also undermines its claim to effective authority. (6) Therefore abolishing police dissolves the state's defining feature. (7) But the same reasoning applies to reform or defunding: if reform strips police of coercive powers, or if defunding reduces them to functional incapacity, then the state again lacks an effective mechanism to suppress private coercion. (8) Hence any policy—abolition, defunding, or deep reform—that impairs the police apparatus undermines state legitimacy itself. I find steps (1) through (3) hard to surrender, yet (8) clearly proves too much, since it would condemn even modest budget adjustments. I want you to tell me exactly which numbered step is the weakest link, explain why that step initially looked plausible, and then price the alternatives: what must the reformist, the defund advocate, and the abolitionist each concede or argue in order to reject that step. In particular, please address whether rejecting it requires accepting what I will call the authorization-execution split—the idea that a state can monopolize the legitimate authorization of force while allowing its direct execution to be distributed among revocably licensed, non-uniformed, non-state actors. Write your response as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a single sustained letter or memorandum.
