As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of logic, I want to understand dialetheism and paraconsistent logic not through abstract formalisms but through the conceptual vocabulary of my former work: redundant sensors yielding contradictory readings, calibration drift, instrument noise, outlier-rejection protocols, and the choice between revising a theoretical model and attributing conflict to malfunction. Restate the core commitments of dialetheism—specifically that some contradictions are true rather than merely apparent—and of paraconsistent logic—specifically that contradiction does not entail arbitrary conclusions—using only that experimental vocabulary, mapping logical relations (contradictory, contrary, subcontrary) onto corresponding experimental situations as precisely as possible. Then provide the residue report: which philosophical claims translate cleanly into scientific practice, which require distortion to fit, and which have no counterpart at all, particularly the metaphysical claim that reality itself can be inconsistent. Finally, assess whether the philosophical dispute tracks a genuine divergence in how scientists ought to treat anomalous data, or whether it imports commitments that have no operational meaning in the laboratory.
