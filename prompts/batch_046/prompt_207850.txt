Next week's guest is a philosopher who defends Daniel Dennett's conception of the self as a center of narrative gravity—the idea that the self is not an inner substance but a fictional construct around which our experiences and actions organize, much as a center of gravity organizes the dynamics of a physical object without being itself a physical part. I want to move well beyond introductory exposition, so please draft six to eight interview questions that treat this view with real philosophical pressure rather than generic curiosity.

Specifically, craft the sequence as a committed skeptical engagement: take a definite stance that the gravitational metaphor risks smuggling in an unacknowledged reification, and press on how the view distinguishes genuine narrative integration from mere confabulation in clinical cases of dissociative identity disorder, where multiple centers seem to coexist. Include at least one question that anticipates the standard reply—that narrative coherence is graded rather than all-or-nothing—and follow it immediately with a sharper probe about whether graded coherence can still ground moral responsibility or personal identity over time. I also want a question contrasting this constructed view with pre-reflective self-awareness as described in phenomenological traditions, framed without citation of specific texts. Finally, conclude with a direct question asking what empirical finding—perhaps in clinical neuroscience or in artificial systems generating sustained autobiographical narratives—would count as decisive evidence against the position, together with your reasoning for why that particular finding would be damaging rather than merely puzzling. Please phrase everything in American English, avoid any request for bibliographies or reading lists, and keep the tone formal and polished.
