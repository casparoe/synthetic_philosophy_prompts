As a retired field ecologist who spent four decades observing phenotypic variation in wild populations and who has only recently begun studying philosophy of biology, I am trying to understand why the debate over adaptationism remains so intractable. I am taking an informal course on evolutionary explanation, and I keep encountering a specific sticking point that my classmates and I have begun calling the modular decoupling problem: Trait B is structurally necessary to support Trait A, which is clearly adaptive, yet B is itself complex and serves multiple apparent functions, leaving it genuinely unclear whether B's sub-features were independently selected or are instead indivisible architectural residues of A's construction. To move beyond circular discussion, please draft a joint adversarial-collaboration statement, written in American English, that could in principle be signed by two opposing camps. Camp One is a rigorous adaptationist who holds that natural selection is the default and primary explanation for phenotypic complexity and that any structural entanglement should be treated as a hidden adaptation until empirically disproven. Camp Two is a Gould-Lewontin-style pluralist who insists that spandrels, exaptations, and developmental constraints explain substantial portions of organismal design and that structural residues are often genuinely non-adaptive rather than merely undiscovered adaptations.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims about evolutionary explanation in general, about the existence of spandrels and exaptation in principle, and specifically about the modular decoupling problem as defined above. It should then present the precise disagreements in wording that each camp would accept for its own position. Identify the central cruxes—particularly whether structural necessity can ever be empirically separated from functional optimization, and whether evolutionary reversibility or experimental decoupling of B's sub-features can adjudicate the dispute. For each crux, specify a concrete biological case or argument both sides would agree bears on it—such as the evolutionary history of mammalian middle-ear ossicles derived from jaw articulation, or a comparable synthetic analog—together with each camp's predicted interpretation of that evidence. Conclude with an honest assessment of which feature of the case performs the actual argumentative work, what happens if that feature is removed or altered, and how the camp that comes out poorly would attack the framing. Please avoid bibliographies, citation requests, or appeals to highly obscure theoretical apparatus that a well-read but non-specialist model could not reasonably reconstruct from standard knowledge.
