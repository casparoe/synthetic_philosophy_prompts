I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who spent thirty years working on variational problems with hard and soft constraints, and over the past two years I've moved into philosophy of language and translation theory. I'm applying for a six-month independent-study grant to develop a focused research proposal—intended eventually as a monograph chapter—on translation under severe formal constraint, using the classic case of rendering Clément Marot's sixteenth-century poem into English while preserving rhyme scheme, meter, register, and semantic content.

The precise question is this: when a translator generates dozens of candidate versions and selects one, does the selection reveal a hidden hierarchical ordering among semantic, phonetic, and syntactic constraints—analogous to the distinction between non-negotiable conservation laws and adjustable boundary conditions in physical systems—or does it instead demonstrate that these constraints are genuinely incommensurable, leaving the choice irreducibly underdetermined?

Existing accounts leave this open. Functional-equivalence theorists treat form as a dispensable vehicle that can be traded away for semantic fidelity, which cannot explain why translators reject versions that preserve meaning but violate meter. Formalist accounts treat every constraint as absolute, which cannot explain why translators routinely accept minor semantic shifts to save a rhyme. Neither framework captures the iterative, rejective process by which actual practitioners navigate the space. I want to formalize that space and determine whether viable translations cluster in discrete, non-comparable islands or form a continuous convex region where trade-offs are systematically ordered.

My approach is conceptual analysis aided by formal modeling, not empirical data collection. Month one: catalog the explicit and implicit constraints operating in the well-known English renderings—rhyme, syllable count, emotional valence, syntactic parallelism, and register consistency. Month two: construct a multi-dimensional representation of each candidate as a point in constraint space, using information-theoretic measures to quantify semantic preservation relative to formal fidelity. Month three: analyze whether rejected versions violate only "soft" constraints, or whether they violate combinations that admit no consistent weighting scheme. Months four through six: determine whether a minimal set of hard constraints partitions the solution space, or whether no stable hierarchy exists.

A successful result would be either (a) identification of a principled distinction between hard and soft constraints in this domain, with an account of why the hard ones are non-negotiable, or (b) a rigorous demonstration that no consistent ordering exists and that translator choice is therefore structurally underdetermined. The project fails if the formalization collapses into arbitrary weight assignment, or if the constraint space proves too unstructured to support any philosophical conclusion beyond "it depends on taste."

This scope is realistic for six months: it covers one bounded case study in depth but explicitly excludes extension to other languages or poetic forms, which I will flag as necessary follow-on work. I am not requesting bibliographic resources; I will supply citations myself. What I need is evaluation of whether this framing—importing the hard-constraint/soft-constraint distinction from physics into translation theory—yields a genuinely new angle on an old problem, or whether it merely dresses familiar literary intuitions in unnecessary formalism.
