I write about the philosophy of science and its overlap with predictive social science. I'm working on a feature about Karl Popper's argument in The Poverty of Historicism — that there can be no scientific laws of history because the growth of knowledge is itself an unpredictable historical force. Rather than another retrospective, I want to identify which open conceptual questions and live lines of research growing out of this critique actually matter now, especially in light of renewed attempts at macrohistorical forecasting, whether statistical, structural-demographic, or algorithmic. If you were advising a small interdisciplinary team — philosophers, theorists, and modelers — with about three years of funding and no obligation to produce empirical datasets, which three problems or implications should they prioritize, and in what order? Defend the ranking: for each, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is without resolving Popper's entire epistemology, and what kind of conceptual or formal result over the next few years would cause you to revise its place. I'm particularly interested in whether Popper's anti-historicist argument applies with equal force to deterministic laws versus probabilistic or pattern-based predictions, and whether that distinction has been adequately developed. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need reasoning that stands on its own for readers without access to specialized libraries.
