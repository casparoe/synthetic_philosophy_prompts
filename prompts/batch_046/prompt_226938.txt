Hey, I've been playing with an argument about Tolstoy's What Is Art and now I think its implodes, but I can't spot the leak.

(1) Tolstoy defines art as emotional infection: the artist genuinely feels something and transmits it to others through external signs.
(2) He also claims that if the feeling is exclusive to one class—like the aristocratic pleasure he says Shakespeare and Beethoven pander to—then it's counterfeit art, not real art.
(3) So genuine art must transmit a feeling that is in principle accessible to anyone, regardless of education or background.
(4) But every actual artwork relies on specific symbols, languages, or conventions; even a folk song assumes you know the melody and the social context.
(5) By (3) and (4), even Tolstoy's own praised examples—folk tales, the parables he admires, and yes even his novel Resurrection with all its legal and theological references—fail the universality test and count as counterfeit.
(6) So either Tolstoy is wrong that those works are art, or his distinction between real and counterfeit art collapses.
(7) The only rescue is to weaken (3) and say the feeling can be universal even if the packaging needs context.
(8) But then counterfeit art just means art that infects a smaller audience, and the whole taxonomy becomes a popularity contest.

Im inclined to save (1) and (2), but I feel like (4) is doing sneeky work—maybe it confuses the particularity of the feeling with the particularity of the vehicle. What's the cost of separating content from packaging here?
