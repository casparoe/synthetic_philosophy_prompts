As a journalist covering decision theory and institutional design, I keep encountering a strategic structure I have not been able to name with precision. The pattern involves an agent who derives value from being perceived—before any interaction—as the kind of party who will execute a specific, costly response should condition C arise. Others, anticipating that response, alter their behavior in ways that benefit the agent: they cooperate, invest, or expose themselves to risk. Once condition C is satisfied and those anticipatory benefits are secured, however, actually carrying out the response offers no further strategic gain; it is pure cost, and the agent has every reason to avoid it. Crucially, the agent understands this structure in advance.

Concrete instances vary. In regulatory design, a body may gain widespread compliance because market participants expect it to impose a severe, self-damaging sanction for a specific violation; once the violation has occurred and compliance is already achieved, enforcing the sanction harms the regulator and the market alike, with no remaining deterrent effect because the interaction is non-repeating. In scientific practice, a researcher may build long-term credibility by committing in advance to abandon a favored model should a particular experimental outcome occur; once the outcome arrives and her credibility is established, actually discarding the model destroys years of work without yielding any additional reputational reward, since the community has already observed her integrity.

The tension is not merely time-inconsistency, because the ex-ante value comes specifically from the anticipated ex-post action, not from any direct benefit of the action itself. It also feels different from a standard credible threat, because a credible threat is usually valuable precisely when execution remains possible; here, execution after the trigger is strategically empty.

Does this configuration—where cultivating a disposition to perform a costly, trigger-dependent action is rational, but performing the action once triggered is not—have an established term in philosophy or economics? I suspect it may sit near a well-known desert-stranding thought experiment about promises and rational commitment, but I want to know whether there is a precise label, how it differs from ordinary commitment devices, prisoner's dilemmas with pre-commitment, and simple failures of willpower, and where its boundaries lie—for instance, whether the response must be publicly observable, whether the agent's type must be inferred rather than contractually guaranteed, and whether the pattern collapses if the interaction might repeat. If no exact term exists, I would welcome a careful delineation of what distinguishes it and what it should be called.
