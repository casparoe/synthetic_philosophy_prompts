As a journalist covering science and ideas, I file several stories each week that turn on whether a study or forecast should be reported as probable, speculative, or genuinely unquantifiable, and I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes before deadline. Ground it in Keynes’s distinction between quantifiable risk and radical uncertainty, and in his claim that the “weight of argument”—the balance and breadth of relevant evidence—can shift independently of any calculated probability. The deliverable should be an ordered set of tests that takes a claim, its statistical framing, and its replication context, and produces: (a) a classification into one of three newsroom categories—“report as risk,” “report as weighted possibility,” or “report as speculation only”; (b) prescribed language for the lede and headline; and (c) a mandatory flag for editorial sign-off when evidential weight is low but stakes are high. Mark every step that remains a judgment call rather than a calculation, distinguish unknown base rates from unknown mechanisms, and state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle. Apply it to three hypothetical cases: a large meta-analysis with narrow confidence intervals; a single forty-person study proposing a surprising but unreplicated mechanism; and a long-range climate projection built on an untested feedback loop that I am eager to headline—here the procedure should push back with an answer I will not want to file. Finish by stating the hard limits of the method and the precise points where an editor must intervene.
