I'm a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and next month I'm delivering a lecture—part research seminar, part public talk—at an interdisciplinary institute on naturalizing mental content. My thesis is that teleosemantics fails as a general account of representation not because of the familiar swampman or disjunction objections, but because it implicitly binds content to evolutionary selection history in a way that rules out synthetic organisms and mature artificial systems; I defend an alternative I'm calling stabilization teleosemantics, which holds that a state represents some feature not in virtue of Millikan-style proper function but in virtue of being a counterfactual attractor in the system's error-correction dynamics—specifically, the dynamics converge back to that state across a range of perturbations if and only if the represented feature is present, regardless of phylogenetic origin. The audience is about thirty people: philosophers of biology and cognitive science who defend selection-based accounts of content, enactivists and predictive-processing researchers who think representation should be dissolved into dynamical coupling, and AI researchers asking whether large language models can have genuine mental content. They are suspicious of journalists importing physical formalisms into philosophy of mind, they care about whether this applies to actual artificial systems, and they will test whether I'm merely relabeling predictive processing.

Prepare me for the objections in the order they will actually arise: first the committed teleosemanticist, then the enactivist, then the AI researcher. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that interlocutor would phrase it, then specify what I should concede up front, what I should hold without retreat, where my reply is weakest, and whether this is the one I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. Include the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Finally, take a definite, committed position: tell me which single objection is actually fatal if I can't answer it in under two minutes, defend that ranking with reasons rather than presenting options, and tell me exactly what reply has to land for the proposal to survive—no "it depends on your framework" escape routes, and no bibliography or citation requests, since I need to defend this without notes.
