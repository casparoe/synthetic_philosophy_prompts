In my philosophy of language seminar this term we have been examining Wittgenstein’s claim that the unity of concepts such as “game” or “language” is not secured by a shared essential feature but by a network of overlapping similarities—what he calls family resemblance. I take this to mean that there is no single necessary and sufficient condition that every language game must satisfy, only criss-crossing resemblances among particular practices. From this, however, I seem to derive a tension that I cannot resolve. If “language game” is genuinely a family-resemblance concept, then its application should depend on a cluster of surface similarities rather than on the possession of some deep, uniform substrate. Now consider an artificial system that can generate context-sensitive corrections, ask clarifying questions, and deploy syntactic structures in ways that overlap with several nodes in the family-resemblance network—yet it lacks any biological embodiment, shared social practices, or form of life. By the lights of family resemblance alone, it looks as though such a system should count as engaging in language games, since it exhibits enough of the relevant overlaps. But this seems to collapse Wittgenstein’s distinction between language and form of life, making the latter appear like an illicit essentialist residue that family resemblance was meant to dissolve. Is the mistake here in treating family resemblance as an extension criterion for particular entities rather than as an account of conceptual coherence? Or is there a more subtle way in which form of life functions not as an essential property but as a grammatical condition that family resemblance itself cannot replace? I would appreciate guidance on where the confusion lies.
