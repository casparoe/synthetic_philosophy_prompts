Question: Some philosophers maintain that omissions—such as failing to water a plant or failing to throw a life preserver—can be genuine causes of what follows, while others argue that omissions are mere absences and therefore causally inert. To examine this, apply the distinction between a "structural absence," in which an agent lacks both the practical capacity and any recognized social role that would make intervention expected, and an "intervention failure," in which an agent possesses both the capacity and a recognized role that renders non-intervention significant. First, determine whether a trained lifeguard who is on duty but distracted by his phone, and who therefore fails to blow his whistle to prevent a swimmer from entering a rip current, is a cause of the swimmer's subsequent injury. Second, determine whether the analysis changes if the lifeguard is off-duty, asleep in a nearby cabin, unaware of the situation, but physically capable of having intervened had he been awake and present. In your answer, address whether counterfactual dependence—if the agent had acted, the outcome would have differed—is sufficient by itself to establish causation by omission, or whether some normative or role-based condition is also necessary.

Student's answer:
Omissions can cause outcomes whenever counterfactual dependence holds: if changing the absence changes the result, then the absence is part of the causal story. Applying the distinction, the on-duty distracted lifeguard is clearly an intervention failure because he has the role and the capacity, so his failure to blow the whistle causes the injury—if he had acted, the swimmer would not have entered the current. The off-duty sleeping lifeguard is a structural absence because he is not working and has no recognized role in that moment. Nevertheless, he still causes the injury, because if he had been awake, walked to the beach, and blown the whistle, the outcome would have changed. Therefore, counterfactual dependence is sufficient for causation in both cases, and the distinction between structural absence and intervention failure only affects how much we blame someone, not whether they caused the result. Normative expectations are irrelevant to causation itself.
