I cover the philosophy of social science for a publication on science and ideas, and I am preparing a Q&A with an unnamed scholar who defends a revived Weberian thesis: cultural values operate not as efficient causes but as modal filters—elective-affinity structures that constrain which material possibilities become historically actualized—thereby avoiding both reduction to economics and dismissal as epiphenomena. Draft exactly five interview questions for her, delivered as a numbered list from 1 through 5 with no introduction or conclusion; each question should be one or two sentences long and must include a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The first should ask how values can explain historical outcomes if they possess no material efficacy, anticipating her reply that they restructure the salience of options rather than pushing them, with a follow-up asking why this is not merely multicausal interaction redescribed. The second should press her on whether this modal-filter view can resist reduction to class-interest correlation, with a follow-up addressing her likely appeal to the autonomy of meaningful adequacy over causal adequacy. The third should ask what empirical evidence could distinguish modal filtering from ordinary historical sociology and what finding would disconfirm her view, with a follow-up about the risk of unfalsifiability. The fourth should probe whether her framework preserves Weber's distinction between causal adequacy and meaningful adequacy or collapses them into a single constraint relation, with a follow-up on the specific consequence for the Protestant ethic thesis. The fifth should present the counterexample of rapid cultural transformation following catastrophic material collapse—where elective affinity seems absent—and ask how she accounts for it without retreating to material determinism, with a follow-up asking whether this forces her to concede that culture is only causally active in stable conditions.
