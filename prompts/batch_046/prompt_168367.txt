At a workshop on historical explanation, two colleagues — not me — argued over whether "the Renaissance" and "the Scientific Revolution" name real phases or are retrospective bundles invented for later disciplinary purposes. Elena said both terms are anachronistic impositions: no 15th-century Italian humanist called himself a "Renaissance man," and 17th-century natural philosophers shared no unified method they labeled "scientific"; the categories merely impose a false progressive narrative with discrete acts. Marcus replied that anachronism is not necessarily error — we speak of a "Bronze Age" without anyone self-identifying as Bronze Age people — and that these terms track genuine institutional shifts, such as the spread of humanist curricula and the founding of scientific societies. Elena countered that Marcus conflates scattered local practices with a grand story; there was no single revolution spanning Galileo to Newton, only heterogeneous activities that 19th-century historians packaged together for nationalist and disciplinary ends. Marcus then offered a sharper claim: even if the bundle is invented, the invention was causally efficacious — by framing the 17th century as a revolution, later educators and institutions actually reshaped scientific practice, so the category is descriptively false but functions as a self-fulfilling institutional script. Elena said this concedes too much: if a category's only truth is that people later believed it, then it explains nothing about the period it purports to describe, and we should abandon it in favor of local, practice-specific accounts. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: rate Elena's anachronism claim, Marcus's Bronze Age analogy, Elena's heterogeneity objection, Marcus's self-fulfilling script claim, and Elena's final rebuttal from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification for each. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing about how periodization actually operates in historical explanation.
