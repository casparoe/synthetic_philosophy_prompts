Consider the public track record of an independent comparative-politics review board that has classified five recent regimes for democratic backsliding. Its verdicts are these: it labeled Hungary since 2010 and Poland during 2015–2023 as unambiguous backsliding; it labeled Turkey since 2016 as backsliding but stressed that its trajectory involved overt authoritarian rupture rather than incremental legalism; it classified the United States during 2017–2021 as suffering severe democratic stress and norm erosion yet explicitly declined the backsliding label, citing the absence of sustained institutional capture; and it classified a hypothetical regime—call it State X—in which the judiciary was systematically packed and election laws were amended through entirely constitutional procedures, yet opposition parties retained formal ballot access and media pluralism persisted, as an unambiguous case of backsliding.

The board has never published a formal methodology, but its members have defended these judgments by appealing, variously, to (i) whether governing actors expanded power through formally legal but substantively anti-democratic mechanisms; (ii) whether horizontal accountability was durably weakened regardless of remaining electoral competitiveness; and (iii) whether the governing party displayed an identifiable intent to entrench itself permanently.

Now consider State Y. There, a long-ruling party facing no serious electoral threat enacts a series of administrative reforms that leave constitutional text untouched, leave courts intact, and impose no direct media restrictions. Instead, career civil-service directors in election-management and national statistical agencies are replaced with partisan appointees; the legal criteria for voter eligibility are narrowed through bureaucratic interpretation rather than legislative amendment; and public-procurement rules are redirected to favor firms that finance the ruling party. Elections remain technically free, opposition candidates win in some subnational jurisdictions, and international observers report no overt fraud. Independent analysts, however, document a steady, statistically significant decline in opposition vote share precisely in those regions where the administrative reforms were implemented first and where the ruling party’s financing network is densest.

Predict how the board would classify State Y. Defend your prediction by identifying the implicit rule—or competing rules—drawn from considerations (i)–(iii) above, or from alternative frameworks such as competitive authoritarianism, stealth authoritarianism, or bureaucratic de-authorization, that best explain all five prior verdicts jointly. Specify which prior case is the closest analog and why. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, lay out the principle the board would be adopting in each direction, and propose one further hypothetical case—designed to isolate the disagreement between your leading candidate rules—that would settle which principle the board is actually running.
