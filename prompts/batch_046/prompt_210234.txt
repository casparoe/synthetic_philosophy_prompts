I've come round to a pretty specific take on the procreation asymmetry. It's not just "no duty to breed happy people" — I think the asymmetry holds because bringing someone into existence only creates obligations if their interests get frustrated, not satisfied, which requires treating non-existence as lacking the right kind of modal standing to ground beneficence duties. This only works if we reject modal actualism about possible people and if we think the non-identity problem doesn't dissolve the harm side, since comparative harm to non-existence is coherent in a way comparative benefit isn't. So what would actually count against it? I want the defeaters laid out: arguments that the asymmetry vanishes under population ethics, formal results showing my "interests precondition" collapses into standard consequentialism, intuitions or thought experiments where we feel we ought to create, and empirical findings about actual reproductive behaviour that don't match the philosophical framing. Rank them from fatal to just awkward. What does this view predict that rival accounts — say, a straight person-affecting view or a symmetry-breaking consequentialist view — don't? And has it been qualified into unfalsifiability with all these conditions about modal standing? If so, is that a problem? I'd also like to know what defenders of this kind of asymmetry would themselves concede is damaging, not just what critics say. I'm particulary worried it might be well-insulated but useless — tell me if that's fair.
