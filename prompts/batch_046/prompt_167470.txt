I'm working on a conceptual taxonomy for a graduate seminar on AI safety, and I'm trying to disentangle what gets collapsed under the umbrella term "the alignment problem." Many discussions treat it as a monolith—ensuring that advanced systems pursue intended goals—but I'm convinced it fragments into several conceptually independent issues that happen to share a vocabulary. Consider the following five framings, arranged roughly from the widely discussed to the more idiosyncratic.

First, the instrumental convergence framing: regardless of an agent's terminal goals, certain sub-goals such as resource acquisition and self-preservation emerge as instrumentally rational, creating misalignment with human interests. Second, the preference aggregation framing: the difficulty is not what an individual system wants, but that no coherent social-choice mechanism exists to aggregate diverse, possibly incommensurable human preferences into a single objective function. Third, the ontological misgeneralization framing: alignment fails not because goals are wrong, but because the system's internal ontology—its categories for value, harm, or agency—stabilizes in ways that diverge from ours in high-stakes out-of-distribution contexts. Fourth, the reflective stability framing: the core challenge is ensuring that an agent's values remain coherent through recursive self-modification, so that alignment survives the very process of the agent becoming more capable. Fifth, the hermeneutic opacity framing, which I propose as a distinct and under-theorized variant: the problem is that as systems scale, their reasoning becomes interpretively opaque not merely in a causal sense but in a semantic one; we cannot recover whether a given behavior expresses a stable commitment or a context-sensitive performance, making verification conceptually impossible regardless of access to weights or traces.

Please rank these five framings in tiers—S, A, B, C, D—according to how well each captures a genuinely distinct conceptual challenge that is not reducible to the others. For each tier, provide one sentence explaining why it belongs there, with particular attention to whether the fifth framing introduces something that the first four miss, or whether it collapses into one of them. In addition, assign a rough weight to each framing (summing to 1.0) representing what fraction of serious philosophical work on alignment should address it, given your ranking. I am especially interested in whether you think the alignment problem is best understood as one problem with five symptoms, or as five problems that happen to share a name.
