I am preparing the diagnostic framework for the second term of my jurisprudence seminar, in which we have deliberately moved beyond the familiar framing of the 'counter-majoritarian difficulty' to examine what I have begun calling the problem of democratic exhaustion—the tendency of elected assemblies, across successive electoral cycles, to become locked in path-dependent commitments that prevent self-correction not through deliberate malice but through accumulated institutional inertia. For our session on comparative constitutional design, I want an instrument that classifies positions on whether unelected judges ought ever to be permitted to overrule primary legislation, but it must sort respondents by the concrete commitments they actually hold rather than by the jurisprudential labels they prefer.

Please write a branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—whose root question asks whether a judiciary lacking democratic mandate may invalidate legislation enacted by a representative assembly. From there, branch first on whether democratic majorities possess intrinsic authority or merely instrumental value; second, on whether courts enjoy a distinctive epistemic advantage over legislatures regarding constitutional interpretation; and third, on whether judicial intervention is restricted to structural or procedural failures—such as violations of the rules governing democratic participation itself—or extends also to substantive rights-claims. At the leaves, place the named positions of parliamentary sovereignty, strong-form rights-based review, weak-form constitutional dialogue, and the view I term conditional suspension, which permits review solely when a legislature demonstrates sustained, path-dependent incapacity for self-correction; include any genuinely unoccupied logical combinations, each briefly diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Every node must be phrased as a concrete institutional question—e.g., whether a court may strike down a statute that restricts voting access versus one that regulates commercial contracts—so that a respondent can answer without prior knowledge of constitutional theory. Finally, flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot genuinely separate, and ensure that no branch merely asks whether one supports judicial review in the abstract.
