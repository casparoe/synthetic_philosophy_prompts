I have just emerged from a long, unresolved argument with a friend about William James's defense of the will to believe and W.K. Clifford's evidentialist opposition. We kept circling back to a particular twist that neither of James's standard examples fully captures: reflexive belief-formation, where holding or suspending a belief alters the very evidential conditions that Clifford insists must govern belief-formation. For instance, believing that one's own reasoning is reliable may be a functional precondition for the reasoning that could justify that belief, while withholding belief may actively erode the cognitive stability required to gather further evidence. My friend insists this collapses Clifford's position into pragmatism; I think it reveals an unacknowledged exemption, but I could not make the tension visceral rather than abstract.

For an upper-level undergraduate philosophy workshop (16 students, 75 minutes; participants have read James's essay on the will to believe and Clifford's essay on evidentialism but are not specialists), design a structured simulation — a game with assigned roles, hidden information, and small but real stakes — that makes participants feel this reflexive evidential bind before anyone names it. The facilitator should not explain James, Clifford, or the reflexive twist until the debrief. The scenario should present a forced, collective choice about whether to endorse a belief that directly affects the group's ability to coordinate or reason, where withholding assent is not epistemically neutral but degrades the conditions for future evidence-gathering.

Please provide: (a) the physical or digital setup and materials; (b) specific roles, rules, and a payoff or incentive structure that makes belief-adoption and belief-withholding carry different practical consequences; (c) a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; (d) a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto James's notions of live, forced, and momentous options versus Clifford's evidentialist constraint, with explicit attention to the reflexive twist; (e) an honest assessment of what the simulation can reproduce about reflexive belief and which feature it necessarily cannot reproduce (the facilitator must supply this), plus predictable failure modes — for example, students treating belief purely as strategic coordination rather than as an epistemic commitment — and how to handle them; and (f) a clear statement of what participants should come away seeing about the ethics of belief.

Verdict: the single feature of James's account of voluntary belief that this classroom simulation is structurally incapable of reproducing.
