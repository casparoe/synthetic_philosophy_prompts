I serve on the Graduate Curriculum Oversight Committee for our university's joint M.A. program in Philosophy of Mind and Cognitive Science, and I need a formal decision memo addressed to the Dean for circulation before next week's meeting. We face a restructuring proposal triggered by an internal accreditation review. The program currently requires every student to complete two parallel core sequences: one framed within the representational-computational framework, with its vocabulary of mental representations, algorithmic architectures, and information-theoretic measures of content; and one framed within the enactive-embodied framework, with its vocabulary of sensorimotor coupling, affordance spaces, dynamic self-organization, and phenomenological fidelity as a theoretical virtue. One faction of faculty contends that requiring both sequences is pedagogically incoherent because the frameworks exhibit what they term deep incommensurability—not merely different terminologies, but divergent taxonomies of mental phenomena, incompatible weightings of virtues such as predictive accuracy versus lived-body description, and a lack of neutral observational sentences that could adjudicate cross-framework disputes without circularity. They propose Option C, allowing students to declare a primary paradigm and complete only one core sequence, with exposure to the other tradition limited to electives. The opposing faction—whose concern must be treated with full seriousness—argues that research-level incommensurability does not entail pedagogical paralysis; students can learn disciplined code-switching, and graduating specialists fluent in only one tradition creates an unacceptable institutional risk in interdisciplinary hiring, peer review, and grant evaluation contexts where cross-paradigm literacy is now expected. They favor Option A, which preserves the dual-track requirement but adds a mandatory translation seminar in which students map arguments from one framework onto the other using only locally shared premises, or Option B, which collapses the two sequences into a single comparative course taught by paired instructors with explicitly contrasting commitments.

The memo should recommend one concrete option—A, B, or C, or a modified version with conditions—and provide a clear bottom line that the committee can vote on, including a phased timeline if appropriate. It should address whether the incommensurability claim, precisely construed as a thesis about taxonomic divergence, evaluative weighting, and observational neutrality, actually undermines co-teaching and co-learning in this specific curricular context; whether a local-translation protocol can bridge the gap without distorting either tradition's internal norms of explanation and evidence; and whether the institutional risk of producing single-paradigm graduates outweighs the risk of conceptual confusion or false assimilation. Please treat the strongest argument on the losing side honestly: if we reject the translation-seminar approach, we must explain why disciplined code-switching is either impossible here or not worth the pedagogical cost; if we reject single-paradigm tracking, we must explain why cross-fluency is achievable without violating the integrity of either research tradition. The document should be formal, addressed to the Dean, actionable by the committee, and should not include bibliographic requests, citation lists, or appeals to literature review—our purpose is institutional reasoning grounded in the specific curricular structures and faculty capacities at hand.
