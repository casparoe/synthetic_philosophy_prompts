I'm a lawyer trained in contract drafting, tort liability, and administrative law, with only passing familiarity with analytic political philosophy. Restate the core debate among consent-based, fairness-based, and associative theories of political obligation — together with Rawls's standard conditions for justified civil disobedience — entirely within the vocabulary of contract formation, consideration, implied terms, breach, remedy, and the public-law distinction between ultra vires and legitimate authority. To avoid a conventional analogy, frame the state-citizen relation as a layered protocol architecture: constitutional constraints form a hard-coded base layer, statutory obligations operate as a modifiable middleware layer, and civic duties are instantiated at the application interface. Within this frame, treat civil disobedience as a deliberate, public breach at the middleware layer that seeks not private remedy but rollback and renegotiation at the constitutional base. Then provide the residue report: which philosophical distinctions — such as the gap between natural duty and associative obligation, or Rawls's 'nearly just' society condition — translate cleanly into legal doctrines; which survive only with distortion (for example, rendering 'fair play' as consideration or estoppel); and which have no counterpart in legal vocabulary at all, particularly any claim about moral reasons that bind independently of enforceable obligations or procedural legitimacy. Conclude by assessing whether the residue indicates that legal vocabulary lacks a category of non-contractual moral bindingness, or whether the philosophical debate imports distinctions that lack practical institutional expression.
