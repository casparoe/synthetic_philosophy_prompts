You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaphysics, mereology, and the philosophy of science. Before you begin, take a moment to brainstorm the core distinctions you will need: synchronic versus diachronic identity, ontological dependence versus supervenience, local pairwise relations versus global organizational structure, and the difference between an arrangement relation and an addition relation.

In popular and philosophical discussions of whether composite objects exist over and above their parts, interlocutors frequently invoke the jigsaw-puzzle analogy. The claim runs as follows: a completed jigsaw is nothing more than its interlocking cardboard pieces arranged correctly on a flat surface; there is no extra substance or entity called the puzzle that exists in addition to those pieces and their spatial relations. From this, it is argued that the special-composition question is a pseudo-problem—if all the parts are present in the right configuration, asking whether the whole also exists is like asking whether the picture exists separately from the arranged pieces. The analogy is used to license a reductionist or nihilist conclusion: composition is not special, and wholes are merely parts arranged.

Now inject a contrasting conceptual case. Consider a holographic mosaic in which each tile does not merely display a local color patch but instead encodes a distributed fraction of a global image, such that the removal or alteration of any single tile degrades the entire pattern non-locally, and the functional role of each tile is determined by the global configuration rather than by pairwise adjacency relations. Here the organizational structure is not decomposable into local interlocks; the whole exhibits a form of downward determination on its parts.

Audit the jigsaw-puzzle analogy as it is deployed against special composition. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: identify what in the target domain (physical objects, biological organisms, social institutions, or abstract structures) plays the role of the pieces, the interlocking edges, the completed picture, the table or background space, and the alleged extra substance. Second, locate the precise points where the mapping breaks—especially where local, pairwise arrangement fails to capture global, non-decomposable organizational dependence, and where diachronic persistence conditions for the whole diverge from the persistence conditions of any particular subset of parts. Third, examine whether the analogy quietly imports a reductionist ontology about arrangement being ontologically sufficient. Does that imported feature survive once we recognize that some candidate composites exhibit functional unity that is not recoverable from any proper subset? Fourth, evaluate whether the conclusion—that special composition is illusory—actually survives these disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along. Finally, if the jigsaw analogy is inadequate for at least one class of composites, propose a better analogy for the kind of composition that might genuinely be special, and state whether the special-composition question remains live after the audit. Be precise, avoid bibliographic appeals, and do not treat the holographic mosaic as an empirical claim but as a conceptual probe.
