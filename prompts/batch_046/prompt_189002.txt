I write for a publication that tracks the history of computing and neuroscience, and I am preparing a long-form piece on John von Neumann's late lectures comparing the brain and the computer—specifically his claim that neural computation relies on a hybrid analog-digital statistical logic, allowing unreliable neuronal components to yield reliable cognition through probabilistic threshold mechanisms operating in massive parallelism. He insisted this architecture was structurally unlike the discrete deterministic logic of digital machines, yet he also treated computation and self-replication as substrate-independent, realizable in vacuum tubes, silicon, or tissue alike. From these premises I have built the following argument, and I find it suspicious because it seems to collapse von Neumann's own framework.

(1) Von Neumann argued that the brain achieves reliable cognition only through a specific hybrid analog-digital statistical architecture—probabilistic threshold logic across massively parallel, unreliable neuronal components—that cannot be decomposed into discrete deterministic steps.

(2) If a mental process requires a specific computational architecture, then it can occur only in physical systems that literally instantiate that architecture.

(3) Therefore, cognition can occur only in systems possessing the brain's particular hybrid analog-digital statistical architecture.

(4) Any digital computer, including one running a high-fidelity simulation of neural dynamics, operates through discrete deterministic steps at the physical level; it does not literally instantiate analog-statistical logic.

(5) Therefore, no purely digital system—including any artificial intelligence running on conventional hardware—can be genuinely cognitive.

(6) Yet von Neumann also maintained that computation and self-replication are substrate-independent: the same logical organization can be realized in silicon or neural tissue without altering the functional outcome.

(7) So if a digital system precisely replicates the logical organization of the brain's statistical computation—simulating its probabilistic thresholds and parallel interactions—then by (6) it should realize cognition, regardless of its discrete physical substrate.

(8) But (5) denies this, and there is no principled boundary within von Neumann's framework that permits substrate-independence for computation in general while forbidding it specifically for cognition.

(9) Hence the position proves too much: it either denies cognitive status to every artificial system von Neumann helped design, or it dissolves the architectural specificity he used to distinguish brains from computers.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am inclined to preserve (1) and (6) as faithful to von Neumann, which forces a choice between (2) and (4). Is the error an equivocation between physical architecture and logical organization—so that (4) misdescribes what a digital simulation achieves at the functional level—or is (2) too strong in tying mental processes to physical architecture despite von Neumann's functionalist leanings? If I reject (2), do I lose the reason to think brains are special computing machines at all, or can the specificity be preserved at the level of implementation without blocking substrate-independent cognition?
