I'm preparing the interview schedule for an episode of a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a contemporary moral philosopher who has recently defended a revisionist reading of Glaucon's challenge in Plato's Republic. Rather than treating the ring of Gyges as evidence that justice is merely conventional, she advances a thesis she calls 'witness-dependent normativity': the claim that moral reasons carry normative force only within modal contexts in which they could, in principle, be observed and shared by another rational agent, regardless of whether any actual observer is present. She insists this is not a psychological thesis about fear of punishment, but a conceptual claim about the shareability conditions of practical reason; on her account, solitary, genuinely unwitnessed 'justice' is as conceptually incoherent as a private language on certain Wittgensteinian readings. Consequently, she answers the 'why be moral?' question not through self-interest or social contract, but by arguing that the very structure of practical reason requires an implicit audience.

I should like a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond asking her to restate the view. Please include: at least one question pressing how her position differs from standard contractarian readings she rejects; one confronting her with the case of a shipwrecked survivor with no prospect of rescue who nevertheless appears to act justly in complete isolation; and one that anticipates her probable reply—that the required 'witness' need only be an internalised rational interlocutor within a divided self—followed immediately by a sharper follow-up asking whether this internalisation renders her view empirically indistinguishable from a refined egoism. I also want a closing question that asks what kind of evidence or conceptual counter-example could genuinely count against witness-dependent normativity, rather than merely serving as another illustration of it. The questions should assume familiarity with the Republic text but should not request bibliographies, further reading, or citations.
