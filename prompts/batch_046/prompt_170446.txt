Below are working notes for an essay tentatively titled "Geometric Rationality and Epistemic Leverage: Extending the Kelly Criterion Beyond Capital Allocation." The central claim is that the Kelly criterion reveals a structural feature of rationality—geometric rather than arithmetic rationality—that applies not only to financial betting but to the architecture of belief revision. I propose that rational agents should size their credence in high-leverage propositions not by expected evidential value alone, but by a multiplicative risk factor analogous to drawdown management in portfolio theory.

Outline and argument sketch:

1. The standard Kelly formulation maximizes expected log wealth, yielding the optimal fraction f* = (bp − q)/b. The usual interpretation treats this as a special case of risk-averse utility maximization.

2. I want to resist that reduction. Instead, I treat geometric rationality as a distinct normative structure: rationality is not primarily about maximizing expected arithmetic payoff, but about preserving and growing multiplicative capital—whether financial or epistemic—over time. Arithmetic rationality asks, "What is the expected value of this belief?" Geometric rationality asks, "What is the expected growth rate of my overall epistemic portfolio if I hold this belief at full strength?"

3. To make this concrete, I introduce the concept of belief leverage (L): the ratio of derivative commitments entailed by accepting a proposition P to P itself. A belief with high L—say, a sweeping metaphysical framework—does not fail in isolation; its failure cascades, undermining a network of subsidiary commitments and producing an epistemic drawdown that is multiplicative rather than additive.

4. From this, I derive a Kelly-inspired constraint on credence allocation. For high-L beliefs, the agent should hold a credence lower than the naive Bayesian posterior suggests, not out of mere risk aversion, but because the geometric cost of being wrong (a catastrophic reduction in coherent explanatory resources) exceeds the arithmetic gain of being right. The agent sizes the belief as one sizes a Kelly bet: f* scales with edge divided by leverage-adjusted risk.

5. The obvious objection is that this conflates instrumental rationality (avoiding ruin) with epistemic rationality (tracking truth). My reply distinguishes epistemic capital—an integrated system of explanatory and predictive resources—from mere belief count. Losing epistemic capital is not like losing one true belief among many; it is like suffering a portfolio collapse. Therefore, managing geometric risk is not a departure from truth-tracking but a meta-level condition on sustainable truth-tracking over time.

6. I conclude that geometric rationality should function not as a replacement for Bayesian updating, but as a meta-filter on which high-leverage commitments an agent can afford to maintain at full strength without exposing their epistemic portfolio to ruinous drawdown.

I would particularly value comments on the following: (a) whether the analogy between financial capital and epistemic capital holds up at the point where beliefs lack liquid value, or whether it collapses into metaphor; (b) whether the notion of belief leverage (L) is sufficiently precise to do normative work, or whether it smuggles in unexamined assumptions about the structure of theoretical commitments; (c) whether the argument successfully distinguishes geometric rationality from standard decision-theoretic risk aversion, or whether it merely rebrands it; and (d) whether there is an internal tension between advocating Kelly-style sizing for high-leverage beliefs and maintaining that Bayesian updating should still govern the base-rate credence from which the sizing is calculated. General remarks on coherence, on whether the injection of "epistemic drawdown" clarifies or obscures the underlying point, and on any overlooked objections are also welcome.
