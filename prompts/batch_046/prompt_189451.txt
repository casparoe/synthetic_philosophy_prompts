Read Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five through the philosophy of time and the thermodynamic arrow. Billy Pilgrim becomes unstuck in time and the Tralfamadorians perceive all moments simultaneously, while the novel's recurring refrain accompanying every death suggests a fatalistic acceptance of events as fixed. I want a reading that takes both interpretations seriously: on one hand, the Tralfamadorian view as a dramatization of eternalism or the block universe, in which temporal passage is illusory; on the other hand, the possibility that Vonnegut is depicting not an objective metaphysics but a traumatic breakdown of the psychological arrow of time, where memory ceases to track the entropic gradient that ordinarily distinguishes past from future. Does the novel treat the irreversibility of death and decay—especially in the Dresden firebombing—as grounded in an objective asymmetry of entropy, or does it reduce that asymmetry to a subjective feature of human cognition? Where does the text resist being read as a simple illustration of either eternalism or presentism, and in what scenes does Vonnegut's fatalism risk conflating statistical determinism with metaphysical determinism in ways a philosopher of physics should correct? I am preparing this for a philosophy of science discussion group and want to know what the work adds to the debate over whether the direction of time derives from physical law or from conscious experience that philosophical argument alone does not capture.
