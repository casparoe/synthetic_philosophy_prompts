I want a rigorous specification of what an adequate theory of historiographical judgment must meet if it is to claim that historians can, in principle, transcend the normative and conceptual standards of their own era when interpreting the past. Do not start with existing authors; derive the requirements from the problem itself. Write the desiderata: the explanatory targets the theory must cover (for instance, how historians understand practices embedded in evaluative vocabularies with no direct modern equivalent; how they distinguish explanatory reconstruction from moral endorsement; and how they account for conceptual change without treating it as mere error), the specific cases it must handle correctly (for example, assessing past institutions that violate present human-rights norms; describing pre-modern cosmologies without anachronistic scientific framing; and identifying when a historian's own categories have distorted the archival record), and the distinctions it is required to preserve (such as that between unavoidable perspectival situatedness and culpable anachronism; between grasping an actor's reasons and endorsing them; and between translating a concept and substituting a modern analog). For each requirement, provide the motivating consideration or failure-case that makes it non-optional, and flag which demands are genuinely non-negotiable conditions on adequacy versus which reflect camp preferences—e.g., the strict contextualist's demand for total normative suspension, the critical presentist's insistence on unavoidable moral evaluation, or the dialogical hermeneut's claim that understanding transforms both present and past frameworks. Also address whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: in particular, does the requirement of complete normative neutrality conflict with the requirement that historical interpretation remain conceptually accessible and significant to present readers, and does the demand for conceptual translation without analogical substitution collapse once hermeneutic circularity is taken seriously? Identify which single desideratum, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest number of popular positions. Finally, briefly—without producing a full scorecard—indicate how a strict historicist stance, a critical presentist stance, and a dialogical hermeneutic stance fare against your list, noting for each whether it fails on a non-negotiable point or merely sacrifices a negotiable preference. Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria and their conflicts; the evaluation of particular stances should be subordinate.
