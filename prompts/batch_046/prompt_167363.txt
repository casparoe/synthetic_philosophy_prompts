I just finished a long argument with a close friend about G. E. Moore's account of intrinsic goods, specifically the relationship between friendship and beauty in his best-known ethical treatise. We were trying to read Moore's list of supreme goods—personal affection and the appreciation of beautiful objects—not as a mere inventory, but as a claim about how these goods hang together structurally. My friend took one side, I took the other, and neither of us conceded. I want you to adjudicate our exchange point by point, and I want a committed answer: take a definite position, give your reasons, and do not just lay out interpretive options for me to choose between.

Here is how the argument went.

My friend began by insisting that Moore treats friendship and aesthetic enjoyment as two independent entries in his axiological catalog. She said the principle of organic unity applies to each good separately—so friendship is an organic whole composed of emotional states and behaviors, and aesthetic enjoyment is an organic whole composed of perception and feeling—but there is no further claim that the two goods are interdependent. On her view, a person could possess deep personal affection with no shared aesthetic life and suffer no axiological loss.

I replied that this misses the way Moore pairs the goods in his discussion of the best things in life. I argued that if friendship is an emotional state directed at another person's consciousness, and if Moore holds that the value of such a state depends partly on the quality of the consciousness it appreciates, then friendship is already oriented toward aesthetic qualities in the other. Therefore, isolating friendship from beauty violates the organic-unity principle: the whole of a life containing both is not merely the sum of two high-value parts, but a unified state in which each good is enhanced by the presence of the other.

She answered that I was importing a modern relational theory of friendship that Moore never endorsed. She pointed out that Moore's taxonomy is descriptive, not a social psychology of intimacy. For Moore, she said, the emotional connection is itself the intrinsic good; whether the friends share tastes or admire the same objects is irrelevant to the value of that connection. She added that if my view were correct, Moore should say that friendship lacking shared aesthetic appreciation is deficient, but he never makes that claim.

I came back with a structural argument. I said that Moore's doctrine of organic unity requires that the value of a complex be determined by the value of its parts and their arrangement. If friendship and beauty were genuinely independent, then combining them should yield a value equal to their sum. But Moore clearly treats a life that contains both as disproportionately valuable—more than twice as good—which suggests an interaction effect rather than simple addition. I claimed this interaction is best explained by the idea that friendship is partly constituted by shared aesthetic experience.

She replied that the disproportion can be explained without interaction: it is just the aggregation of two very high-value goods within one life, each contributing its own large value to the larger whole of that life. There is no need to posit that friendship requires beauty. She also noted that I had offered no direct textual evidence that Moore makes friendship depend on aesthetic appreciation, and that without such evidence my reading was speculative.

I then offered what I took to be textual support. I observed that Moore describes the supreme goods in language that links them—he speaks of the pleasures of human intercourse together with the enjoyment of beautiful objects, as if they belong to a single category of elevated experience. I argued that this pairing is not accidental but reflects Moore's view that the best emotional states are those that combine interpersonal warmth with perceptual richness. I concluded that a friendship stripped of shared aesthetic engagement is, for Moore, an incomplete realization of the good he is describing.

She closed by saying that even if Moore pairs them rhetorically, pairing is not constitution. She said I was conflating the co-occurrence of goods in an ideal life with a logical or metaphysical dependence between them, and that this confusion undermines my entire reading.

Please go through our exchange move by move: rate each of my friend's moves and each of my moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then give your definite verdict: does Moore's framework actually bind friendship and beauty together in the way I claimed, or does it leave them as separate but co-occurring goods? Do not survey all possibilities—pick one and defend it. Also, please write your response in American English, and do not include a bibliography, citation list, or suggested reading.
