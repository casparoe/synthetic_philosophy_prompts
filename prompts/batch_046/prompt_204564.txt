I'm designing a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy graduate students whose only prior exposure is the standard brain-in-a-vat and Chinese Room literature. I want to teach functionalism not as "the mind is software," but as the claim that mental states are individuated by their causal-profile within an economy of other states—specifically, by drawing the distinction between role-functionalism (the causal role) and realizer-functionalism (the mechanism that occupies it), and by treating multiple realizability as either a feature or a threat depending on which version one holds. To keep the idea from feeling like a reheated computer analogy, I want the carrying case to be a synthetic hydrogel tissue engineered to detect damage through mechanical strain-gating rather than ion-channel nociceptors, yet integrated into a protective withdrawal reflex that is behaviorally indistinguishable from biological nociception. Plan the seminar's explanatory arc: the exact dependency order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the role/realizer distinction feels inevitable rather than ad hoc; what this hydrogel case must have and must lack in order to carry the idea, and why a computer simulation or an alien neurobiology would fail as the central example; the misconceptions these students will bring from popular science writing—particularly that functionalism makes substrate irrelevant, or that multiple realizability collapses into panpsychism for any sufficiently complex machine—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be defused; one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that reveals whether a student genuinely grasps the distinction or is merely repeating it; and what I should explicitly bracket and refuse to develop at this level—perhaps the literature on organizational invariance or on species-relative functional ascription—while acknowledging that it exists. Do not ask for readings or citations; I need the pedagogical architecture, not a bibliography.
