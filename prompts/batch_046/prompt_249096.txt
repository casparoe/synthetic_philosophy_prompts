In a graduate seminar on political morality, we have been examining transitional justice not as a simple choice between punishment and reconciliation, but as an institutional-design problem that arises when a state owes its citizens both accountability and stability. For this exercise, I want to apply a specific framing—what I will call epistemic-reparative exchange—to a well-known historical episode. The framework holds that when a state has systematically concealed large-scale violence, its primary remaining moral obligation is not necessarily punitive but epistemic: to reconstruct, verify, and publicly certify the factual architecture of that violence, and that this restitution of knowledge can, under certain conditions, partially discharge the moral debt owed to victims. I ask you to analyze South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, established in 1995 and operating through 1998 under the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act, through this lens. The common-knowledge outline is sufficient: individual amnesty was granted only upon full disclosure of politically motivated acts; perpetrators who applied and testified received immunity from civil and criminal prosecution; victims were offered public hearings but no direct veto over amnesty grants; promised material reparations were eventually delivered, though slowly and unevenly; and victims’ groups mounted constitutional challenges to the amnesty provisions. Please do not treat this as an invitation to summarize standard restorative-justice literature or to compare South Africa with Argentina or Rwanda; instead, treat the TRC as a case study in whether epistemic-reparative exchange can coherently substitute for retributive distribution.

Your analysis should run in both directions and remain disciplined about the mapping. First, using the framework, identify what moral problem the Commission’s architects faced given the negotiated transition and the credible threat of destabilization; specify who counts as the agent bearing obligations (the state, individual perpetrators, or both), what counts as the relevant evidence of harm, and what institutional form the theory prescribes—particularly whether exchanging truthful disclosure for legal immunity counts as satisfying the debt or as compounding it. Second, examine which features of the actual TRC the framework explains well (for example, why perpetrators had an incentive to disclose hidden structures of violence, why victims experienced the hearings as partially restorative despite the absence of punishment, or why the state prioritized public record over prosecution) and which features it misdescribes, renders invisible, or cannot accommodate (such as the delayed and inadequate material reparations, the specific objections raised by victim families who sought criminal trials, or the tension between domestic amnesty and emerging international norms against impunity). Be explicit about where the framework is silent—for instance, on whether international criminal-law obligations can override a domestically negotiated transitional bargain—rather than forcing it to speak.

Finally, let the episode test the theory. Does the TRC’s partial success and its well-documented failures confirm that epistemic restitution can stand in for punitive justice, or do they show that the two obligations are incommensurable and that attempting to substitute one for the other produces an unstable hybrid that satisfies neither perpetrators’ accountability nor victims’ demands? Where does the framework overreach by treating fundamentally political trade-offs as resolvable through moral accounting, and where does it genuinely illuminate a feature of transitional design that other approaches miss? I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or reading recommendations; I want a conceptual case study that respects both the institutional realities of the South African transition and the internal logic of the epistemic-reparative framework.
