I'm working through Berkeley's philosophy of money—his view that coins and notes are essentially tickets or counters for labor and commodities rather than valuable in themselves—and trying to see whether there's a single principle that captures all my interpretive judgments. Here are the verdicts I keep returning to.

First, a gold sovereign is valuable not because of any intrinsic property of the metal, but only insofar as it operates as a claim on labor and goods; if it stops circulating, its value to the holder collapses. Second, paper notes issued against land or industry are in principle no different from gold coins, since neither has value apart from what it represents. Third, debasing the coinage—keeping the face value while reducing the metal—is a kind of fraud, because the ticket now claims more labor than the backing warrants. Fourth, because Berkeley denies that material substance exists independently of perception, there is nothing metaphysically "real" about gold that paper lacks; the absence of metal should not trouble a consistent Berkeleyan. Fifth, a merchant who hoards coins in a chest is not genuinely rich, since wealth consists in the active claim on labor, not in the possession of inactive tokens.

But I also keep feeling that Berkeley treats an influx of foreign gold into a nation as genuinely enriching, not merely as an increase in the number of domestic tickets. That seems hard to square with the idea that money is purely a conventional claim on local labor. Is there one principle—perhaps tied to circulation, representation, and his immaterialist metaphysics—that saves all five judgments and also explains the sixth, or do I have to drop one?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause handles each case, and tell me what it predicts for a situation I haven't listed: a fully digital ledger that records labor-credits with no physical token at all, neither coin nor paper. Also be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and why.

Finally, say what would change your mind about the principle you propose—what argument or consideration would lead you to overturn one of my verdicts rather than adjust the principle? And flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail, especially if it depends on attributing to Berkeley a consistency between his metaphysics and his economics that the texts may not support.
