I was discussing distributive justice with two friends after a seminar, and we got stuck on whether prioritarianism actually escapes the leveling-down objection in cases where welfare losses are highly asymmetric. My friend Mara argued that if a city spends two million dollars maintaining an exclusive cultural venue used almost entirely by affluent residents, and closing it would free up four hundred thousand dollars that could be redirected to public health clinics in poor neighborhoods—with the rest lost to transition costs—then prioritarianism clearly favors closure: the poor gain real benefits, and the wealthy lose luxuries they can absorb. My other friend Jonas pushed back that this misapplies the view. He said prioritarianism weights benefits to the worse off more heavily, but it does not weight them infinitely, so we still need to compare the weighted gain (the four hundred thousand to the poor, multiplied by some priority factor) against the weighted loss (the wealthy losing two million in value, multiplied by a lower factor). He then added a quantitative twist: if the priority weight is, say, five-to-one, then the poor need to gain at least four hundred thousand in weighted terms to match a two-million loss weighted at one-to-one, which means the raw poor-gain must reach exactly that threshold just to break even. So prioritarianism might be genuinely indifferent, or even favor keeping the venue open if the weight is lower, despite the inequality. Mara said this proves prioritarianism is just egalitarianism with extra steps—if you are willing to destroy substantial net welfare just because the gap shrinks, you have smuggled equality back in under another name. Jonas replied that Mara is conflating two things: prioritarianism cares about absolute weighted welfare, not the gap itself, and in his calculation the poor are genuinely made better off in absolute terms, so there is no leveling-down in the strict sense. But Mara countered that the poor are only made slightly better off while society is made much poorer overall, and that calling this “not leveling-down” is a technicality that hides the same underlying intuition—that reducing disparity can justify large absolute losses. They asked me to referee, but I honestly could not tell where the error lies. Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about which specific point? In particular, please address whether Jonas’s quantitative framing correctly captures how prioritarian weights are supposed to function, whether Mara is right that prioritarianism collapses into egalitarianism in high-loss cases, and whether there is a principled way to fix the priority weight that avoids both of their extremes without simply appealing to intuition. What, if anything, are both of them missing?
