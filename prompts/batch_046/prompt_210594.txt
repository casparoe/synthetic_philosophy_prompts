I hold the position that Buddhist anattā, particularly as developed in Theravāda Abhidhamma analyses of citta-sequences and in Madhyamaka critiques of svabhāva, amounts not to a simple denial of selfhood but to a positive metaphysics of “serial dependency without substrate”: personal continuity is a pure causal topology of conditioning (paṭiccasamuppāda) in which mental events stand in direct causal relations without belonging to any enduring entity. On this reading, Parfit-style psychological-continuity theories are closer to Buddhism than narrative-identity accounts, yet both still err by retaining a “belonging-relation” between states; Buddhism substitutes distributed process for object-stream. Consequently, the classic fission and fusion puzzles dissolve—not through vagueness, but because there is never an entity to split or merge. Moral responsibility is reconceived as accountability internal to the conditioning-pattern rather than attribution to a persisting agent.

What would genuinely count against this view? I want the arguments, observations, conceptual results, or phenomenological data that should move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Specifically: (1) Can this account coherently accommodate the first-personal, diachronic sense of agency and ownership without smuggling back in a minimal self or an implicit subject of experience? (2) Could neuroscientific findings about a unified self-model—e.g., integrated activity in the default-mode network—be interpreted as evidence that the brain constructs precisely the belonging-relation Buddhism denies, and if so, does that undermine the metaphysical claim or only its phenomenological description? (3) Does the causal-topology framework actually resolve branching problems, or does it merely relocate them—if one conditioning-pattern bifurcates, which branch inherits the prior karma, and on what non-arbitrary grounds? (4) What do defenders of this Buddhist position themselves concede as a genuine defeater—not merely what critics allege, but what internal doctrinal tensions (say, between Abhidhamma momentariness and Mahāyāna emptiness doctrines, or between conventional truth [sammuti-sacca] and ultimate truth [paramattha-sacca]) risk collapsing the view into either substance-dualism or eliminativism? (5) Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability—e.g., by treating any apparent self-reference as merely conventional—and if so, is that insulation a methodological strength or a fatal flaw for a metaphysical claim of this kind? (6) Among rival accounts—psychological continuity, narrative identity, or a minimal-self phenomenology—which poses the most serious live threat, and what specific case or formal result makes it genuinely hard to dismiss rather than merely intuitively attractive? Please be honest about whether the view is well insulated and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
