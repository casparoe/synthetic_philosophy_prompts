After a long argument with a friend about source criticism and what we can actually claim to know about the distant past—ranging from Herodotean inquiry to modern archival methods—I want to test whether her judgments cohere around a single principle. Here is her track record across six cases.

First, on a ninth-century battle attested solely by a monastery chronicle composed a century later, with no archaeological trace, she insists we have no knowledge that it occurred. Second, on the existence of a fifth-century settlement supported by carbon-dated ruins and one tax register, she grants approximate knowledge. Third, regarding an emperor's private resentment inferred only from a court panegyric written fifty years after his death, she denies we can know his motives. Fourth, on regional population trends drawn from settlement-pattern archaeology together with a single census tablet, she accepts approximate quantitative knowledge. Fifth, on a council's general position reconstructed from three independent attendee letters, but with precise wording surviving only in one secretary's notes later edited for publication, she says we know the gist but not the verbatim formulation. Sixth, on a widespread famine mentioned in three chronicles from kingdoms with opposing political interests, each composed within a decade, she says we know it happened.

Now consider a new case. The claim is that in 842 a specific tax exemption was granted to a particular monastery, mentioned only in the original charter preserved in the monastery's own archive, with no other textual reference anywhere. However, the monastery's lands subsequently show no tax-related boundary adjustments in later cadastral surveys, whereas neighboring estates consistently show such adjustments—a pattern she herself treats as meaningful negative archival space rather than mere absence of record.

Predict her verdict: does she grant that we know the exemption was granted? Then identify the implicit rule she is running—whether it is an independence-threshold rule requiring at least two distinct evidential streams for any specific claim; a proximity-and-bias rule that privileges direct, unmediated primary sources regardless of singularity; or a granularity-matching rule that permits knowledge when the level of mediation suits the claim's coarseness. Give your best guess at which rule governs her judgments, with the reason for preferring it over the others that also fit the six cases. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively separate the rule you think is correct from its closest rival.
