I serve on the appeals panel for our city's cooperative housing charter, a body of five residents that reviews disputes about the 1978 covenant governing exterior modifications across roughly four hundred units. For decades the covenant's prohibition on non-traditional structures was essentially dormant: the panel met rarely, had no inspection staff, and acted only when a neighbor submitted a handwritten complaint. Eighteen months ago the association adopted an automated satellite-imaging review system that flags color, material, and structural deviations every quarter. We now face thirty-seven flagged properties, including one family who rebuilt their front porch in cast concrete and tinted glass in 2019 and now argues that enforcing a long-ignored rule through robotic precision is not justice but punishment disguised as order.

I have been trying to think through this using ideas I encountered in Blaise Pascal's Pensées, specifically the fragments about justice and force rather than the well-known wager argument. As I understand it, Pascal claims that justice without force is powerless, yet force without justice is mere tyranny, and he adds that people often obey authority not because it is genuinely fair but because it possesses authority — a kind of mystical foundation that makes law feel legitimate even when its content is arbitrary. Applying this to our situation, I find myself caught between two readings: on one hand, the 1978 rule existed for forty years without real enforcement, so it was justice without force, which Pascal might say was never truly effective law; on the other hand, if we suddenly activate it through an algorithm that never sleeps, we are introducing force and then draping it in the old clothing of justice, which seems closer to the tyranny he warned against.

Our panel must circulate a written recommendation by the end of the month recommending either retroactive enforcement against all flagged properties, a grandfathering cutoff tied to the imaging system's launch, or some intermediate phased standard, together with a clear timeline and an honest account of the strongest argument for the position we reject. I need this memo to take the family's Pascal-inspired objection seriously — that retroactive algorithmic enforcement is precisely unjust force clothed as restored justice — while also addressing the counter-claim that a charter that is never enforced eventually loses all authority and that justice may indeed require coupling rules with genuine, consistent power. The document should read as an institutional opinion, not an academic essay: a concrete recommendation, reasoning accessible to residents without philosophy training, and no appeals to outside authorities, since the panel must decide on its own terms.
