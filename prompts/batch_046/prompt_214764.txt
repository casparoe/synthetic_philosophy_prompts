I have been preparing a seminar on moral pedagogy through biography, and I want to clarify the cluster of commitments I hold about Plutarch, hagiography, and the ethics of exemplarity—though I suspect they pull in different directions. First, I treat Plutarch's Parallel Lives as an instrument of comparative moral calibration: by pairing Greek and Roman figures, the reader is invited to weigh different expressions of virtue and vice against one another, sharpening practical judgment without copying either subject. Second, I believe that biography teaches virtue only if it preserves the subject's full moral ambiguity; stripping away failure through heroic idealization or hagiographic sanitization destroys the very material from which practical wisdom is formed. Third, despite that, I think moral formation requires clear aspirational targets, and that sanitized, exemplary lives—including the streamlined narratives of traditional hagiography—provide an indispensable orientation that ambiguous, flawed lives cannot supply. Fourth, I take the comparative structure of the Lives to show that virtue-conceptions are deeply culturally embedded: what counts as magnanimity or temperance differs incommensurably between Greek and Roman contexts, so pairing reveals divergence rather than confirming universal moral traits. Fifth, I insist that biography should never serve direct behavioral imitation, because historical distance makes copying impossible; its value is purely dialectical, meant to provoke internal reflection rather than prescribe action.

If a single change could resolve the greatest number of conflicts, indicate which commitment should be revised and why. Do not summarize Plutarch scholarship or offer a general lecture on biography; trace the logical dependencies among these specific commitments.
