Please argue that the standard opposition between textualism and purposivism should be replaced by a bifurcated interpretive framework governed by what might be called a statute's "institutional inertia." Define institutional inertia as the density of accumulated reliance interests, administrative practice, and secondary regulation that embed a provision's operative meaning over time, such that reinterpretation would cause disproportionate social dislocation regardless of textual ambiguity or evolving legislative purpose. Defend the claim that high-inertia provisions—long-standing frameworks in property, contract, or commercial regulation—should be interpreted textually, not because language is self-interpreting, but because democratic legitimacy in those domains requires stability and protection of settled expectations. Conversely, argue that low-inertia provisions—recently enacted statutes addressing technologies or social conditions absent from the legislative vocabulary—should be interpreted purposively, since there are no entrenched reliance networks to safeguard and judicial adaptation carries lower democratic costs. Address the objection that this framework collapses into disguised purposivism, and explain why recognizing institutional inertia actually preserves textualism's authority in its proper sphere rather than undermining it. Illustrate with general categories of law rather than specific judicial opinions, and do not appeal to bibliographies, reading lists, or named scholarly works.
