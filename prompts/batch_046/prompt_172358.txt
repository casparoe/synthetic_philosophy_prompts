I'm leading a fifty-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of mind for students with no prior exposure to experimental philosophy. I want them to grasp the Knobe effect and its challenge to uniform theories of intentional action, but I will avoid the standard corporate-environment vignettes that circulate in popular writing. Instead, I will carry the session with a paired scenario about an architect who inadvertently improves a building's thermal performance versus one who achieves the same improvement deliberately while knowing it will obliterate a protected historic facade — one outcome praised, the other condemned — to see whether the valence asymmetry replicates outside ecological-moral frames. Design the teaching plan: specify the dependency order (what distinction, such as that between intentional action and mere foresight of side effects, must be in place before the scenarios are shown), identify the carrying example's essential features and why this framing preserves them, state the single misconception students will likely form — that the effect is just a bias in blame attribution rather than a feature of concept application — and the exact point in the sequence to head it off, propose one end-of-session diagnostic question that reveals whether they understand why the asymmetry threatens the idea of a single morally neutral concept of intentionality, and indicate which well-known extensions — for example, studies of non-moral side effects or cross-cultural variation — should be acknowledged only as existing literature and left unexplained. Focus on the explanation structure, not on assigned readings.
