As a novelist constructing a narrative cosmos in which only two identical protagonists exist, separated by an unbridgeable interval within an otherwise empty world, I sought clarification on Max Black's spheres and their bearing on Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. A fellow writer offered the following account: Leibniz maintained that no two distinct entities can share every property; Black responded by imagining a void containing solely two perfect spheres, separated by two miles, which are duplicates in all intrinsic respects and stand in precisely the same spatial relations—each being exactly two miles from the other—so they are numerically distinct yet entirely indiscernible, thereby refuting the principle in any non-trivial form. She further claimed that defenders of Leibniz typically retreat to hidden internal differences or to an observer-dependent frame, but Black preemptively eliminates both by leaving the universe empty and stripping the spheres of any internal structure, leaving no defensible reply. From this she concluded that the principle survives only in a defanged, qualitative version too weak to carry metaphysical weight.

This characterization feels suspiciously conclusive. Is it a fair summary? Please disentangle what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, and where it subtly misrepresents either Black's challenge or the range of Leibnizian responses—particularly with respect to relational properties, spatial location, and whether the argument genuinely defeats the principle or merely constrains its formulation. Where the summary overreaches, indicate what a careful defender would correct.
