I'm preparing a long-form feature on institutional ethics for a publication that covers science and ideas, specifically on how cross-disciplinary review panels navigate corruption allegations when Western legal categories encounter non-market reciprocity traditions. To frame the analysis, I've reconstructed the published ruling pattern of an interdisciplinary oversight body—let's call it the Committee on Institutional Reciprocity—across four cases. In the first, they held that a municipal official's acceptance of customary seasonal gifts from constituents, recorded in a public registry but with no linked policy discussion, did not amount to corruption, emphasizing the absence of transactional intent. In the second, they found corruption where a contractor funded luxury travel for regulators before a bidding round, even with no explicit agreement, citing the creation of a structural obligation. In the third, they permitted participation in a long-standing rotating gift-circle within an ethnic community, provided obligations were diffuse, reciprocal across the group, and fully disclosed, distinguishing collective reciprocity from targeted patronage. In the fourth, they divided: the majority ruled that a multinational's scholarship program for officials' children was corrupt because of foreseeable indirect benefit, while the minority defended it as legitimate social investment consistent with local norms.

The new case I'm examining involves a regional administrator who has accepted an unpaid, ceremonial role as honorary patron of a local cultural foundation. The foundation's director is a long-time personal acquaintance; the administrator's duties are limited to attending gala events partly funded by firms that occasionally compete for regional infrastructure contracts. No direct payment or gift reaches the administrator. However, the foundation recently received a tax exemption approved by the administrator's own office, and critics contend that the administrator's presence generates what I term a halo-effect obligation—a form of second-order reciprocity in which an official's social prestige is effectively borrowed by commercial sponsors to produce an atmosphere of privileged access, without any explicit quid pro quo. The administrator maintains that ceremonial patronage is standard civic practice in the region and that no contract has been awarded to any sponsor.

Given this record, I want you to identify the two or three most plausible interpretive rules that could coherently explain all four precedents, say precisely what each rule predicts about this new ceremonial-patronage case, and offer your best judgment—defended with reasoning—about what the Committee should decide for consistency. You should identify the closest analog among the four cases and explain why. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify what principle the Committee would be adopting with each possible ruling, and name one further hypothetical case that would clearly separate the competing rules. In doing so, please separate what is conceptually settled—such as the distinction between direct transactional corruption and diffuse social reciprocity—from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of institutional ethics, particularly regarding whether anti-corruption norms developed in Western administrative contexts can be applied cross-culturally without reductionism, and do not present any contested position as the consensus view.
