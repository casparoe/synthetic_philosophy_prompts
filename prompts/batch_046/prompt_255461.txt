I am a retired atmospheric physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up moral and political philosophy, particularly the conceptual analysis of institutions. I now serve on the Partnerships and Ethics Board of the Meridian Foundation, which funds international environmental-monitoring collaborations. We must render a formal decision on Proposal 2024-17: a multi-year river-basin study led by European investigators but executed largely through a local research collective in a region where formal contracts carry little enforceable weight. Sustained access and cooperation depend on reciprocal social obligations—ceremonial gifts marking project milestones, shared hospitality that establishes long-term trust, and the hiring of local staff through kinship and patronage networks rather than through documented credential review. The Foundation’s charter prohibits gifts exceeding nominal value and mandates strictly merit-based hiring, norms drawn explicitly from Western bureaucratic and legal frameworks. The applicants argue that enforcing these rules would make the fieldwork impossible, and that labeling these local practices as corruption wrongly universalizes a single institutional logic—bureaucratic impartiality—while ignoring that kinship-based obligation and patronage can operate as legitimate, parallel mechanisms of coordination and accountability in this context.

The philosophical issue is whether corruption should be understood functionally—as any practice that undermines the specific purpose of the institution in question, here the production of reliable, impartial scientific knowledge—or structurally—as any deviation from the procedural norms characteristic of one institutional form, regardless of whether those norms actually serve the institutional purpose in context. Draft the board’s decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions. The memo must open with the bottom-line recommendation in a single sentence, followed by reasoning that treats the strongest opposing consideration with full honesty—specifically, the risk that accommodating local norms introduces hidden bias into data collection or implicitly endorses a dual standard that the Foundation would not tolerate in other partnerships. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or named citations; build the argument conceptually so it stands on its own. Keep the response to no more than 450 words.
