Write a poem of exactly twenty-four lines, arranged in four six-line stanzas that alternate between two voices: one for Mencius and one for Xunzi. Give each voice one refrain line—Mencius's should evoke the four internal sprouts of compassion, shame, deference, and discernment growing toward virtue without external forcing; Xunzi's should evoke the carpenter's frame, the grinder's stone, and ritual as external corrections applied to wood that is naturally crooked. Each refrain must appear twice: once in the first half of the poem and once in the second half. The stanzas must interleave so that the two voices never share a stanza until the final couplet, where the two refrain lines appear side by side without either being grammatically or metrically subordinated to the other. The form should do philosophical work: the shared premise—that human nature requires transformation to become good—should be audible in the parallel structures, while the disagreement—whether transformation is cultivation of an internal root or imposition of an external form—should be visible in the contrasting imagery. Render the ideas without quoting either text directly; use fresh metaphors that a reader familiar with the debate will recognize but that do not depend on verbatim recall. The tone should be lyrical rather than expository, and the disagreement must remain open at the end: the closing juxtaposition should feel like two premises passing each other rather than a synthesis. Make the poem accurate enough that a student of Chinese philosophy can assign each voice correctly, yet complete enough to stand on its own as a poem about the conditions under which virtue arises.
