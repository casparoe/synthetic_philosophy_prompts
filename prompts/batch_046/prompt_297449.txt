As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I want to understand the hidden assumptions behind a practice I see at my company.

We are a large technology firm where engineering leadership uses birth-year cohorts — Gen Z (roughly 1997–2012), Millennials (1981–1996), Gen X (1965–1980), and Boomers (1946–1964) — as a formal taxonomy across three internal systems. Second, an annual compensation-band review applies generational labor-market expectation weights, treating these cohorts as having systematically different risk-tolerance and equity-preference profiles that should adjust offer packaging. Third, our internal documentation wiki uses cohort tags, assigned automatically by birth year, to trigger generation-relevant onboarding context, so that the same API guide is presented with different framing narratives depending on whether the reader is tagged Gen Z or Millennial. Managers also manually annotate cultural alignment scores that rate how closely an individual's work style matches the cohort stereotype.

The official HR documentation insists these tags are purely statistical aggregates lacking essential properties, used only for predictive analytics, and emphasizes that individual variance is enormous. Yet the algorithm treats cohort membership as predictive of cognitive and communicative habits, the compensation system treats it as predictive of economic preferences, and the wiki treats it as predictive of epistemic needs — all in ways that seem to go beyond mere correlation.

My question is: what philosophical commitments about the reality and nature of social groups does this institutional design presuppose? Does it require that generations are real kinds with causal powers — perhaps shaping how people think, communicate, or value compensation — or can the same design be licensed by a purely nominalist view that birth-year ranges are arbitrary bins with no intrinsic social significance? I am especially puzzled by the tension between the anti-essentialist disclaimer and the operational treatment of these categories as having stable, predictive properties.
