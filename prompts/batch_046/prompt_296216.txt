I've been mulling over the inside-view versus outside-view debate in forecasting and decision theory, and I'm genuinely stuck on why the discussion about epistemic modesty hasn't shifted in years. On one side you've got people in the Tetlock tradition arguing that we ought to privilege the outside view almost everywhere, because individual reasoning is noisy, overconfident, and systematically biased — if a reference class of similar people failed at X, that's a direct reason for me to doubt I'll succeed. On the other side you've got philosophers and domain experts saying this collapses once you leave prediction and enter normative or conceptual territory: there is no meaningful reference class for "philosophers reasoning about justice" or "mathematicians judging elegance," so applying outside-view humility here is either empty or just disguised scepticism about expertise itself. Both sides accept the psychology of overconfidence, yet they haven't moved an inch on whether that fact generates normative reasons in ethics, metaphysics, or even highly technical science.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it substantive — a real clash about the scope of epistemic norms and what counts as a reason for belief — or are they answering different questions under one label, with one camp focused on predictive accuracy and the other on justificatory structure? There's also a hidden empirical possibility: maybe philosophers do perform measurably better than laypeople on some structured benchmark, or maybe they don't, and the stalemate is partly fuelled by nobody actually knowing. But I also keep circling back to a more specific puzzle that might be the real engine: the problem of referance-class collapse when you try to apply the outside view to your own reasoning. If I ask "what's the base rate for people like me being right about this argument," do I use all philosophers, all philosophers with my training, all who share my premises, or all who share my political commitments? Each selection either dilutes the data into noise or smuggles the inside view back in through the back door by defining "like me." Has anyone solved this, or is it the hidden reason both sides talk past each other?

Please diagnose the stalemate concretely — tell me whether it's verbal, substantive, about different questions, or bedrock intuition — and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal model, a principled distinction between predictive and normative domains, an empirical study of expert performance, or perhaps just admitting there's no single right answer. Separate what's fairly settled, like the basic psychology of overconfidence and reference-class reasoning, from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present one side's view as the consensus.
