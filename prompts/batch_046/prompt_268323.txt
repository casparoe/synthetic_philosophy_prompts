I want a consistency audit of a philosophical profile I'm developing on rational belief-formation, epistemic modesty, and the relationship between the inside view and the outside view. Here is where I've landed; I need to know whether these commitments hang together.

(1) First-order belief-formation is governed primarily by the inside view: a rational agent should weight her beliefs according to the reasoning, evidence, and arguments accessible from her first-person perspective, not according to statistical base rates about people in general.

(2) Epistemic modesty is a normative requirement, not merely a pragmatic heuristic: agents ought to calibrate their confidence downward when they belong to a reference class with a demonstrably high error rate in the relevant domain.

(3) Applying the outside view to one's own reasoning generates a self-application problem: if I use my inside-view reasoning to conclude that my reasoning is unreliable, that very conclusion is itself a product of the reasoning I have deemed unreliable, yielding either an infinite regress or an arbitrary stopping point.

(4) I block this regress by distinguishing transparent reasoning processes—those whose steps are fully introspectively accessible and, in that respect, self-certifying—from opaque processes, which are not. The outside view applies with full authority only to beliefs produced by opaque mechanisms; transparent reasoning is exempt from reference-class calibration.

(5) The reference class to which an agent belongs cannot be defined by her own inside-view assessment of her competence; it must be fixed by objective, third-person criteria (e.g., cognitive profile, training, domain, contextual conditions).

(6) Epistemic modesty concerns not only confidence levels but the structure of justification: a modest agent holds her beliefs with defeasible authority, always leaving them open to revision by outside-view data that meets appropriate evidential standards.

(7) When inside-view reasoning and outside-view statistics deliver contradictory verdicts—say, I have carefully reasoned that P, but 85% of people in my objectively defined reference class who reach structurally similar conclusions in this domain are wrong—the correct response is not to split the difference but to suspend judgment, because both sources are authoritative and they conflict.
