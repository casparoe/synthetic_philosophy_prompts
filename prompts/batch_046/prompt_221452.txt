Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on democratic legitimacy, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to prove that constitutional monarchy is structurally incompatible with democratic equality, yet I am reluctant to abandon the intuition behind the first premise. I want to know exactly where the reasoning slips, and what I pay if I reject the step you identify.

(1) Democratic equality, in its strongest defensible form, requires that every citizen have an equal formal opportunity to influence the selection of anyone who exercises discretionary state power.

(2) In the constitutional monarchies of the United Kingdom, Sweden, and Japan, the head of state is selected by hereditary succession and holds constitutionally real powers—appointment of prime ministers, dissolution of legislatures, formal assent to legislation—whose exercise is constrained only by political convention, not by enforceable law.

(3) If those powers are genuinely discretionary, then citizens lack equal influence over one office that exercises state power, violating (1).

(4) If those powers are not genuinely discretionary—if they are pure legal fictions that the monarch can never independently exercise—then the constitution systematically misrepresents the actual distribution of authority, requiring citizens to maintain a collective fiction in which a hereditary figure is treated as a co-equal source of sovereignty.

(5) A collective fiction of this kind is not neutral symbolism; it structurally assigns a unique, birth-determined status to one family, which is incompatible with the principle that no citizen is born into political authority over others.

(6) Therefore, constitutional monarchy either violates democratic equality in practice, or violates it in principle by institutionalizing a non-elective, hereditary locus of symbolic sovereignty.

(7) Yet defenders argue that these monarchies function as democracies because conventions are stable and the monarch's role is purely ceremonial.

(8) But if the role is purely ceremonial, then the constitutional allocation of real powers to the monarch is either deceptive or redundant, and the system could be replaced by a republican framework without functional loss—yet it is not replaced.

(9) So the argument returns to its dilemma: either (1) must be weakened so that it applies only to offices with actual discretion, or (5) must be rejected by showing that symbolic hierarchy does not entail political subordination.

Where exactly does this overreach? Is there an equivocation in (2) between powers that are "constitutionally real" and powers that are "politically available"? Does (4) conflate a descriptive legal form with a normative claim about citizens' self-understanding? Or does (5) commit a modal slide from "assigns unique status" to "violates equality"? If I keep (1) and (3), must I accept (6), or can I block the inference by denying that symbolic sovereignty counts as an exercise of state power? And if the flaw lies elsewhere—perhaps in the move from (7) to (8)—what does repairing it cost my view of why these institutions persist?
