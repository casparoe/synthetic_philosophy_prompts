I have recently been forced to think seriously about the cogito after recovering from a prolonged dissociative episode—apparently triggered by sleep deprivation and an adverse reaction to an anticonvulsant—during which I experienced what I can only call ownerless cognition: judgments, memories, and even inner speech seemed to occur without any felt sense that they belonged to a continuous “me.” Reading Descartes now, I keep assuming that his argument establishes not just a bare, instantaneous existent, but specifically a unified, diachronically persistent subject—someone who owns the present thought and was identically the owner of prior ones. Given that during my episode thinking seemed to unfold without such an owner, and given that I now recall those thoughts without feeling I am the same person who underwent them, I wonder whether my case functions as an empirical counterexample to the cogito. Or should I take Descartes to have proven only a “thin,” momentary flash of being—so minimal that it carries no commitment to personal identity, temporal continuity, or the ownership of mental states? I want to know precisely what the argument is licensed to deliver, and whether my experience exposes a genuine boundary in its conclusion or merely reflects a misapprehension of what “I am” is meant to signify in that context.
