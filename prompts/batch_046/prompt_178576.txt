I'm coordinating an adversarial-collaboration exercise for an interdisciplinary seminar on explanation in the sciences. Two participants—one a committed greedy reductionist who accepts the crane-versus-skyhook distinction but insists that every genuine explanation eventually cashes out at the fundamental physical level, and one a defender of autonomous macro-level explanation who holds that higher-level sciences deploy irreducible cranes—have agreed to produce a joint statement rather than continue talking past each other.

Please draft the document they should sign. Begin with a numbered list of claims both can endorse: for instance, about the formal adequacy of microphysical descriptions, about the heuristic value of multi-level modeling, and about the fact that no empirical finding currently violates lower-level laws. Next, present the residual disagreements in clearly delineated sections: formulate the reductionist's position in wording they would accept, such as the claim that macro-explanations are always replaceable in principle by more fundamental ones even if not in practice, and formulate the emergentist's position in wording they would accept, such as the claim that some macro-patterns carry explanatory information that cannot be recovered from any feasible microphysical account without loss. Then identify at least three cruxes—such as whether explanatory exclusivity holds across levels, whether inter-level feedback loops constitute downward causation, and whether predictive success at one level can ever be genuinely independent of lower-level dynamics. For each crux, specify a concrete case or thought experiment both parties agree is relevant; examples might include the explanatory role of natural selection, the use of renormalization-group methods in condensed-matter physics, or the contribution of cognitive architectures in neuroscience. Record, for each side, what outcome or feature of the case they would predict or emphasize. Conclude with a brief assessment of whether any portion of their disagreement is verbal—particularly around terms such as reduction, emergence, and causal power—and, if so, which part and why. The statement should be formal, neutral, and suitable for circulation to the seminar; avoid bibliographies, citations, or appeals to specific texts that would require verification, and do not ask for reading lists. Keep the reasoning self-contained so that a well-informed reader without web access can follow every step.
