I am preparing a long-form magazine interview for my science-and-ideas column with an unnamed philosopher who has spent the last decade defending what she calls grammatical framing invariance: the claim that cross-cultural divergence in philosophical intuitions—whether about Gettier-style knowledge failures, modal essentialism, or reference fixation—largely disappears once experiments control not merely for language family but for how a culture’s primary epistemic-modal and evidential verbs distribute force. She argues that well-known comparative studies have systematically mistaken pragmatic calibration effects for deep conceptual pluralism, and she proposes using deontic-shift scenario pairs—cases in which an obligation-bearing frame is inverted without altering propositional content—to isolate genuinely conceptual responses from grammatically mediated ones. I need a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that treat this as a substantive empirical-cum-philosophical thesis rather than allowing her to retreat into generic skepticism about intuition pumps.

Specifically, I want at least one question pressing why grammatical framing should be privileged over institutional testimony structures—say, differential exposure to scientific authority or legal formalism—as the primary engine of divergence; one that anticipates her likely reply that modal-verb syntax tracks conceptual architecture and asks what specific empirical result, perhaps involving obligatory evidential marking in languages where speakers nonetheless show robust divergence in biological-kind essentialism judgments, would actually falsify that tracking claim; and one that forces her to address whether her framework can accommodate reported divergence in essentialist judgments about natural kinds among speakers of languages with obligatory evidentials, given that shared evidential grammar ought, on her view, to suppress precisely that divergence. For each of these, please draft the precise follow-up I should deploy if she answers with her standard deflection—that the divergence is merely surface-level pragmatic noise—so that the deflection becomes unavailable without her abandoning the core thesis. The tone should suit a serious magazine profile: rigorous but accessible, precise enough that she cannot answer with a slogan, and structured so that the interview builds rather than repeating the same challenge at different volumes.
