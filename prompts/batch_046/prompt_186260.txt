Ok so I've been trying to make sense of Maimonides on sacrifice and I think I've backed myself into a corner with this argument. (1) He says in the Guide that animal sacrifice was basically a divine concesson — people were too used to worshipping animals and idols so God let them keep the form but redirect it toward Himself. (2) He also says the Law is eternal and these sacrificial rules are never going away. (3) Now, some folks read him as saying the concession isn't just for ancient Israelites but for human nature generally: we always need embodied ritual before we can do pure intellectual worship. (4) If that's true, then the concession is permanent in principle, which saves (2). (5) But then it starts to look like sacrifice isn't just a second-best workaround — it has a permanent pedagogical or even cosmological function, almost like training wheels that are indispensible. (6) If it has that kind of intrinsic value, then his whole story about why God didn't just start with prayer collapses: he could have given both from the start, with sacrifice as the embodied path and prayer as the goal. (7) So either he's contradicting himself, or he's secretly committed to sacrifice being indispensible in a way he never admits.

Where's the slip? I feel like there's a hidden move between (3) and (5) — maybe a permanent pedagogical need doesn't make something intrinsically valuable, just permanently useful? But then I'm back to the QWERTY problem: why embed it in eternal law instead of treating it as optional? Tell me which step is weakest and what it costs to reject it. I'd rather keep (3) than (5) if I have to choose, but I'm not sure. And please say what would change your mind — maybe a clear distinction between pedagogical and intrinsic value, or a result showing concessionary laws don't have to expire once the original reason fades. Where's your reasoning most likely to fail?
