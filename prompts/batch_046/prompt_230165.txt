I'm trying to pin down why an argument I sketched for a reading group feels like it hides a slide. The claim is that Bayesian confirmation makes old evidence either worthless or omnipotent, and so can't recover scientific judgment.

(1) Standard Bayesian updating says E confirms H for an agent with background K only if P(H|E,K) > P(H|K).
(2) If E is old—already in K—then conditioning is redundant: P(H|E,K) = P(H|K). So old evidence gives zero diachronic confirmation.
(3) The usual reply is synchronic: compare explanatory coherence across rivals, applying a "design penalty" to theories constructed post-hoc to fit E.
(4) But a single agent at one time holds exactly one prior over one hypothesis space; there is no internal "before-E" state available for comparison.
(5) So the synchronic reply requires simulating a counterfactual prior—what she would have assigned before knowing E—but Bayesian rationality gives no privileged status to counterfactual credences; any such simulation is just another modeling choice.
(6) If we allow those simulations freely, then a model with enough continuous free parameters can also claim a high counterfactual prior, since fitting parameters makes P(E|M,θ) ≈ 1 across a large volume of θ-space with negligible prior cost per point.
(7) Thus Bayesianism either denies all old-evidence confirmation (sticking to actual updates) or permits it indiscriminately for ad hoc parameterized models (via loose counterfactual priors).
(8) Science clearly occupies a middle ground: GR's explanation of Mercury's perihelion is valuable, but not every curve-fitted regression on old data is equally supported.
(9) Hence Bayesianism proves too much in both directions.

Where does this go wrong? If I have to preserve one claim, I'd rather keep (2) than (8). Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis, identify the specific step or distinction you're least sure about, and price what giving up that step costs—especially whether it forces us to accept counterfactual priors as rationally legitimate or to bite the bullet that old-evidence support is always illusory.
