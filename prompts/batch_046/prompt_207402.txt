I am delivering the opening remarks tomorrow at a workshop on public memorials, before an audience that includes professional historians, municipal planners, and relatives of those being commemorated; the occasion is the unveiling of a neighborhood plaque. Over the past month, while clearing my late grandmother’s apartment, I found three incompatible traces of the same wartime episode: a heroic letter she sent to a newspaper, an unsorted archive of daily receipts and private diaries, and a handwritten note quietly debunking the family legend that she had been a resistance fighter. I want an eight-minute address that takes Nietzsche’s distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history not as ornament but as structural architecture: the speech should move through each mode, show why each is indispensable for honoring the dead and why each becomes destructive if it dominates, and it must conclude without resolving which mode should govern the inscription on the plaque. The framework should shape what I can and cannot claim about memory and commemoration; half the room will recognize the categories immediately, the other half will have never heard of them, and neither group should feel lectured or excluded. Write the address in my voice—formal but personally exposed—so that the philosophical tension remains live rather than being resolved into a lesson.
