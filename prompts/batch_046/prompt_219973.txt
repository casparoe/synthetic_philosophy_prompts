Write an essay on the dispute between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are genuine experiences. Malcolm holds that dream reports are retrospective constructions formed upon waking, not memories of occurrent mental episodes; Dennett, by contrast, allows that something experiential occurs during sleep, but argues that the narrative coherence of a dream is often imposed at the moment of recall rather than lived through in sleep. Rather than treating this as a simple either/or, examine whether both positions share an unexamined premise: that experience, if present, must possess minimal temporal integration and a self-referential structure. Develop the alternative that dreams might be “phenomenal fragments”—brief, disintegrated sensory episodes that lack the narrative binding and egocentric framing characteristic of waking consciousness. If this intermediate category is coherent, it suggests that dreams are neither full-blown experiences (as Dennett’s account often implies) nor pure confabulations (as Malcolm insists), but rather degraded phenomenal events whose experiential status is genuinely ambiguous. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: are dreams best understood as degraded experiences, as post-hoc narratives, or as phenomenal fragments of an intermediate kind? In doing so, address the epistemological obstacle—how, if at all, can we verify the presence or absence of consciousness during sleep without circular reliance on the report itself?—and consider whether the requirement of a narrative self for experience is itself defensible or an arbitrary stipulation imported from waking life.
