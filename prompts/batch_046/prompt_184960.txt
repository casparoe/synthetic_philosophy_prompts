Here is an argument that seems to prove far too much about Sartre's doctrine of radical freedom. (1) For Sartre, human reality is radically free—condemned to choose its values and always able to do otherwise, even under apparent coercion. (2) Bad faith (mauvaise foi) is the refusal to own this freedom, typically by treating oneself as a pure being-in-itself with fixed properties, or by appealing to an external determinant—social role, law, or command—as if it mechanically compelled one's action and eliminated the possibility of refusal. (3) Institutional roles (judge, soldier, physician) are precisely structures in which agents justify actions by reference to external norms rather than personal choice: the judge applies statute, the soldier obeys orders, the physician follows protocol. (4) In each case the agent implicitly denies that they could have acted differently, treating the role as a facticity that cancels transcendence. (5) Therefore anyone who acts within an institutional role and justifies that action by reference to the role is necessarily in bad faith. (6) Yet Sartre also holds that being-for-others is constitutive, and in his later analysis of collective existence he describes the practico-inert—the materialized, sedimented products of past praxis—as an unavoidable field within which all social action occurs. (7) So institutional embeddedness is unavoidable for human reality, but according to (5) it is also necessarily bad faith—meaning Sartre is committed to the view that unavoidable social existence is structurally self-deceptive. (8) This is intolerable: it collapses the distinction between unavoidable facticity and culpable denial of freedom, and it makes moral evaluation of institutional conduct impossible because every participant is equally in bad faith.
