I've been trying to work out my own position on Buddhist anatta and its relationship to personal identity, and I want to know whether the package I've assembled is genuinely coherent or whether I'm quietly helping myself to incompatible motivations. Here is where I've landed, in five parts. First, I accept the classical Theravada Abhidharma analysis: a person is nothing over and above a stream of momentary mental and physical events—the five skandhas—with no underlying substratum, owner, or persisting consciousness that could properly be called 'the self'; talk of 'my experience' is therefore always a conventional designation rather than a metaphysical report. Second, I think Derek Parfit's psychological-continuity reductionism is broadly correct about what grounds prudential concern, in that special care for a future stage is justified not by strict numerical identity but by the right kind of connectedness and continuity of psychology and concern, though I want to keep this insight within a Buddhist framework rather than importing a Western metaphysics of persons. Third, I hold that if anatta is taken seriously, then retributive moral responsibility is conceptually impossible, since there is no enduring moral agent who could deserve punishment or reward; at the same time, however, I think that in cases where a wrongdoer retains vivid memory and active endorsement of their past action, reactive attitudes such as resentment are not merely instrumentally useful for social regulation but genuinely fitting responses. Fourth, I believe the doctrine of pratityasamutpada implies that harming another being is, in a substantive sense, harming oneself, but I insist this must be read as deep causal and affective entanglement rather than as any mystical metaphysical identity between self and other. Fifth, I take the self to be a practical fiction—structurally analogous to a legal corporation—constructed for social coordination and narrative coherence, with real practical utility but zero ontological weight. Do these five commitments hang together? I am particularly uneasy about whether my claim in (3) that resentment can be genuinely fitting conflicts with my denial in (1) of any enduring agent and with my reductionist framework in (2), and whether my use of 'oneself' in (4) silently reintroduces the very persisting subject I reject. At the same time, I have a hunch that there is an apparent tension between (2) and (4)—between treating psychological continuity as a basis for prudential concern and treating the self as a mere fiction—that might dissolve once we carefully separate metaphysical reality from practical convention, but I am not sure whether that dissolution holds up or whether it collapses one of the two. I am not asking for a general survey of Buddhist philosophy or a literature review; I want a precise audit of this specific belief set, ordered by the severity of each tension, with each conflict spelled out exactly, the pairs that only appear to conflict clearly distinguished from those that genuinely do, and the cheapest path to consistency mapped out—including which commitment I would have to weaken or abandon and what that would cost me conceptually. Please respond in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter.
