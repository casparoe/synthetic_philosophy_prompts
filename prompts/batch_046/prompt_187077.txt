I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a friend about a conflict on our neighborhood association board. A bylaw—written after a single balcony collapse decades ago—absolutely forbids any attachment to exterior railings. Now, an elderly resident has added a small, engineer-certified handhold to her fourth-floor railing so she can safely tend to her plants; it bears no structural load and is barely visible from the street. The board must vote on whether to compel its removal. My friend argues that allowing this exception, however humane, dissolves the boundary between collective agreement and private judgment; he claims that a rule’s value lies precisely in its resistance to case-by-case revision, otherwise residents learn to treat regulations as suggestions calibrated to personal sympathy rather than as shared architecture. I argued back that applying a safety rule where no safety interest exists is not fidelity but an idolatry of form, and that conscience demands we see the resident rather than the bylaw. I am genuinely unsure which of us has the stronger position, because both intuitions feel deep. Is there a principled way to decide whether consistency itself is a moral good that can outweigh harmless compassion, or is that view merely a dressed-up preference for order? Answer in no more than four sentences: state your bottom-line judgment first, then your reasoning.
