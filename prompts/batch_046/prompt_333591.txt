I serve on the acquisitions ethics board of a major literary archive, and I am also a novelist whose recent work concerns invented economies and gender systems; this dual position is why the chair asked me to draft the opinion for Thursday's vote. We have been offered the complete papers of the Meridian Collective, a semi-isolated agricultural commune active from 1952 to 1988, which maintained an internal exchange system using reciprocity tokens that the Collective explicitly denied were money, and recognized three lifecycle roles—sower, reaper, and threshold—that they insisted were not gender categories but phases of human agricultural being. Our head archivist argues we should catalog the economic records under alternative currency and the membership rolls under non-binary gender taxonomy, with annotations, because standard Library headings ensure grant compliance and researcher access. Our liaison, who interviewed surviving members, contends that imposing these headings commits an ontological misrepresentation: if social kinds are genuinely constructed by collective practice rather than discovered, then applying external taxonomic categories that the Collective explicitly rejected is not neutral documentation but an act of conceptual colonization. The board must recommend by Thursday whether to accept the archive under standard taxonomy with annotations, create bespoke catalog categories that exist only in our system, or defer acquisition until the heirs consent to classification—each option carrying different institutional risks and philosophical implications. Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate: it should state a concrete recommendation with a specific time frame for implementation or deferral, engage seriously with the archivist's practical objections and the liaison's metaphysical objection, treating the latter as more than sentimentality, and address whether our decision requires us to take a stance on whether money and gender are socially constructed kinds in general, or whether we can act while remaining philosophically agnostic. The opinion should be written for board members who are not philosophers, but should not oversimplify the conceptual stakes.
