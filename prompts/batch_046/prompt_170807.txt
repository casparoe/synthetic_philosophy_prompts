I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of science preparing a dissertation chapter on the demarcation problem. In a recent graduate seminar, two colleagues—one who maintains that Karl Popper’s falsifiability criterion remains the only non-arbitrary logical boundary between science and pseudoscience, and one who defends a modified Imre Lakatos-style research-programme view in which the evaluation of progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts replaces logical falsifiability—spent the session arguing past each other without converging. Their dispute kept shifting registers: the Popperian insisted that without the logical asymmetry between verification and falsification there is no normative distinction whatsoever, while the Lakatosian replied that every mature science protects its theoretical hard core with auxiliary hypotheses, so the genuine normative work is done by assessing whether adjustments yield novel empirical predictions, not by asking whether an isolated statement is syntactically falsifiable. I suspect part of their disagreement is verbal—specifically, about whether “demarcation” functions as an honorific classification or as a descriptive taxonomy—while another part is substantive, particularly concerning whether immunization strategies necessarily defeat falsificationism and whether “progressiveness” can be characterized without circular appeal to prior judgments about scientific success.

Rather than asking me to adjudicate, they have agreed—reluctantly—to produce an adversarial-collaboration document. Please draft the joint statement they should sign. The document should contain four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both can endorse: about the logical structure of falsification and the Duhem-Quine thesis; about the actual historical behavior of research communities when confronted with recalcitrant anomalies; about what a normative demarcation is intended to achieve for scientific practice; and about the distinction between logical, methodological, and sociological senses of “scientific.” Second, a disagreement table in which each camp’s position is stated in wording they would accept, addressing specifically: whether falsifiability is necessary, sufficient, or neither; whether all immunization is methodologically vicious or whether some auxiliary adjustments are legitimate; whether progressiveness can be defined independently of consensus judgments about success; and whether any residual normative role remains for logical vulnerability once methodological evaluation is admitted. Third, the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party—including whether a research program that systematically avoids risky predictions should be classified as pseudoscientific or merely as bad science, and whether the long persistence of Newtonian mechanics in the face of well-known anomalies undermines the Popperian claim that scientists actually reject falsified theories. For each crux, provide a concrete hypothetical case or well-known historical pattern that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side’s explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates. Fourth, identify precisely which portion of their dispute is verbal—specify the term or terms at issue and explain why the disagreement dissolves once the ambiguity is removed—and which portion remains genuinely substantive. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for circulation as a philosophy-of-science working document, and do not include bibliographic references, citation lists, or requests for further reading.
