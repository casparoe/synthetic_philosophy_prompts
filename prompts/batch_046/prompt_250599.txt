I've never studied philosophy in any formal setting, but I've spent a long time thinking on my own about whether people have real, stable characters or whether our behavior is mostly driven by whatever situation we're in. Virtue ethicists, as I understand them, claim that traits like courage or honesty are genuine features of a person that shape how they act across different contexts. Situationists, by contrast, point to well-known experiments showing that small changes in circumstance — whether someone is in a rush, whether they're alone or in a crowd, whether an authority figure is present — seem to predict behavior far better than any supposed inner trait does. For decades, the exchange has followed the same pattern: philosophers argue that these experiments miss what virtue actually is, because virtue involves practical wisdom and long-term orientation rather than reflexive response; psychologists reply that if a trait never reliably predicts behavior, then defending it starts to look like moving the goalposts. Nobody seems to move.

My own hypothesis is that this stall isn't mainly about missing data. I think both sides may be using the word "character" for two different things: one side treating it as a statistical predictor of actions, the other treating it as a normative framework for evaluating a whole life. To see whether evidence could even break the deadlock, I've imagined a study I call a "longitudinal micro-dilemma protocol" — tracking hundreds of people through thousands of very low-stakes moral choices spread across a full year, measuring consistency not as a single score but as a narrative pattern of how someone reasons, revises, and returns to commitments over time. Even with that kind of data, I suspect each camp might just reinterpret the findings through its own lens.

So I want a diagnosis, not a victory for either side. Is this disagreement verbal, with "character" playing two different roles? Is there a hidden empirical question — perhaps about the right scale or grain of behavioral measurement — that could actually settle it? Or are the two sides answering entirely different questions, one about how people ought to be assessed and one about how they actually behave, while using the same vocabulary? Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, a particular kind of study, a formal result, or should we accept that the question is ill-posed and stop expecting resolution?
