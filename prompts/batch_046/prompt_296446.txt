I am a doctoral student in aesthetics and philosophy of art, and during a recent seminar prep session with two fellow graduate students—both working on political philosophy and the history of African American thought—we got into an extended disagreement about how to interpret W. E. B. Du Bois's claim in his well-known essay on the criteria for Negro art that art is necessarily propaganda. We were preparing for a session on aesthetic autonomy, and the dispute turned on whether this propaganda thesis is meant primarily as a normative prescription directed at black artists—urging them to abandon what Du Bois regards as a misguided pursuit of disinterested formal beauty—or whether it functions instead as a descriptive, universal sociological observation that applies to every artistic tradition and therefore carries no particular prescriptive force for black creators specifically.

My colleague A argued for the normative reading. She maintained that Du Bois is clearly chastising those black artists who seek only technical perfection or pure aesthetic form without an explicit message of racial advocacy; on her view, Du Bois treats the propaganda function not as an unavoidable structural fact but as a duty that black artists have been wrongly taught to neglect in favor of white-defined standards of universality. When I asked whether that reading doesn't reduce artistic autonomy to political messaging, she insisted that Du Bois is precisely rejecting the myth of autonomy—what she called autotelic formalism—as an ideology that masks the real social function of art.

My colleague B resisted this interpretation. He contended that Du Bois is making a structural, descriptive claim: every artwork serves some interest or ideology, so identifying black art as propaganda merely strips away the false mystification that white European art is somehow neutral or universal. B added that if Du Bois intended a normative command, the essay would be redundant, since he already establishes that propaganda is unavoidable; the real work, B said, is epistemic—correcting black artists' false consciousness about their own practice—rather than prescriptive. A replied that B is illegitimately importing a Marxist framework of false consciousness that Du Bois never explicitly invokes, and that B's reading eliminates artistic agency by collapsing all aesthetic choice into ideological determination. B countered that A, in defending artistic autonomy, is precisely reproducing the mystification Du Bois aims to dismantle, and that without acknowledging the descriptive universality of propaganda, one cannot see why black formalism is especially self-deceptive.

Could you adjudicate this exchange round by round? For each move—A's normative reading, B's descriptive counter, A's charge of redundancy, B's epistemic-correction reply, A's false-consciousness objection, and B's final charge of mystification—please assess whether the point is well-supported by Du Bois's position, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with brief justification. Also tell me what both interlocutors appear to be missing about how the descriptive and normative dimensions might interact in Du Bois's argument, rather than treating them as mutually exclusive. Please do not declare an overall winner; I am interested in the point-by-point verdict, and in whether both parties are overlooking a middle path between prescriptive advocacy and structural description.
