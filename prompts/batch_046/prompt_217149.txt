I hold that capital punishment can only be justified on retributive grounds, and specifically on a symmetry principle: the punishment must match the wrong in kind, which is why life imprisonment, however long, can never truly equal taking a life. At the same time, I think this symmetry is conceptually fragile—if the state executes an innocent person, it does not merely commit an empirical error, but becomes an agent that deliberately inflicts undeserved death, which is structurally identical to murder. So my view is that execution is justified in principle, but self-defeating in practice, because any real-world risk of error corrupts the very retributive logic that authorizes it. What would count against this? I want more than the usual objections: which observations, arguments, or hypothetical cases should actually move me—say, proof that deterrence works specifically for calculated killings, or a scenario in which verification technology eliminates error risk entirely? Which of these would force me to abandon the symmetry requirement, and which would merely be awkward? What would a committed defender of this view concede as genuinely damaging, and what would they dismiss as missing the point? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, and tell me honestly whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every failure is explained away by saying "the conditions weren't perfect," has the view become empty?
