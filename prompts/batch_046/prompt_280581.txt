I am writing to request your critical assessment of a short philosophical essay I have drafted for an informal seminar I am attending since my retirement. After thirty-five years in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have recently begun studying philosophy of time in earnest, and I find the standard dialectic between presentism and eternalism somewhat unsatisfying when examined through the lens of thermodynamic asymmetry. The notes below sketch a position I have tentatively called local thermal presentism, and I would be grateful for your detailed comments on its coherence, on whether it misapplies physical concepts to metaphysical questions, and on whether the central move genuinely distinguishes itself from eternalism in disguise.

The core argument runs as follows. Conventional presentism holds that only present objects and events exist, yet it faces a well-known difficulty in special relativity: without absolute simultaneity, there is no unique global hyperplane that can serve as the ontologically privileged now. Eternalists resolve this by treating all times as equally real within a four-dimensional block. I wish to propose, however, that presentism can be rehabilitated if we abandon the idea of a universal present and replace it with a locally defined temporal boundary tied to the thermodynamic arrow. For any localized system, the present can be characterized as the spatiotemporal region in which the local entropy gradient determines informational accessibility—specifically, the boundary where records of lower-entropy states remain physically available, while configurations corresponding to higher-entropy futures remain causally open but not yet actualized for that system. Because local entropy gradients do not depend on a globally synchronized coordinate frame, each localized observer-system can possess a genuine ontological present without requiring a universal slice of simultaneity. The past then comprises those local states that have left irreversible traces, and the future comprises thermodynamically possible configurations not yet realized within that boundary. Temporal flow, on this view, is not an illusion projected onto a static block but an emergent feature of local thermodynamic openness; and because different local systems may possess differently oriented or extended boundaries, there is no contradiction with relativistic simultaneity relations.

I am particularly concerned that I may be conflating an epistemic limitation—our inability to access future macrostates—with an ontological claim about existence. I also worry that by allowing different local presents, I have merely redescribed a block universe with extra epistemic decoration, or that I have mischaracterized the relation between entropy and causation in a manner that a philosopher of physics would find naive. Could you please provide a point-by-point evaluation: first, on whether the inference from local entropy gradients to localized ontological presence is valid or commits a category error; second, on whether the view genuinely avoids standard objections to presentism or simply relocates them; and third, on whether framing temporal flow as thermodynamic openness adds anything conceptually new, or whether it is essentially a restatement of existing process-philosophy or causal-set approaches? I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations, but rather a direct assessment of the reasoning as presented.
