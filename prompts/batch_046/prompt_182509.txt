I've spent the last few months working through relational and communitarian critiques of liberal personhood—mostly the Ubuntu tradition and the care-ethics literature on relational autonomy—after a long thread on rationalist forums about whether "person" is a primitive or a derived status. I keep running into the same conceptual knot, and I'm not sure whether it has an established name or whether I'm carving at a genuinely unrecognized joint. Before you answer, please think step by step: start by brainstorming the nearest established distinctions—structural versus historical identity, relational essentialism versus functionalism, narrative continuity, social constitution—then map exactly where the described phenomenon sits relative to each, and only then address the core identification question.

Here is the pattern in concrete terms. In arguments that ground moral status or personal identity in social relations, defenders often insist that particular relationships are metaphysically constitutive: not merely that I happen to stand in caring relations, but that I am partly made of them, so that destroying a specific bond damages me in a way that destroying a replaceable social role does not. Yet when confronted with a gradual-replacement thought experiment—in which every one of my particular relationships is substituted by functionally identical analogues, new caregivers with the same emotional dynamics, new community roles with the same obligations, a new family structure with the same reciprocal patterns—the same defenders retreat to a structural claim: what matters is that the web of relations persists, not that these exact nodes remain. The result is a kind of double bookkeeping. The metaphysical weight is carried by the particularity of the relations, which is used to resist reductionist or brain-transplant accounts of identity; but the normative resilience is carried by structural equivalence, which is used to resist the claim that migration, adoption, or community turnover destroys personhood. I have seen versions of this in discussions of Ubuntu, where the familiar proverb about being a person through other people is interpreted sometimes as a claim about specific others and sometimes as a claim about the abstract fact of being-in-relation, and in care-ethics debates about whether relational autonomy requires continuity of specific caregivers or only continuity of care-structures. The phenomenon is not mere ambiguity; it is a systematic oscillation between two incompatible readings of "constitutive" that share the same vocabulary.

Does this specific tension—using particular-relational metaphysics for anti-individualist force, but structural-relational metaphysics for stability across change—have an established name in the philosophy of personhood or in Ubuntu scholarship? If so, how exactly is it defined, and which side of the oscillation does the named concept usually privilege? If not, can you coin and define a term that distinguishes it from ordinary narrative-identity concerns, where the problem is temporal continuity of a self-story, and from plain social-functionalism, where particular relations never carried metaphysical weight to begin with? I want the carving, not just a label: specify the boundary cases, state what the phenomenon shares with its nearest neighbors, and show precisely where it diverges.
