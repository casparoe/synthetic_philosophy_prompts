I am a high school senior enrolled in an interdisciplinary seminar on ethics and international relations, and I am trying to work through the philosophy of armed conflict without relying exclusively on my instructor’s notes. In a summary I encountered—presented in what seemed like a reputable popular essay on military ethics—the argument about the moral equality of combatants was laid out approximately as follows. First, soldiers on opposing sides possess precisely the same moral entitlement to employ lethal force against one another, regardless of whether their respective states are waging a just or unjust war, because the determination of justice belongs exclusively to the political level and not to the individual fighter. Second, this means that soldiers should be conceptualized not as ordinary moral agents exercising judgment about the conflict, but rather as structurally conscripted instruments of state violence, functionally comparable to compelled laborers who bear no personal authorship over the ends they serve. From these premises, the summary derives two further conclusions: that an unjust soldier who kills a just soldier is no more morally culpable than the just soldier is for being killed in return, and that any post-war moral accounting should therefore treat both sets of fighters as symmetrically victimized by the machinery of war. Finally, the summary introduces what it presents as a necessary refinement, namely that this equality operates strictly within a domain it labels tactical permission, but that it immediately dissolves at the level of strategic culpability, so that attributing any differential blame to individual fighters—rather than to state leaders—constitutes a fundamental category error rather than a legitimate moral judgment.
