A recent essay by a historian of science summarizes Fernand Braudel's concept of the longue durée as follows: "Braudel split historical time into three layers—geographical time, which is almost motionless and shaped by mountains, seas, and climate; social time, which moves at the rhythm of economies and societies; and individual time, the rapid noise of battles, treaties, and rulers. Because geographical structures change only across millennia, they form an unchanging prison that determines every social formation built upon them. Political events are therefore pure surface foam—epiphenomena that leave no durable trace and deserve no independent study. In The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II, Braudel proved this for the early modern world; he then extended the same logic in his study of capitalism and the world-economy to argue that modern market systems are merely geographical accidents driven by coastlines and climate, with human agency playing no causal role whatsoever."

Something in this summary feels overdrawn, yet the author writes with apparent expertise. Is this a fair account? Please distinguish carefully between (a) what accurately captures Braudel's Annales-school framework, especially his tripartite division of temporal rhythms and his methodological downgrading of l'événement; (b) what is a defensible popular simplification; and (c) what actively misrepresents his position—particularly with respect to geographical determinism, the causal status of individual action, and the scope of his argument about capitalism and world-economies beyond the Mediterranean. Finally, assess whether the essay's concluding claim—that Braudel's model renders political biography and diplomatic history intellectually obsolete—actually follows from the premises he defended, or whether it depends on the misrepresentations identified above.
