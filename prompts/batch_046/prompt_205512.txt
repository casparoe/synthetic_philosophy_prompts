I'm writing a prospectus for a one-semester philosophy term paper—roughly 6,000 words—on Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, and I need help sharpening the framing so it is both conceptually precise and genuinely open. The standard literature either treats the tortoise's demand for an explicit license to apply modus ponens as a pragmatic impossibility or resolves it through conventionalism, pragmatism, or appeals to the object-language/meta-language distinction. I want to ask a more specific question: does Carroll's regress expose a real vulnerability in the justification of deduction, or does it depend on an unstated model of rational agency that treats acceptance of an inference rule as requiring a separate, propositional belief that can itself be justified? Specifically, I will investigate whether the paradox can be dissolved—without circularity—by reconceiving logical entitlement as a practice-embedded, procedural competence rather than as a hierarchical system of justified beliefs about rules.

The existing answers leave the issue open because they respond to the tortoise's challenge at the level of logical theory without examining the underlying conception of what it is for a rational agent to apply a rule. Conventionalist accounts tell us that logical rules are conventions, but they do not explain why a particular agent in a particular case is rationally entitled to invoke them; pragmatist appeals to indispensability explain why we cannot abandon deduction, but they do not address the tortoise's internal demand for warrant. My approach will therefore distinguish between "justificatory ascent" (giving reasons for a rule in the abstract) and "procedural licensing" (being entitled to apply it in reasoning), using Carroll's dialogue as a test case for whether this distinction is stable or collapses into either circularity or unargued conventionalism. I will reconstruct the dialogue's logical structure with care, identify exactly where the tortoise's request becomes vicious rather than merely tedious, and then evaluate whether a skill-based or practice-governed model of inference genuinely stops the regress.

A successful result will provide a clear criterion for when a demand for justification in reasoning is legitimate and when it is a category mistake, with Carroll's dialogue serving as the central example; failure will be shown if the proposed distinction between propositional justification and procedural entitlement either collapses back into circular justification or reduces to an unmotivated conventionalism. Given the one-semester scope, I will restrict the analysis to Carroll's original text and to conceptual argumentation, avoiding extensive formal logic or broad historical scholarship, and I will supply all specific citations and references myself. What I need from this proposal is a precise statement of the question, an honest assessment of why the standard anti-circularist responses miss the structural point, a clear explanation of why the practice-embedded framing is the right angle rather than the obvious alternatives, and a realistic account of what could make the project too large for a single term.
