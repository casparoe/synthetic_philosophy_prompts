Then discuss whether it's compatible with his ruling in Mishneh Torah that sacrificial law remains eternally binding. Does he assign sacrifices any intrinsic value, or treats them as purely instrumental? And relate this to his broader claim that divine law adapts to human psychological limits - almost like institutional path-dependence locking in religious practice over time.

Student answer:

Maimonides says God only allowed sacrifices because the Israelites were stuck in pagan sacrificial habits and couldn't quit cold turkey. In the Guide he compares it to training wheels: once people learn to pray they don't need the sacrifices anymore. He thinks prayer is the real point and sacrifices have zero intrinsic value; they're just a concession to human weakness. This is consistent with Mishneh Torah listing them as eternal laws, since he also says they'll be restored in the messanic era, which proves he sees them as permanent fixtures even if they're not the highest form of worship. He also agrees with Nachmanides that sacrifices substitute for the sinner and affect divine attributes, though he puts the psychological explanation first. His broader theory is that God designs law like a regulator adapting to market failures - religious practice gets locked in by history, so the law has to work inside existing institutional habits rather than imposing an ideal system from scratch. So sacrifices are instrumentally useful for maintaining social order but carry no spiritual weight on their own.
