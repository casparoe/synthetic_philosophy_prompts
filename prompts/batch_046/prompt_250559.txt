Analyze Émilie du Châtelet's final year—specifically her decision to continue intensive work on her French translation and commentary on Newton's Principia Mathematica through a high-risk pregnancy, culminating in her death in childbirth in September 1749 and the posthumous publication of the work in 1759—through the lens of her own philosophy of happiness articulated in the Discours sur le bonheur. Do not treat this as biography; treat it as a disciplined mapping problem. Identify the actors and stakes in her situation: her place within the network of French Newtonians (including Clairaut's parallel commentary efforts), her departure from the intellectual household she had shared with Voltaire at Cirey, her pregnancy by Jean-François de Saint-Lambert, and the social prohibition against sustained theoretical labor by women. Then apply her framework: her distinction between destructive passions and sustaining illusions, her claim that study is a necessary but insufficient condition for women's happiness, and her implicit taxonomy of the illusions—regarding love, reputation, and the durability of intellectual work—required to endure labor without succumbing to despair. What should she have done by the lights of her own view? Should she have suspended the translation to mitigate physical risk? Should she have treated the commentary itself as one of the sustaining illusions, perhaps the illusion that the work would outlast her? Or should she have recognized the drive to finish as itself a destructive passion she failed to master? Be explicit about where the mapping succeeds and where it breaks: does her theory have the conceptual resources to explain why she continued working through evident danger, or does it misclassify that drive as virtuous study rather than obsessive passion? Does the episode support her broader claim that intellectual labor offers women a distinctive, available path to happiness, or does it reveal that such labor can become a form of self-destructive absorption that her ethics cannot distinguish from flourishing? Finally, what does the case show about her view of happiness itself—does it confirm that happiness for women in constrained positions requires carefully managed illusions rather than authentic self-assessment, or does it expose a gap between the ethics she articulated and the existential economy she actually practiced?
