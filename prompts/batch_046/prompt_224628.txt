I am a doctoral student in democratic theory, and in recent discussions with colleagues I have been trying to move beyond purely abstract treatments of the boundary problem—the question of who belongs to the demos—by testing its standard principles against an actual institutional decision. The episode I keep returning to is the franchise for the 2016 United Kingdom referendum on European Union membership: UK citizens could vote; so could citizens of Ireland, Malta, and Cyprus, together with qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in Britain; but long-settled EU nationals from other member states were excluded, despite being directly subject to UK law and profoundly affected by the result. I want you to analyze this franchise rule as a live case study in democratic boundary-drawing, but not through the usual binary lens of "all affected" versus "all subjected."

Instead, apply what I will call an affectedness-gradient framework, in which affectedness runs as a scalar property from direct legal subjection through intense economic and mobility exposure to diffuse, indirect spillover, and keep in view the problem of recursive closure—the fact that the demos deciding its own boundaries must already have bounded itself, so that the 2016 franchise was not merely an application of a prior principle but an act that constituted the very demos making the decision. Please map the referendum's actual voter eligibility onto this gradient, asking whether it tracks any coherent point on the scale or instead reveals an arbitrary path-dependent closure—note, for instance, the lingering legal architecture of Commonwealth citizenship that included some non-citizen residents while excluding others who faced comparable or greater exposure. Say what the all-subjected and all-affected principles respectively imply under this scalar framing, identify where the theory explains the episode and where it is silent—particularly regarding affected persons outside the UK who were never seriously considered for inclusion—and then turn the analysis back on the theory itself: does the case confirm the boundary problem as a genuine dilemma, expose it as a post-hoc rationalization of contingent historical closure, or suggest that recursive closure renders principled boundary-drawing impossible in practice? Throughout, please separate what is settled in democratic theory about these principles from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested view—such as the claim that the all-affected principle is uniquely correct—as though it were the consensus.
