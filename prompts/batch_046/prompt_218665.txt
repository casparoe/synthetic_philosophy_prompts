I've been re-reading Brave New World for a course on biotech ethics and I keep thinking about the hedonistic imperative - the idea that we should use genetic engineering and drugs to abolish suffering altogether, not just manage it with pills. Most people compare soma to modern antidepressants, but I want to push further and ask whether Huxley is actually critiquing a serious abolitionist project or just a shallow consumerist version of pleasure management. Read the novel through that lens: focus especially on Bernard's refusal of soma and John's rejection of the feelies and the civilized world's painless pleasure. Do these characters defend something like authentic suffering that a real abolitionist would have to take seriously, or are they just romanticizing pain out of ignorance? There's also Lenina's experience in the orgy-porgy ritual - does the novel imply that once suffering is eliminated, meaningful attachment disappears too, or is that just Huxley's conservative bias talking?

I'm writing an essay for my philosphy of techology seminar and need a reading that doesn't reduce the book to drugs are bad. Where does the novel actually land a punch against the idea of hedonic recalibration, and what would a serious defender of the hedonistic imperative say Huxley misses about how pleasure and attachment actually work? I don't want a summary; I want a reading that takes both the novel's psychology and the biotech abolitionist view seriously, and says honestly where the work resists the philosophy and where the philosophy makes the work look naive.
