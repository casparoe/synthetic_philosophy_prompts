Write a Petrarchan sonnet—fourteen lines in iambic pentameter, with an octave and a sestet divided by a volta at the ninth line—on the idea of moral progress as an expanding circle of concern, but treat the expansion not as smooth radial growth but as generating friction zones: points where extending moral consideration to a new class of beings (for example, wild animals or potentially sentient artificial systems) does not merely add obligations but restructures the weight and character of obligations already owed to beings nearer the center. Use the octave to trace the familiar arc from kin preference through universal human concern to broader sentience, letting the language of radius, boundary, and inclusion dominate. At the volta, turn to the claim that inclusion is not costless: the attention, resources, and conceptual frameworks required to honor the outer ring may redistribute moral weight rather than supplement it. In the sestet, develop the implication that moral progress might be better modeled as a shifting topology—where pushing the circumference outward pulls the center off-axis—than as a simple enlargement of area. The closing couplet should leave unresolved whether this restructuring undermines the coherence of the expanding-circle framework or only complicates it; suspend the question in a single image rather than resolving it. The poem should be accurate to the underlying Singerian idea that the boundary of moral concern is contingent and expandable, yet should render the friction-zone objection with conceptual precision rather than as a generic complaint about competing interests. Do not resolve the tension; let the form itself—especially the volta—do the work of showing that expansion and redistribution are inseparable.
