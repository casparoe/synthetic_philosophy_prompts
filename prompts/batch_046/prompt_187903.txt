I am preparing a presentation on metaphysics for an advanced undergraduate seminar, and I have been working from a summary I found in an unnamed philosophy blog that attempts to condense David Lewis's modal realism into a single coherent picture. The post states that, according to Lewis, other possible worlds are not abstract descriptions or mental constructs but concrete, spatiotemporally disconnected universes that exist with the same ontological status as our own; that the predicate actual functions indexically, much like here or now, so that every world counts as actual from the perspective of its own inhabitants; and that since no individual literally exists in more than one world, Lewis replaces transworld identity with counterpart theory, according to which every object in our world has precisely one counterpart in each other world, defined by sharing all and only that object's essential properties. The summary goes on to claim that this ontology solves the problem of modal knowledge by treating our access to other worlds as a form of conceptual navigation among concrete realities, and that the familiar objection from ontological extravagance fails once we recognize, as the blog insists Lewis implicitly maintained, that properties are world-indexed entities that cannot be instantiated across worlds—thereby preventing any direct cross-world comparison except through counterpart relations. The post concludes that critics who reject modal realism on grounds of parsimony are therefore attacking a straw man, since the theory's explanatory advantages for counterfactual reasoning and causal modal claims outweigh any cost in ontology. Before I present this to my class, I want to check its reliability. Please go through the summary point by point and indicate which claims are faithful to Lewis's arguments in his major work, which are acceptable simplifications that a defender might accept with qualification, and which are misrepresentations—particularly the thesis that there is exactly one counterpart per world, the claim that properties are world-indexed in Lewis's own view, the characterization of modal knowledge as conceptual navigation, and the dismissal of the parsimony objection. Does the conclusion that critics are attacking a straw man actually survive once the misrepresentations are corrected?
