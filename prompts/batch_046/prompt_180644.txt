I work with the vocabulary of embodied predictive processing and active inference—terms like generative model, precision-weighting, interoceptive prediction error, sensorimotor contingency, and free-energy minimization—and I use them with reasonable fluency. Take Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment, in which a person is suspended in a void, blind, with limbs untouched and no prior sensory history, yet is said to be immediately aware that he exists, and restate its core argument entirely within that predictive-processing vocabulary. Be faithful: do not smuggle in dualist commitments and relabel them as translations. For each key move—the total absence of bodily and exteroceptive awareness, the immediate non-inferential affirmation of existence, the claim that this reveals something about the self that is not identical to the body, and the inference that the knowing subject must therefore be an immaterial soul—say whether it translates cleanly, whether it can only be captured by distorting part of the framework, or whether it has no expression at all in these terms. To make the exercise sharper, inject the following variant: suppose that not only exteroceptive and proprioceptive channels are suppressed, but interoceptive ones are globally silenced as well, so that there are no heartbeat, breath, or visceral prediction errors, leaving only a bare, atemporal self-representation with no sensory past and no anticipated future. Once you have mapped what survives and what does not, tell me whether the residue indicates a real limitation in predictive processing—perhaps its inability to accommodate a non-inferential self-model that persists when all precision weights are driven to zero—or whether it reveals that Avicenna’s original claim was less substantive than it looks, trading on an ambiguity between direct awareness and the absence of contradictory error signals.
