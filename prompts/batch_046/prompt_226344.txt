I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop I'm helping to organise at our local library — it's called "Seeing the Pattern," and it's meant to bring together people who've worked with concept-learning experiments and neighbours who've simply always enjoyed puzzles. Roughly half the audience will be researchers in cognitive science or artificial intelligence; the other half will be retired teachers, students, and friends with no specialist background. I'd like the talk to last about eight minutes, and I want it to feel like one continuous argument rather than a collection of observations.

The idea I've been turning over on my own is the Bongard problem — those puzzles where you are shown twelve frames divided into left and right sets, and you must induce the hidden rule that separates them, usually from the examples alone with almost no explicit instruction. I keep thinking about how humans seem able to do this with remarkably sparse data, while machines typically require enormous training sets. But I don't want the speech to collapse into a sentimental claim that human intuition is magical, nor do I want to pretend that current systems have solved the problem when I know the researchers in the room will disagree. I want the speech to be structured by the tension between induction from very few examples and the danger of overfitting — that is, seeing a pattern that isn't really there — so that it actually shapes what I say about teaching, about trusting our first impressions, and about the limits of pattern recognition.

There's one specific framing I'd like you to treat seriously, even if it sounds strange. I've started calling it "negative-space induction": the idea that in some Bongard-style puzzles we learn the rule not primarily from what is present in the examples on one side, but from what is systematically absent across both sides — the gaps that remain constant. I don't know whether this has a technical name, and I'm not asking for references or a reading list; I simply want you to build the remarks around this notion. Take a definite position: either argue that negative-space induction captures something real and distinct about human concept formation that current statistical learning misses, or argue that it is an illusion and that all induction is ultimately feature-based, but do not remain neutral. Give your reasons, make definite choices, and write so that the scientists hear a real claim being made while the neighbours understand why it matters to them. Please write in British English, keep it formal but not academic in tone, and avoid any bibliography or citation requests — I want a speech that stands on its own reasoning.
