I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, mostly philosophy of mind and biology, and I've been asked to draft a proposal for a small six-month fellowship — basically a research essay plus a workshop pitch rolled into one. The topic I'm circling is that old Aunt Hillary and Ant Fugue thing: can an ant colony actually have a mind, or is it just a catchy metaphor? I don't want to rehash the usual consciousness-versus-complexity debate that's been done to death in every popular science book. Instead, I want to inject a slightly different angle: whether attributing a mind to the colony requires treating it as having a unified narrative self — a continuous 'I' that persists even as every individual ant dies and is replaced — and whether that condition is actually satisfied or just projected onto it because we humans like stories with characters. I think this opens a gap that the standard functionalist and emergentist answers miss: they ask whether the colony processes information like a brain, but they don't ask whether the colony has the kind of temporal identity and first-person point of view that makes mind-attribution more than an analogy.

The proposal needs to state the question precisely, explain why existant positions don't settle it, outline an aproach that compares the colony's temporal structure to other distributed systems we don't call minds — like fungal networks or immune systems — and say what a useful result would look like, probably a set of criteria for collective mind-attribution that don't depend on narrative coherence. It also needs to say what counts as failure — maybe if it turns out the narrative condition is unavoidable after all — and keep the scope realistic for six months without me needing to dig up obscure library texts I can't access. I want it committed: take a definite line, don't just lay out options and leave me to pick. Can you write this up? I was thinking of calling it something like "The Ant Fugue and the Missing Self: why collective minds might fail for narrative reasons rather than cognitive ones" — though I'm open to tweaking. Keep it casual, there's probably a typo or two in my draft already, I don't mind.
