I have been reflecting for some time, without formal philosophical training, on whether people ought to be able to invoke religious beliefs directly when arguing about laws and public policy, or whether they must first translate those beliefs into language that anyone, regardless of faith, could in principle accept. I recently spent many months in good-faith disagreement with a close friend who takes the opposite view, and rather than continuing to talk past each other we want to produce the kind of document an adversarial collaboration might yield: something precise enough that both of us could sign our names to it, with the remaining disagreement made exact rather than rhetorical.

The two camps are these. One holds that democratic legitimacy in a deeply pluralistic society requires what might be called a Translation Requirement: citizens who support coercive policies must justify them through reasons that do not depend on revelation or sectarian authority, because shared governance demands shared premises. The other holds a Direct Witness position: requiring religious citizens to recast their deepest convictions in secular vocabulary imposes an asymmetric cognitive and expressive burden, and it is enough that they accept democratic outcomes and refrain from coercing belief; their arguments may remain in their own vocabulary provided they are offered with respect for those who reject them.

We want the joint statement structured in four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—about the reality of irreconcilable pluralism, the moral equality of citizens, the distinction between arguing for a law and coercing religious assent, the legitimacy of democratic decision procedures, and the importance of mutual civic respect—phrased so that neither of us feels misrepresented. Second, a disagreements table with two columns, one for each view, in which each position is formulated in wording its own proponent would accept, without straw-manning the other side. Third, the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that resolving one in a particular direction would genuinely move at least one of us. I believe the central cruxes include whether there is a meaningful asymmetry in the burden of cognitive translation (does asking a religious person to secularize an argument alter its substance and motivational force in a way that asking a secular person to understand religious language does not?), and whether public accessibility requires genuinely shared premises or only a shared procedural commitment to democratic outcomes regardless of premises. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete hypothetical institutional scenario or policy case that both of us agree would bear on it, together with the explicit prediction each side makes about what that scenario reveals.

Please draft this document in a formal, polished register appropriate for a public statement, but write from the perspective of two non-specialists who have argued carefully and want clarity rather than academic display. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; we need claims we can evaluate on their own. Where the disagreement touches on specific theories, paraphrase them without attributing verbatim quotations. Ensure that the agreed-claims section is neither vacuous nor slanted, that the table separates genuine disagreement from mere verbal confusion, and that each crux is accompanied by a test case with both sides’ predictions recorded in advance.
