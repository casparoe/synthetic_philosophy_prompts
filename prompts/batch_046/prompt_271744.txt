Consider the following passage from Kant's Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, in the section where he introduces the formula of universal law and distinguishes perfect from imperfect duties. Kant argues that some maxims contain an internal contradiction when one attempts to think them as universal laws of nature: if every rational being adopted such a maxim, the practice or institution that the maxim itself presupposes would be undermined, so that the maxim could not be coherently willed as a universal rule. He illustrates this with the case of a person who considers making a false promise whenever it is advantageous, noting that if this maxim were universalized, the very institution of promising—which the maxim relies upon—would collapse, yielding not merely an undesirable state of affairs but a kind of practical self-defeat. He then contrasts this with maxims that do not destroy their own conditions in this way, yet remain incompatible with a rational will that necessarily wills the development and efficacy of its own capacities, such as the neglect of one's natural talents.

My question concerns the logical form of the contradiction Kant identifies in the first case. Is the contradiction best understood as a strict logical contradiction—something like a formal inconsistency within the universalized maxim itself—or is it a pragmatic or teleological contradiction, in which universalization renders the maxim self-defeating because it eliminates the background conditions that make the action intelligible? If it is the latter, does Kant's test smuggle in a hidden premise about the necessary preservation of particular social institutions, or can it be reconstructed purely in terms of the rational agent's own necessarily willed ends? I am especially interested in whether this distinction maps onto contemporary distinctions in deontic logic between contradictions in conception and contradictions in will, and whether the latter introduces something like a strategic instability—analogous to universal defection collapsing a coordination equilibrium—rather than a logical impossibility in the narrower sense. How should one read the structure of the test so that it remains genuinely normative rather than merely descriptive of institutional fragility?
