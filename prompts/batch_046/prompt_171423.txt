My partner received a terminal diagnosis six months ago, and I have been forced to confront the ethics of physician-assisted dying not as an abstraction but as a decision we may actually face. I am a senior reliability engineer with deep fluency in fault-tolerant design, graceful degradation protocols, controlled shutdown procedures, formal verification of termination conditions, and the distinction between user-authorized termination and uncommanded system failure, but I have no formal training in moral philosophy. Restate the central ethical arguments surrounding voluntary euthanasia and assisted suicide—specifically the autonomy-based case for access, the sanctity-of-life and non-abandonment objections, the killing-versus-letting-die distinction, concerns about slippery slopes or social coercion, and the doctrine of double effect—entirely within that engineering vocabulary. Be precise in the mapping: patient autonomy to system-owner authorization protocols; the prescription and administration of lethal medication to a verified, user-triggered controlled-shutdown sequence; withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment to passive maintenance-window expiration; and social-context risks to cascading-dependency or external-pressure fault injection. Then provide the residue report: for each philosophical claim, indicate whether it translates cleanly into this framework, requires conceptual distortion to fit, or has no counterpart whatsoever. In particular, address whether an "irreversible moral harm" distinct from functional termination has an analog in reliability engineering; whether professional role-integrity obligations (e.g., a physician's duty not to kill) map onto design constraints or safety invariants that can be overridden by user consent; and whether the biological-continuity objection survives translation at all. Conclude by assessing whether the untranslatable residue points to a genuine moral dimension that engineering vocabulary necessarily omits, or whether it instead reveals that some distinctions in this philosophical debate are artifacts of a vocabulary that obscures rather than clarifies the underlying decision structure.
