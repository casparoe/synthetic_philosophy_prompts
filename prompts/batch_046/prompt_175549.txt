Here's an argument I sketched about mental causation and now it feels like it proves way too much. (1) If mental property M causes physical behavior B, and M is realized by neural state N at that moment, then N is also a sufficient cause of B. (2) By teh causal exclusion principle, B can't have two independent sufficient causes, so M is redundant—N does all the causal work. (3) So mental properties are causally inefficacious unless they're identical to their specific realizers. (4) But mental properties are usually diachronically realized: the belief that Paris is capital might be carried by neural state N1 on Monday and N2 on Tuesday after synaptic drift, so M isn't identical to any single N. (5) Apply the same reasoning to any macro-property that can swap its realizer—biological species, economic markets, languages—and they all collapse into being causally redundant too. (6) So either exclusion eliminates causation at every level except quantum fields, which is absurd, or we must reject one of the premises. (7) But if I reject (2) just for mental properties, that looks ad hoc; if I reject it everywhere, exclusion does no work at all. Where's the exit? Tell me which step to give up, what giving it up costs specifically for mental causation, and whether there's a non-ad-hoc way to restrict exclusion so it bites mental properties but not macro-ones like hearts or presidents. Also, if you think the argument secretly equivocates on "realization," point to exactly where the slide happens.
