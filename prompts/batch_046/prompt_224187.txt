The question asked students to explain Arrow's impossability theorem for an undergrad audience: cover the four conditions (unrestriced domain, Pareto efficiency, IIA, and non-dictatorship), state the result precisely, and discuss whether it's specifically a problem for democratic voting or for aggregation methods more generally.

Here's what the student wrote:

"Arrow's impossibility theorem, published in Arrow's 1951 book Social Choice and Individual Values, shows that no social choice rule can satisfy four reasonable conditions when there are three or more alternatives and two or more voters. The conditions are unrestricted domain — voters can rank options however they want; Pareto efficiency — if everyone prefers A to B then society does too; independence of irrelevant alternatives — the social ranking of A vs B depends only on individual rankings of A vs B; and non-dictatorship — no single voter always determines the outcome. Arrow proves that any mechanism satisfying all four must be dictatorial.

This is important because it essentially proves democratic voting is impossible. If we want rational collective choices and no dictator, we have to drop one of the conditions, which means real-world elections are always flawed. That's why a lot of economists think we should just use markets instead of voting for social decisions.

There's also a misconception that Arrow needs cardinal utility, but he only needs ordinal preferences, so the result is actually stronger. The proof works by setting up a Condorcet cycle — voter 1 likes A then B then C, voter 2 likes B then C then A, voter 3 likes C then A then B — and showing this forces a contradiction unless one voter is in charge. Sen later simplified this in the 1970s with his liberal paradox argument."
