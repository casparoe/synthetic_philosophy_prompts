Two members of a philosophy seminar I attend have been arguing about the coherence of Rousseau’s hostility to theater, particularly as articulated in his Letter to d’Alembert on the Theater. The first, Marcus, contends that Rousseau’s objection is fundamentally moral-psychological: theatrical representation cultivates a taste for artificial passion, teaches spectators to enjoy the display of vices they ought to suppress, and replaces genuine civic engagement with the passive consumption of scripted emotion. The second, Elena, points out that Rousseau himself composed theatrical works—including the opera Le Devin du village and the play Narcisse—so that if the art form itself were corrupting, he should have abstained entirely. She concludes that his real target is not drama per se but the institutional form of a permanent, commercial theater in a small republic like Geneva, where professional actors and a divided audience introduce hierarchy and vanity into civic life.

Marcus replies that writing a private opera for a court or salon differs categorically from sustaining a public troupe supported by ticket sales and patronage; yet Elena presses him on whether the distinction is principled or merely ad hoc. She notes that Rousseau praises public festivals and games in Geneva precisely because citizens participate as both performers and spectators, and she introduces a modern parallel: in immersive or participatory theater, the audience moves through the space, interacts with performers, and sometimes shapes the narrative, apparently dissolving the rigid spectator-performer hierarchy Rousseau condemned. Marcus grants that the social architecture changes, but he argues that even in these formats a professional company still controls the script, the space, and the emotional arc; the audience’s agency remains choreographed, so the core problem—human action staged primarily for the pleasure of onlookers—persists. Elena then asks whether, on Marcus’s reading, Rousseau’s critique applies to virtually all modern entertainment, from film to sports broadcasting, and whether that universalization makes the view more compelling or implausibly severe. They asked me to adjudicate, since I was sitting in on the discussion.

Please evaluate the exchange point by point: assess the strength of Marcus’s moral-psychological claim, Elena’s hypocrisy objection, her institutional interpretation, and her immersive-theater counterexample. Say who is right about which specific point, indicate what both are neglecting—particularly regarding Rousseau’s distinction between natural and artificial sentiment in civic life—and give an overall judgment about whether Rousseau’s theater critique is best read as a universal aesthetic condemnation, a context-specific republican warning, or something that requires a third framing altogether.
