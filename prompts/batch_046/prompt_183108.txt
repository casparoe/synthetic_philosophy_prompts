I was in my phil of mind seminar last week and we were talking about the color phi illusion — you know, where they flash a red dot and then a green dot in a different spot realy fast, and people swear they saw the color change halfway through the motion even though there was never an actual intermediate color. My prof said some people interpret this as a kind of postdictive revision, like your brain is editing the past after the fact. But then he used this analogy about Orwellian versus Stalinesque revisions, and I got totally stuck.

He said an Orwellian revision is like the Ministry of Truth rewriting the history books so the past itself becomes different, while a Stalinesque one is just airbrushing someone out of the photo — the old record is erased but not rewritten into something false. I think I get the political distinction, but I don't see how it applies to consciousness. If color phi is Orwellian, does that mean my original experience of the first red dot literally gets overwritten in real time, like there was an exsistant experience that gets replaced? Or is it Stalinesque, meaning the original experience never fully registers and just gets removed? I'm probably mixing metaphors with neuroscience here, but does that difference actually correspond to anything in how the brain times experience, or is the whole framing just a confused way of asking whether a conscious moment has a definite boundary before it gets revised?

Also, if the brain is constantly doing this kind of editing, doesn't that mean our stream of consciousness is full of false memories of what we just saw? Or is there a way to say the "first experience" was never stable enough to be true or false in the first place? I feel like I'm missing something obvious about temporal experience, but I can't tell if the problem is in the analogy or in my understanding of postdiction.
