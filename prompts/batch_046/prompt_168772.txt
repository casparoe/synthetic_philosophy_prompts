hey i need a classroom game for my grad bioethics seminar—16 people, 90 minutes. i want them to actually feel the authority problem with advance directives, not just rehash the same dementia lecture for the millionth time. inject a twist so it's not pure boilerplate: frame the directive not as a preference list but as a contract signed between two legally-distinct "stages" of one life, so honoring it means enforcing a promise made by a party who no longer psychologically exists.

design the activity with concrete rules, roles, and small stakes. start with everyone writing a one-paragraph "stage contract" refusing one specific treatment if they ever enter a defined value-inverted state—don't use generic dementia, make it concrete like "if i no longer recognize music and only want comfort." then break into groups of four. in each group: the student who wrote the contract plays "Party B" (the present patient, happy, values totally flipped, no recognition of the old contract); two others are "Past-Stage Advocates"; one or two are "Court" adjudicators. the Court draws a decision rule from an envelope: (1) contracts bind regardless of psychological change; (2) only continuous psychology can bind; (3) present welfare always wins; (4) substituted judgment (what would Party A want now?). they run a token economy—enforcing the contract costs "Welfare Tokens," breaking it costs "Autonomy Tokens"—and must reach a verdict with reasoning spoken in legal, not personal, terms. the key is Party B genuinely argues for release, the advocates genuinely argue for binding authority, and the Court hits the wall that substituted judgment is impossible because Party A's values are exactly what's gone. they should notice they've arrived at a paradox about authority only at the end, not be told up front.

then the debrief: map Round 1 onto expressing precedent autonomy, Round 2 onto the metaphysical/psychological discontinuity challenge, the token tradeoff onto the incommensurability problem, and the Court's failure onto why substituted judgment collapses. say what's predictably gonna go wrong—sympathy for Party B will swamp contract logic, and people will revert to "but it's still me"—and how to handle it (have the facilitator interrupt with "remember, you are a court, not a friend"). be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: the metaphysical question of whether A and B are literally one person or two, since the contract framing assumes separation; the facilitator has to supply that ambiguity themselves.

also please include a note on where your reasoning is shakiest—like which part assumes people can role-play against their own interests, or what argument about diachronic personal identity would make you scrap the token economy entirely. write it casual, a typo or two is fine.
