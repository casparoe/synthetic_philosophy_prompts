Consider the following examination question and a student’s response.

Question: In Fear and Trembling, Johannes de Silentio presents Abraham’s willingness to sacrifice Isaac as an instance of the teleological suspension of the ethical. (a) Define the teleological suspension of the ethical and distinguish it from a mere conflict of duties within a single normative framework. (b) Contrast the knight of faith with both the knight of infinite resignation and the tragic hero (using Agamemnon’s sacrifice of Iphigenia for the welfare of the fleet as your example of the latter), paying particular attention to each figure’s relationship to the universal. (c) Evaluate whether Abraham’s silence can be read as a form of second-order ethical reasoning—that is, as a decision about which normative framework applies—rather than as a total rupture with the universal. Provide a concise but philosophically precise answer.

Student answer:
"The teleological suspension of the ethical occurs when an individual’s absolute relation to God takes precedence over the universal moral law. Unlike a simple conflict of duties—where two ethical obligations clash within the same framework—the suspension places the ethical itself in abeyance for the sake of a higher telos. Johannes de Silentio uses Abraham to illustrate this: Abraham is commanded to sacrifice Isaac, violating the universal prohibition against killing one’s child, yet he obeys because his duty to God is absolute.

Abraham differs from the tragic hero, exemplified by Agamemnon sacrificing Iphigenia for the welfare of the state. Agamemnon acts within the universal: he sacrifices the particular (his daughter) for a higher ethical end (the people and the fleet). He can therefore speak, weep, and be understood by the community. Abraham, by contrast, sacrifices Isaac for no universal reason; he acts solely on a private command. This places him outside ethics and makes him the knight of faith. The knight of infinite resignation makes the movement of renunciation—giving up Isaac for the eternal—but remains in resignation, unable to reclaim the finite by virtue of the absurd. Abraham makes both movements: he resigns Isaac and then, through faith, receives him back.

As for Abraham’s silence, it should not be interpreted as second-order ethical reasoning. If Abraham had tried to justify his action within a universal framework—claiming it served a greater good—he would have collapsed into either a tragic hero or a mere murderer. His refusal to speak is therefore absolute: he will not translate his act into ethical language at all. Some readers influenced by Kantian meta-ethics suggest that Abraham’s duty to God functions as a higher-order framework that reorders moral obligations. But Kierkegaard rejects any such meta-ethical ordering; for him, the paradox of faith is irreducible, and silence is the only appropriate response."
