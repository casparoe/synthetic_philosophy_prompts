You are giving the after-dinner remarks at the closing banquet of the Decade Consortium, a ten-year interdisciplinary program structured like a sequential cooperation game: each year, partner institutions could withdraw their accumulated share—ending the collaboration—or roll it into a joint pool with exponentially greater payoff, knowing one defection collapsed everything. Standard backward induction predicts withdrawal in year one, yet the consortium survived all ten rounds. Half the room are decision theorists who will spot the centipede-game structure; the other half are the scientists, administrators, and family members who lived through it. Write a five-minute speech in which the backward-induction paradox does real structural work: you must avoid saying the partners were irrational or merely lucky, and you must avoid a condescending technical lecture. Instead, use the paradox to frame why treating each round as if continuation were still possible—despite the logical pressure to defect—functioned as a rational commitment that reshaped the payoffs themselves. Inject one specific conceptual twist: present the consortium as a case where common knowledge of rationality was jointly suspended rather than assumed, so backward induction failed not through error but because the players deliberately refused to make their own rationality common knowledge. The speech should land as affection and insight for both audiences, anchored by one concrete, non-technical image that lets the non-experts feel the paradox rather than learn it.
