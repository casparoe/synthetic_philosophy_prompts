I serve as lead planner for a regional post-labor-transition authority that is piloting a 22-hour guaranteed work week with universal baseline income, and my evaluation team now receives a steady stream of proposals for how municipalities should structure the liberated time and surplus. I need a reusable, 30-minute decision procedure grounded in Keynes’s theory of the good life—specifically his distinction between absolute and relative needs, his claim that the economic problem is not permanent, and his vision of leisure as a non-instrumental space for art, contemplation, and genuine choice rather than as a mere recovery period for further productivity or as an occasion for competitive consumption. The procedure should be ordered as a sequence of testable questions: first, whether the proposal treats freed time as an end in itself or as an input to economic output; second, whether it respects the boundary between needs that are genuinely absolute and those that are socially comparative and therefore potentially insatiable; third, whether it assigns collective design or preserves individual autonomy in how leisure is used; and fourth, whether it avoids conflating the good life with the accumulation of luxury goods. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a clear rejection, and where it must hand the decision to a human judgment because Keynes’s framework is genuinely silent or ambiguous on values. Apply the procedure to three concrete proposals: (1) a universal basic leisure stipend restricted to non-market cultural goods and education; (2) a mandatory eight-hour weekly civic-education curriculum for all adults; and (3) a tax-funded expansion of luxury retail and tourism infrastructure marketed explicitly as “rewarding the freed worker,” which my board will politically favor but which the procedure should likely reject. Include the hard case where the framework forces an uncomfortable choice between collective cultivation and individual liberty, flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and state clearly what the procedure is unable to do—particularly whether Keynes’s vision can be operationalized without collapsing into paternalism. Finally, take a committed position: defend or reject the claim that this framework is genuinely usable for governance, giving your reasons rather than laying out options.
