I'm giving the after-dinner remarks at the retirement celebration of my former doctoral advisor, who spent thirty-five years teaching ancient historiography and source criticism to both undergraduates and archivists. The room is split: half are family members and university administrators with no background in the philosophy of history, and the other half are historians, museum conservators, and a few philosophers of science who have followed her work closely. She herself is a committed skeptic about our direct epistemic access to the distant past; she always insisted that knowing ancient events requires navigating what she called the three distances—temporal separation from the event, the chain of testimonial transmission, and the interpretive gap between surviving artifacts and the claims we build from them. I want the speech to honor her rigor without betraying it: I should not speak as if she simply "uncovered the truth" in any unmediated sense, and I need to avoid language that treats the past as if it were present to us, because she would consider that philosophically false and her colleagues would catch it immediately. At the same time, the remarks must be warm, intelligible to her relatives, and structurally shaped by this epistemological framework rather than merely decorated with it—if the framework is doing real work, it should constrain what I can legitimately claim about her achievements and what I must leave open.

Over coffee last week, a colleague and I argued about whether it is even possible to toast a historian of this kind without implicitly committing to a correspondence theory of truth she rejects, and that tension is exactly what the speech needs to navigate. Please write a six- to seven-minute address that takes the three-distance model as its organizing backbone. Contrast Herodotus's willingness to relay distant oral testimony—sometimes across generations and cultures—with Thucydides's stricter rejection of the mythical and the unverifiable, using that contrast to illustrate how her own scholarship lived in the disciplined tension between openness and rigor. Include a brief, concrete example—perhaps her work reconstructing administrative records from fragmentary papyri—showing how interpretive distance operates in practice, and make clear why that labor matters even though it does not yield certainty. The tone should be affectionate but precise; the philosophical commitments should determine which praise is permissible and which would misrepresent her view, not just ornament the sentiment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the speech must stand entirely on its own for an audience that includes people who have never read either ancient author.
