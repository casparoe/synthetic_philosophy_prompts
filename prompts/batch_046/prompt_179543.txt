I am drafting material for a dissertation chapter on collective action and the ethics of democratic participation, and I need a tightly structured dialogue to work through a conceptual tension that standard treatments tend to flatten. Write a dialogue between two equally competent political philosophers—Dr. A, who defends a position she calls “threshold consequentialism about voting,” and Dr. B, who defends what she terms “expressive non-complicity”—debating the case of a voter in a first-past-the-post election who must choose between two major candidates, both of whom endorse a policy the voter regards as gravely unjust, though one endorses a marginally less severe version. Dr. A should argue that casting a ballot for the less harmful candidate satisfies a pro tanto duty of harm reduction, that the causal difference between the two outcomes is real and institutionally registered regardless of the voter’s private mental state, and that treating the vote as pure expression ignores its function in an aggregative mechanism. Dr. B should argue that in systems where votes are structurally interpreted as bundled endorsements of entire platforms, voting for the lesser evil produces what she calls “expressive contamination”: the voter’s act is publicly incorporated as support for the unjust policy itself, and this contamination cannot be cancelled by comparative reasoning or mental reservation; she should maintain that abstaining, or casting a vote for an unelectable protest candidate, is the only way to preserve the integrity of one’s political expression, even when it does not alter the outcome. Both characters must respond directly to each other’s actual arguments—Dr. A should address the contamination claim specifically, and Dr. B should address the causal-difference claim specifically—rather than talking past one another. Neither should be rendered as a strawman. The dialogue should reach no forced resolution, but by its end each side should have clearly articulated why the other’s framing misidentifies what is morally at stake in the act of voting itself.
