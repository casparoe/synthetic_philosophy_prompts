At a departmental governance meeting, two colleagues debated whether Dr. Elena Voss should recuse herself from a disciplinary panel. Voss once incorporated unpublished data from a prior grant into a new proposal without disclosure—an act she regards as self-plagiarism, though it was never investigated. The panel must recommend sanctions for a graduate student caught copying substantial sections into a dissertation chapter. Maya argued that Voss lacks moral standing to blame the student: having committed the same category of wrong, she is a hypocrite, and the student can legitimately reject her censure regardless of procedural authority. Jonas replied that standing is not monolithic; Voss’s institutional role gives her decision-making authority that is independent of her personal moral record, and in any case self-plagiarism in grant writing and dissertation copying differ in kind and severity. Maya countered that institutional authority and moral standing are distinct—one can obey a verdict without accepting its moral force, and hypocrisy specifically undermines the latter—so Voss can chair the meeting but cannot sincerely blame. Jonas answered that if any past flaw disqualifies one from blaming, then nearly every disciplinary body collapses, and he added that Voss’s own failure might actually deepen her understanding rather than invalidate her judgment. They asked me to referee and I could not decide. Please go through their exchange step by step: say who is right about each specific claim, identify what both sides are missing about the relation between institutional role and moral standing, and state who is more right overall about whether Voss should recuse herself.
