I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has tried to work through the philosophy of mind without relying on the symmetry-breaking metaphors I once used for crystal structures. I have become convinced that illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—the view that subjects do not literally possess phenomenal properties but systematically misrepresent themselves as possessing them—is at least coherent, yet the literature treats it almost exclusively as an all-or-nothing thesis. I want to examine a more granular variant I am calling valence-selective illusionism: the claim that sensory phenomenal properties (for instance, the reddish qualitative character of a visual experience) are genuinely instantiated and functional, whereas affective phenomenal properties—specifically the intrinsic badness of pain and the intrinsic goodness of pleasure—are illusory representations produced by evaluative circuits that mistakenly confer normative force upon intensity. Before I settle my own view, I want your credence that valence-selective illusionism is correct, or at least closer to the truth than both global illusionism and full-blown phenomenal realism. Do not give me a bare number. Decompose the judgment into at least four terms: first, the prior probability that some form of illusionism is correct rather than realism or eliminativism about consciousness altogether; second, the probability that phenomenal properties, if they exist, can be ontologically partitioned into sensory and affective kinds with genuinely independent statuses; third, the probability that evolutionary and neurocomputational accounts of nociception and reward can fully explain the appearance of intrinsic valence without leaving a phenomenal residue; fourth, the probability that preserving sensory phenomenology allows this selective view to evade the standard explanatory-gap objections that weaken global illusionism. Assign each term a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine into an overall credence, and then identify which term you are least confident in and what specific empirical or conceptual finding—perhaps in the representational logic of affective content or in the neuroscience of pain modulation—would most shift that term and therefore your final number.
