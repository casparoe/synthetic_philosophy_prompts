I have constructed the following argument for a seminar on Goodman’s new riddle of induction, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to undermine the very practice it discusses. Here are the steps:

(1) For any observed regularity expressed with a predicate F (e.g., all observed emeralds are green), there exists a systematically definable rival predicate G (e.g., grue) such that every observed instance falls under both F and G, yet F and G entail contradictory predictions for all unobserved cases.

(2) If two hypotheses are equally compatible with all available evidence, then rational inductive practice must either assign them equal support or identify a legitimate non-evidential asymmetry between them; absent such an asymmetry, preference for one is arbitrary. Call this the neutrality constraint.

(3) Applying neutrality to F and G: since the evidence underdetermines them, induction must either license both or license neither.

(4) Licensing both is impossible for a rational agent, because F and G generate contradictory expectations about future observations.

(5) Therefore induction must license neither—global inductive skepticism follows.

(6) Yet we clearly do rely on induction; for instance, we rationally expect the sun to rise tomorrow and plan accordingly. So (5) must be rejected.

(7) Hence the neutrality constraint in (2) must fail: there must be a legitimate asymmetry that distinguishes F from G without appealing to the evidence itself.

(8) The standard Goodmanian asymmetry is entrenchment: F is more entrenched than G in virtue of our actual history of projecting F.

(9) But entrenchment is not an intrinsic logical or metaphysical feature of the predicates; it is a contingent historical fact about which ones a particular linguistic community has projected in the past.

(10) Therefore, appealing to entrenchment to justify preferring F over G presupposes that those past projections were already legitimate inductive inferences—i.e., it presupposes the very inductive reasoning it was meant to justify.

(11) So the only available defense of induction against skepticism is viciously circular.

(12) Consequently, we must either accept inductive skepticism (rejecting (6)) or accept that induction is justified circularly (rejecting the demand for non-circular justification).

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to preserve (1) and (6)—that the grue-style underdetermination is genuine and that we do rationally rely on induction—but I want to know which premise is the weakest link and what abandoning it costs. In particular, does (2) contain a hidden equivocation between evidential compatibility and inductive support? Does (4) slide from “cannot jointly act on both” to “cannot jointly believe both”? And does (9) mischaracterize entrenchment as an inductive claim rather than a descriptive fact about linguistic practice that can ground rational preference without circularity?
