I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have taken up philosophy of science with the same systematic discipline I once applied to calibration protocols. I now serve on the review board of a science communication institute that approves public-facing exhibit texts combining recent empirical findings with broader conceptual framing; we receive a proposal every two weeks, I am expected to identify conceptual missteps within thirty minutes, and any recommendation that crosses into foundational questions must be co-signed by a staff philosopher. After working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty in a reading group, I have become convinced that many disputes we encounter—authors treating framework assumptions as ordinary empirical hypotheses, or treating genuinely contested measurements as beyond doubt—are best understood through the distinction between hinge propositions and the empirical claims that turn within them. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a one-time judgment: an ordered checklist or flowchart in words that I can apply to any claim in a proposal, specifying at each stage what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a clear refusal, and where it must be handed to my co-signer because the issue is one of framework choice rather than fact.

The procedure should be grounded in hinge epistemology—taking seriously the idea that certain propositions stand fast for us not because they are well-confirmed empirically but because they form part of the framework within which inquiry operates—while remaining fully operable by someone without formal philosophical training. It must handle a hard case I have begun to notice: when authors use novel AI-generated simulation outputs or large-scale replication datasets to challenge what has traditionally functioned as a hinge commitment, such as the direct mapping between a mathematical formalism and physical measurement, or the conventional threshold for statistical significance in a given field. I want ordered tests that determine whether a claim is being treated as empirically testable within the current framework; whether it actually functions as a hinge that makes such testing possible; whether there is evidence of hinge migration, where a proposition shifts status across contexts or disciplines; and whether the challenge to it is genuinely empirical or rather a proposal to change the framework itself. The procedure should also be explicit about its failure modes—where it might misclassify a genuinely revolutionary empirical challenge as a category error, or protect a moribund hinge as if it were still functional—and should conclude with an honest statement of what such a procedure can and cannot accomplish for a board like ours.

Please apply the procedure step-by-step to three worked cases drawn from recent proposals: first, an exhibit text claiming that quantum decoherence measurements have "falsified" the long-standing hinge commitment that macroscopic objects possess definite positions; second, a proposal arguing that climate-model parameterizations should be evaluated by the same direct empirical standards as instrument readings, without recognizing their hinge-dependent status; and third—a case where I expect my own scientific instincts to resist the outcome—a proposal using large-scale biomedical replication data to argue that the conventional statistical-significance threshold is migrating from hinge to empirical status and should no longer stand fast. For each case, show the ordered application, flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must go to my philosopher co-signer, note any answer the procedure delivers that I will not like, and finish by stating exactly what this kind of operationalized hinge epistemology can and cannot do for practitioners in my position.
