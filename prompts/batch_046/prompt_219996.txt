I've been trying to pin down my view on reasons for action after a long discussion thread about internalism versus externalism, and I've landed—only provisionally—on a hybrid I'd call access-internalism with external anchoring. The idea is that a consideration counts as a reason for an agent only if it's reflectively accessible to them, not hidden behind opaque causal mechanisms or empirical discovery they couldn't in principle make; but the normative weight of that reason is calibrated by external relational properties—reliability, evolutionary function, or coherence with the agent's broader belief system—that the agent need not represent. So the filter on what counts is internalist, while the metric on how strongly it counts is externalist. I take this to avoid Williams's motivational objection without falling into the arbitrariness worry often pressed against pure externalism, but I'm unsure whether the combination is genuinely stable or just a diplomatic split-the-difference move.

What would actually count against this specific hybrid? I want more than a rehearsed list of standard objections to internalism or externalism taken separately; I want the defeaters that target the joint structure. For example, are there cases where the anchoring mechanism assigns normative weight to considerations that fail the accessibility filter—say, a reliably formed but entirely opaque desire—or where accessibility without anchoring produces normatively inert "reasons" that can't guide deliberation? Could the view survive only by redefining "access" or "anchoring" so broadly that it absorbs every counterexample, and if so, does that insulation make it unfalsifiable in a damaging way for a theory of practical reason? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, note which ones a committed defender of this hybrid would concede versus dismiss, and be honest about whether the position is well insulated—if it is, say whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice here. Finally, please say what would change your mind: which argument, case, or formal result would push you toward pure internalism, pure externalism, or abandoning the hybrid altogether, and where your reasoning on this is most likely to fail.
