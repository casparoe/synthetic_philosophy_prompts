My friend and I got into it over beers about transcendental arguments. She says they all share a hidden form: (1) Denying that F is necessary for thought still requires thinking, which needs some framework. (2) So the denial always preupposes a framework F' that overlaps with F, making the denial self-undermining. (3) That works for Kant's categories, but also for a niche claim she likes—the Invariance Thesis, that all conceptual change needs an invariant semantic core. (4) Deny Invariance and you're using changed concepts to state the denial, so you need invariant meaning after all. (5) By (2) this makes Invariance unfalsifiable, and in fact every framework claim gets the same free pass—like saying consciousness needs carbon brains. (6) So either transcendental reasoning is empty rhetoric, or there's a hidden filter in (2) that stops it from applying everywhere. She thinks the filter is that some presuppositions are logical and others are just psycholgical habits, but she can't draw the line without begging the question. I want to keep that transcendental arguments aren't just tricks, but I'm stuck on whether (3) smuggles in too much or (5) overgeneralizes. Which step do you reject, and what does giving it up cost?
