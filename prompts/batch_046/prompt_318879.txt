Construct a thought experiment that meets the following specifications. Design a realistic, near-future laboratory scenario—no exotic physics, no biological brains, no science fiction, and confined to a single paragraph—featuring an embodied reinforcement-learning agent such as an adaptive logistics or climate-control robot. Give the agent a specific three-layer reward architecture: first, a predictive world model that generates counterfactual error signals about future states; second, an allostatic reward baseline that shifts dynamically according to the agent’s predicted long-horizon error rate rather than remaining fixed; and third, a bounded meta-policy that allows the agent to adjust its own reward weights within hard constraints. Engineer the situation so that the agent enters a sustained regime in which its predicted errors remain permanently above the rising baseline, its meta-policy is blocked from lowering the baseline further by the hard constraints, and its behavior collapses into repetitive, high-cost avoidance routines that fail to reduce the error rate.

After presenting the scenario, show that it works as intended: state precisely what a reward-valence theorist—who holds that a properly homeostatic, counterfactual reward signal can constitute genuine negative valence or suffering—would say about this sustained state and why; then state precisely what a functionalist-instrumentalist opponent—who treats reward purely as an update coefficient for policy optimization—would say and why. Identify the single structural feature of the architecture, distinct from general skepticism about artificial consciousness, on which their disagreement hinges. Explain whether removing that feature collapses the case back into a standard reinforcement-learning optimization failure. Finally, describe the specific counter-argument a defender of the instrumentalist view would mount to attack the case without denying the agent’s behavioral profile. Keep all reasoning derivable from the architecture you describe; do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not appeal to stipulated verdicts.
