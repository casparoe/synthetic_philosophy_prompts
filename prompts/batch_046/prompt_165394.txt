Our working group on the justification of deduction has evaluated five proposals for resolving Carroll's tortoise problem—the infinite regress that arises when one demands a further premise for every application of modus ponens. Our precedents are as follows. First, we rejected a proposal that declared modus ponens an unanalyzable primitive of rational inference, offering no mechanism beyond bare assertion. Second, we rejected a proposal that justified the rule through its coherence with other logical principles, viewing this as benign circularity that fails to halt the ascent. Third, we approved—subject to the condition that no hidden meta-premise be smuggled in—a proposal that dissolved the challenge by distinguishing the use of a rule from the assertion of a premise, arguing that applying modus ponens is an inferential act rather than an additional hypothetical to be accepted. Fourth, we rejected a proposal that grounded the rule in empirical cognitive science, treating logical justification as reducible to psychological architecture. Fifth, we approved a transcendental proposal arguing that any representation or questioning of modus ponens already presupposes it, making the rule indispensable to the very framework within which the challenge is raised.

We now face a new proposal. It introduces a "termination token"—a structural license built into formal systems representing deductive dialogue. The token authorizes the transition from premises to conclusion without being a premise itself. It is not justified by further premises, not defended as a psychological fact, and not presented as a transcendental condition of thought. Its authority is purely functional: without such a token, formal representation of reasoning could never terminate, and its role is defined solely by its architectural position. The authors insist this is neither a bare primitive like our first case nor a pragmatic dissolution like our third, but an internal convention of formal practice.
