Question: Functionalism holds that mental states are constituted by their causal-functional roles rather than by intrinsic physical properties. Multiple realizability—the claim that a given mental state can be instantiated by diverse physical substrates—has traditionally been treated as a primary motivation for functionalism. But some philosophers argue that if multiple realizability is pushed too far, it actually threatens functionalism by making mental-state attribution excessively liberal. Consider in particular the hypothetical case of "functional mirroring": a distributed, non-biological system (for instance, a blockchain-based ledger architecture with distributed nodes) that replicates every causal-functional relation characteristic of human belief-formation—including feedback loops, error-correction, and representational updating—without sharing any evolutionary history or biological substrate with human cognizers. Would a standard causal-role functionalist be compelled to attribute beliefs to such a system? Does this case strengthen the functionalist program, or does it reveal a need for supplementary constraints (teleological, representational, or otherwise)? In your answer, discuss the relationship between Putnam's original arguments for multiple realizability and later objections—such as those involving liberal attribution or absent qualia—that attempt to limit functionalism's scope.

Student answer: Functionalism identifies mental states by causal role, not by physical makeup, so if a silicon brain or distributed computer network replicates the relevant inputs, outputs, and internal transitions, it should have the same beliefs and pains as a human. Multiple realizability supports this: Putnam showed that pain isn't identical to C-fibers because an octopus could feel pain with different biology, and Block extended this insight with his Chinese-nation thought experiment, showing that even an entire population arranged to simulate neural firing might realize mental states. So more realizers mean stronger functionalism. However, if we allow every physical system that matches the causal profile—including the economy of Bolivia or a blockchain ledger that copies belief-formation dynamics—to count as having beliefs, we face the problem of liberalism. To block this, some functionalists appeal to evolutionary history, like Millikan's biosemantics, which adds a teleological constraint on proper function. Others, like Fodor, restrict mental content to narrow representational roles computed over internal symbols, but Fodor's computationalism is actually incompatible with functionalism because he insists that mental representations require causal-historical ties to their contents rather than pure causal-role relations. In the functional-mirroring case, I think functionalism should just bite the bullet: if the blockchain really duplicates every functional relation of belief, then it has beliefs. The worry only arises if we confuse belief with phenomenal consciousness, but functionalism was designed for propositional attitudes, not consciousness, so the tension dissolves once we keep those separate.
