I am preparing a feature on the logic of everyday "if"-statements, and a philosopher of language I interviewed offered the following summary of David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. "Lewis proved that if indicative conditionals—sentences like 'if the battery is charged, the device works'—are assigned probabilities via Adams's thesis, so that the probability of the conditional equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, then together with the ratio formula and import-export closure, the probabilities collapse. Specifically, for any propositions A and B, the probability of 'if A then B' ends up equal to the unconditional probability of B, which renders the antecedent probabilistically irrelevant. Because of this collapse, indicative conditionals cannot carry any genuinely probabilistic content, which is precisely why Stalnaker abandoned probability altogether and developed his selection-function semantics, evaluating a conditional by examining the closest possible world in which the antecedent holds. Lewis intended the result as a definitive refutation of every non-material account of conditionals, and that is why contemporary linguists have largely dropped probabilistic frameworks for ordinary 'if'-clauses in favor of purely modal or causal analyses."

The summary sounds authoritative, but I worry it conflates formal premises, historical responses, and claims about linguistic practice. Could you audit it for fairness? In particular, please separate what is accurate from what is simplified or mistaken regarding: (a) the exact premises Lewis requires and whether import-export closure belongs among them; (b) whether Stalnaker's selection-function account was developed as a direct reaction to Lewis and whether Stalnaker ever endorsed probabilistic extensions of his view; (c) the scope of Lewis's result—does it refute all non-material analyses, or only those that combine Adams's thesis with specific structural conditions; and (d) whether it is fair to say that linguists have abandoned probabilistic models for indicative conditionals, or whether probabilistic and decision-theoretic treatments persist. Most importantly, I want to know whether the summary's conclusion—that Lewis's result dooms any coherent probabilistic semantics for indicative conditionals—follows once these corrections are made, or whether it overreaches.
