Next week I defend, in our metaphysics and comparative philosophy seminar, the thesis that Gongsun Long's claim that a white horse is not a horse should be reconstructed not as a fallacy about class membership or a linguistic ambiguity, but as an intuition pump about sortal ontology: horse and white-horse answer to different conditions of diachronic identity, so they denote entities with different persistence conditions, anticipating modern distinctions between natural kinds and phase sorts. The audience is split between analytic metaphysicians, who will treat this as an anachronistic projection of contemporary kind-essentialism onto the School of Names, and scholars of early Chinese philosophy, who will object that I am ignoring the Mohist logical context and the dialogue's actual rhetorical aim of refuting commonsense distinctions. Give me the objections in the order they will likely come, phrased the way each camp would put them, with a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a frank note on where each reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—specifically, the one that targets the claim that persistence conditions can be read out of a dialogue that never mentions change over time. Also sketch briefly the three premises of my argument that these objections should aim at: (1) the dialogue's examples turn on substitutability failures that mirror modern sortal-relative identity conditions; (2) Hui Shi's criticisms in other fragments presuppose a distinction between enduring kinds and transient qualified entities; (3) treating the argument as purely linguistic misses its normative force about how we ought to individuate entities in practice.
