I am not an academic philosopher, though I have spent considerable time thinking about whether "truth" denotes anything genuinely real, or whether it is merely a linguistic device for endorsing a claim without repeating its content. Next week I am recording an interview for a small podcast programme aimed at intellectually curious listeners who lack specialist training. My guest is an unnamed philosopher who holds a strict deflationary position: she maintains that the predicate "true" serves solely as a logical instrument for disquotation, and that asking what makes a statement true is a grammatical confusion—comparable to asking what makes a bachelor unmarried. She is adept at deflecting objections with the phrase "that is only a feature of endorsement, not a difficulty for my account."

Please draft, in British English, a sequence of interview questions that makes this deflection progressively harder to deploy. I want the questions to engage with actual philosophical pressure points rather than asking for a restatement of the view. Specifically, include questions on: (1) how her account handles universal generalisations involving truth, such as "everything predicted by this climate model is true"; (2) how it treats counterfactual claims about truth, for example "if Newton had never lived, the law of gravitation would still be true"; and (3) how it accommodates normative endorsements like "it is true that cruelty is wrong." For each of these, provide the likely reply she will offer, followed by a sharper follow-up that anticipates and undercuts that reply. Conclude with a single question about what kind of evidence—whether conceptual, empirical, or logical—could genuinely falsify her position, rather than being reinterpreted as yet another feature of endorsement. The tone should remain respectful but rigorous, suitable for an intelligent lay audience. Please do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
