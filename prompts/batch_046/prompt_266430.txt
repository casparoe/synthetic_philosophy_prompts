In our philosophy of language seminar we have been working through the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument and the claim that first-language acquisition requires substantial innate structure. One comparison that keeps surfacing — in lecture and in assigned texts — likens a child acquiring syntax to a kitten developing binocular vision: both capacities appear to depend on exposure to a specific kind of environmental input during a narrow critical period, and deprivation during that window produces a permanent deficit. The analogy is deployed to argue that language is not genuinely learned but rather biologically programmed to unfold, with experience serving only as a trigger.

Please audit how far this correspondence holds. Provide the mapping — child, linguistic input, critical-period closure, deprivation outcomes, underlying biological mechanism — onto the visual-development case. Then show where the analogy breaks down: is linguistic input as sparse and modality-specific as visual stimulation, does language have a direct analog to monocular deprivation, and does the comparison quietly import the assumption that language is a perceptual system rather than a generative cognitive one? Finally, assess whether the nativist conclusion survives once these disanalogies are explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along. Answer in American English.
