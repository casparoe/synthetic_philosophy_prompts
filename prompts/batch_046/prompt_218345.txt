I have been discussing institutional design with colleagues in a reading group on democratic theory, and I keep returning to a pattern I have noticed in recent proposals for long-term governance — particularly in AI oversight frameworks and climate treaty architectures — that does not seem to fit neatly into the standard vocabulary of Ulysses contracts or constitutional precommitment. In the familiar cases, a present agent binds a future agent against a predictable shift in preference or will: the constitutional clause prevents future legislatures from weakening rights, or the central bank charter prevents future policymakers from inflating for short-term gain. The enemy is weakness of will, not error of judgment. Yet there is a distinct configuration in which the binding targets future belief-formation rather than future desire. The designers write rules that anticipate future evidence — say, data showing that a safety protocol is overly restrictive — and specify in advance that such evidence will not trigger revision unless it crosses a predefined severity threshold and proceeds through a deliberately cumbersome procedure. The aim is not to prevent future selves from being tempted away from the good, but to prevent them from being rationally persuaded away from the framework by arguments or observations that the present designers regard as genuinely possible but likely misleading.

This strikes me as conceptually different from ordinary entrenchment, from simple institutional inertia, and from standard conservative updating, but I am unsure whether democratic theory or constitutional philosophy already has a precise label for it. Is there an established concept — perhaps discussed in the literature on epistemic democracy, constitutional rigidity, or commitment devices — that captures this specific form of precommitment against epistemic revision? If not, I would like you to name and define the phenomenon, delineate its boundaries against related ideas (including the ordinary Ulysses contract, rational stubbornness, and path-dependent lock-in), and explain where its conceptual edges become unstable.
