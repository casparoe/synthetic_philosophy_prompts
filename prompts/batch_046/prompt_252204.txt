I have been thinking about Galen Strawson's argument that true moral responsibility is impossible because nobody can be causa sui—there is always some prior cause of one's character that one did not choose. I would like you to write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors debating this, but set inside a specific thought experiment that keeps puzzling me: imagine a person who, over many years, voluntarily undergoes a long series of guided psychological and neurological adjustments that gradually replace nearly every original motivation and trait that shaped her actions, and who remembers and genuinely endorses each step. The first speaker should defend Strawson's Basic Argument, pressing that since the original self that began the process was not self-created, the final self still inherits an unchosen foundation, and that every subsequent ratification is just another determined effect in an endless regress. The second speaker should argue that moral authorship need not come from a single uncaused point of origin; instead, it can belong to the whole narrative chain, with later selves genuinely authorizing earlier changes so that responsibility is distributed across the trajectory rather than located at its start. They must respond directly to one another—the Strawsonian should anticipate the narrative reply and ask whether ratification is merely another caused event, and the defender should meet that objection head-on rather than changing the subject. Please present the exchange as a natural but intellectually serious dialogue, and please write your answer in British English.
