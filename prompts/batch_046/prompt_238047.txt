Please write the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Generalization and Its Limits," co-hosted by a philosophy of science program and a computer-science department that studies out-of-distribution robustness. The audience is split: roughly half are philosophers of science deeply familiar with Goodman's new riddle of induction, and roughly half are machine-learning researchers who think constantly about generalization but have never encountered the predicate "grue." You must frame the day's discussion in about four minutes of spoken remarks, without slides, bullet points, or lists. The philosophical idea must do real structural work: use it to show both groups that their shared problem—justifying predictions about cases not yet observed—contains a hidden symmetry that only becomes visible when translated across their vocabularies. To make this concrete, introduce an invented predicate called "glume," defined precisely as follows: for any predictive model evaluated before an arbitrarily chosen date T, "glume" applies if and only if the model's error rate on post-T data remains within a fixed bound B; for any model evaluated after T, "glume" applies if and only if its error rate exceeds B, with no detectable internal architectural change at the boundary. Use "glume" to demonstrate that past consistency under one descriptive projection does not entail future consistency under a logically coextensive but differently projected description, and argue that this structural obstacle should reshape how the workshop discusses "robustness" without pretending to resolve it. Write in a formal spoken register; do not cite authors, years, or specific paper titles—refer only to "Goodman's riddle" and "the cutoff at T." Do not ask the audience to take action, vote, or choose sides; let the tension stand as the framing problem for the entire day.
