I was recently appointed to a regional land-use board in a semi-arid valley where centuries-old irrigation networks still shape settlement patterns, but where current proposals are almost always justified with five-year economic projections and individual political mandates. I need a reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist with clear stop points—that my team can run when evaluating development proposals, grounded specifically in Fernand Braudel’s distinction between the longue durée (geography, climate, and slow-moving collective structures, including mentalités) and the short-term domain of events, individual actors, and electoral cycles. For each step, specify what counts as sufficient evidence that a proposal respects or violates a long-duration structure, where the procedure yields a definite approval or rejection, and where it must halt and hand the question to human judgment because the conflict is fundamentally one of values rather than historical or geographical fact. Then apply the procedure to three concrete cases: constructing a new reservoir that would alter regional hydrology for generations; building a luxury waterfront district on land that has served as seasonal floodplain for centuries; and restoring an abandoned communal canal network that predates modern property boundaries. Include at least one case where the procedure recommends against development despite strong local economic and political support, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle on its own. Write the response in American English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
