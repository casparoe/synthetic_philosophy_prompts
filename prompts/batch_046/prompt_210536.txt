The term “collapse” in archaeology and comparative history functions as a structural analogy: a society is treated like an edifice whose load-bearing institutions—palace economies, literacy systems, long-distance exchange networks, or monumental construction programs—can suffer catastrophic failure, leaving rubble in the form of abandoned cities, lost scripts, and sharp demographic declines. The analogy is routinely invoked to argue that societies do not merely change form but can genuinely fail, since a building either stands intact or falls; there is no stable intermediate state of “collapsed-but-functional.” I want an audit of how far this holds across three cases that are often grouped under the same label: the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean (where Linear B administration and palace-centered redistribution dissolved), the Classic Maya lowlands (where urban centers were abandoned and stelae carving largely ceased, though populations and cultural practices persisted elsewhere), and Rapa Nui/Easter Island (where moai production halted and severe ecological degradation occurred, yet the population did not vanish). First, provide an explicit correspondence table—what in the society plays the role of the foundation, the load-bearing walls, the stress fractures, the rubble, and the architect or engineer whose absence might explain the failure. Second, identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the intended conclusion: societies lack a single designer, can redistribute functions across decentralized nodes, and often preserve cultural continuity despite institutional discontinuity. Does the analogy therefore smuggle in an unjustified teleology—that societies are engineered to maintain a particular height of complexity? Third, determine whether the conclusion that these three cases represent a shared phenomenon of societal “failure” survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether “collapse” should be replaced by a different conceptual model (not an organism dying, not a market correcting, but perhaps a specific alternative you specify). In your assessment, separate the disanalogies that undermine the “failure” narrative from those that are merely decorative, and say whether any revised analogy could do the same argumentative work without importing the same hidden assumptions.
