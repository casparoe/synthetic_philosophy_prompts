I'm in this philosophy discord where people keep throwing around Sartre's line that we're "condemned to be free" everytime someone says they had no choice about thier career or whatever. One guy used it to shut down a friend who said she "had to" take a bad job because of student debt — he said that's just bad faith, full stop, since Sartre proves we can always choose. But that feels like it's smuggling in a ton of different claims under one slogan.

Can you separate out the precise theses hiding inside "condemned to be free"? I'm thinking there's at least: (1) freedom as an unavoidable ontological burden — we can't opt out of being the kind of beings who choose; (2) freedom as total absence of external constraints — we can literally do anything regardless of circumstances; (3) freedom as absence of predetermined essence — we don't have a fixed human nature or destiny. For each, is it a platitude, a serious Sartrean claim, or just false? And then, which of those readings would actually support his argument that the friend is in bad faith for saying she "had to" take the job? I also wonder if bad faith here is just generic lying-to-oneself, or if Sartre means something more specific like denying our own facticity or transcendence — but I don't want to drag in jargon unless it actually changes the answer. One more thing: does the slogan apply differently to people who are genuinely coerced (like, physical force) versus people who just face bad options? Or is that distinction missing the point of existential freedom?
