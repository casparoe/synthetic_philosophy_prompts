I’ve inherited my grandfather’s small workshop, where he maintained a nearly obsolete technique for repairing antique navigation instruments. I’m the last person fully trained in it, and if I stop, the knowledge disappears with me; but there is no income in it, only continuous, unbroken practice. At the same time, my younger sister has developed a chronic condition that requires expensive, ongoing care, and I’ve been offered a stable corporate logistics job that would cover her treatment completely. A colleague told me I should settle this by listing everything I’d lose in each path—heritage and mastery versus security and family support—and then asking which loss feels heavier, since every value can eventually be translated into a personal cost. I tried that, but it feels wrong: the corporate role demands exactly the kind of fragmented schedule and geographic stability that makes the workshop impossible, so it isn’t that I’m weighing two goods on one scale; it’s that accepting one option seems to destroy the very conditions that make the other one exist. Is my resistance just emotional attachment, or is there a real philosophical problem with trying to resolve choices like this by measuring losses against each other?
