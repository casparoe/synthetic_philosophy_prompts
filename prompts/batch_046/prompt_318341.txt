I have been wondering whether it is always irrational to continue a course of action mainly because you have already invested heavily in it. The usual answer—that sunk costs should be ignored because only future costs and benefits matter—seems too simple, because it treats every past investment as if it were just a number in an account. But I have been thinking that some investments change what the project means: once you have poured your labor, your pride, and your identity into something, walking away might not be efficient, yet it can feel like a betrayal of the person you became through that work. I am not sure how to name this idea—perhaps it is a kind of backward-looking loyalty, or a claim that costs can become constitutive of a commitment rather than merely instrumental to it—but I want to see if it holds up.

Read The Bridge on the River Kwai through this tension. Colonel Nicholson commits his men and himself to building a bridge for their captors, takes fierce pride in its perfection, and when he discovers it must be destroyed, he resists—not out of treason, but out of loyalty to the work itself. Where in the film does his attachment look like the classic sunk-cost fallacy, and where does it look like integrity or even moral necessity? Does the final scene, in which he collapses onto the detonator and asks what he has done, settle the question, or does it leave the two interpretations in conflict? More broadly, is there a coherent way to distinguish destructive sunk-cost reasoning from the kind of loyalty that gives a life continuity, without simply saying the difference is revealed by the outcome? I ask because I have no formal training in philosophy or economics, but the problem keeps returning in my own life when I think about commitments I have kept long after they seemed practical to maintain.
