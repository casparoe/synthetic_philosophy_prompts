I have been working through an unnamed development-studies textbook that summarizes Amartya Sen’s entitlement theory of famine. The author writes as follows. Sen holds that famines are rarely caused by an absolute shortage of food; rather, they occur when people suffer a sudden collapse in their exchange entitlements—the legal and economic capacities that allow them to command food through production, trade, or labor. In the Bengal famine of 1943, rural laborers lost purchasing power because wartime inflation and administrative priorities disrupted rice markets, even though aggregate availability was not exceptionally low. The author extends this framework to the Irish famine, claiming that Sen treats it as proof that famine is never a natural disaster but always a political crime: British administration deliberately protected the exchange entitlements of landlords and merchants—who could still export grain—while allowing the direct entitlements of peasants to vanish entirely. Turning to the Great Leap Forward, the author asserts that Sen shows how collective procurement policies eliminated what Sen calls institutional entitlement buffers—protective mechanisms that, in Sen’s account, insulate rural households from direct entitlement collapse—thereby making the Chinese case a pure entitlement failure rather than a compound of ecological stress and policy error. From all this, the author concludes that Sen’s framework necessarily implies that democratic governments never experience famines, because democratic accountability functions precisely as such an institutional entitlement buffer that prevents exchange collapse.
