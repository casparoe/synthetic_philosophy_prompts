I'm drafting a proposal for a four-month independent research project (final paper: about 6,000 words, one semester) in the ethics of public commemoration. I want to investigate a specific framing that I haven't seen treated systematically: the idea that statues and street names do not both belong to a single category of "honoring," but instead operate in different moral registers that I will call focal and distributed commemoration. On my working distinction, a statue functions as a focal, performative act of celebration—an object that invites present-tense emotional engagement and stands in a spatial center—while a street name functions as a distributed, infrastructural coordinate embedded in a navigational system, carrying reference rather than praise. My core question is whether this distinction can justify differential removal policies (for example, taking down a statue while retaining a street name for the same figure), or whether it collapses once we recognize that both depend on a prior collective decision about which figures deserve sustained presence in shared space.

Please write the proposal with the following sections, in this exact order, with approximate lengths indicated:

The Precise Question (about 350 words): State the research question so it has a determinate answer. Define focal versus distributed commemoration precisely, explain how the distinction applies to statues versus street names, and frame the normative issue: does the difference in moral register generate different obligations, or is the distinction merely descriptive?

Why Existing Positions Don't Settle It (about 500 words): Describe the standard debate—arguments for removal based on harm or disrespect, arguments for retention based on historical context or slippery slopes—without citing specific authors by name or requesting a literature review. Explain why these positions tend to treat all commemorative objects as belonging to one moral kind, and identify the gap that the focal/distributed distinction is meant to fill. Note what an answer on either side would have to establish.

The Approach and Method (about 600 words): Explain how the project will proceed conceptually rather than empirically. Specify what kinds of cases and boundary conditions you will examine (for instance, renamed streets that retain old names in local vernacular, or statues relocated to museums versus destroyed). Clarify why a philosophical rather than historical or sociological method is appropriate here, and identify the obvious alternative framing (treating all commemoration as a single speech act) and why you are not adopting it.

What Would Count as a Result or Failure (about 400 words): State the possible conclusions—perhaps that the distinction holds and justifies differential treatment, that it holds descriptively but has no independent normative weight, or that it collapses because distributed references still depend on prior focal honor. Say what considerations would decide between them, and specify what would count as the project failing (for example, if the distinction turns out to be purely aesthetic with no ethical relevance).

Scope and Realistic Assessment (about 300 words): Assess whether this is tractable for a single semester at this length. Identify the risk that the project becomes too broad—say, by expanding into a general theory of public space or by requiring extensive case studies—and say how you will limit the scope to avoid that. Give an honest evaluation of what a useful deliverable looks like for people actually deciding on local commemoration policies.
