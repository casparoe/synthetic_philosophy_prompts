I'm serving on my university's strategic research funding committee, and we've been approached by a private foundation offering an unrestricted forty-million-dollar endowment on a peculiar condition: our committee must reach unanimous agreement that we sincerely intend, as a collective body, to impose a permanent moratorium on funding any project that uses large language models for data analysis or interpretation, but the foundation will verify only the sincerity of that shared intention at the moment of contract signing, not any subsequent enforcement. Once the funds are transferred there is no legal obligation to reject such proposals, and our own evaluation criteria strongly favor innovative methodology, so every member privately expects we would not actually apply the moratorium. Some colleagues argue that rational institutional agency permits exactly this kind of strategic intention-formation—forming an intention we foresee abandoning—because the collective benefit outweighs any internal inconsistency, while others object that a deliberative body cannot coherently hold a shared intention it already knows its members will not honor, particularly since the committee must continue to function as a unit after signing, and they worry that signing would constitute a form of collective bad faith that undermines the conditions for rational group agency.

I need to draft a short written opinion for circulation before Tuesday's meeting recommending a concrete course of action with a clear time frame—either to sign the pledge and formally record the intention, with a specified date by which we would confirm our internal stance, or to decline the offer and articulate our refusal—and it must treat the strongest opposing view seriously rather than merely restating our contractual freedom to change course later. Please present the recommendation in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold formatting, or tables, written in the manner of a formal internal letter or email, engaging both the analog to individual rational intention-formation and the distinctive problem of collective intentionality that arises when a deliberative body must live with the psychological and procedural consequences of an intention it has already decided not to execute.
