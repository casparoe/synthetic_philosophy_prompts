I am writing a feature on the philosophical afterlife of Erwin Schrödinger’s essays on consciousness, and I have become tangled in an argument that seems to swallow its own tail. I should be grateful if you could help me locate the exit, and I would ask that your reply be composed in British English.

Here is the chain as I have reconstructed it from my notes:

(1) Schrödinger maintained, particularly in his later reflections on Advaita Vedanta, that the plurality of individual minds is an illusion (Maya); there is ultimately only one universal consciousness, of which each apparent self is a localised expression.

(2) He proposed that quantum mechanics offers a structural parallel: just as entangled systems are not described by separate wavefunctions but by a single, non-separable joint state, so apparently distinct minds might be non-separable manifestations within one conscious field.

(3) If this is correct, then the traditional problem of other minds—how I can justify attributing consciousness to beings other than myself—simply dissolves, for there is no ontologically distinct “other mind” to justify; all experience occurs within the same substrate.

(4) But by the same token, the functional and moral distinction between self and other also dissolves: if harming another being is, at the metaphysical level, harming oneself, then egoism and altruism become structurally identical, and moral philosophy loses the asymmetry it requires.

(5) Nevertheless, Schrödinger consistently articulated an ethics of universal compassion, treating other beings as genuinely other recipients of moral concern rather than as mere modalities of his own consciousness.

(6) Hence, either his quantum-Vedantic metaphysics proves too much by undermining the very distinctions that make his ethics coherent, or one of the preceding steps must be rejected.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) or (5), since both seem central to Schrödinger’s mature outlook, but I recognise that holding both may be precisely what forces the difficulty. Please set out your diagnosis with the same numbered references, and explain whether the flaw is a subtle equivocation—perhaps between metaphysical identity and phenomenal perspective—or whether one of the premises is simply less defensible than it appears.
