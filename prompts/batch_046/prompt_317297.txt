Consider the following argument, which begins with the standard Rietdijk–Putnam reasoning about special relativity and ends in what looks like self-refutation.

(1) If special relativity is correct, there is no absolute simultaneity, hence no frame-independent global present.

(2) So the block universe is correct: all spacetime events exist tenselessly and are equally real.

(3) In the block, causation is not an asymmetric relation of production but only a structural correlation across the four-dimensional manifold.

(4) Rational agency requires that an agent can bring about outcomes that are not yet real; without this production asymmetry, deliberation collapses into mere description of what is already fixed in the block.

(5) Therefore diachronic agency is impossible within eternalism.

(6) But the scientific reasoning that supports (1) and (2) is itself an exercise in diachronic agency—forming beliefs, testing predictions, revising theories over time.

(7) Hence the block universe undermines the rational processes that establish it, and we should reject (2) in favor of recovering an objective present.

The conclusion strikes me as wrong: I want to keep (1) and (2), and presentism is not saved. But I cannot locate the exact slip. Is the error in (3), in the move from (3) to (4), or in (6)? For each vulnerable step, explain what rejecting it costs—especially whether it forces the eternalist to abandon a defensible account of causation or of practical reasoning. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed block theorist would concede versus dismiss. If you think the argument is valid and the eternalist must bite a bullet, identify which bullet—loss of agency, loss of scientific self-support, or a hidden tensed premise—is least damaging.
