Construct a thought experiment in which the knowledge norm of assertion and the rational-belief norm come apart in the direction that is usually difficult to engineer: the speaker has a true, rationally justified belief that p, yet lacks knowledge of p for reasons other than falsity, unjustified belief, or a standard Gettier-style environmental accident. To do this, inject the following niche structural feature: the speaker's belief is produced by an opaque, institutionally certified epistemic pipeline—she operates a calibrated diagnostic or measurement protocol correctly, receives a true output, and is rational in trusting the institution's validation, but she cannot access, understand, or personally verify the internal calibration steps that guarantee reliability. Constraints: place the scenario in an ordinary professional setting (a clinical laboratory, a civil-engineering inspection unit, or a forensic accounting office), limit it to one paragraph, avoid exotic technology, AI oracles, or perfect predictors, and keep the speaker a single human agent rather than a collective. After presenting the case, demonstrate: (i) why the knowledge norm forbids assertion, specifying the belief, the method, and the nearby worlds in which the pipeline misfires despite correct operation; (ii) why the rational-belief norm permits it, showing that the speaker's evidence and reasoning are intact; (iii) which feature of institutional opacity does the work; (iv) whether removing that opacity collapses the case into a familiar false-belief or Gettier scenario; and (v) how a defender of the knowledge norm might reply—whether by appealing to a distinction between personal and delegated knowledge, or by arguing that institutional certification can transfer knowledge despite individual opacity.
