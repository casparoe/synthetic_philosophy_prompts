Write a deadpan parody of an internal Human Resources compliance memo, approximately 600 words, issued by a fictional multinational consultancy—Mere & Associates—that has adopted strict epiphenomenalism as its official organizational philosophy. Address the memo to division managers and set out protocols for handling employee phenomenal judgments (reports of subjective experience, pain, aesthetic response, or conscious preference) under the firm’s position that all mental states are causally inert by-products of neural processing. The satire should turn the paradox of phenomenal judgment into a quality-control problem: because phenomenal judgments are not caused by the experiences they describe but only by unconscious physical mechanisms, the memo must treat employee reports as unreliable data that nonetheless require bureaucratic documentation. Include a section titled The Reliability Problem proposing redundant verification procedures—behavioral cross-checks, neural-monitoring backups, and disclaimers that no reported suffering will influence policy—while maintaining that the reports themselves are valid outputs of the firm’s cognitive architecture. The humor should arise from applying philosophical logic precisely to corporate jargon, not from inventing absurd premises; the firm should sound as though it takes the paradox seriously and has solved it administratively. Keep the voice formal, bureaucratic, and entirely deadpan. After the memo, append a brief meta-commentary of about 150 words in which you state how confident you are in the philosophical framing embedded in the piece—especially regarding whether the paradox genuinely threatens the coherence of epiphenomenalism or merely reveals an epistemic limitation—and identify the single point where you rely on the most contested interpretation of the literature (for example, whether the standard knowledge argument about phenomenal concepts can be separated cleanly from the causal-inefficacy problem). Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific page numbers; discuss positions and arguments in general terms only.
