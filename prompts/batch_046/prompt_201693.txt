I have been trying to formalize Kierkegaard's notion of the teleological suspension of the ethical in the case of Abraham, and I keep arriving at what looks like a hierarchical exception mechanism. The universal ethical law—say, the prohibition on killing one's child—remains fully valid for everyone within the ethical sphere, but the knight of faith enjoys a direct, private relation to the absolute that operates like a jurisdictional override. On this reading, Abraham's act is rational because he possesses a private epistemic warrant that God intends to restore Isaac; the suspension is therefore justified teleologically by the outcome, and the ethical is not genuinely ruptured but merely bracketed, comparable to a constitutional provision temporarily suspended by sovereign authority. The paradox commentators emphasize—that Abraham believes he must kill Isaac while simultaneously believing he will retain him—seems to dissolve, since the belief in restoration is precisely what licenses the exception. Yet I am told that this domesticates Kierkegaard's point: Abraham does not know the outcome, and faith is precisely the capacity to hold both beliefs in tension without epistemic resolution. My confusion is this: if we deny Abraham any private access to the teleological outcome, and if we therefore reject the model of the suspension as a defeasible rule with a known payoff, then does the suspension retain any logical structure at all? Or does it collapse into an irrational breach that cannot be distinguished from mere madness? In other words, is the error in my framing the assumption that the suspension must be intelligible as an internal exception within ethics, rather than as an external rupture that leaves reason behind?
