In my graduate seminar on epistemology and religion, we have spent several sessions circling the classic dispute between W.K. Clifford and William James over whether belief formed on insufficient evidence is ever permissible. Clifford insists that it is wrong everywhere and always—believing without adequate evidence makes one credulous, weakens critical faculties, and risks propagating error—while James permits belief in what he calls live, forced, and momentous options, such as religious commitment, precisely when evidence is genuinely indecisive but practical stakes are high. Despite mapping every argument—Clifford's evidentialist closure principle, James's distinction between intellectual and passional decision—the class never moves; some students read James as licensing epistemic recklessness, others read Clifford as ignoring practical rationality altogether. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about ethics at all, but about a hidden disagreement over the ontology of belief itself: Clifford treats belief as a discrete, all-or-nothing propositional attitude governed exclusively by evidential norms, whereas James treats it as a scalar, volitional stance that can be adopted without full warrant provided it is not contradicted by available evidence. Are they actually disputing the ethics of belief, or are they answering different questions—one about epistemic duty, one about practical agency—under a single heading?
