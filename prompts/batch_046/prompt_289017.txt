I'm a journalist covering the philosophy of law and institutional design, and I'm observing a doctoral seminar—twelve to fourteen students in legal theory or political philosophy, ninety minutes—where I want participants to feel, rather than be told about, the structural tension at the heart of debates over same-sex marriage and what marriage is for. Please design a runnable simulation with explicit rules and rounds: split the group into four committees representing different constitutional traditions and charge each with constructing a modular rights-bundle framework for recognizing intimate partnerships in a fictional, religiously pluralistic jurisdiction. Give them cards representing discrete legal benefits—inheritance, medical decision-making, joint tax filing, immigration sponsorship, and parental presumption—and require them to attach these modules to defined relationship categories using only public-reason justifications that do not invoke comprehensive doctrines. The mechanics should be arranged so that, through negotiation alone, participants discover that any category broad enough to include same-sex couples without relying on gender also pulls in non-romantic care relationships—siblings, lifelong caregivers, business partners—while any category narrow enough to exclude those relationships must invoke criteria like sexual exclusivity, romantic intent, or procreative potential, thereby either violating state neutrality or excluding some same-sex partnerships. After the simulation, provide a debrief that maps each deadlock and collapse onto the corresponding philosophical positions—without naming them until the very end—predicts which group dynamics will fail (for instance, one committee collapsing all rights into a single universal contract, or another smuggling in a hidden religious criterion), explains how the facilitator should handle each failure in real time, and is frank about which feature of the debate—likely the inescapability of a teleological element in institutional design—cannot be reproduced by the modular mechanism itself and must be supplied by the facilitator. Please also specify what the students should come away seeing: not a settled answer, but the lived experience of why equality and institutional purpose pull in opposite directions once the architecture of recognition is made explicit.
