In a legal system governed by a single meta-rule—call it R—which authorizes the amendment of any provision, including R itself, exclusively through the procedure that R specifies, suppose R is amended via R to include an irrevocability clause: henceforth, R may not be amended by any means whatsoever. At the moment of enactment, R clearly satisfies its own conditions of validity, since it was altered through R; yet under its amended content, R now forbids precisely that alteration. This is commonly treated as a sequencing conflict resolvable by temporal priority—the old R authorizes the change, the new R governs thereafter. But I want to press a stronger, more structural concern: does this not instantiate a genuine logical antinomy, structurally parallel to the liar paradox, such that the post-amendment system lacks any consistent model of legal validity? If every norm in the system ultimately derives its authority from R, and R is simultaneously satisfiable and unsatisfiable under its own criteria of amendment, has the legal order not collapsed into formal inconsistency—where no coherent interpretation of compliance is possible—rather than into a mere hierarchical contradiction? How should one address this without falling back on chronological ordering?
