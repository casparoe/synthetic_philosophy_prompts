I keep running into this move in debates about war ethics. People start out saying soldiers on both sides are basically in the same boat — neither picked the conflict, they're just fighting — so it's not murder when they kill eachother. But then when one side starts doing something clearly awful, like systematically hitting civilians, the same people suddenly say well those specific soldiers forfeit their rights, and now the other side can kill them but not vice versa. The shift doesn't depend on whether those indivdual soldiers volunteered or knew; it's just that the enemy collective is bad enough. Is there a name for this exact pattern, where a symetrical rights-in-combat principle gets suspended selectively based on the other side's collective behavour rather than on any fighter's personal culpability? I don't want just hypocrisy — I'm asking if there's an established concept that captures even the principled version, and where its edges sit: how it differs from standard unjust-combatant liability, whether it's ever internally consistent, and what boundary cases break it.
