I'm mapping a recurring argumentative slide I see in debates about Buddhist anatta and personal identity, and I want to know whether specialists have already named, defined, and bounded it precisely.

Here is the pattern. A speaker accepts the metaphysical claim that there is no permanent, unified, self-owning substance behind experience or action — standard anatta. But when the discussion shifts to moral responsibility, continuity of practical concern, or why future states of this stream matter specially, the speaker introduces a functional replacement: "the continuum of consciousness," "the narrative thread," or "the causally linked series of mental states." This thread is treated as doing all the ethical and explanatory work a self would do — it bears blame, inherits obligations, and grounds special concern across time. The speaker insists it is not a self because it is impermanent and constructed, yet the normative and explanatory burden has not been eliminated; it has only been relocated onto a different bearer. I have noticed versions of this in Buddhist discussions of karma and rebirth and in contemporary attempts to reconcile anatta with reductionist theories of personal identity.

My question is whether there is an established concept for exactly this maneuver — not the general conventional/ultimate distinction in Madhyamaka, not standard reductionism about persons in analytic philosophy, and not mere inconsistency — but the specific move of dissolving a substantial self and then smuggling back an equally weight-bearing functional surrogate under a non-substantialist label. If such a concept exists, what is it called, and what are its boundary conditions? If it does not, please coin a term, define it sharply, and distinguish it clearly from (a) the conventional-truth account of the person, (b) reductionist psychological-continuity theories, and (c) simple equivocation.

Please separate what is well-established in Buddhist philosophy and philosophy of personal identity from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the identification or denial of the concept, with its proposed or existing name and core distinction — then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
