I'm working through different accounts of societal collapse for a conceptual analysis course, and I've realized that before comparing particular theories I need the evaluation framework itself. We keep invoking the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island as if they were interchangeable instances of failure, but I'm skeptical that any single model can cover them without distorting at least one. So I want the full specification: what an adequate theory of collapse—or of whether societies merely change—must actually be able to deliver.

List the phenomena it must explain, such as rapid demographic contraction, state-level institutional discontinuity, shifts in material culture, and ecological degradation, but also the more neglected requirement that it account for institutional memory persistence—where local practices, settlement patterns, or linguistic continuity survive the disappearance of centralized authority—since this is precisely where most binary collapse narratives fail. For each case—the Late Bronze Age network dissolution, the Classic Maya political fragmentation without corresponding demographic disappearance, and the Rapa Nui ecological trajectory—specify which feature it tests and why collapsing these into one template violates a non-negotiable desideratum. Then lay out the distinctions the theory must respect: between demographic decline and cultural extinction, between endogenous fragility and exogenous shock, and between retrospective diagnostic labeling (where archaeologists call something a collapse only after defining civilization narrowly) and a process observable to participants. Identify the archaeological and anthropological results it cannot contradict—such as the persistence of local settlement after palace economies vanish, or the absence of uniform climate determinism across these cases. Flag which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences, like whether collapse requires intentional human agency or can be an emergent systemic property. Tell me which single desideratum eliminates the largest share of popular theories—whether it's the prohibition on imposing a single causal mechanism across heterogeneous contexts, or the demand that collapse be distinguishable from gradual transformation rather than defined post hoc. Finally, briefly score how the systems-failure model, the resilience-and-transformation framework, and the complexity-cost view fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves. No citations or reading lists—I'm asking for the conceptual architecture, not scholarship.
