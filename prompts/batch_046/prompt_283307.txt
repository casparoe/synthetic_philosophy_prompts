I run a two-hour reading group on formal epistemology with eight graduate students. I want them to feel the specific tension in the Principal Principle — the demand that rational credence align with known objective chances, especially when an agent also holds strong independent evidence formed through direct observation of the very process whose chance is being reported. Design an in-class allocation game with real but small stakes (tokens convertible to a shared coffee budget) that makes this conflict visceral rather than abstract. The setup should work as follows: participants first observe a noisy physical process — for instance, draws from a container whose internal weighting they can only infer from a short, ambiguous sequence — and form private prior credences. Then they are shown an authoritative “factory specification” giving the true objective probabilities of the process. Each participant receives a fixed budget of credence-points to split between bets that honor their observational prior and bets that defer to the revealed chance, with a payoff scheme that penalizes both miscalibration against actual outcomes and “disloyalty” to the evidence they personally observed. The rules should be arranged so that perfect deference to the chance feels like a betrayal of one’s own eyes, while perfect loyalty to the prior feels like willful ignorance of authoritative information, and neither strategy dominates across rounds. Include the exact materials, the timeline across the session, the debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the components of the principle (the chance-credence link, the problem of inadmissible evidence, and the limits of deference), and an honest note on what will predictably fail the first time — particularly whether participants treat the revealed specification as just another opinion to be weighed rather than as a normative constraint on credence. Also be frank about which feature of the principle, such as the precise restriction to admissible evidence, the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally in the debrief. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the deliverable is the runnable design.
