Question: Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique challenges the adaptationist assumption that every trait is a direct product of natural selection, arguing instead that many features are non-adaptive byproducts—spandrels—of other structures or constraints. Consider a hypothetical mammalian lineage in which a cortical region originally shaped by selection for spatial navigation is subsequently recruited for arithmetic reasoning; over evolutionary time, selection refines its connectivity specifically for arithmetic computation. At the same time, a philosopher of biology proposes a distinction between architectural spandrels (non-adaptive byproducts arising from physical or structural necessities, analogous to the spaces between arches in San Marco) and developmental spandrels (byproducts of ontogenetic pathways that persist because altering them would be pleiotropically costly).

First, explain why this arithmetic-refined region might initially appear to be a spandrel, and then determine whether it should ultimately be classified as an adaptation, an exaptation, or a spandrel, with careful attention to the difference between adaptation and exaptation. Second, evaluate whether the philosopher's distinction between architectural and developmental spandrels clarifies this particular case, or whether it introduces unnecessary confusion. Address specifically whether selection acting on the region for arithmetic changes its evolutionary classification, and whether developmental spandrels can be subject to selection at all.

Student answer: Gould and Lewontin argued that adaptationists wrongly treat every trait as if it were designed for its current role, when in fact many traits are spandrels—byproducts that exist not because selection favored them, but because they are structurally necessary consequences of other features. The arithmetic region initially looks like a spandrel because arithmetic reasoning was not the function for which the region originally evolved; its original adaptive role was spatial navigation, so its arithmetic capacity appears to be an accidental byproduct.

Once natural selection begins refining the region's connectivity specifically for arithmetic, however, the trait becomes a full adaptation. The difference between an adaptation and an exaptation is essentially temporal: an exaptation is just an adaptation that has not yet been refined by selection for its current use over a long enough period. Because selection has now acted directly on the region for arithmetic computation, its evolutionary status shifts from exaptation to adaptation, and the original spatial-navigation function becomes irrelevant to its present classification.

Regarding the philosopher's distinction, architectural spandrels are physical byproducts like the triangular spaces between arches, whereas developmental spandrels arise from growth pathways that are locked in place. The arithmetic region is best understood as a developmental spandrel, because its presence depends on the ontogenetic pathway that constructs the cortex, and altering that pathway would disrupt other traits. Gould and Lewontin actually anticipated this very distinction in their original critique, treating developmental constraints as a subclass of architectural constraints. Therefore, the philosopher's split does not add any genuine conceptual clarity; it merely divides spandrels into two categories that were already unified in the original account. Furthermore, because developmental spandrels are protected from selection by pleiotropic lock-in, the fact that selection is refining this region for arithmetic proves that it cannot really be a developmental spandrel after all. So the distinction either collapses back into the original spandrel concept or is contradicted by the case itself.
