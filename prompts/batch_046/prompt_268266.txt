I have been discussing this with colleagues who insist that such a model collapses into mere proxy representation, and I want to test the position against a committed defender who will not retreat to safer ground.

Please play the part of an unnamed philosopher who holds this view with full conviction—not a caricature, but a sharp interlocutor with genuine conceptual resources, willing to concede small distinctions but never the central claim that standing authorization is normatively distinct from unilateral representation. My opening challenge is this. Consider two long-time collaborators, A and B, who work in the same research area and have an implicit understanding that they co-author papers together. I press you with a comparison: if your model is correct, B was already bound by the standing authorization, so B's anger is merely retrospective regret rather than a legitimate denial of joint authorship. How do you defend the coherence of B's denial, or the reality of the joint action, without conceding that moment-by-moment mutual awareness is required?
