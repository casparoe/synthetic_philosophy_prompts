Having recently retired from a career in experimental physics and having only lately begun to read seriously in philosophy of mind, I have found myself drawn to Daniel Dennett's proposal that the self is best understood not as a Cartesian substance but as a centre of narrative gravity — an abstract point about which a person's behaviours, beliefs, and biographical threads are dynamically organised. I should like you to construct a careful argument for the claim that such a centre possesses ontological standing precisely analogous to that of a centre of mass in a non-rigid body: it is not itself one of the constituent parts, yet it is indispensable for explaining the dynamics of the whole, and its position is fixed relationally rather than intrinsically.

In particular, please defend the view that narrative coherence operates upon the self in the manner of an order parameter in statistical mechanics — a macroscopic feature that emerges from microscopic disorder yet subsequently constrains the behaviour of the constituent elements. You should address the objection that local disruptions, such as episodic dissociation or sharply contradictory first-person reports, dissolve the centre of gravity altogether. Argue instead that these disruptions merely displace its coordinates within a broader configuration space, leaving the global narrative structure intact. I am not asking for a survey of authors or texts, but I do want the physical analogies treated with precision: explain why reducing narrative identity to discrete mental states is blocked in the same fashion as reducing temperature to the kinetic energy of a single molecule.
