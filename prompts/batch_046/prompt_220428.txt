I am compiling notes for a seminar on textual hermeneutics, and I have been tracking the consistent rulings of one particular respondent—an experienced interpretive scholar whose judgments we have discussed across five cases over the term. Her verdicts so far are these. First, on a modernist poem for which the author’s manuscript clearly reads “love” but the first printed edition, authorized for publication, reads “lore” due to a compositor’s error: she ruled that the interpretive object is the word “lore” as it stands in the authorized text, and that speculation about the compositor’s mistake belongs to bibliography, not to interpretation. Second, on a well-known novel whose author, decades after publication, insisted in interviews that the work was intended as a satire of market economies, despite the narrative’s unambiguous celebration of commercial exchange: she ruled that the author’s retrospective framing is biographically interesting but does not alter the semantic properties of the sentences in the published book. Third, on a canonical play left unfinished at the author’s death, for which a final scene exists only in fragmentary notes and a later editor reconstructed it for the standard edition: she ruled that interpretation of that scene must proceed by reference to the author’s documented intentions for its dramatic function, not by treating the reconstructed text as an autonomous artifact. Fourth, on a memoir in which the author later admitted to inventing a central dialogue for narrative effect, though the dialogue appears in the published volume without any framing that marks it as fictional: she ruled that the dialogue should be read as part of the memoir’s intended artistic architecture, with interpretation guided by the author’s structural purpose rather than by a reportorial standard of factual accuracy. Fifth, on a religious text for which modern philology has shown that a key verb in the canonical translation was almost certainly mistranslated from the original language, altering the theological implication: she ruled that the received canonical wording is the proper object of interpretation, and that reconstructed original meanings are the province of historical philology, not of hermeneutic practice.

The new case is this. A contemporary poet published a digital poem that includes an interactive hyperlink embedded in line fourteen. The poet has since stated in an interview that she intended the hyperlink to direct readers to Text A, which establishes a specific intertextual resonance; however, due to a coding error in the published file, the hyperlink actually directs to Text B, which produces a markedly different associative network. The erroneous file has been widely archived, accessed, and taught in its current form. The poet now asks interpreters to treat the link as pointing to A, since that is what she meant.
