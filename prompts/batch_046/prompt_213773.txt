As a retired physicist recently turned to philosophy of science and cognitive psychology, I have been studying Bongard's puzzles on the induction of concepts from a handful of examples. I keep encountering a configuration I cannot name. In some puzzles, the left-hand figures do not share any static feature—no common shape, color relation, or pairwise arrangement—that is absent from the right; rather, the left set collectively exhausts a continuous parameter space, such as orientations or sizes, without redundancy, while the right set clusters redundantly within that same space. No single left figure violates a local rule; only the global distribution of the ensemble reveals the boundary. Is there an established concept for this kind of ensemble-level or distributional induction, as opposed to feature-based or relational induction? If there is no standard term, how should it be distinguished from an auxiliary hypothesis invoking hidden variables, and does its reliance on properties of the set as a whole pose a particular difficulty for theories that explain concept acquisition from only a few individual instances?
