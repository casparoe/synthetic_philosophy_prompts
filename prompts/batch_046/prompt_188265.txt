Consider a tribunal that applies Karl Jaspers’s fourfold taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical, as developed in The Question of German Guilt—to cases of collective and intergenerational responsibility. Its record is as follows.

First, after a regime change in State A, the tribunal held that the state as a legal-political entity bears political guilt requiring institutional reform and reparations; citizens bear moral guilt only if they personally acted or knowingly failed to resist; and descendants born after the fall bear neither moral nor criminal guilt, though they inherit the political obligations of the continuing state.

Second, regarding a corporation dissolved in 1980 and reconstituted in 2005 with entirely new shareholders and management, holding assets originally accumulated through forced labor, it ruled that the present corporation bears no moral or criminal guilt—there being no continuous moral agents—but bears a derivative political responsibility to remedy the structural injustice tied to the assets it holds.

Third, in a small village where every adult knew of a systematic hidden abuse but none spoke out, though none personally committed the acts, it ruled that the village as a present collective bears moral guilt through complicity by silence, because shared knowledge and inaction constituted a collective moral failure.

Fourth, concerning a generation born after an environmental catastrophe caused by its grandparents’ industrial policies, now living in the restored region, actively participating in restoration, yet continuing to benefit from the economic infrastructure built by the polluting era, it ruled that this generation bears no metaphysical guilt—having not chosen survival over protest—and no moral guilt, lacking knowledge or complicity at the time of harm, but shares in a non-culpable political responsibility for ongoing remediation.

Fifth, regarding a diaspora community that preserved its cultural identity in exile, never held citizenship in the offending state, but maintained cultural practices originating in the same region where atrocities occurred, and now seeks recognition as a stakeholder in reconciliation, it ruled that because there was no institutional or causal continuity with the state apparatus, neither political nor moral guilt attaches; however, a form of metaphysical solidarity may exist, though it does not translate into culpability.

Now consider the new case: a non-territorial digital polity founded in 2042 as a decentralized autonomous organization, governed by smart contracts and democratic member vote, with no territorial base and no legal continuity with any prior state. Its founding treasury was capitalized entirely using profits from a twentieth-century extractive industry that caused widespread harm to a specific population. The current members all joined after the polity’s founding, hold no individual ties to the historical industry, and have adopted a constitutional mandate to redistribute ten percent of treasury revenue to descendants of the affected communities. The tribunal is asked whether this polity bears any form of Jaspersian guilt—criminal, political, moral, or metaphysical—toward those descendants, and whether any such guilt is inherited, structural, or self-assumed through the constitutional choice.

What should the tribunal decide if it wishes to be consistent with its own precedents? Identify the closest analog among the five earlier rulings, and explain whether the precedents genuinely determine the outcome or underdetermine it. If they underdetermine it, specify the competing principles that would be adopted with each possible choice, and state what further case—preferably one that distinguishes structural inheritance of benefit from institutional continuity—would best reveal which principle the tribunal is actually running. Give your best-guess verdict with reasons, but be explicit about which of your own commitments in the reasoning depend on resolving that ambiguity.
