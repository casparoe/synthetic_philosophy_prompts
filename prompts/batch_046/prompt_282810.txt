Make an argument that the "man of his time" defence actually works best for Churchill and worst for Columbus, but for a reason that runs backwards from how people usually talk. Most folks treat it like a timeline thing—Jefferson gets a partial pass because slavery was "normal" in Virginia, Columbus gets slammed because we now know genocide is wrong. I think that's the wrong axis entirely. The defence should hinge on whether there was a live, internal moral counter-voice inside the figure's own culture that they could have accessed without stepping outside it. Jefferson had Quaker abolitionists and anti-slavery pamphlets circulating right in Philadelphia; Columbus had Las Casas literally arguing in the Spanish court about the full humanity of the Taino. Both lived in societies where the moral echo chamber was already cracking. Churchill, by contrast, was making imperial famine policy in a Britain where even liberal critics largely took imperial resource extraction as the background condition of governance—there wasn't a serious domestic tradition saying "let Bengalis starve" was wrong in the specific way he enacted it, just technical disagreement over logistics and priorities. So propose an "internal dissent" criteron: the defence is stronger when a society's reasoning was genuinely monolithic on an issue, and weaker when internal critics were already visible and ignored. Argue that this makes Churchill the most defensible of the three and Columbus the least, even though our usual moral insticts run the other way. And say what this costs us—does it mean we should praise Churchill more, or just condemn Columbus with extra force?
