In a philosophy seminar on migration ethics, an interlocutor defended strict immigration controls with the following claim: "A nation is essentially a large-scale private club—the citizens collectively hold the territory, set admission rules, and may blackball outsiders just as a club excludes applicants." The speaker invoked this to reject the idea that nationality is morally arbitrary, insisting that the right to exclude is constitutive of collective self-determination and needs no further justification. I want to audit that analogy precisely. Lay out the correspondence table—what in the club maps onto citizens, territory, immigration statutes, the board of directors, and non-member outsiders who live nearby or depend on the club's resources—then identify the points of breakdown, with particular attention to whether citizenship is voluntary like club membership, whether the property metaphor presupposes a theory of collective territorial ownership that must be defended separately, and whether the analogy illegitimately imports the associative freedom of private clubs into the coercive, non-voluntary domain of state power over both insiders and outsiders. After separating the disanalogies that matter for the exclusionist conclusion from those that do not, say whether the analogy genuinely supports the claim that open borders violate a basic right, or whether it was carrying the argument all along. If a more accurate analogy for state border control exists—say, a landlord-tenant relation, a gatekeeper of a public resource, or something else—state it and explain why its mapping holds up better.
