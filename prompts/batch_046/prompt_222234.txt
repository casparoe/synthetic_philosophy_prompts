I write structurally ambitious fiction in which ensemble casts develop collective purposes that outstrip any individual character's articulated aims. For my current project — a novel in which five protagonists, each rendered through independent focalization streams, gradually commit to restoring a corrupted archive without any one of them ever stating the collective goal in full — I have developed a working vocabulary drawn from narrative craft: ensemble choreography, distributed focalization, dramatic irony, emergent plot dynamics versus authorial design, and what I term "narrative debt," meaning the binding obligations that earlier plot promises and unrevealed internal commitments place on later collective action, even in the absence of explicit agreement.

I am fluent in this vocabulary but need philosophical clarity. Please take the three principal philosophical accounts of collective intentionality and joint action — the shared-cooperative-activity model built on meshing subplans and mutual belief, the plural-subject model centered on joint commitment and the normative "we," and the biological-primitive model that treats we-intention as irreducible — and restate each one systematically within my narrative-craft framework. For each translation, specify precisely what carries over without distortion, what must be bent or reconceived to fit (for example, whether mutual belief survives the asymmetry of dramatic irony, or whether joint commitment can be expressed solely through distributed narrative debt), and what, if anything, has no analog in narrative theory whatsoever. Then deliver a residue report: do the untranslatable elements indicate that narrative craft genuinely lacks the conceptual apparatus for collective agency, or do they suggest that the philosophical accounts smuggle in assumptions — about biological primitives, about explicit agreement — that are not as indispensable as they appear?
