Having spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics and only lately begun to read philosophy of cognition, I find I can no longer treat analogy as a mere teaching aid. Hofstadter and Sander's argument — that analogy is the core mechanism of thought, not its decoration — now strikes me as more plausible than the formalist accounts I grew up with. Write a sestina for me: six stanzas of six lines, each line ending with one of six rotating words, followed by a three-line envoi that uses all six. Choose three end-words from crystallography — lattice, defect, symmetry — and three from moral philosophy — obligation, dignity, judgment — so that the domains are initially distinct. The speaker should be someone with a scientific training who now suspects that the mental operation behind reading an X-ray diffraction pattern and the one behind recognizing an unjust act are structurally the same. Most importantly, the envoi must perform the knight-move slippage that the authors describe: by the final three lines, the crystallographic terms should carry unmistakable moral force, and the moral terms should read like descriptions of physical structure, demonstrating that analogy generates rather than transfers meaning. Keep the tone rigorous and unsentimental — no sermon, no resolution — and make the verse precise enough that a reader familiar with the book recognizes the mechanism, yet coherent as poetry for someone who has never heard the phrase knight-move. Do not include citations, references, or a bibliography.
