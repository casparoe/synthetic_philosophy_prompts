I recently joined a worker-owned cooperative and am helping draft its founding ethical guidelines; the immediate dispute is whether promises made during the initial organizing meetings should continue to bind members who have since changed their view of the enterprise's purpose. In our debates, members repeatedly invoke an analogy: they say that making a promise is like ratifying a patch to a shared social protocol—the promisor proposes an update to the normative code governing interaction, the promisee relies on that update to recalibrate behavior, and to break the promise is to revert the patch unilaterally, thereby corrupting the system for everyone who had adapted to the new rules. Audit this analogy in depth: provide an explicit correspondence table (promisor, promisee, promise content, ratification, reliance, corruption), show where the mapping fails (particularly on questions of consent, reversibility, and whether interpersonal morality has anything like a centralized repository), and assess whether the conclusion that promise-breaking is primarily a form of systemic harm survives those failures. Also examine whether the analogy illegitimately imports an institutional or collective picture of obligation into one-to-one relationships, and propose whether a different analogy—say, promises as private calibration signals rather than public protocol updates—might yield the opposite normative conclusion about release from promises made under changed conditions. Throughout, please indicate how confident you are in each stage of your analysis and explicitly identify the point about which you are least sure.
