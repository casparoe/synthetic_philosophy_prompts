As a doctoral student in philosophy of religion writing a dissertation chapter on metaphysical commitments and practical reason, I am analyzing a case study that raises issues I need to resolve rigorously. A close friend completed an extended Vipassanā retreat and subsequently informed her partner of ten years that she considers herself released from a mutual agreement they made half a decade ago: specifically, to co-parent a child together and to relocate internationally for his career. She grounds her refusal not in changed preferences but in a philosophical shift: she now accepts the Buddhist doctrine of anicca together with the Abhidharma thesis that mental states are strictly momentary, each cognition being replaced instantaneously by a numerically distinct causal successor. From this she infers that there is no enduring, substantial self that could serve as the continuous bearer of the original promise, so the present agent—being ontologically discontinuous with the one who agreed—cannot be bound by that past commitment. Her partner regards this as a rationalization for evading responsibility. Drawing on Buddhist anattā and contemporary analytic accounts of personal identity, is there a coherent philosophical defense of her position? Or does the appeal to momentariness collapse into a reductio that undermines all long-term obligation? I want a definite conclusion—argue either that metaphysical discontinuity genuinely dissolves interpersonal commitments in this context, or that psychological and moral continuity can survive even a denial of substantial selfhood—not a non-committal survey or therapeutic guidance.
