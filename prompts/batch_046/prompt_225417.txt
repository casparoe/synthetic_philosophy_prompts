Since my mother's recent admission to intensive care, I have found myself unable to stop reflecting on whether the number of lives at risk should determine whom one saves. My verdicts are these. If I may rescue either one stranger or five strangers with whom I have no connection, I believe saving the five is permissible but not obligatory; selecting by lot seems acceptable. If the choice is between my sister and five strangers, I believe I may save my sister, and the greater number does not generate an overriding reason. If the choice is between five strangers now and one stranger who is the sole carer for dependants with a rare condition, I believe I should save the one, on grounds of dependency rather than number. Yet if the choice is between saving one person today and five people in twenty years—who do not yet exist—I feel certain that I should save the five, which seems to make number decisive after all. Meanwhile, if one person will survive regardless of my action and five will die unless I act, I judge that I must save the five, though this feels like a judgement about preventing harm rather than about counting lives.

Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing special obligations from impartial duties, or treating future persons and present dependants differently—that can accommodate all four verdicts? If not, which one should be abandoned? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and predict its verdict for a situation I have not considered: where I can save either two people whose lives are so deeply intertwined that the death of one would effectively destroy the other, or three people with no such bond.
