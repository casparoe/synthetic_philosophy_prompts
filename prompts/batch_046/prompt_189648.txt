As a novelist drafting a work about a translator who moves between languages without ever feeling she has arrived, I find myself holding the following commitments about meaning, reference, and interpretation—commitments I need for both my craft and my philosophical instincts, but which I fear may pull in incompatible directions:

(1) I take Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation seriously: given all observable behavioral evidence, there is no unique correct manual for rendering an unknown language into ours, and the native term “gavagai” might denote rabbits, temporal rabbit-stages, or undetached rabbit parts with no deeper fact of the matter settling which.

(2) Nevertheless, in my practice as a reader and writer I treat mistranslation as a genuine error. If a translator renders a specific line in a foreign novel in a way that violates the internal logic of the narrative, I say she has gotten it wrong—not merely adopted an alternative, equally adequate manual.

(3) I am drawn to Quine’s ontological relativity: what exists is relative to the background theory we adopt, so there is no absolute, theory-independent fact about whether the entities posited by one translation manual are “really” there rather than those posited by another.

(4) At the same time, I believe that authorial intention fixes the meaning of a literary text in a non-relative way. There is a real fact about what the author meant by a particular passage, and this fact is not merely a construct of our interpretive framework.

(5) I also hold that we never have direct, unmediated access to an author’s mental states; intention must be reconstructed from the text, its historical context, and behavioral traces—making the recovery of meaning as theory-laden and underdetermined as Quine’s translation scenario.

(6) Finally, as a conceit for my novel, I have begun to think that fictional worlds generate their own “local ontology,” in which reference within the fiction is fixed not by correspondence to reality but by internal narrative coherence, and that this local ontology should constrain translation choices even when global ontology remains indeterminate.

Do these hang together? Specifically: can (2) survive (1) and (3) without special pleading? Is (4) compatible with (5) once we abandon direct mental access, or does Quinean underdetermination undermine the very idea of a non-relative authorial fact? Does (6) resolve the tension between (1) and (2), or does it introduce a fresh conflict with (3) by smuggling in a kind of local absolutism? I want an audit, not a lecture: rank the tensions by severity, state for each what commitment would have to be abandoned or qualified, and identify any apparent conflicts—especially those involving (6)—that dissolve once the distinctions are drawn carefully. Please separate what is relatively settled in philosophy of language and translation studies from what remains genuinely contested among informed practitioners, and do not present any contested view as though it were the consensus.
