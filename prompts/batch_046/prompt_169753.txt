Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on the paradox of tragedy: why audiences seek out and positively value artworks that elicit sadness, grief, or distress.

Camp A holds that the paradox dissolves through a control-and-distancing framework: negative affect in art is experienced within a protected representational frame, allowing safe emotional simulation and cathartic regulation without real-world cost, so the enjoyment is enjoyment of that regulated affect itself.

Camp B holds that the paradox is misformulated because the term enjoyment conflates hedonic pleasure with eudaimonic appreciation; sad art is valued not for the negative feeling it produces but for the cognitive-moral insight or existential understanding it yields, with negative affect serving as an instrumental cue rather than the object of positive evaluation.

Your deliverable should be a document both camps could sign. Structure it exactly as follows, with approximate lengths indicated:

1. Agreed Claims (numbered list, about 300 words): State the shared commitments about the phenomenon—e.g., that audiences genuinely report positive overall evaluations of tragic works; that the elicited negative emotions are phenomenologically real rather than feigned; that any adequate explanation must cover both solitary and collective reception, and both representational and participatory media. Include specifically one agreed claim about how interactive or participatory narratives (e.g., video games in which player choice produces an unavoidable tragic outcome) complicate both camps' frameworks without resolving the dispute. Do not cite authors or texts.

2. Disagreement Table (about 250 words): Present at least three precise disagreements in table format. For each, provide the wording that Camp A accepts for its own view and the wording that Camp B accepts for its own view. The disagreements should cover: (a) whether the positive evaluation is directed at the negative affect itself or at a distinct object (insight, form, meaning); (b) whether the protective representational frame is a necessary condition for positive valuation or merely a facilitating context that explains prevalence but not value; (c) how to interpret reports of being moved or elevated—as modified forms of pleasure or as non-hedonic valuations.

3. Cruxes and Predictive Tests (numbered, about 350 words): Identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. One crux must concern participatory media specifically. For each crux, describe a concrete case, hypothetical study, or designed experience that both sides agree bears on it (e.g., measuring physiological arousal and self-reported meaning-making in viewers of the same tragic narrative presented in static versus interactive form; or a scenario in which the safety frame is explicitly removed but the work remains valued). Then record each camp's explicit, advance prediction about what the evidence would show.

4. Residual Verbal Disputes (about 150 words): State whether any portion of the disagreement is purely about word choice—such as enjoyment versus appreciation versus being moved—and articulate the resolution or agreed translation both sides accept.

5. Open Boundary Conditions (about 150 words): List conditions both camps agree remain unsettled, such as whether the explanation extends to non-narrative art (e.g., melancholic instrumental music) or to tragic traditions outside the Western canon with which neither camp has direct phenomenological familiarity.

Keep the tone formal, philosophical, and neutral. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for external sources. Do not adjudicate which camp is correct; the goal is a constructive, signed joint document with residual disagreement made exact.
