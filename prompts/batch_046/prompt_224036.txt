I'm taking a seminar this semester on the metaphysics and ethics of action, and after last week's discussion of omissions in institutional settings, a line of reasoning that a classmate proposed has been bothering me. The argument seems to collapse the moral distinction between killing and letting die by showing that any omission can be paired with a corresponding action that is causally and intentionally identical except for bodily movement; but when it is applied to distributed responsibility, it appears to prove far too much. Here are the steps as I understand them:

(1) In any case of letting die—say, a physician withholding antibiotics—the agent's omission is a necessary part of the actual causal sequence producing death, and a counterfactual intervention by that agent could have interrupted it.
(2) There is always a corresponding action—administering poison—that produces the same biological endpoint through the same cellular pathway, with identical foreseeability and intention.
(3) The only physical difference is the presence or absence of a specific bodily movement; there is no other causally relevant distinction between the two scenarios.
(4) If bodily movement is not intrinsically morally significant, then there is no morally relevant difference between the corresponding action and omission.
(5) Therefore, if withholding treatment is permissible, the corresponding active killing must also be permissible; conversely, if active killing is wrong, withholding treatment with identical intent must be equally wrong.
(6) The reasoning extends to distributed systems: imagine a life-support apparatus with ten independent safeguards, each monitored by a different technician who must press a button hourly; the patient dies only if all ten fail. For any single technician, omitting the button is necessary but not sufficient for death, and counterfactual intervention by that technician alone—while the other nine still omit—would not have saved the patient.
(7) By the symmetry in (2)-(4), each technician's failure to press the button is morally equivalent to that technician actively administering lethal poison, since the causal and intentional structure is parallel.
(8) Consequently, the argument entails that in any collective omission—say, a government failing to distribute a stockpiled vaccine—each official's failure is morally on a par with actively poisoning the affected population.
(9) Since we do not treat bureaucratic negligence or distributed failures to save as equivalent to mass murder, the killing/letting-die distinction must be abandoned, or our moral judgments about collective omissions are systematically wrong.

I want to preserve the intuition that there can be a genuine moral asymmetry between acting and refraining, especially in cases of distributed responsibility where no single agent controls the outcome, but I also want to avoid claiming that bodily movement is magically decisive. Which step should be rejected—perhaps the move from causal symmetry to moral symmetry in (4), or the application to distributed cases in (6)-(7)—and what does rejecting it cost? Does it require adopting a non-counterfactual theory of causation, or accepting that institutional roles generate special permissions to omit?
