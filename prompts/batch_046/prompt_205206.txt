I'm preparing questions for a panel interview with a philosopher—let's call her the guest—who has recently advanced a strict version of the multiple drafts model of consciousness. Her view is not merely that there is no Cartesian theater in which experience is presented to a central observer, but that there is no functional analog of such a theater even at the level of narrative self-modeling. She holds that what we call phenomenal unity is a post-hoc confabulation produced by a distributed editorial process, and that temporal anomalies such as the color-phi phenomenon are best read not just as counterexamples to a theater but as positive evidence for what she terms stochastic drafting: competing sensory interpretations are resolved without any single stream ever being broadcast to anyone. To account for the persistent intuition that there is a single subject of experience, she appeals to indexical anchoring—the claim that first-person pronouns are linguistic tags attached to drafts by evolutionary and social practice, not pointers to a metaphysically unified observer.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press her on the actual content of this position rather than asking her to restate the original arguments that introduced the model. I want at least one question that challenges whether indexical anchoring can genuinely explain the phenomenology of agency—especially the sense that I am the one initiating an action—without implicitly smuggling in a homuncular audience that receives the anchored draft; include a follow-up for the reply in which she says this is just how the editorial process assigns causal roles. I want at least one question on how stochastic drafting handles cases of stable, long-duration perceptual tracking—such as watching a sustained flame or listening to a continuous tone—without positing an implicit theater that holds the content steady across time; include a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to evolutionary continuity with non-human animals. I want at least one question on whether her denial of phenomenal unity commits her to a form of eliminativism about the self that she explicitly wants to reject, with a follow-up for her answer that distinguishes her view from standard bundle theories. Finally, end with a question about what specific empirical result—whether in neuroscience, artificial intelligence, or clinical psychology—would actually threaten her framework rather than being absorbed into it as yet another instance of draft competition or editorial revision.
