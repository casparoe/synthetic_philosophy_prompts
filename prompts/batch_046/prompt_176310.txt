I am taking an upper-division seminar on comparative moral sociology, and after several long discussions with a classmate about whether dignity culture represents a genuine moral advance over honor and victimhood frameworks, we decided to attempt an adversarial collaboration rather than continue arguing past each other. The collaboration is between two committed positions: one that defends dignity culture as morally superior because it grounds human worth in non-contingent, internal properties that do not depend on social recognition, and one that defends victimhood culture as morally necessary because it makes structurally hidden injuries visible and creates institutional obligations to correct them, even at the cost of making individuals morally dependent on collective acknowledgment. Please draft the joint statement that these two interlocutors could both sign after good-faith negotiation.

The statement should contain, first, a numbered section of agreed claims about all three cultures—how honor cultures regulate status through public ritual and third-party enforcement, how dignity cultures internalize worth and rely on procedural equality, and how victimhood cultures institutionalize grievance and generate what some call moral dependency—plus any empirical agreements about how these frameworks actually operate in modern institutional settings such as university grievance boards or workplace arbitration protocols. Second, include a disagreements table or structured list in which each side's view is phrased in wording that side itself would accept; I am especially interested in whether the dispute over whether victimhood culture erodes autonomy is substantive or partly verbal, and whether the concept of status liquidity—the ease with which social standing can be lost and regained—helps clarify or confuse the disagreement. Third, identify the cruxes: the specific sub-questions that, if settled, would actually move one or both sides. I suspect the central cruxes involve whether moral worth can remain genuinely non-contingent in societies marked by deep structural inequality, and whether institutional recognition of harm necessarily produces dependency or can instead be designed to preserve autonomy. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete institutional case or design feature both sides agree is a valid test—such as an anonymous reporting system coupled with mandatory restorative circles, or a conflict-resolution protocol that distinguishes between status repair (restoring one's hierarchical position) and recognition debt (the institutional obligation to acknowledge harm regardless of rank)—and record each side's explicit prediction about what that case will demonstrate. Fifth, conclude with a brief diagnosis of which parts of the dispute are genuinely substantive and which are merely verbal, particularly around the distinction between status repair and recognition debt, which I want you to treat as a potentially spurious conceptual injection rather than an established theoretical term.

Please do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholars, and do not rely on highly obscure theories that a well-trained generalist model could not be expected to recall. If you reference standard distinctions from the literature on moral cultures, paraphrase them without quotation marks. The document should be self-contained, formal, and structured so that an outside reader can see exactly where agreement ends and disagreement begins, with predictions recorded in advance rather than adjusted after the fact.
