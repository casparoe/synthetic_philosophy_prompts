I am a philosophy professor preparing material for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on the limits of formalization. A colleague in jurisprudence circulated the following summary of Gödel's incompleteness theorems and their philosophical bearing beyond mathematics: "Gödel's first theorem demonstrates that any consistent formal legal system complex enough to encode basic arithmetic will contain valid legal propositions that the system itself cannot adjudicate. His second theorem establishes that no such system can demonstrate its own consistency, which entails that no constitutional framework can ever guarantee it will not eventually produce contradictions. Consequently, human judges must be relying on a non-formal, intuitive capacity—similar to what Gödel described as mathematical intuition—to resolve these undecidable cases, which proves that algorithmic legal reasoning is impossible in principle. Furthermore, the theorems establish the existence of objective truths about justice that are independent of any formal system, in direct analogy with true but unprovable arithmetical statements." I suspect this conflates several distinct issues, but I want a precise audit. Please evaluate the summary sentence by sentence: distinguish what is accurate, what is a defensible but simplified gloss, what subtly misrepresents Gödel's results or the philosophical arguments (such as the anti-mechanist interpretation) that invoke them, and whether the conclusions about jurisprudence and mechanized reasoning actually follow. I am especially interested in whether the move from arithmetical incompleteness to legal incompleteness is legitimate, and whether the anti-mechanist claim is being presented as a necessary consequence rather than a conditional argument.
