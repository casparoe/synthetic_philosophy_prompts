I run a graduate seminar on AI alignment with 8 participants, meeting for 90 minutes. Design a structured deliberation game with explicit rules, assigned roles, and a point-scoring mechanism that makes participants feel, before it is named, the problem of defining "avoid side effects" for an autonomous agent without supplying a full specification of value.

The scenario: a shared grid environment (drawn on a whiteboard or paper) represents a resource space with multiple cell types. The group controls an agent that can alter cell states according to proposed rules. The task is to write a one-sentence "impact boundary" that restricts the agent's actions to prevent harmful side effects, without listing which states are good or bad.

Roles and incentives: assign three participants as "designers" and five as "stakeholders," each stakeholder holding a hidden preference card (e.g., preserve biodiversity, maintain energy efficiency, prevent change to human-occupied cells). Designers propose boundary rules; stakeholders may veto any rule if they judge it permits an irreversible change they care about. The group scores +10 points for every permitted agent action (autonomy reward) but loses 30 points per veto. Run three 15-minute rounds with increasing grid complexity.

Arrange the rules so that participants discover through play — especially in rounds two and three — that any purely structural boundary (e.g., limits on state-change frequency, homogeneity thresholds, entropy bounds) either fails to block a stakeholder veto or implicitly privileges one stakeholder's values. They should construct the sequence of failed rules themselves and only recognize the pattern at the end.

Then provide the debrief structure (about 20 minutes): map each round's crisis onto a feature of the alignment problem — structural proxies smuggling in value, the absence of a value-neutral criterion for "irreversible change," and the collapse of boundary rules into full utility specification. Predict the predictable failure mode in which the group settles on an unstated shared value (typically preserving human-occupied cells) without admitting it is a value judgment, and explain how the facilitator should expose it. Be frank about which feature of the real problem the simulation cannot reproduce — specifically the computational intractability of complete state-space specification and the dynamic emergence of unforeseen side effects in high-dimensional environments — and state clearly that the facilitator must supply this limitation verbally rather than through game mechanics.

Specify the setting, group size, time, and exactly what participants should leave seeing: that a value-agnostic impact measure is unstable under plural stakeholder preferences and that any workable boundary secretly imports a value framework.
