Me and my flat-mate got into it last night over whether consent is just one flat yes/no or if it's "layered" like an onion. (1) She reckons that if you say yes to something based on a particuar understanding of why it's happening — like letting your mate stay in your spare room because they said they're grieving and need quiet — and then it turns out they lied about the reason and actually threw a party, then your original yes never covered the party situation. The deception wasn't about the room itself, it was about the kind of situation you were agreeing to enter, so treating them as the same is like saying you consented to surgery when you only consented to a check-up. (2) I said that's mixing up consent with motives. You said yes to them being in the room; their private reason is none of your buisness, and if hidden motives can cancel agreement then every contract and every yes ever said is on shaky ground. (3) She came back that some motives change the risk or the moral shape of the act — it's not just "why they wanted it" but "what you actually signed up for". If I consent to a quiet grieving-person flat, I haven't consented to a party flat, and treating them as identical ignores that competence and understanding depend on knowing what you're actually getting into, not just the bare physical facts. (4) I said she's inventing a distinction that doesn't hold up — "the kind of situation" — and that if consent depends on matching hidden mental pictures then nobody can safely rely on anyone's word. I also said that if you're sober, understand English, and aren't being physically forced, your yes binds you, full stop; otherwise consent becomes useless because you can always claim later that your mental image was wrong, and competence isn't some dimmer switch you can turn down whenever it's convenient.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the four points from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Who's right about what here, and what are both of us missing? In particular, does deception about purpose invalidate consent differently from deception about the physical act itself? And where does competence actually sit in all this — is it a dimmer switch or an on/off thing?
