I'm running a 75-minute graduate ethics seminar with about twelve students. Design an in-class simulation that makes participants feel the force of non-comparative incommensurability—specifically the kind of hard choice in which two options are neither equally good nor rankable on any shared scale, and in which choosing one partly constitutes the chooser—rather than merely discussing it. To prevent the exercise from collapsing into a generic budget-allocation problem, frame it as a collective "constitutive value conflict": the group must assign a single, indivisible purpose to a legacy institutional endowment, selecting either the preservation of an irreplaceable but currently unused research archive (representing historical continuity) or the creation of a living-stipend program for emerging scholars (representing generative care). Give each student a public role-card—"Archivist" or "Patron"—that binds them to defend one value in deliberation, plus a hidden private-preference card that may align with or oppose their role; forbid splitting the endowment, forbid translating either good into a proxy metric such as projected citations or attendance numbers, and require a final binding vote with no abstentions, followed by a brief written justification that enters the permanent record. The payoff should be social and emotional rather than monetary: visible faction formation, the discomfort of a forced public commitment, and the revelation that private preferences diverged from public roles. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments—participants inventing a hidden comparison scale, proposing a compromise split, or treating role-cards as mere preferences rather than identity-constituting commitments—onto the conceptual structure of hard choice (parity, the failure of small improvement to resolve the tie, the agent-constructive character of the decision). Be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce on its own: participants may still privately believe one option is "really" better, so the facilitator must supply the metaphysical claim that no common metric exists, without turning the session into a lecture. Finally, list the three failures that will predictably occur on the first run—smuggling in quantitative proxies, treating the vote as a preference aggregation rather than an identity-forming act, and attempting to divide the gift—and for each specify both a rule-based block and a brief recovery line the facilitator can use.
