Consider the widespread practice of dramatizing real, deceased people in historical fiction and biopics: filmmakers invent dialogue, compress chronologies, and imagine private emotional states, often framing the result with disclaimers like "based on true events" or "inspired by real lives." Meanwhile, the same culture enforces strict prohibitions on falsifying archival records or misrepresenting the dead in legal and documentary contexts, and in some legal systems the dead retain limited posthumous personality rights. What philosophical commitments about truth, representation, and our obligations to the dead does this bifurcation presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—whether it distinguishes "narrative truth" from historical fact, whether it assumes the dead possess dignity but not epistemic authority, or whether dramatic form itself is thought to morally transform fabrication. Assess whether that framework is internally coherent, identify which parts the practice strictly requires versus those it merely accommodates, and describe what would have to change in either filmmaking norms or legal doctrine if we adopted a unified view—such as treating all representations of real persons by the same evidentiary standards, or granting the dead full narrative sovereignty over their lives.
