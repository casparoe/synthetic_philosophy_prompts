For my high school philosophy independent study, I am examining whether classical accounts of weakness of will can be extended beyond individual agents to institutional actors. I want you to analyze the final eighteen months of Lehman Brothers—specifically, the firm’s continued reliance on Repo 105 accounting to mask leverage, the record bonuses paid to executives in 2007, and the board’s rejection of stricter internal risk caps in early 2008—through the combined lens of Aristotelian akrasia and modern hyperbolic discounting theory.

Treat this as a disciplined, two-directional case study. First, map the episode onto the concepts: what counted as the firm’s “better judgment” regarding solvency and risk, how the preference for immediate quarterly rewards over long-term survival illustrates hyperbolic discounting, and whether pre-commitment strategies analogous to Ulysses contracts were available but ignored. Second, identify which features of the collapse the theory explains well—such as cyclical earnings pressure functioning as a short-term reward structure—and which it misdescribes or leaves silent, including competitive dynamics with rival banks, regulatory ambiguity, and the problem of distributed cognition across committees. Finally, argue what the Lehman episode shows about the theories themselves: does applying akrasia to an institution require inventing a notion of collective practical reasoning, or does hyperbolic discounting lose explanatory power once decision-making is spread across many agents?

Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need an analysis built only on common knowledge of the 2008 crisis and on conceptual reasoning. I am particularly interested in whether this case reveals that institutional weakness of will is merely aggregated individual failure, or whether it introduces an anomaly that the standard person-level framework never anticipated.
