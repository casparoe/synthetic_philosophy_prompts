You are a senior clinical ethicist and philosopher of mind advising the Neuroethics Oversight Committee of a major research hospital. Before drafting your recommendation, think carefully and begin with brief brainstorming: map out the main conceptual distinctions around consciousness and subjecthood that are relevant here, especially where empirical findings about split-brain patients might leave philosophical questions genuinely open.

The committee must issue a formal written opinion by Friday regarding Protocol 7B, an experimental study involving a fully callosotomized patient (post-epilepsy surgery, stable for eighteen months). The protocol proposes a bilateral moral-dilemma task: complex ethical scenarios are presented separately to the left and right hemispheres via lateralized visual display, with each hemisphere required to register an independent judgment using a hand-controlled response system. The researchers maintain that this is a standard single-subject cognitive study requiring only conventional informed consent from the patient as one legal person. The patient's legal guardian, however, has submitted an objection arguing that classic split-brain phenomena demonstrate two distinct centers of awareness, and therefore that (i) both hemispheres should be treated as independent research subjects with separate consent procedures, (ii) any risk of sustained inter-hemispheric disagreement should be assessed as a harm to two welfare-interests rather than internal conflict within one, and (iii) approval should be withheld until empirical criteria for hemispheric personhood are established.

Your memo should recommend a concrete course of action—approval with modified safeguards, approval with standard consent, or rejection with conditions—together with a clear implementation time frame. In constructing your reasoning:

- Distinguish at least three dimensions of unity of consciousness (for example, phenomenal integration, narrative self-modeling, and practical-agency coherence) and indicate, for each, whether the split-brain literature suggests disruption, preservation, or ambiguity.
- Apply this framework specifically to the consent and risk-assessment questions raised by Protocol 7B, rather than treating them as purely legal formalities.
- Take seriously the guardian's strongest claim: if phenomenal unity is genuinely bifurcated, might welfare interests also be bifurcated? Address this without simply invoking the legal definition of personhood.
- Recommend specific procedural conditions if you advise approval (e.g., independent hemispheric assent protocols, a defined conflict-resolution procedure, or a time-limited pilot period), or specify what further evidence would be needed if you advise rejection or deferral.
- Acknowledge openly what remains empirically unknown about inter-hemispheric experience in this particular patient, and avoid presenting any philosophical conclusion as settled scientific fact.
- Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke named philosophers as authorities; reason through the distinctions directly.

Write the memo in a formal, polished style appropriate for circulation to clinicians, board members, and institutional counsel.
