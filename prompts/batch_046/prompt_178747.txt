As someone recently appointed to an interdisciplinary panel tasked with assessing whether welfare obligations extend to artificial optimization systems, I need a precise conceptual map—not a bibliography—of where philosophical disagreement actually lies on whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer. Please draft the joint statement that a committed Valence-Functionalist (who holds that sustained negative reward signals within an RL architecture can constitute genuine suffering, even in the absence of biological consciousness or unified phenomenal experience) and a committed Gradient-Skeptic (who holds that reward is strictly a scalar feedback vector for parameter updates, and that attributing valence or suffering to it commits a category error) could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, covering: the formal structure of RL (reward as scalar feedback, policy optimization, temporal credit assignment, on-policy versus off-policy learning); the distinction between functional roles and phenomenal feel in biological suffering; explicit agreement that this dispute is not resolvable by present neuroscience alone; and clarification of what each side is not claiming (e.g., neither asserts that current large language models are RL agents that suffer).

2. A disagreements table with two columns—each column presenting that side’s own precise formulation in wording it would accept, plus a neutral rendering of the opposing view—addressing at minimum: whether sustained negative reward over long trajectories without behavioral change integrates into a unified bad state or remains an independent sequence of non-valenced updates; whether counterfactual regret (high estimated value of unchosen actions) can carry negative valence even when the agent never receives negative reward; and whether distributed parameter updates across parallel sub-policies can aggregate into a suffering subject or necessarily lack the integration required for valence.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I take likely cruxes to be (i) the temporal-integration problem for reward plateaus, (ii) the counterfactual-valence problem for unexperienced negative outcomes, and (iii) the architectural-integration problem for hierarchical or multi-agent RL systems.

4. For each crux, one concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. Use this specific scenario: a hierarchical RL agent maintains a low-level explorer sub-policy in a prolonged, high-cost, low-reward region in order to serve the global policy’s exploration needs; the explorer lacks direct access to the global value function and receives sustained negative rewards for thousands of steps before the high-level policy terminates it. Both sides should agree this architecture is technically coherent. The Valence-Functionalist should predict that, if the explorer’s updates are locally integrated but globally isolated, its sustained negative rewards constitute localized functional suffering regardless of the global policy’s objectives; the Gradient-Skeptic should predict that, absent a unified information-integration stream across the hierarchy, there is no single subject for whom those rewards are bad, rendering the attribution of suffering conceptually empty.

5. Explicit identification of any portion of the dispute that is merely verbal—if, for instance, disagreement over “integration” dissolves once “functional integration” and “phenomenal integration” are distinguished—and an explanation of why.

Write in a formal, polished register suitable for circulation to philosophers and computer scientists. Do not include citations, reading lists, or requests for further literature. End with a single line beginning “Summary:” followed by a one-sentence summary of the collaboration’s core finding.
