I don't want a list of named philosophers; I want a decision tree built from the logical space of commitments, then mapped onto actual positions.

Start with a concrete case: a global climate model treats the atmosphere as a continuous fluid, entirely omitting discrete molecular structure, yet yields accurate large-scale temperature predictions. Build the tree from four binary diagnostic questions that someone could answer without knowing which philosophical label they point to:

1. Does predictive success in this domain license belief in the model's structural claims, or only in the predictions themselves?
2. Is the false assumption recoverable—can we systematically relax the continuity idealization and converge to the same macroscopic results—or is it constitutive, meaning the phenomenon explained (large-scale circulation patterns) literally wouldn't exist without treating the system as continuous?
3. If predictive success coexists with false ontology, does that specifically undermine scientific realism about the omitted entities (molecules, in this framing), or does it show that ontology was never the point of the model?
4. Does the F-twist apply uniformly across sciences, or is there a principled distinction—say, between physics, where idealizations are usually recoverable by deeper theories, and economics or ecology, where some may be permanent?

First, lay out the full logical space of answer combinations (sixteen leaves collapsed by consistency). Then map the clusters onto named or unnamed positions: a hard-line Friedmanite who accepts the F-twist across the board; a structural realist who accepts predictions but restricts them to recoverable idealizations only; a selective anti-realist who permits constitutive idealizations in climate science but treats them as fatal in social science; and any unnamed combinations that remain unoccupied, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why each is unstable or unexplored (e.g., "accepts F-twist for recoverable cases, insists on full ontology anyway, but denies that ontology matters for belief").

Every named or unnamed leaf should carry one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. The tree must include at least the positions above, and you should flag any pairs it cannot genuinely separate—for instance, whether a pragmatist who cares only about intervention and an instrumentalist who cares only about prediction end up on the same branch, and if so, say so explicitly.

Frame every node as a direct question, not as a theory name. Specify which distinctions matter for my purpose: the recoverable/constitutive divide is the central axis, and the physics-vs-social-science boundary is the secondary split.
