I'm advising a municipal chronic-disease management office that is revising its intake protocol for patients who repeatedly fail to adhere to treatment regimens despite expressing strong commitment. The case managers—each carrying a caseload of roughly thirty patients and conducting fifteen-minute monthly check-ins—must decide whether to renew a behavioral commitment contract (a precommitment mechanism) or to refer the patient to a practical-reasoning counseling track aimed at repairing judgment. We want the decision grounded in the combined framework of philosophical weakness of will (akrasia), understood as acting against one's retained all-things-considered judgment, and hyperbolic discounting from behavioral economics, understood as a structurally rational preference reversal driven by a non-constant discount rate. The observable behaviors overlap—skipping medication for an immediate reward—so we need a reusable, documented procedure: an ordered sequence of tests, a flowchart in words, explicit ambiguity flags, and clear points where managerial judgment remains unavoidable. The procedure should distinguish judgment-binding failure, in which the patient endorses the long-term plan at the moment of action but fails to act on it, from preference-structural reversal, in which the near reward genuinely dominates in the patient's present evaluative structure. To operationalize this, include a nested-frame audit that presents the same intertemporal trade-off both in isolation (today versus next week) and embedded in a distant sequence (week three versus week four), noting whether reversal disappears when both options are remote; pair it with a belief-stability check that records whether the patient immediately re-endorses the health plan after the lapse. The manager has no philosophical training, must complete a one-page decision form, and requires supervisory sign-off only when an ambiguity flag is triggered. Provide worked applications for three profiles: a clear hyperbolic reverser, a clear akratic patient who retains judgment, and one ambiguous case in which the procedure recommends denying contract renewal despite the patient's visible distress—explicitly flagging that this is the kind of result the manager will resist. Finally, specify exactly what the procedure cannot settle—such as simultaneous operation of both mechanisms, or unstable endorsement that masks hidden judgment revision—without obscuring those limits.
