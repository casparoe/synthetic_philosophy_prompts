As a novelist teaching an interdisciplinary seminar on narrative form and human value, I am composing the final examination for upper-level undergraduates and need an essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard summary of Nozick's experience machine. I want you to invent a conceptual variant—call it the Authorship Machine—which does not merely supply pleasurable sensations but gives the subject the sustained, phenomenologically rich experience of having lived a coherent life narrative: struggle, recognition, retrospective meaning, and the felt sense of having authored one's own plot, even though every event was scripted by an external architect and no actual achievement or relationship occurred. The exam question should ask students to determine whether this variant undermines well-being in precisely the same way Nozick claims, and it should force them to navigate the tension between narrative-identity conditions (the thesis that a good life requires that the subject genuinely live out and author her own story) and both hedonist and desire-satisfaction theories of well-being. In particular, students should be required to separate three possible objections to entering the machine—an epistemic objection (not knowing the narrative is false), a metaphysical objection (not being the true causal and authorial source), and an axiological objection (the value of the narrative being hollow regardless of experience)—and to say which objections survive if we assume the subject knows the machine is running but chooses to stay inside. Please provide the essay prompt itself, suitable for a forty-five-minute answer worth thirty marks; a model answer of approximately seven hundred words that makes the specific conceptual moves required for full credit; and a marking scheme that clearly distinguishes an A-grade response—which must identify the precise point at which narrative-authorship conditions conflict with experiential satisfaction and must avoid collapsing the three objections—from a B-grade response, which typically summarizes Nozick, lists well-being theories without applying them to the variant, and treats the authorship problem as purely about deception. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation prompts; the assessment should depend only on conceptual reasoning available without external sources.
