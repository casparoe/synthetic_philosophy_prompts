Next Thursday I defend my job-market paper, “Provisional Teleology Without Providence,” in a philosophy-of-history workshop. My thesis is that historical change exhibits a weak, non-guaranteed directional bias toward justice—not through metaphysical teleology, dialectical necessity, or divine providence, but through the long-run instability of regimes that systematically suppress institutional error-correction. I call this “institutional teleonomy”: democratic and rights-respecting institutions survive longer not because history is guided toward them, but because authoritarian regimes accumulate unresolvable internal contradictions that eventually cause collapse, producing a selection effect that mimics progress without guaranteeing any endpoint. The argument depends on distinguishing teleonomy (apparent direction without intentional design) in normative institutions from both biological teleonomy and Hegelian self-actualization, and on showing that survival tracks a minimal capacity for corrective feedback rather than mere coercive efficiency.

The audience is precisely this: two senior Hegelians who read history as the progressive actualization of freedom through necessary dialectical stages; two critical theorists—one broadly Foucauldian, one working in decolonial historiography—who treat any narrative of moral progress as ideological cover for domination; one evolutionary epistemologist sympathetic to selectionist explanations but skeptical of applying them to normative domains; and three analytic philosophers of social ontology and collective action who will press on whether “institutional survival” tracks anything genuinely moral rather than merely adaptive or technologically superior.

Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the voice and vocabulary each faction would actually use. For each objection: state the core challenge, whether I should concede part of it, what I must hold, a concise reply suitable for a Q&A setting, and the weakest point in that reply. Include the hidden objection I am hoping nobody asks—namely, that my selection story requires assuming authoritarian collapse is usually caused by internal moral failure rather than by external military defeat, resource depletion, or contingent catastrophe, and that without this assumption the directional bias disappears—which I need to defend rather than hope they miss. Also flag the weakest point in my own position: my inference from “institutions that suppress feedback tend to collapse” to “therefore there is a directional bias toward justice,” since long-run survival might track coercive capacity, economic extraction, or luck rather than justice.

Finally, please say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument or empirical-historical consideration would lead you to reject institutional teleonomy in favor of either strong Hegelian providence or pure historical contingency, and identify the precise step in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to being undermined.
