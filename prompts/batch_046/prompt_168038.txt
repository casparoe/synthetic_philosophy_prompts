A public-health ethics committee applies Scanlon-style contractualist reasoning—asking, for each affected party, whether they could reasonably reject the principle that permits a proposed policy—to evaluate risk-imposing interventions. Its established rulings are these. First, it rejected a trial that assigned 100 participants to a placebo arm carrying a 5% risk of serious clinical deterioration, even though successful validation would save an estimated 10,000 lives, on the grounds that each participant could reject being exposed to substantial harm for the statistical benefit of strangers. Second, it approved, with monitoring conditions, a trial that exposed 10,000 participants each to a 0.01% risk of minor adverse reaction for the same therapeutic purpose, holding that no individual could reasonably reject such a de minimis burden. Third, it permitted a mass-casualty triage protocol directing that one critically injured patient with a very low survival probability be passed over so that three patients with high survival probabilities receive immediate treatment, reasoning that every party in the pool could accept the allocation principle ex ante. Fourth, it permitted a screening campaign requiring 500 people to undergo a mildly unpleasant thirty-minute procedure with no physical risk in order to identify one person with a treatable fatal condition. Fifth, it rejected a factory-emissions policy that imposed a 1% risk of chronic respiratory illness on fifty nearby residents in order to preserve two hundred jobs and maintain essential production, holding that those fifty could reasonably reject bearing concrete health risk as a means to others' economic welfare.

The committee now faces a new proposal. A municipal water authority seeks to allow a trace industrial byproduct at 0.3 parts per billion—within federal safety margins but measurably above the local baseline—in the supply serving 300,000 residents, in order to fund a filtration upgrade that eliminates a 0.8% risk of fatal legionella infection for 600 residents in an adjacent, underserved district with aging infrastructure. The two populations do not overlap; no resident is in both groups. The incremental lifetime cancer risk to each of the 300,000 is estimated at roughly one case per 50,000. The authority argues that the risk is sub-regulatory, distributed, and non-interactive, while critics argue that contractualist justification requires that each affected party be able to accept the principle linking their specific burden to a specific beneficiary.
