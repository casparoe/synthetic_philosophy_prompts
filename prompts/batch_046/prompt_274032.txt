Hey, I'm a retired ecologist who got into philophy lately after retiring and I'm trying to make sense of Zhuangzi's take on death, but my gut verdicts on cases are all over the place. Here is what I actually think.

If I imagine my own body decomposing and feeding the soil, that feels right, almost beautiful, like carbon just shifting pools. A salmon dying after spawning feels the same way -- just nutrient cycling, not a real loss. But my sister dying of cancer felt like a genuine tragedy, not just transformation, and I can't shake that. Even stranger: I think cutting down an old tree that's going to rot anyway is okay, but killing a healthy tree for no reason feels wrong, even though the carbon ends up in the same place. I also think assisted suicide for suffering patients is ethically okay, like letting them transform back into nature, but I think spending huge resources to prevent species extinction is also right, which is preventing transformation/death. So sometimes death is acceptable and sometimes it's a catastrophe.

I tried framing it with system boundaries and whether unique information is lost, but that fails because both my sister and the salmon are unique patterns. Is there a principle, maybe tied to Zhuangzi's idea of non-attachment to specific forms, that can hold most of these together? Which verdict do I have to drop? And I'm going back and forth on what to tell my grandkids -- is it okay to say death is just like falling asleep and changing rooms, or does that miss the real loss of a particular person?
