I am auditing a graduate seminar on moral luck and have come to defend what I call deliberative-core isolationism: the claim that moral appraisal should target only the structure of an agent's all-things-considered practical judgment at the moment of decision, while outcomes, formative histories, and constitutive traits outside that reflective endorsement belong to a separate domain of prudential or causal evaluation. On this view, two agents with identically structured deliberative cores merit identical moral assessment regardless of whether one succeeds through an unforeseeable event or was raised in deprivation rather than privilege, since neither result nor background is part of the core that morality addresses. I want a rigorous map of what could genuinely defeat this position—observations, arguments, formal distinctions, or internal tensions—not merely a list of familiar objections, and I want to know whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a defect.

Present your response in American English as a numbered list of exactly five items, each limited to one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should identify a specific kind of counterevidence or argument, distinguish whether it would be decisive or merely awkward, state what defenders of isolationism themselves ought to concede, and assess whether the position risks collapse into a rival account. Include at least one item addressing collective-action contexts in which individual deliberative cores are causally interdependent, and address whether the isolationist can consistently maintain the moral-prudential boundary without that distinction dissolving under pressure.
