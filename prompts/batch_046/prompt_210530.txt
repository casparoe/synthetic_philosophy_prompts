I was arguing for hours last night with a friend who serves as a mediator for a regional development council, and we kept returning to the same unresolved tension: she must repeatedly adjudicate compromise agreements between stakeholder groups—communities with customary land tenure, secular environmental coalitions, religious institutions, labor collectives—whose core commitments are not merely different but structurally incommensurable, and she needs a principled, reusable way to distinguish a rational "gain from trade" through moral compromise from a simple corruption or dilution of one party's non-negotiable values. I want you to translate the underlying principle—roughly, that compromise is legitimate only when each party concedes jurisdiction within a sphere they treat as separable and protected, receiving in return protection within a sphere they treat as sacred, but becomes corruption when values are forced into a single metric of exchange—into an operational decision procedure she can actually run in a two-hour session without a research library.

The deliverable should be a step-by-step protocol of five to seven ordered tests, each with explicit pass/fail criteria, clear "stop and reject" conditions, and clearly marked "stop and escalate to human judgment" nodes where the distinction between jurisdictional separation and metric dilution cannot be resolved mechanically. You must include a "jurisdictional boundary test" that asks whether the conceded and protected goods belong to genuinely separable spheres of value for each party, and an "asymmetry check" that verifies both sides gain in terms of their own framework rather than one merely suffering less. The procedure must be honest about its misfires: identify the specific case types it will misclassify, flag where a mediator's judgment about what counts as "sacred" versus "negotiable" is doing hidden work, and state explicitly what categories of value conflict are structurally outside its scope. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citation prompts—she has no access to texts in these sessions and needs something she can apply from memory.

Please apply the procedure to three concrete proposals: first, a religious community permitting a secular mental-health facility on historic grounds in exchange for a permanent covenant shielding an adjacent burial site from development; second, a land council with customary tenure accepting limited peripheral resource extraction in exchange for absolute veto authority over a sacred watershed; and third, an environmental coalition supporting a transit expansion they normally oppose in exchange for a legally binding restoration trust for degraded wetlands elsewhere. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a verdict that feels morally wrong, and explain precisely where the procedural logic fails to capture the moral reality. Finally, specify exactly what this framework cannot do—whether it fails with deeply asymmetrical power relations, with single-party internal conflicts, or with values that resist sphere-separation altogether.
