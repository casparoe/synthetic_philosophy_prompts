You serve as the permanent staff ethics officer for a regional legislative assembly that convenes weekly and regularly confronts proposals in which members must choose between honoring a procedural or moral constraint and preventing a serious, foreseeable public harm. Over the past year, members have invoked the "problem of dirty hands" in debates about selective disclosure of confidential records, strategic misrepresentation to oversight bodies, and coercive bargaining with private actors. They now require a reusable decision procedure—not a one-time advisory opinion—to determine whether a specific proposed action falls within a permissible dirty-hands exception (a genuinely wrongful act that is unavoidable, proportionate, and must be publicly acknowledged with institutional cost) or instead amounts to corruption, rationalization, or an avoidable breach.

Construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions that a non-philosopher staffer can run in under thirty minutes on a written proposal. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient affirmative or negative answer; mark the exact termination points where the procedure yields a clear "do not proceed" or "this is corruption, not dirty hands"; and flag every juncture at which the answer depends on contested value judgment rather than verifiable fact, requiring the staffer to escalate to the assembly's ethics committee rather than decide unilaterally. The procedure must preserve the conceptual distinctions that separate dirty hands from ordinary political compromise: the means must be genuinely wrongful (not merely unpopular or inconvenient); the wrong must be unavoidable by any less harmful available path; the agent and institution must bear a real cost or exposure rather than derive private advantage; and concealment or denial transforms the act into corruption regardless of the outcome achieved.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete proposals:
(a) A committee chair proposes to leak selectively edited internal audit documents to an investigative journalist in order to force removal of a contractor who is falsifying safety reports on a public-works project, after confirming that formal whistleblower and inspector-general channels have been systematically obstructed by that contractor's political allies;
(b) A senior legislator proposes to delay approval of an emergency housing appropriation—supported by a broad bipartisan majority—unless the mayor appoints the legislator's unqualified relative to a vacant planning board, framing the delay as necessary "leverage" to secure more accountable governance;
(c) A proposal to make a non-consensual audio recording of a private conversation between two opposition members, in which they discuss exploiting an obscure procedural loophole to deny emergency services to a specific neighborhood, and then to release selected excerpts through an unofficial channel, knowing that formal disclosure rules prohibit the recording method but that inaction risks immediate harm.

For each case, trace the path through your ordered questions, state the final classification (permissible dirty hands, impermissible corruption, or procedurally indeterminate), and identify precisely where judgment rather than procedure governs the outcome. Include at least one case in which the procedure reaches a conclusion that the proposing member will strongly reject, and explain why the procedural logic nevertheless requires that result despite the member's framing.

Finally, specify what the procedure explicitly cannot do: the categories of proposals for which it is not designed; the forms of moral reasoning it deliberately excludes; and the conditions—such as irreconcilable value conflict or inadequate information—under which the ethics committee should override even a technically correct procedural result.
