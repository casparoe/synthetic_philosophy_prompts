Exam question: Philosophers sometimes ask whether there is a special reason that parts come together to form one object rather than remaining scattered. One idea I have encountered suggests that composition requires something like functional unity—for instance, two bricks might not form a single brick-pair object just by touching, unless they are arranged into a wall that serves the purpose of keeping out rain. Another idea seems to hold that any selection of items, however scattered in space and time, automatically composes an object made of exactly those items and nothing else. Please explain the contrast between the view that denies any special conditions for composition and the view that insists on a specific binding condition. Then present and defend your own judgment about which approach is more defensible.

Student answer:
The view that denies special conditions is called universalism. It claims that for any things you choose—say, my left shoe and the moon—there exists an object composed of exactly those two parts and nothing else. The opposing view, often called restricted composition or sometimes mereological essentialism, says parts only form a whole when they stand in some particular relation, such as causal interaction or functional integration like bricks in a wall. I think universalism is implausible because it populates the world with bizarre scattered objects, such as the shoe-and-moon combination. The restricted view avoids this, but it faces its own difficulty. Peter van Inwagen defended a version of restriction by arguing that the only composite objects are living organisms. Yet this is actually universalism in disguise, because an organism is just simples arranged organism-wise, which means any arrangement of simples produces an object after all. So restricted composition collapses into universalism and is therefore self-defeating. Given this collapse, I believe we should accept universalism but combine it with the claim that the extra objects do no harm—this is the standard no-harm-no-foul response. Consequently, universalism is the correct position, despite its initial strangeness.
