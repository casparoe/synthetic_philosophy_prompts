As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading seriously in philosophy of history and moral psychology, I have been trying to understand the long-running disagreement between Christopher Browning and Daniel Goldhagen over the perpetrators of the Holocaust—specifically, the members of Reserve Police Battalion 101. Browning's account treats situational dynamics—peer pressure, bureaucratic incrementalism, and the absence of sanctions for refusal—as the primary explanatory vector, suggesting that these were in some sense ordinary men placed in extraordinary institutional conditions. Goldhagen, examining much of the same archival material, argues that a pervasive, culturally specific eliminationist antisemitism was both necessary and sufficient, rendering situational factors largely ornamental. Decades later, the debate seems frozen: each side accuses the other of selectively reading testimony, yet neither specifies in advance what kind of documentary discovery would compel a revision.

My own suspicion, shaped by years of thinking about boundary conditions and emergent behavior in physical systems, is that this is not a disguised empirical dispute but a disagreement about explanatory architecture. Browning appears to offer something analogous to a statistical-mechanical explanation: given certain boundary conditions—the institutional framework, the gradual escalation of tasks, the social costs of dissent—the observed behavior is what one should predict from average human agents without invoking special psychological properties. Goldhagen, by contrast, demands what looks like a law-level cultural cause operating at the collective level, one that cannot be reduced to or substituted by situational parameters. If so, they may be answering different questions—one predictive and structural, the other causal and normative—under the single heading of why these men participated.

But I want to test this framing. Is the stall actually about hidden empirical disagreements—say, about whether post-hoc testimony can establish pre-existing motivation—or about incompatible standards for what counts as an explanation in human affairs? Could a conceptual distinction between enabling conditions and triggering causes, or a formal model of collective action under social pressure, actually move either side, or is the impasse a sign that the framing itself is ill-posed? I am not asking for a literature survey or for citations. Rather, I want a precise diagnosis of the disagreement's logical structure—whether it is verbal, substantive, methodological, or metaphysical—grounded in what each author actually claims, together with an honest assessment of whether any distinction, archival finding, or change of question could break the deadlock, or whether progress requires abandoning the opposition between situation and culture altogether.
