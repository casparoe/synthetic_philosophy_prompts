I was recently appointed to lead the redesign of the "Moral Exemplars" module in our school's advanced humanities curriculum, and the debate among my colleagues has become unexpectedly sharp. For years we taught biography as straightforward inspiration, but after revisiting Plutarch's practice in the Parallel Lives—where paired comparison is meant to train judgment by placing virtue and vice in embodied action rather than to encourage direct imitation—I want a framework that avoids both uncritical hero worship and cynical debunking. I need a reusable evaluation procedure my team of teachers, who have no formal philosophy background, can run in about thirty minutes per proposed subject, with a clear sequence of questions, defined stopping points, mandatory sign-off from our ethics committee for borderline cases, and explicit flags for where value judgments must replace factual checks. To keep the framework from collapsing into standard hagiography, I want to inject a distinction I am developing in my own notes between "mirror lives," intended for direct student identification and emulation, and "lantern lives," meant only to illuminate a specific moral quality from safe narrative distance, together with a "hagiographic risk audit" that tests whether a biography's source tradition has systematically suppressed contradictory evidence about the subject's actions.

Please construct a step-by-step decision procedure: the ordered questions to ask about a proposed biographical subject, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure terminates with rejection, where it permits inclusion only under mandatory framing conditions (mirror versus lantern designation, required comparative pairing, or explicit distancing protocols), and where it must defer to human judgment because the issue is one of educational values rather than biographical fact. The procedure should be grounded in Plutarch's actual theory that biography teaches through comparative examination of lives, but it should also account for modern critiques of hagiography that show how unexamined admiration distorts moral perception. Then apply it to three proposals: first, a widely revered civil-rights leader whose legacy is now being critically re-examined, where the procedure should flag high hagiographic risk and either reject direct mirror framing or demand a parallel comparison with a flawed counterpart; second, a technically brilliant but morally ruthless industrial innovator, where the procedure should allow inclusion only as a lantern life with strict distance protocols; and third, a beloved local historical figure whose documented record is thin but whose symbolic value to the community is high, where the procedure should yield an unwelcome answer—likely rejection on evidentiary grounds—while precisely flagging which value-based exception the committee would need to invoke to override it. Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: which kinds of moral disagreement among staff it will not resolve, which biases in available source material it inherits and amplifies, and what kind of proposed case would break the framework entirely.
