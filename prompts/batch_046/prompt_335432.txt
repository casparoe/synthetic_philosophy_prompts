I'm preparing a consistency audit for a philosophy-of-social-science reading group I'm joining as a non-specialist (I'm a software engineer by training). I've been thinking about whether organizational culture can be a genuine cause of system behavior—by analogy to Weber's thesis that the Protestant ethic was causally implicated in the rise of capitalism—rather than just a post-hoc narrative. Here is where I've landed, and I want to know if the package holds together.

My commitments are:

(a) Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis is essentially correct: the value-orientation associated with Calvinist predestination anxiety and the idea of a "calling" (Beruf) functioned as a real causal factor in the emergence of rational capitalist enterprise, not merely as an ideological rationalization of material interests.

(b) Any explanation that appeals to culture or collective values is legitimate only if it can ultimately be cashed out in terms of the subjective reasons, beliefs, and intentions of individual actors; collective entities such as "the spirit of capitalism" have no independent causal powers of their own.

(c) I accept Weber's notion of elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft): material economic conditions and cultural value-systems can align and reinforce each other without either being reducible to the other, so that explaining capitalism requires citing both but reducing neither to the other.

(d) I reject methodological individualism: social-level properties, including institutional value-patterns, can exert downward causal influence on individual behavior in ways that are not fully decomposable into individual psychology.

(e) I think values explain human action only in the mode of interpretive understanding (Verstehen)—they render behavior intelligible to us—but they do not operate as efficient mechanical causes in the way that physical forces do.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe is each tension? Rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. For each genuine tension, identify the least costly revision and what it costs me—particularly whether giving up (b) or (d) forces me to abandon (a) or (c). Also flag whether (e) undermines my right to call the Protestant ethic a "cause" in (a), or whether that can be salvaged by reading "cause" through the lens of elective affinity in (c). Finally, tell me whether there is a single revision—giving up one commitment—that cleans up the most conflicts, and whether that revision is worth it.
