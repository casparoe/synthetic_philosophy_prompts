I have been developing an argument that I call "franchise continuity," according to which democratic legitimacy requires extending the vote to anyone whose life is systematically shaped by a polity's binding decisions, with the specific mechanism—whether compulsory participation, a lowered age threshold, or the suspension of citizenship requirements—being secondary to the underlying principle of affectedness. Under this framework, compulsory voting addresses citizens who abstain despite being governed; lowering the voting age addresses near-adults with long-term stakes in present policies; and votes for non-citizen residents address governed people who lack membership status. If franchise continuity is sound, then standard objections to non-citizen voting—appeals to national self-determination, the special obligations of citizenship, or the need for a bounded demos—seem to apply with equal force to compulsory voting and youth enfranchisement, both of which are treated as legitimate democratic reforms rather than categorical violations of the polity's boundaries. Does this asymmetry reveal that democratic theorists are inconsistently applying the affectedness principle, or is there a principled internal distinction within franchise continuity that makes citizenship status a genuinely non-negotiable boundary, even for those who accept both compulsory voting and a voting age well below the age of majority?
