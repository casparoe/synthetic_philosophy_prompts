I run a small creative studio with exactly one year of operating reserves that I can deploy across speculative projects. This season I am weighing four opportunities: a reliable, low-margin contract that preserves capital but offers little growth, and three experimental collaborations that are largely uncorrelated. Each experimental project demands an upfront commitment of time and materials. If it succeeds, the payoff is not merely additive—success in one domain raises my pricing power and reputation in a way that multiplies the value of future work. If it fails, I lose the invested resources entirely. A mentor urges me to size each commitment proportionally to my subjective confidence and to maximize the arithmetic expectation summed across all four ventures, treating them as independent draws from a distribution. I object that this reasoning seems to presuppose that I live through many parallel lives and can average over them; in reality I traverse a single sequential path, and a severe loss on one front could leave me without the capital to exploit later successes, breaking the compounding chain. Still, I worry that avoiding all risk is itself a form of guaranteed erosion. How should I evaluate the right scale of commitment? Is the framework of maximizing summed expectation appropriate here, or does the sequential, multiplicative, and ruin-sensitive nature of my actual situation call for a different structure of rationality?
