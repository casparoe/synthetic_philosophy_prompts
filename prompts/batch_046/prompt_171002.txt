I am preparing a handout for an upper-level philosophy of language seminar—about fifteen postgraduate students—on vagueness. I want a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts a respondent’s actual commitments, not the labels they prefer, into named positions on the logic and metaphysics of vague predicates.

Please build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of view-names. Start with concrete diagnostic cases or claims: for instance, whether a respondent accepts that there must be a precise millisecond at which a gradually greying hair becomes non-brown; whether they think a hidden, single correct precisification exists that we merely fail to know; whether they allow that ‘borderline brown’ can itself be vague without collapsing into contradiction; whether admissible precisifications vary with conversational context; and whether they reject bivalence altogether for borderline sentences. Each internal node should be a question answerable without knowing philosophical jargon—e.g. ‘If neuroscientists eventually mapped the exact retinal threshold for seeing colour X, would that show that all prior borderline judgments were mistaken?’ Each leaf should name the position—epistemicism, supervaluationism, contextualism, degree-theoretic or fuzzy-logic approaches, ontic vagueness, or ‘unnamed’—and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment.

Please ensure epistemicism and supervaluationism appear as distinct leaves. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, and note any unoccupied combinations in the logical space with a brief diagnosis (incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect). Keep the tree to no more than eight decision nodes. Write the response in British English, using British spelling and punctuation conventions.
