I'm in a history PhD program and we're having this running argument about archives that I can't shake. I was talking with my advisor about it and she said I'm overthinking it, but I think there's something philosphically weird going on.

National archives and big university collections spend enormous budgets preserving state papers, census aggregates, diplomatic cables, elite correspondence — basically anything that comes from institutions with letterhead. But for non-elite people, the collection logic is totally different: either the papers survive by accident and get donated, or, in some newer digitization programs, archivists apply a kind of 'representitive sample' filter where they only preserve private papers that match demographic averages for a cohort, treating statistical outliers as noise not worth storage space. At the same time, these same institutions fund microhistory projects that deliberately chase the outliers — the single heretic trial, the anomalous labor contract, the weird peasant inheritance dispute — on the theory that singular cases reveal the hidden flexibility of social structures that aggregate data hides. So the same system treats deviation as garbage in one wing and gold in the other.

What implicit theory of historical value and knowledge does this split practice presuppose? Does the archive's sampling logic assume that history is fundamentally about populations and structures, and that individual deviation is just noise? Does microhistory presuppose the opposite — that structures are only visible at their breaking points? Are these two practices philosophically consistent within one institution, or do they rest on rival ontologies of the social? And if we rebuilt the archive system on genuinely microhistorical commitments, what exactly would have to change — would we save every scrap of paper, or just a different kind of outlier? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theories, say where the practice is actually underdetermined and could be licensed by several views, and tell me what a consistent policy would have to look like.
