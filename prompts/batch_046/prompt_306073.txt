I've been trying to formalize the unilateralist's curse for a seminar on collective rationality and I'm stuck on what a real solution would even have to look like. You know the basic idea: if every member of a group acts on their own best judgment, and each is only slightly more likely right than wrong, the group can end up doing something catastrophic because the mistakes don't cancel—they stack. But I'm less interested in another restatement and more in the full spec for an adequate theory of how to avoid or manage it.

So write the desiderata: what any decent account of mitigating the curse actually has to deliver. It needs to cover the cases—like the difference between one expert acting alone versus a crowd of amateurs, or between reversable choices (pulling funding from a project) and irreversible ones (approving a geongineering trial)—with the reasoning behind each. It needs to respect distinctions that people often collapse, especially between being epistemically humble and being practically paralized, and between deferring to someone else's judgment and just outsourcing your moral responsibility. I'm also injecting a framing I haven't seen discussed much: the second-order curse, where people apply unilateral reasoning to the very decision of whether to defer—so they either refuse to yield because they overestimate their meta-judgment, or they defer reflexivly and lose the error-correction that deference was supposed to provide. Your spec should say whether a theory has to solve that second-order loop to count as adequate.

Then flag which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences—like whether individual autonomy has to be preserved intact, or whether empirical equivalence with democratic voting procedures matters. And tell me if there's a subset that's jointly unsatisfiable, maybe because preserving full first-order decision rights for everyone and achieving collective error-correction pull in opposite directions. I want you to take a definite position here, not just list options: say which requirements you'd hold onto even if it costs you popularity with one side, and briefly score how deference norms, hierarchical authority, and democratic aggregation do against your list—keep it short, since the criteria are the point.
