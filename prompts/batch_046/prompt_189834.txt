At a seminar on normative ethics, two participants—Dr. Elena Voss and her colleague Dr. Marcus Chen—disagreed over whether a widely observed asymmetry in moral judgment reveals a cognitive bias or a legitimate normative preference. The asymmetry concerns what they called “threshold exclusion”: people consistently rate an active decision to deny a scarce resource to a specific, identifiable individual as worse than a rule-based exclusion that produces the identical outcome for that same individual, even when both are fully transparent and voluntary.

(1) Voss argued that this asymmetry is best explained by the omission-commission bias, well documented in behavioral ethics. She claimed that the psychological mechanism is identical: agents feel stronger causal responsibility for direct actions than for outcomes mediated by rules, and this feeling is a heuristic left over from evolutionary social coordination, not a reason-sensitive moral distinction. Therefore, she concluded, the judgment should be corrected by normative theory rather than accommodated.

(2) Chen replied that labeling it a bias assumes what needs proof—namely, that the normative landscape is flat with respect to causal mode. He proposed that active exclusion carries a distinct moral property: it treats the individual as a direct object of agency, whereas rule-based exclusion treats them as falling under a general principle. The preference for the latter, he said, reflects a genuine commitment to impartiality and respect for moral status, not a cognitive error.

(3) Voss countered that Chen’s distinction collapses under scrutiny. If the rule is designed specifically to exclude that individual—say, a threshold calibrated precisely so that only she fails—then the causal structure is identical to direct exclusion, yet the judgmental asymmetry persists. This, she said, demonstrates that the relevant factor is not the normative property Chen identifies, but the surface framing of the agent’s movement.

(4) Chen conceded the calibrated-threshold case but introduced what he called a “procedural residue” argument: even in the calibrated case, the agent’s mental state differs. In direct exclusion, the agent’s intention is directed at the individual; in rule application, it is directed at the principle, and the harm is foreseen but not intended in the relevant sense. He argued that moral preferences often track intentional structure, and that dismissing this as bias risks flattening all of moral psychology into error theory.

(5) Voss then proposed a diagnostic: if a judgment is genuinely preference-driven, it should remain stable across counterfactual variations in descriptive framing that leave normative features constant; if it shifts with framing, it is bias-driven. Applying this to the exclusion case, she noted that reversing the narrative order—presenting the rule first versus the individual first—reverses the strength of judgment, which she took as evidence of framing-dependent bias.

(6) Chen responded that this diagnostic is circular. Stability under framing, he said, may simply indicate that the preference is deeply embedded in our normative framework rather than superficial. He further noted that many uncontroversial moral distinctions—such as the difference between killing and letting die—also exhibit framing sensitivity in psychological studies, yet we do not treat them as biases to be eliminated. Therefore, he concluded, the burden is on Voss to show why this particular asymmetry belongs in the error category rather than in the category of legitimate moral taste.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say which party is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly with respect to whether there exists a non-circular criterion for distinguishing moral preferences from cognitive biases in domains where causal structure and framing are entangled.
