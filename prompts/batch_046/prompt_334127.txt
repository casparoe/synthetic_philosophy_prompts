I'm in a bio-phil seminar and we just read Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique — the idea that a lot of traits are just architectural byproducts, not adaptations. I'm trying to apply it to Lord of the Flies for a short essay. The usual reading is that the boys' savagery is an adaptive responce to being stranded, but what if it's actually a spandrel? Like, the conch, the assemblies, Piggy's glasses — these aren't failed tools of civilization, they're structural leftovers of british boarding-school culture that accidently organize violence. Could you read the novel through that lens? Which scenes (maybe Simon's death or the final hunt) does it illuminate, and where does Golding's text push back and insist on real adaptive breakdown? I want a reading that takes the evolutionary argument seriously without just using biology as a lazy metaphor, and that says what the book adds that the philosophy papers miss.
