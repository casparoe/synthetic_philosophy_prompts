I've been working through the implications of social-choice results for mechanism design in collective forecasting and decentralized governance—specifically, how groups might aggregate ordinal rankings over outcomes or policies without opening themselves to strategic distortion. In that context, a colleague summarized the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem for me as follows.

"The theorem proves that with three or more alternatives, any deterministic voting rule that isn't a dictatorship will necessarily produce cyclical majorities, which is why sincere preference revelation is impossible in groups: at least one voter can always misrepresent her ranking to secure a better result. This means that if you want non-dictatorial aggregation, you must either accept strategic gaming or move to probabilistic mechanisms where voters influence outcomes only indirectly. The usual fixes—restricting preferences to single-peaked domains or using cardinal scoring—are essentially workarounds that break one of the theorem's premises, and Arrow's impossibility result captures the same underlying problem from a different angle."

Something about this feels off, especially where Arrow is invoked and where cyclical outcomes are mentioned. How fair is this summary? Please audit it claim by claim: which parts are technically accurate representations of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result, which are defensible simplifications for a mechanism-design audience, which conflate this theorem with Arrow's or with Condorcet's paradox, and whether the conclusion that probabilistic rules or domain restrictions are the only escapes actually follows from the result as stated. I'm particularly interested in whether the summary correctly distinguishes strategy-proofness failures from intransitive social preference, and whether its framing of "sincere preference revelation is impossible" overstates the scope of the theorem beyond deterministic ordinal rules with universal domain.
