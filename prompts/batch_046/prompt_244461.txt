In the engineering literature on constraining artificial agents, one proposed safeguard is to penalize an AI for any deviation from a counterfactual baseline—the world-state that would have obtained had the system never been activated—rather than rewarding only task completion. The idea is that the agent can pursue its objective while keeping side effects near zero, and this is meant to work even when designers have not supplied a full account of what is valuable or harmful. Having spent a career measuring perturbations in physical systems, I am struck by the philosophical load carried by that baseline: to call one trajectory unchanged and another damaged seems to require a prior theory of which differences matter and why. What conception of harm, value, or agency makes this design choice coherent? Does the practice implicitly commit to a negative consequentialism in which all change is prima facie bad, to a deontological constraint against interfering with an independently running world, or to something like a causal-accountability view of agency? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, distinguish what the safeguard strictly requires from what merely makes it attractive, and say whether it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with rival accounts of welfare—or whether it collapses without a specific, unstated theory of value. If that hidden theory were replaced, what would have to change in how the safeguard is implemented?
