I've been trying to work out for myself why some moral disagreements feel like clashes of taste and others feel like someone is blinded by prejudice, and I'm not convinced there's a clean line between the two. Here are my judgments about particular situations, and I'd like to know whether they point to a single principle or force me to choose between saving every intuition and accepting a simpler account.

First, when someone says factory farming is wrong mainly because the smell and sight of slaughterhouses make them physically nauseated, and they admit they'd feel differently if they'd grown up on a farm, I tend to call that a bias — specifically, an aesthetic disgust that has been mislread as moral knowledge. Second, when a person insists that breaking a promise to a dead friend is gravely wrong even though no living being suffers and no reputation is at stake, and they can't defend it except by saying it just violates something fundamental, I treat that as a genuine preference or commitment rather than a bias, because it survives the test of imagining every culture disagreeing. Third, consider someone who refuses to drive a gasoline car because of climate harm, but who also admits they enjoy the feeling of moral superiority more than they enjoy walking; I think the preference for walking might be partly self-flattering, yet the underlying climate judgment isn't automatically a bias just because it's emotionally rewarding. Fourth, there's the case of a wealthy person who donates almost nothing and says charity is optional because people should earn their comfort; I call that a bias, but mainly because it tracks their self-interest so neatly that it collapses under the veil-of-ignorance thought experiment, not because it's emotional.

The pair that really troubles me is this: one person feels intense revulsion at eating insects and concludes it's immoral, yet can clearly imagine being raised in Thailand and feeling the opposite. By contrast, another person feels intense revulsion at polyamorous marriage, can also imagine being raised in a culture that practices it, and still insists it's immoral — but in this second case I find myself more willing to call the revulsion a bias, perhaps because the first case involves harm-avoidance and the second involves purity. So my own judgments seem to depend on whether I can map the intuition onto some independent harm or fairness story, but I'm not sure that's consistent, since I just said promise-keeping survives without such a map. What principle, if any, distinguishes a moral preference from a moral bias across these cases? Does it come down to stability under cultural counter-imagination, independence from self-interest, coherence with a broader system, or something like what I'd call epistemic transparency — the degree to which the holder can explain the judgment without circular appeals to the feeling itself? And if there's no single principle that saves all four of my verdicts, which one should I overturn?

Finally, apply whatever principle you settle on to a case I haven't mentioned: a scientist who feels that deliberately editing human embryos for intelligence is unnatural and therefore wrong, who admits this feeling is partly rooted in religious upbringing, but who also points to long-term evolutionary risks that she genuinely believes are serious. Is her judgment a preference, a bias, or a hybrid — and what does that tell us about the principle?
