A legal scholar who advises international tribunals and memorial commissions—call her Dr. Voss—has issued a consistent series of advisory opinions on whether specific historical events should be designated genocide both under Article II of the 1948 Convention and in public moral-political discourse. Her rulings are as follows.

First, on the 1994 killings of Tutsi in Rwanda: she classifies them unambiguously as genocide in both registers.

Second, on the killings of Cham Muslims and ethnic Vietnamese carried out by the Khmer Rouge between 1975 and 1979: she classifies them as genocide under the Convention, emphasizing targeted destruction of identifiable protected groups.

Third, on the Holodomor of 1932–1933: she rules that it does not meet the Convention's criteria, arguing that the primary intent was political and economic coercion rather than the destruction of Ukrainians as a national group; she also argues that it should not be named genocide in public discourse, because applying the label where specific intent is ambiguous weakens its moral and legal force.

Fourth, on the Canadian and Australian residential-school systems for Indigenous children, operating from the late nineteenth century through the late twentieth: she rules that they do not constitute genocide under Article II, since there was no state intent to kill members or prevent births; nevertheless, she supports naming them genocide in moral and political discourse, on the grounds that the systematic forced assimilation and destruction of family structures were intended to eliminate these groups as distinct social and cultural entities.

Now consider a new case. A state institutes a mandatory genetic-screening program for a recessive disorder that occurs almost exclusively within a particular ethnoreligious minority. The program is administered through universal public-health infrastructure but is enforced only in regions populated by that minority. Internal planning documents describe the policy as addressing the demographic imbalance caused by high in-group birth rates and project a reduction of the minority's population by roughly half over four generations, achieved through state-subsidized selective termination and social incentives for exogamy. No direct killings occur.

Predict Dr. Voss's verdict: would she classify this as genocide under the Convention, support naming it genocide in public discourse, do both, do neither, or split the registers? More importantly, identify the two or more competing principles that could consistently explain her first four rulings—one that might treat the legal category as strictly limited to direct physical destruction with documented specific intent, another that might treat cultural and biological destruction of group existence as equally covered, and any other plausible reconstruction. Say what each principle predicts about the genetic-screening case. Give your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasoning for preferring that interpretation. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—different in mechanism or context—that would most clearly distinguish the leading principles you identified.
