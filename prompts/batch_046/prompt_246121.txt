As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading political philosophy in any systematic way, I have been trying to understand whether democratic citizens bear a genuine moral obligation to vote, and how the familiar "lesser evil" problem should be analyzed without reducing it to mere strategic calculation. In a recent discussion, a well-read colleague—who is not a professional philosopher but has followed the democratic-theory literature for years—offered the following reconstruction of where the debate now stands, which I have done my best to preserve from my notes.

He maintains that contemporary philosophers largely agree there is no strict moral duty to vote in large-scale democracies, because the causal efficacy of any individual ballot is negligible; he frames this as applying a Parfitian structure of collective action to democratic participation, in which each voter is causally inert in isolation yet collectively decisive. From this premise, he says, the literature divides cleanly into two camps: an expressive camp, which treats voting purely as a symbolic declaration of values carrying no further moral weight, and an instrumental camp, which evaluates a vote solely by its expected effect on outcomes. He then claims that the familiar lesser-evil argument—casting a ballot for the less harmful candidate in a two-party system—is merely instrumentalism in disguise, since it weighs probable harms against probable benefits. More strongly, he asserts that critics have demonstrated that the expressive position collapses into incoherence: if expressing support for a harmful platform makes one complicit in the harm that platform produces, then expression itself generates instrumental obligations not to vote poorly. Consequently, he concludes that the only philosophically consistent stance is the one defended by Jason Brennan and similar authors: one should either abstain entirely, or vote only if one possesses sufficient epistemic competence to identify the genuinely best option; voting for the lesser evil out of strategic resignation is, on his account, a form of moral self-deception in which one substitutes symbolic satisfaction for an honest acknowledgment of one's negligible but real contribution to collective harm.

Something about this sounds too tidy to me—particularly the claim that philosophers generally deny any voting duty, and the sharp separation he draws between expressive and instrumental reasoning. I also wonder whether the Parfitian analogy of causal impotence genuinely undermines expressive justifications, or whether it quietly imports assumptions about collective responsibility that expressive theorists would reject. Could you audit this summary? Please separate what is broadly accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what subtly misrepresents the actual positions; identify any conflations of distinct claims or overstatements of modest theses; and tell me whether my colleague's final conclusion—that strategic lesser-evil voting is incoherent—actually survives once the errors and omissions in his framing are corrected. I am especially interested in whether the problem of complicity he invokes applies with equal force to abstention, and whether his framing omits any important intermediate positions between pure expression and pure instrumentality.
