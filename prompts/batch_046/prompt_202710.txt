I host a long-form interview series aimed at curious listeners without formal academic training in history or psychology. Next week’s guest is a historian—unnamed in our publicity, but known to specialists—who has written on Police Battalion 101 and defends a view she calls “occupational moral compartmentalization.” She argues that the Browning “ordinary men” account (situational peer pressure and authority) and the Goldhagen “willing executioners” account (deep, culturally specific eliminationist antisemitism) both miss a distinct causal pathway: the shooters interpreted mass killing through pre-existing professional scripts about police duty, hygiene, and administrative cleanliness, treating murder as an unpleasant but legitimate occupational task rather than as an expression of either social conformity or personal hatred. She insists this is not a compromise between the two classic positions but a separate mechanism rooted in role identity, and she draws analogies to modern bureaucratic moral distancing to show how generic occupational framing can enable extreme violence without requiring either exceptional brutality or exceptional ideology.

I am drafting questions for the interview and want to avoid having her merely summarize the familiar Browning-Goldhagen debate, since our audience has already encountered those books in popular summaries. I need at least one question that presses her on the documented cases of men who refused to participate in the shootings without suffering severe punishment—if occupational framing made killing routine, why did some break the frame? For that, please include a follow-up keyed to her most likely reply, namely that refusal required an explicit rejection of the professional identity itself, and then push further on whether that makes her account collapse back into an individual-character explanation rather than a genuinely situational one. I also want a question about the comparison she draws with modern bureaucratic ethics—specifically whether her analogy risks normalizing the perpetrators by treating their violence as structurally similar to ordinary workplace moral distancing—and a follow-up asking what empirical evidence could distinguish her mechanism from Goldhagen’s claim that the culture itself was already saturated with dehumanizing attitudes toward Jews. Finally, please draft a question that addresses the criticism that her framework ignores the specific content of Nazi racial ideology: if the mechanism is genuinely about generic occupational framing, why did these same men not apply it to other groups they were ordered to kill, and what does that selective application tell us about whether ideology was doing hidden work after all? Throughout, anticipate standard defensive moves and build in the follow-ups that make them harder to deploy.
