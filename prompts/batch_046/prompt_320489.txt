I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar for third-year undergraduates who have read Dennett’s Consciousness Explained, specifically the chapters on the Cartesian Theater and the Multiple Drafts model, together with a brief excerpt from Baars on global workspace dynamics, but who have not been assigned any specialized literature on split-brain phenomenology or on narrative-binding objections to anti-theater views. I am writing a three-hour closed-book final exam, and I need one essay question worth forty percent of the paper that tests conceptual control rather than summary recall. The question should present a conceptually precise but somewhat artificial scenario: a split-brain patient in whom the left hemisphere verbally reports calm acceptance of a stimulus presented to the right visual field, while the right hemisphere communicates emotional distress through left-hand pointing, and in whom a unified autobiographical narrative is confabulated only minutes afterward. Students must use Dennett’s framework to explain why this does not establish two consciousnesses in one skull, but also why it does not vindicate the Cartesian Theater’s commitment to a single presentational stage with a definite temporal and spatial location. Crucially, they must confront the objection that Dennett’s model secretly reinstates a Cartesian Theater at the level of the editorial or narrative-binding draft—the idea that whichever draft achieves verbal report functions as a de facto center of consciousness. The model answer should be approximately seven hundred words and must contain five specific moves: a precise characterization of the Cartesian Theater’s spatiotemporal commitments; an explanation of how the multiple drafts model dissolves the need for a canonical “when” and “where” of experience; an application to the split-brain case that treats competing drafts as lacking a privileged observer; a direct reply to the hidden-theater objection that distinguishes editorial selection from presentational centralization; and an honest identification of one genuine weakness in Dennett’s position that can be named without appealing to external authorities. The marking scheme should separate an A answer, which demonstrates that narrative binding is functionally different from presentational presence and correctly identifies the hidden-theater objection, from a B answer, which summarizes both models and applies them to the case but treats the editorial draft as merely a weaker version of the Cartesian Theater. Lower marks should be assigned for answers that invoke hemispheric language dominance as if it resolves the philosophical problem, or that describe the clinical details without engaging the drafts framework at all. Please write the exam question, the model answer, and the marking scheme with explicit point allocations for each required move.
