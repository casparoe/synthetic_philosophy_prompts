After a long argument with a friend about whether illusionism about phenomenal consciousness collapses under its own ethical weight, I am preparing interview questions for a podcast guest who defends affective illusionism: the view that sensory phenomenology—colors, sounds, textures—is genuinely real and introspectively available, but affective phenomenology—the intrinsic badness of pain or the intrinsic goodness of pleasure—is a cognitive illusion generated by evaluative framing, with no corresponding qualitative property. I need a focused sequence that moves from clarification to sustained pressure. First, ask how she draws the sensory-affective boundary without smuggling in the very qualitative distinctions she denies exist. Second, ask whether, on her view, the moral urgency of alleviating suffering becomes a kind of category error, since there is no intrinsic badness to eliminate, only a misattributed evaluation. Third, anticipate her standard reply that this illusion is functionally indispensable for motivation and survival, and include a sharp follow-up asking what specific introspective or neurocognitive finding could falsify that functional story rather than merely redescribe it. Fourth, ask what evidence—introspective rather than theoretical—could genuinely count against affective illusionism, given that she permits sensory phenomenology to be revised by science but insists affective illusion is immune to parallel correction. Please avoid generic biography; every question should turn on the actual content of the position, and end with one that asks what it would take for her to concede that affective phenomenology is real after all.
