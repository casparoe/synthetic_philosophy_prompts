Me and some friends in a history-adjacent reading group have been stuck for like a year on whether Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung is actually comparable to how Japan, Turkey, or the American South deal with their pasts. We all know the usual examples -- Stolpersteine, Yasukuni shrine controversies, Lost Cause monuments being pulled down -- but the arguement never moves. One camp says Germany 'worked through' its past because it built institutional memory; another says it just replaced one narrative with another and the emotional work never happened; someone else keeps bringing up Turkey's denial of the Armenian genocide as proof that legal frameworks don't matter without civil society pressure. I think the stall might be that we're using 'memory culture' to cover three different things -- state archives, public monuments, and private family transmission -- and arguing about them interchangeably. But maybe I'm wrong and it's actually a normative disagreement about whether societies have a duty to remember that can't be resolved by sorting concepts. What's the actual structure of this impasse? Is it verbal, because 'Vergangenheitsbewältigung' doesn't travel across these contexts? Is it empirical, hiding behind vague claims about 'healing' that nobody measures? Or are we just avoiding the fact that comparing Germany to Turkey involves comparing democratic reckoning regimes with denial regimes, and nobody wants to admit that's a category error? And if you think it's conceptual confusion, what distinction -- maybe between commemorative infastructure and affective internalization -- would actually unstick us?
