I'm working out my commitments on diachronic rationality and money-pump vulnerability, and I want to know whether they hang together or force me to abandon one.

Here are my verdicts:
(i) Cyclic strict preferences within a single framing—A over B, B over C, C over A—are irrational if an adversary can chain trades and extract value; the correct response is to eliminate the cycle.
(ii) If preferences cycle only across mental states (calm versus anxious) and each state is internally transitive, with no single state facing the full chain, then there is no practical pump and the context-dependence is not a rational failure.
(iii) A preference reversal between A and B from Monday to Tuesday, with no new information and driven solely by an unanticipated mood shift, is diachronically irrational—parallel to changing a belief without evidence.
(iv) But if on Monday I foresee Tuesday's shift and bind myself to A through an irrevocable commitment, that self-binding is rational management, not irrational rigidity.
(v) Hyperbolic discounting—preferring $100 now to $110 in one week, yet preferring $110 in 53 weeks to $100 in 52 weeks—strikes me as irrational because it permits an intertemporal money pump; nevertheless, I think it can be rational to revise one's discount rate upon learning about one's own impatience, even when that revision reinstates a hyperbolic pattern.

The tension I feel is between (ii) and (iii): if context-dependence excuses cycles in (ii), why doesn't an unanticipated mood shift in (iii) count as entering a new legitimate context? And in (v), does allowing preference-revision undermine the condemnation of hyperbolic patterns?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on whether a coherence failure lies within the agent's epistemic reach and can be exploited without crossing contexts—that saves (i) through (iv) but forces me to drop either the condemnation in (v) or the permission to revise? State the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and apply it to this further case: an agent who applies different risk-weightings when deciding for herself versus for her child, knows the two frames can be chained by an adversary, but claims she never faces both frames in a single deliberative episode.
