I'm leading a 75-minute seminar for twelve advanced undergraduate philosophy students who have just worked through the standard situationist literature—Doris on the fragmentation of character, Harman's argument that global traits are empirically unsupported, and the classic Milgram and prison-study results. The difficulty is that the students consistently reduce situationism to a psychological claim about weakness of will or self-deception, missing the deeper explanatory challenge: that virtue terms like courage or integrity might actually be tracking regularities in situations rather than stable properties of agents. To push past this, I want an in-class activity, not a lecture, that makes them feel the distinction in real time. I've also been struck by a structural parallel to the frame problem in artificial intelligence—just as an acting system must determine which representations are relevant to update without exhaustive search, a virtue ethicist must determine which features of a situation are morally relevant to the exercise of a virtue without already possessing the practical wisdom being tested. I want the game to stage this "frame problem for practical wisdom" directly.

Please design a runnable classroom simulation with explicit rules, assigned roles, and small real stakes—points, temporary speaking privileges, or public commitments, but no actual money—plus a hidden institutional architecture that shifts situational incentives without announcing the shifts. Participants should be assigned what they take to be stable character roles (for example, the honest broker, the loyal mediator, the courageous dissenter) and then placed in a structured deliberation where the criteria for earning credit change based on collective behavior they cannot fully observe. They should only discover afterward which actions were actually being rewarded, then be required to justify their choices using virtue-language. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between character-based and situation-based explanation; it should be frank about which aspect of the situationist critique this reproduces—the misattribution of situational control to agentic disposition—and which it necessarily cannot reproduce, specifically the long-term longitudinal stability data. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially the risk that students will interpret the hidden rule shifts as facilitator deception rather than as the philosophical point. Write the response in American English, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want only the activity design, rules, debrief structure, and failure-mode analysis.
