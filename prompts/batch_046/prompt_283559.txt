Write a closed-book, 45-minute final-exam essay question (25 marks) for an upper-level seminar on hedonism and well-being, focused on the heterogeneity problem: what, if anything, do all pleasures share? Do not allow answers that merely summarize standard objections. Instead, frame the question around a constructed distinction between a phenomenal-uniformity response (pleasures share one intrinsic qualitative feel) and a functional-equivalence response (pleasures share only a common causal and behavioral role), and require students to apply both to a comparative case: a bodily pleasure with rich sensory and intentional content (e.g., tasting food) versus an objectless pleasure produced by direct cortical stimulation that lacks both sensory modality and intentional object. The prompt should force students to defend one response and reject the other in explaining whether hedonism can treat both as instances of the same basic good. Provide the exam question, a model answer that takes a committed position—clearly stating whether hedonism survives and on exactly which grounds—and a marking scheme. The marking scheme must identify the specific moves required for top marks (e.g., showing why family-resemblance accounts cannot support quantitative well-being comparisons, or explaining why functional equivalence risks collapsing hedonism into preference-satisfaction) and the specific errors that should lose marks (e.g., assuming without argument that neural-stimulation pleasure is phenomenally identical to bodily pleasure, or confusing heterogeneity with the problem of false pleasures). Request a committed response with reasons rather than a balanced survey. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
