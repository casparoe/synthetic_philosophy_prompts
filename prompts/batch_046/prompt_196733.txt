In the course of composing a long historical novel, I have become preoccupied with a structural feature that seems to lie just beyond the vocabulary of craft. My manuscript contains a fully realized narrative path—call it Path A—while my private outlines preserve an equally coherent alternative, Path B, which diverges at a single decision and is never mentioned, alluded to, or contradicted within the text itself. What strikes me is not merely that I, the author, can imagine Path B, but that Path B appears to exert a kind of negative pressure on Path A: characters behave as if they are constrained by possibilities they do not enact, and attentive readers report sensing an unresolved "shadow" trajectory that the novel never explicitly acknowledges. I have observed analogous configurations in other fictions, where unrealized but internally consistent plot-lines seem to possess an ontological weight that shapes psychology and interpretation without ever becoming part of the represented world. Does this pattern—where non-actual yet coherent narrative possibilities appear to constrain and inform an actual fictional world—have an established name in philosophy or literary theory? I am particularly interested in whether it is treated as a variant of counterfactual reasoning about fiction, or whether it connects to broader metaphysical claims about the reality of unrealized possibilities. If there is no precise term, I would want to know how to draw its boundaries: must the phantom path be internally consistent with the actual narrative, or is mere authorial availability sufficient? And does the phenomenon depend on the reader's awareness, or can it operate entirely within the implicit architecture of the text?
