My friend and I just spent the evening arguing over It's a Wonderful Life: she claims George Bailey is the perfect altruist, while I think the film is accidentally Randian. Read the film through Rand's critique of altruism—specifically her claim that altruism demands self-sacrifice as a moral ideal, and her distinction between benevolence and what she called the sanction of the victim—and tell me whether George's choices illustrate one or the other. The narrative rewards every renunciation: he abandons travel and education to save the Building and Loan, and in return the town saves him from ruin, Harry saves soldiers, and Clarence pronounces him the richest man in town. Does this structure show that George's sacrifices were actually long-term investments in his own flourishing, or does it smuggle altruism back in by treating those returns as contingent moral gifts rather than earned outcomes? I want a reading that takes both the plot mechanics—the bank-run scene, the counterfactual Pottersville sequence, the angel's economic bookkeeping—and Rand's ethics seriously, and says where the film genuinely resists her framework and where she would likely dismiss the angel's ledger as a fraud.
