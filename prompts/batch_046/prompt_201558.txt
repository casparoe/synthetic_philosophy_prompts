Analyze the 2016 Brexit referendum as a case study in the democratic boundary problem — who belongs to the demos when the vote itself rewrites the rules of membership. The franchise included UK citizens, Irish and Commonwealth residents over 18, and overseas Brits who'd left within the last 15 years; it exlcuded EU citizens living in Britain, long-term expats past that cutoff, and 16-17 year-olds who'd been allowed to vote in Scotland's 2014 independence referendum.

Apply the all-affected-interests principle and the all-subjected-to-coercion principle: who should have been included, and which groups fall through the cracks of one but not the other? Then try a twist I just made up called the 'exit-cost asymmetry rule' — if it's harder for you to leave the jurisdiction after the decision than for the median voter, that strengthens your claim to be in the demos. Does that change the analysis for EU citizens or for the expats?

Say what the actual voter roll reveals about which principle the UK implicitly used, what the episode explains well and what it misdescribes (for instance, the Scottish teenagers), and whether Brexit is an argument for or against letting affected non-citizens vote. Where does the theory stay silent?
