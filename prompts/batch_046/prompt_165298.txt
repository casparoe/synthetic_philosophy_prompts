I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar in philosophy of science focused on conceptual change and scientific revolutions, and I also serve as an editorial assistant for our university's interdisciplinary undergraduate research journal, which receives submissions from biology, sociology, economics, and history of science. The editorial board has asked me to design a reusable evaluation procedure—something I can apply in about fifteen minutes per manuscript—to determine whether a submission's central argument operates within the shared conceptual framework of its home discipline, represents an anomaly that the framework can eventually accommodate, or instead relies on terms and standards that are incommensurable with that framework in a way that makes conventional peer review impossible. The procedure should translate the theoretical distinctions among normal science, crisis, and incommensurability into concrete, ordered steps that a non-expert can execute without having mastered every subfield, but it must also mark precisely where judgment is unavoidable—where I am essentially making a discretionary call disguised as a formal test. I need it to specify the hard cases the procedure must handle: for example, a neuroscience paper that replaces null-hypothesis significance testing with predictive accuracy metrics, a sociology manuscript that applies evolutionary game theory to kinship structures without redefining its core variables, and a history of science article claiming that a canonical experiment was systematically misread because its original practitioners used a taxonomic scheme that no longer exists. For each of these, I want you to walk through how the procedure classifies the submission, including at least one case where the result is one I will find unwelcome—perhaps labeling an innovative but methodologically heterodox paper as incommensurable and therefore outside the journal's current review capacity. The answer must also state explicitly what the procedure cannot accomplish, such as predicting whether an anomaly will actually trigger a revolution, resolving empirical disputes, or substituting for disciplinary expertise, and it should acknowledge that any recommendation I produce requires sign-off from the faculty advisor who oversees the board. Please present everything in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written as you would in a long letter or email, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
