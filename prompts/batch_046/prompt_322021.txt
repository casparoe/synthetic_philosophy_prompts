Since my father’s death three months ago, I have caught myself constructing reasons to believe that consciousness survives bodily death—reasons that have nothing to do with evidence and everything to do with endurance. This has returned me to the wrong-kind-reasons problem in belief, but with a twist I cannot shake: pragmatic encroachment suggests that when stakes are existential, practical considerations do not merely tempt belief but may actually lower the threshold for what counts as sufficient evidence. I want a poem that holds these two thoughts—corruption versus encroachment—without resolving them.

Write a Petrarchan sonnet in strict iambic pentameter from the voice of this mourner. In the octave (ABBA ABBA), present the practical case for belief in survival: not as evidence, but as a life-raft that feels almost like proof. At the volta in line 9, pivot sharply: the speaker realizes that high stakes do not only supply wrong-kind reasons but may restructure what “enough” means, so that the therapeutic need is not merely beside the point but changes the point. The sestet (CDECDE) should leave the distinction between corrupted belief and legitimately lowered epistemic thresholds suspended, ending on an ambiguity rather than a verdict. Let the enclosed rhyme of the octave echo circular self-justification, and the sestet’s shifting pattern echo an unresolved epistemic question.

After the poem, include a single section titled “Commentary on Form and Philosophical Work” (approximately 150–200 words). In it, explain precisely how the volta, the rhyme architecture, and the meter perform conceptual labor—particularly how they keep wrong-kind reasons and pragmatic encroachment distinct without collapsing them into one another. The poem should be accurate enough that someone working in epistemology recognizes both positions; the commentary should make the structural choices explicit.
