I am drafting a take-home essay exam for an advanced undergraduate or early graduate seminar on interpretation, formal systems, and the philosophy of meaning. The exam should center on the three-layer model of message interpretation—frame messages that establish an interpretive context, outer messages that present explicit syntactic or propositional content, and inner messages whose semantic activation depends on being decoded within the frame set by the other two layers—familiar from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. I want to avoid simple taxonomy-recitation, so embed the framework in a self-contained, hypothetical case that students have not seen before.

Construct the following scenario for the exam: Two automated verification agents, Alpha and Beta, share a nested communication protocol. Layer 1 (the frame message) specifies that all symbols received thereafter are to be interpreted as meta-logical commentary on a particular formal system S, and that system S is defined exclusively within this frame. Layer 2 (the outer message) contains the explicit syntactic instruction REJECT(p), with no internal reference to S. Layer 3 (the inner message) contains the content p is valid in S, but the validity conditions for p in S are only available inside Layer 1. A third party, Gamma, receives Layers 2 and 3 intact but fails to parse Layer 1 due to an encoding error. An external observer, Delta, can access all three layers but has no knowledge of the designers' intentions or of system S outside the protocol.

Write an exam question that asks students to do three things: (i) determine where the meaning of the exchange resides for Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta respectively; (ii) defend a reasoned position on whether meaning in this case is frame-relative, syntactically fixed, or constructible only through the integration of all three layers; and (iii) respond to the objection that the outer message alone—REJECT(p)—carries a complete, frame-independent meaning that makes Layer 1 redundant.

The exam should be designed for 60 minutes, with answers expected in 800–1000 words. Provide:
1. The full exam question, including the scenario and the three sub-questions, written in formal exam language.
2. A model answer of approximately 600 words. The answer must apply the frame/outer/inner distinction precisely to the protocol breakdown, take a clear position on frame-relativity, address all four perspectives (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta), and handle the redundancy objection without merely summarizing Hofstadter's original discussion.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 points, listing five specific analytical moves that distinguish top answers. For each move, assign marks (so the total is 20), state the specific misconception or omission that typically costs points, and include a brief note on how an A answer differs from a B answer. The scheme should identify at least one subtle error—such as conflating the outer message's syntactic form with its semantic force, or treating Gamma's partial access as equivalent to Delta's full access without accounting for the missing frame—that a hasty grader might miss.

Constraints: The question and answer must be fully self-contained. Do not ask students for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to Gödel, Escher, Bach. Do not include verbatim quotations from any real text; paraphrase the three-layer framework without quotation marks. Do not rely on obscure formal theorems or highly specialized results that a student could not be expected to derive or recall without the source text; the difficulty should come from conceptual analysis of layered interpretation in the invented protocol, not from memorized content.
