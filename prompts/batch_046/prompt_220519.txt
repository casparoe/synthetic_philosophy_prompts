In my philosophy of science seminar we have spent the better part of a term circling the same point about Karl Popper's appraisal of evolutionary biology, and the discussion has stalled in a way that feels structural rather than informational. One camp holds that Popper's 1978 retraction of his earlier 1974 charge—that Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme—was decisive: they point to quantitative predictions about allele-frequency shifts, to experimental evolution with microbes, and to the risky forecasts made by adaptive dynamics, arguing that evolutionary biology now meets any reasonable falsifiability criterion. The other camp agrees that Popper withdrew the tautology objection but insists he never resolved the deeper immunity problem: given that evolutionary narratives can always be reconstructed post hoc by invoking hidden selection pressures, drift, niche construction, or unknown environmental variables, the theory never genuinely risks refutation at the level of specific macroevolutionary pathways. Both sides have read the same secondary literature and work with the same canonical examples—industrial melanism, antibiotic resistance—so the deadlock does not seem to come from ignorance of biology or of Popper's texts.

My own suspicion is that the disagreement is not really about Popper's biography or about evolutionary biology at all, but about what kind of predictive risk a historical science owes us, and specifically whether risk must be lineage-specific—forecasting which particular clade will emerge—or whether population-level statistical predictions about relative fitness are sufficient. To sharpen the issue, consider a hypothetical constraint-first evolutionary model in which physical laws of development channel all morphological variation so narrowly that differential survival becomes purely epiphenomenal: the model predicts no particular adaptive outcome, only that whatever survives will fall within a physically permitted morphological space. Would Popper's framework classify this as scientific if it made precise quantitative predictions about that permitted space, even though it made no selectionist forecasts? I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication: is our stall verbal—hinging on an ambiguity in what counts as falsifiable—substantive—reflecting a real disagreement about evidentiary standards for sciences with unrecoverable initial conditions—or are we answering different questions under one heading, with one side asking whether evolutionary theory is normative science in Popper's sense and the other asking whether it behaves like physics? If the diagnosis involves a hidden cross-purpose disagreement, is there a conceptual distinction—say, between selectionist explanations that invoke differential survival and constraint-based explanations that invoke developmental physics, or between retrodictive coherence and predictive risk—that could actually move the discussion, or should we expect permanent stalemate because our bedrock intuitions about what science owes us diverge?
