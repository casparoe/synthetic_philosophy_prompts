I've noticed a strange asymmetry in how people defend introspective access, and I'm not sure whether it has a specific name. When asked to report on very basic, immediate mental states—say, the exact qualitative character of a headache or the precise hue of a mental afterimage—subjects often hesitate, revise their descriptions, or admit that framing changes the report. By contrast, when they report on highly structured mental states—such as the ranked reasons behind a moral judgment or the narrative arc of their career ambitions—they speak with marked confidence and consistency, and they tend to treat these complex reports as the clearest examples of successful introspection. Critics then point to confabulation and post-hoc rationalization in these very complex cases to argue that introspection is unreliable, while defenders reply that the basic-state hesitations merely show introspection has a limited scope, not that it is systematically flawed; they cite the stability of the complex reports as evidence that introspection works well within its proper domain. The result is an inversion: the mental states most shaped by interpretation, language, and social framing become the benchmark for introspective reliability, while the states closest to raw experience are treated as peripheral edge cases where introspection is expected to be noisy. Is there an established term for this specific reversal—where conceptually mediated self-reports are taken as the paradigm of introspective success? How exactly does it differ from standard claims about introspective transparency or about the limits of self-knowledge? And if there is no established term, how should we delineate this pattern from ordinary debates about whether introspection is generally reliable?
