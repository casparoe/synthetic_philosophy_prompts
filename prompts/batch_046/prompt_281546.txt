I am drafting an essay for a small literary-philosophical review, and I would value your comments on its conceptual architecture before I attempt the full prose. The piece takes up sphexishness—the stereotyped, loop-like behavior of the digger wasp Sphex, which will repeatedly check and re-check its burrow if disturbed, as if running a fixed subroutine rather than responding to changed circumstances. I want to use this not merely as a biological anecdote about mechanical rigidity, but to press a distinction I have not seen developed clearly in the literature: between first-order sphexishness, the repetition of an action despite its futility, and second-order sphexishness, the repetition of an interpretive frame despite contradictory evidence. The wasp, I suggest, exhibits only the first; its rigidity is motor, not hermeneutic. Human agents, by contrast, may be flexible in action—capable of altering behavior—while remaining sphexish at the second order, rationalizing away anomalies to preserve a narrative of autonomy. From this I want to draw a tentative conclusion about free will: that the philosophically interesting failure is not the inability to do otherwise in a single instance, but the inability to revise the framework within which "otherwise" is even conceived. I am concerned that this collapses into a trivial claim about cognitive bias, or that I misrepresent the entomological evidence by attributing too little representational capacity to the wasp. Could you comment on whether the first-order and second-order distinction holds conceptual water, whether the move from wasp rigidity to human hermeneutic rigidity is more metaphor than argument, and whether there is a more precise way to state what free will requires if we separate behavioral flexibility from framework revision?
