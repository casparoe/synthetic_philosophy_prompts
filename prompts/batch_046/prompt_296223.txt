I'm working through some older writings on craft ontology for a project on how conceptual boundaries slip in small, technical domains, and I've gotten stuck on a passage from an 1890s treatise on decorative ceramics. The author distinguishes two ways a workshop might reproduce a celebrated vase: what he calls mimetic echo, where the surface appearance—glaze, silhouette, ornament—is copied without grasping the underlying chemical and firing process, and structural imitation, where the technical formula is reproduced but applied to an altered form. He then argues that praising the mimetic echo as a masterpiece involves a kind of conceptual misplacement: the criterion of authentic craft is being pulled from its proper domain—technical mastery and material understanding—into the domain of visual resemblance, so that the copycat object is being evaluated by the wrong conceptual ruler. He adds that in workshop culture these concepts don't stay fixed; they travel tacitly between artisans, which means the boundary between original and copy is unstable not because people disagree, but because the concept itself shifts its conditions of application as it moves.

I'm not sure whether he is making a normative claim—that there is a correct, stable domain for authentic craft and the error is applying it elsewhere—or a descriptive claim about conceptual fluidity, that the concept naturally slides between domains and the misplacement is just what it looks like when we try to freeze it. Could someone help me parse this? Specifically, does conceptual misplacement imply that concepts have proper homes, or that they are inherently copycat-like, replicating across contexts in ways that destabilize any fixed taxonomy? And how should I understand his remark about tacit transmission—does he mean that conceptual slippage in microdomains happens through practice rather than explicit definition, making the copycat phenomenon a feature of how ideas move rather than a failure of originality?
