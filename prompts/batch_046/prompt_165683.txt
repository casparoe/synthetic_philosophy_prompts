Exam question:
Consider the paradox of dogmatism: if a subject S knows that P, then S appears able to know that any apparent counterevidence E is misleading, and therefore S ought to disregard E. In your answer, do three things. First, distinguish the standard regulative formulation of the paradox—which concerns norms guiding rational inquiry—from the Bayesian formulation that appeals to conditionalization failure and maximized priors. Second, explain why the regulative version is sometimes interpreted as collapsing into practical irrationality—an unwillingness to gather information—rather than remaining a purely epistemic defect. Third, evaluate whether adopting a threshold view of knowledge, on which belief counts as knowledge only when its strength exceeds a context-sensitive cutoff, successfully blocks the paradox without committing us to widespread skepticism about ordinary knowledge claims.

Student answer:
The paradox of dogmatism arises when S knows P. Because knowledge is factive, S can infer that any evidence E apparently contradicting P must be misleading, since P is true. This licenses S to ignore E, which conflicts with the norm that rational agents should respond to counterevidence.

The regulative formulation frames the problem in terms of norms for inquiry: knowing P gives S a reason to treat contradictory evidence as deceptive, thereby permitting dogmatic closure. The Bayesian formulation addresses the same issue through probabilistic mechanics—specifically, the failure to update on E because the prior for P is already maximized at 1. Both versions depend on the same core problem, namely that a maximally certain belief blocks revision by counterevidence; they differ mainly in notation, with one using regulative language and the other using the calculus of conditional probabilities.

Regarding the threshold view: if knowledge requires only that belief strength exceed a context-sensitive cutoff—perhaps 0.9 in ordinary contexts and 0.99 in scientific ones—then knowing P never entails absolute certainty. Since the belief remains below 1.0, there is always logical space for counterevidence to reduce confidence. Thus S cannot legitimately conclude that E is misleading; she can only hold that her confidence is high but revisable. This preserves openness to revision without skepticism, because ordinary claims easily meet the lower thresholds, while no belief ever reaches the ceiling needed to dismiss counterevidence. The only significant cost is that we must accept a form of contextualism about knowledge attributions.
