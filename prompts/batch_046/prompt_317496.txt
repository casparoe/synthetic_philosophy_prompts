I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about the boundary problem—who properly belongs to the demos—after we discussed a regional council deciding whether to approve a long-term carbon-capture and geological-storage facility that would draw water from an aquifer shared with a neighboring jurisdiction and hold waste for millennia. My friend kept returning to the principle that “all affected interests deserve representation,” using it not merely to argue for resident voting rights but to insist that democratic legitimacy required formal voice for unborn future generations, for downstream non-citizen communities, and even for the ecological systems whose stability the project threatened. I want to understand exactly what machinery that slogan is running, because it seemed to me that depending on how one parses “affected,” “interests,” and “representation,” the claim either collapses into a tautology about physical causation or explodes into an unworkable mandate.

Could you lay out, as a precise menu, the distinct theses that “all affected interests deserve representation” could actually be expressing? For each reading—distinguishing, for instance, between causal-impact accounts, risk-probability accounts, moral-patienthood accounts, and functional-stakeholder accounts—please indicate whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and specify which version of “representation” (proxy voting, weighted influence, deliberative presence, or symbolic recognition) it actually requires. Crucially, I need to know which of these precise formulations genuinely support extending democratic membership or voice to future generations and to non-human ecological entities, and which ones merely sound radical while applying only to present, sentient, human agents with identifiable preferences. In other words, map the slogan’s ambiguity so I can take the result back to my friend and show her exactly where her argument stands or falls.
