I keep encountering a pattern in disputes over institutional authority that I cannot place precisely within the philosophy of recognition. One group demands to be acknowledged as an equal co-author of shared norms; the other responds by offering a form of recognition that is structurally subordinate—inviting the first group to advise or consult, while retaining unilateral authority over the final framing. The first group rejects this as failing to acknowledge equality, because the gesture of inclusion simultaneously reasserts the hierarchy it purports to dissolve. Rather than breaking the relationship, both sides enter a stable equilibrium: they remain materially interdependent, the demand for parity is never withdrawn, and the offer of consultative status is never withdrawn, yet neither party risks open rupture. The dynamic echoes Hegel's account of lordship and bondage, but here there is no life-and-death struggle, no transformative labor, and no eventual reversal—only an indefinite suspension. Is there an established concept for this specific stalemate, in which recognition is continuously proffered in a form that reinforces the asymmetry it addresses? If the literature does not name it exactly, how should it be carved away from the dialectic of lordship and bondage, and from ordinary political compromise?
