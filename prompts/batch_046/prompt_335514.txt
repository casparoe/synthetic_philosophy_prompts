I am preparing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of death, and I want to begin not with the familiar brain-versus-heart dichotomy but with the specification of adequacy itself. Write out the desiderata that a philosophical theory of human death must meet if it is to adjudicate between brain-death and circulatory-death criteria without merely legislating for one medical convention. For each desideratum, state the case or conceptual consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp.

In particular, the theory must:

(i) account for the divergence between total brain failure (including brainstem) and irreversible cessation of circulatory-respiratory function, explaining whether these are two measures of one phenomenon or genuinely distinct events;

(ii) distinguish the biological death of the human organism from the death of the person, and say whether they can come apart in cases of prolonged somatic maintenance—such as brain-dead bodies sustained for gestational support—without collapsing into substance dualism;

(iii) address what I shall call the problem of distributed vitality: when cardiac, pulmonary, and metabolic homeostasis are technologically maintained in the absence of central neural integration, does the organism persist as an integrated living whole, or has vitality become merely distributed across machines and tissue?

(iv) accommodate the integration thesis (the loss of the organism’s anti-entropic unity) alongside vitalist objections that isolated reflexes or cellular activity do not constitute life, without reducing death to a purely legal or institutional status;

(v) explain whether death is an instantaneous boundary or an extended process, and what that implies for the epistemic and ethical authority of declaring it at a specific moment;

(vi) handle edge cases—anencephalic infants, decorticate states with isolated brainstem function, and successful resuscitation after temporary circulatory arrest—without ad hoc adjustments;

(vii) satisfy a normative condition: it must explain why death matters to the one who dies, or to those left behind, without presupposing an immortal soul or reducing the concern to mere social utility.

Then, briefly, indicate whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether treating death as an instantaneous event can be reconciled with both the integration thesis and the accommodation of prolonged somatic maintenance. Finally, score the main existing positions—the whole-brain integration view, the higher-brain/personhood view, and the traditional circulatory-respiratory view—against your list, keeping the weight on the criteria rather than on the scorecard. Write the answer in British English, and avoid any bibliographic references or reading lists; the task is conceptual specification, not scholarly citation.
