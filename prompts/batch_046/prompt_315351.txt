Construct a thought experiment, confined to one paragraph and set in a realistic setting, that isolates whether the existence of a monetary debt depends on mental stability or on sustained social practice. The scenario should take place in a small, isolated trading community that has adopted a strict immaterialist ontology of the Berkeleyan kind: there are no physical coins, notes, or written ledgers; all obligations exist solely as shared ideas sustained by collective perception and spoken agreement. In this community, a merchant extends credit to a fisherman for nets and provisions, with the debt of exactly twelve units witnessed only by the merchant, the fisherman, and three other villagers. Now engineer the perturbation: after a storm disrupts communication, the three witnesses genuinely come to hold different ideas about the amount—some recall eight, others fifteen—while the merchant and fisherman remain divided, and there is no external object or inscription to settle the matter. Design the case so that Berkeley's account of money—as a representative token of actual labour and goods rather than being wealth itself—delivers the verdict that the obligation dissolves once the shared idea fragments, because without a stable mental representation anchored to real commodities there is nothing in which the debt can consist. By contrast, design it so that a modern functionalist or institutional account of money delivers the opposite verdict: the debt persists as long as the social rule of recognition is in principle maintainable, even if current mental states diverge. After presenting the scenario, do the check: state precisely the claim at issue (that a debt of twelve units remains), the verification method available within the world (mutual perception and verbal testimony only), the relevant nearby worlds (one in which all five minds align, one in which only the merchant retains the original idea), and explain why each theory returns its verdict. Then identify the feature doing the work—the total absence of any non-mental substrate for the record—and explain what happens if that feature is removed: if even a single physical token or inscribed tally were present, Berkeley's view could treat it as a stable idea-object and the divergence between the two theories would collapse. Finish by noting what the case is for—testing whether immaterialism about objects forces an immaterialism about institutional facts that makes them too fragile—and by stating how a functionalist defender would attack the scenario: they would likely argue that Berkeley has smuggled in a physicalist criterion of stability, and that collective misremembering is merely an epistemic failure of record-keeping rather than an ontological dissolution of the obligation. Keep the prompt formal and polished, write in British English, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts by title or date.
