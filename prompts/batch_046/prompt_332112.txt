I'm presenting next Thursday in a philosophy-of-language graduate seminar to defend a thesis about strategic ambiguity in Grice's framework. My claim is that the distinction between what is said and what is implicated collapses in cases where a speaker deliberately utters a proposition p that both parties recognize as literally false or conversationally irrelevant, and relies on that very recognition of failure as the primary vehicle for conveying an implicature q. I argue that here the speaker's communicative intention targets q through the audience's awareness that p is not meant, so recovery is not cooperative inference from a said content but a reflexive cancellation that Grice's account cannot accommodate without collapsing saying into implicating. The audience will be two senior Grice scholars who teach the canonical cooperative-principle literature, three relevance theorists in the Sperber-Wilson tradition who reject Gricean maxims in favor of cognitive-effort optimization, and two experimental pragmatists who will ask for processing predictions. Prepare my Q&A defense sheet: list the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased in the vocabulary each camp uses, with what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and the weakest structural point in my argument—the boundary between strategic ambiguity and ordinary irony or metaphor—that I should defend actively rather than hope goes unnoticed. Include the specific objection I am dreading, likely from the relevance theorists, that these cases are already covered by Grice's notion of making as if to say. Finally, state explicitly what argument or evidence would change your assessment—whether demonstrating that strategic ambiguity reduces to standard non-literal use, or showing that audiences still process these utterances through cooperative inference—and identify the part of your reasoning that is most vulnerable to collapse.
