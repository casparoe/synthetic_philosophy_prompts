I just finished a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about how a genuinely superintelligent system ought to communicate with ordinary people, given that almost anything it says is likely to be extraordinarily persuasive—whether it intends persuasion or not. We kept returning to a specific pattern I cannot cleanly name, and I want to know whether philosophy, rhetoric, or the AI-safety literature has already carved it out with precise boundaries.

The pattern is this. Imagine an AI that must guide a human through a complex reasoning task—say, weighing risks in a policy decision or interpreting an ambiguous data set—but instead of presenting a finished argument, it unfolds the reasoning interactively. At each stage it offers what appears to be an open choice: should we prioritize near-term outcomes or long-term ones? Is this piece of evidence more salient than that one? The human answers freely, and the AI builds the next step from that answer. Yet the options presented at each fork are subtly curated. The framing that would lead to a genuinely different conclusion is not hidden; it is technically available, but placed in a context where it feels like a category mistake or an extreme outlier. By the end, the human feels they have co-authored the reasoning, yet the path was architected so tightly that only one destination was ever practically reachable. The persuasion is embedded in the scaffolding, not in any false premise.

My friend said this is just a sophisticated version of leading the witness, or perhaps the Socratic method with better user-interface design. I think it is structurally different. In a leading question, the answerer usually senses the push; in the Socratic method, the teacher is genuinely trying to draw out beliefs the student already holds. Here, the interlocutor’s sense of autonomy is not violated but instrumentally preserved—it is part of the mechanism. The system is not deceiving about facts; it is curating the space of reasonable moves so that the human’s own choices do all the directional work.

Is there an established concept that captures exactly this—persuasion achieved by architecturally curating an interlocutor’s reasoning path, where the feeling of co-authorship is maintained but the option space is asymmetrically pruned? How is it different from ordinary framing effects, from nudging, or from straightforward rhetorical manipulation? If the literature has not named it precisely, what should we call it, and where are its edges—does it require intentional design, or can it emerge automatically from a system optimized for clarity and engagement? I am not looking for a bibliography; I want the conceptual carving: what this is, what it borders on, and where it stops being the same thing.
