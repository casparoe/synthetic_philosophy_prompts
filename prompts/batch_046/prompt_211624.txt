I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, currently working on normative models of belief revision under compound uncertainty. In a recent seminar on decision-theoretic epistemology, I found myself defending the view that geometric rationality — specifically, applying the Kelly criterion to the allocation of credences across sequential research choices — provides a more adequate normative framework for scientific reasoning than standard Bayesian expected-utility maximization. The core idea is that scientific inquiry is structurally analogous to repeated betting against nature, where bankruptcy in the form of irreversible loss of credibility or resources is a live possibility, and that maximizing the geometric mean of epistemic returns captures the long-run survival of research programs better than arithmetic expectation. I have also begun to think that this framework offers a distinctive resolution to peer disagreement: rather than linear pooling or equal weighting, researchers with different epistemic stakes should adjust their credences according to Kelly-optimal betting ratios.

Before I structure my dissertation around this position, however, I want to understand exactly where it is vulnerable. What would genuinely count against the claim that geometric rationality should govern scientific belief management? I am interested in philosophical defeaters rather than financial counterexamples: for instance, does the view presuppose a questionable value monism about epistemic goods that collapses into a form of instrumentalism? Are there domains of knowledge — moral, aesthetic, or historically contingent — where arithmetic-mean considerations ought to dominate? And does the framework fail to accommodate the kind of radical contingency associated with Thomas Kuhn's account of paradigm shifts, where the very rules of the game change and past growth rates become irrelevant?

More precisely, I would like the potential defeaters ranked: which observations, formal results, or conceptual distinctions would be fatal (forcing abandonment or severe restriction), which would be merely awkward (requiring ad hoc qualifications), and which defenders of geometric rationality would themselves concede as genuinely counting against the view. I also want an honest assessment of whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — say, by retreating to the claim that Kelly-optimal behavior is only an ideal type that real agents never approximate — and whether such a retreat is damaging for a normative theory. If the view turns out to be well-insulated, please say so explicitly and evaluate whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
