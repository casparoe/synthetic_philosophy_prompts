Examination question: Alan Turing proposed that, rather than attempting to program an adult intelligence directly, we ought to build a machine with approximately the mental capacity of a child and then educate it. In your response, (i) distinguish the process Turing calls education from direct programming or instruction; (ii) explain the role he assigns to rewards and punishments within that process; and (iii) address whether he thinks the initial machine must possess some minimal innate architecture or whether it can begin as an entirely unorganized system. Conclude by assessing whether his educational model is essentially a behaviorist reinforcement scheme, or whether the universal computing substrate of the machine plays an active structural role in determining what can be learned.

Student answer: Turing's distinction between education and programming is central to his well-known 1950 essay on computing machinery and intelligence. Programming corresponds to writing explicit instructions for a universal computing machine, whereas education is analogous to raising a child: we supply experience, feedback, and environmental interaction rather than predetermined internal rules. Turing argues that we should begin with a child-machine that is essentially a blank slate—an entirely unorganized system lacking any pre-existing architecture—because any built-in structure would prejudice the outcome and prevent the emergence of genuine intelligence. The teacher functions as a source of positive and negative reinforcement, administering rewards and punishments so that correct responses are strengthened and incorrect ones are suppressed. Over time, these external stimuli shape behavior until adult-level competence is reached. Because the initial substrate is unorganized, the universal computing capacity is merely a passive container; it contributes nothing active to learning except the storage of associations formed by reinforcement. The internal state of the machine is therefore irrelevant except as a record of past rewards and punishments. It follows that Turing's model is thoroughly behaviorist: all knowledge originates in the environment, direct programming should be abandoned altogether in favor of pure experiential conditioning, and the machine's innate computing power plays no guiding role in what it can eventually understand.
