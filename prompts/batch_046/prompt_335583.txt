I'm running a graduate seminar on moral psychology, and for several weeks we've been circling the same stalemate without anyone changing position. The comparative literature on WEIRD versus non-WEIRD populations consistently shows that people in different cultural contexts weight harm, purity, loyalty, and authority in markedly different ways. One faction in the group treats this divergence as a direct challenge to moral objectivity: if the weightings vary systematically with culture, they argue, then moral judgments cannot be tracking culture-independent truths. The opposing faction treats the variation as superficial parameterization of a single underlying moral faculty, analogous to grammatical differences across languages, and insists that objectivity is preserved at a deeper structural level.

What keeps the discussion from advancing is a specific pattern that neither standard response addresses well. In some of the ethnographic cases we've examined, moral evaluation does not merely reorder familiar weights; it seems to apply to an entirely different domain of action. Rather than assessing whether an act causes suffering or violates individual autonomy, members of these communities evaluate whether an act disrupts a particular social equilibrium or restores a relational balance. When I ask the objectivity defenders to account for this, they tend to reinterpret the equilibrium concern as an indirect expression of welfare maximization. When I ask the skeptics, they treat it as proof that the very concept of wrongness is constructed locally. Both moves feel like translations rather than engagements, and the seminar hasn't moved past them.

My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not empirical or anthropological at all, but conceptual: the two sides are operating with different criteria for what "objectivity" requires—identical surface judgments, identical underlying reasons, or merely non-arbitrary standards internal to a practice. Is that diagnosis accurate, or is there a hidden empirical question about whether these communities genuinely lack harm-based concepts, or a hidden formal question about whether moral evaluation can be genuinely domain-restricted without collapsing into arbitrariness? And if your diagnosis is correct, what would actually count as progress—a precise formal model of moral grammar, a specifically designed cross-cultural experiment, or a conceptual distinction that clarifies what we are asking objectivity to do?
