I was chatting with two mates from my archaeology course after a seminar on material culture, and they got into a proper row over a hypothetical site our tutor described — not a real excavation, just a thought experiment. The scenario was a pit near what might be a house wall, filled with broken pottery sherds from three different ceramic traditions, a handful of charred barley grains, and deer bones that had been split for marrow. Friend A said this was clearly a structured deposit marking cyclical renewal: mixing old and new vessels, burning seeds, and adding animal remains to "feed" the ground. Friend B reckoned that was pure romantic projection — the pit was probably just a borrow-pit filled with domestic rubbish and butcher waste, and calling it ritual was falling for the Pompeii premise, where every layer gets interpretated like a frozen snapshot of belief.

Then Friend A pointed out that the barley wasn't just burnt, it was burnt in discrete little clusters with clean spaces between them, which wouldn't happen in normal cooking or random dumping. Friend B said that depends on fuel stacking and wind ventilation through the pit, so the pattern is just taphonomic noise rather than symbolic grammar; he also said if you did refitting analysis on the sherds you'd probably find they came from vessels broken elsewhere and dumped together, not ritually smashed in place. Friend A came back with the orientation: the pit's long axis lined up within a few degrees of the winter solstice sunrise over the local ridge, which in several ethnographic cases is tied to renewal myths. Friend B said that's classic post-hoc reasoning — draw enough axes through a settlement and one hits a solar event by chance, and besides the houses followed the river terrace, so any allignment was probably practical siting being over-interpretated.

They asked me to settle it, but I don't really understand formation processes well enough to call it. Can you go through their exchange move by move? Rate each point from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say whether either is missing something obvious about how archaeologists actually move from broken pots and burnt seeds to claims about belief. Don't just tell me it's ambiguous — I want to know where the reasoning holds and where it slips, and whether there's a principled way to seperate the ritual explanation from the rubbish explanation here.
