As a novelist constructing a manuscript in which an unreliable narrator spends three hundred pages condemning others for precisely the moral failings she conceals, I have been discussing with friends whether hypocrisy truly strips one of the authority to blame or merely weakens its force, and the answers I have received feel too tidy for the ambiguity I require in fiction. I am preparing a proposal for a three-month workshop fellowship, and I need it rendered as a formal document: please begin with the proposal text itself, with no preface or commentary, leading in its very first sentence with the bottom-line claim about the project's viability and why the proposed framing matters.

My half-formed idea is this: the philosophical debate over standing to blame and hypocrisy has stalled because both sides treat "standing" as a single, unitary normative status—either you possess it or you do not—whereas I suspect it is actually a cluster of at least three separable capacities: the entitlement to feel and express moral resentment, the authority to demand that the wrongdoer render an account, and the license to invoke shared moral reasons in public censure. Hypocrisy, on this view, might corrode one capacity without touching the others, yet existing accounts—whether they dismiss hypocrisy as a negligible cosmetic stain or treat it as a total disqualifier—never examine this internal architecture. The proposal should state the precise research question—under what conditions does hypocrisy undermine which component of standing, and is there any irreducible core that survives?—and explain why the unitary framework leaves this open. The method should be realistic and month by month: perhaps month one devoted to conceptual disaggregation of the three capacities using canonical cases of hypocritical blame; month two to constructing paired thought experiments in which the capacities come apart; month three to assessing whether the cluster can be reconstructed into a unified relation or must remain genuinely plural. It must specify what counts as failure—for instance, if the capacities prove conceptually inseparable in every relevant context, or if the cluster model yields no predictive or normative advantage over a contextualized unitary account. It should also defend this decompositional approach against the obvious alternative of retaining a single standing relation and simply adding contextual modifiers. I will supply all bibliographic references myself, so please do not include a literature review, citation list, or reading list.
