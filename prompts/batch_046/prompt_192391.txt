I work in site reliability engineering, and our organization follows the standard blameless postmortem protocol: after an outage, we document the technical chain without assigning personal fault, treating human actions as predictable system failures rather than moral failures. Yet there is a sharp institutional exception. If an engineer manually overrides an automated circuit-breaker—forcing a deployment despite red metrics—the review board treats that override as a genuinely blameworthy choice, even when the engineer’s reasoning is structurally identical to the missing logic in the automation. At the same time, our hiring rubric rewards an “ownership mindset,” which management describes as feeling personally responsible for outcomes largely determined by automated pipelines, opaque third-party dependencies, and organizational constraints that no individual controls.

I am trying to understand what philosophical theory of free will and moral agency makes this combination coherent. Does our practice presuppose that only beings with a particular kind of conscious, uncaused choice—sometimes discussed in philosophy under headings like libertarian freedom—can be proper targets of blame, while automated systems are excluded because they lack that specific capacity? Or could a compatibilist account, where responsibility tracks responsiveness to reasons and appropriate causal control rather than ultimate self-origination, justify blaming the human override while excusing the script? Furthermore, is the practice internally consistent: we deny moral standing to algorithms on the grounds that they lack the capacities relevant to responsibility, yet we demand that human agents exercise those very capacities in contexts where automation severely constrains their control. Reconstruct the implicit theory of free will that our institutional norms assume, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether a framework that asks which varieties of free will are actually worth wanting—one focused on capacities that make a real difference to moral status rather than on metaphysical possibility—would force us to redesign our postmortem rules or our hiring criteria. Finally, say explicitly what would change your mind about this diagnosis, whether a specific argument about the nature of control, a hidden empirical claim about human cognition, or a point where your reconstruction of our practice is most likely to fail.
