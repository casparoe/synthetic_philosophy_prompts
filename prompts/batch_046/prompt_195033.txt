I was in a thread about twin studies the other day and I think I've built myself a subtle contradiction I can't unwind. Everyone keeps saying heritability isn't about individuals, just population variance, but I was trying to formalize what that actually means for prediction. So I assumed that if h² is 0.8 in some cohort, then for each person in that cohort there's an underlying genetic potential G and an enviroment deviation E, with G accounting for 80% of the observed spread and E for the rest, and that equalizing enviroments just shrinks the E variance without changing G. That seems consistent with heritability rising when enviromental variance drops, but it also feels like I'm smuggling in a per-person causal decomposition that the statisticians say doesn't exist. Is there actually a rigorous model—maybe with reaction norms or genotype-enviroment covariance—where you can separate an individual's phenotype into a genetic baseline and an enviromental adjustment, or is the 'no individual heritability' point stronger than that: not just that h² doesn't apply one-to-one, but that the very idea of a fixed genetic share for a specific person is mathematically incoherent? I want to understand where my mental model breaks, not just memorize the slogan.
