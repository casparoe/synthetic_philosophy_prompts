Consider a freight firm that has fully automated its long-haul fleet but retains a human employee in every cab. The worker’s designated role is “ethical oversight”: they sit beside the autonomous system, may press an emergency-stop button, and must sign a digital log attesting that each delivery occurred “under human supervision.” In practice the vehicle steers, navigates, and brakes without input, and the worker is penalized in performance reviews for any halt not pre-approved by the algorithm. Meanwhile, labor regulations classify these employees as “vehicle operators,” granting them full commercial-driver wages and benefits, and the company markets the jobs as “human-centered automation” that preserves dignity through retained responsibility. What philosophical commitments about work, human agency, and moral accountability does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes retaining a non-controlling human seem coherent rather than ceremonial. In particular, distinguish what the practice strictly requires—about whether meaningful work needs causal efficacy, whether consent to risk is valid when exercising the veto is punished, and whether responsibility can attach to a purely supervisory role—from what merely makes the arrangement more palatable. Then say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: for example, could the same structure be licensed by a view of work as social participation rather than skillful contribution, or by a theory of vicarious liability rather than direct agency, and do those alternatives conflict with the regulatory classification?
