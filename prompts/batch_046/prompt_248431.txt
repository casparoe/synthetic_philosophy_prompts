I am designing a single ninety-minute seminar for a philosophy reading group—mostly metaphysics and ethics graduate students, joined by a visiting decision-theorist who understands formal preference models but has little background in value theory. Rather than rehearsing familiar sorites puzzles, I want to focus on a more specific claim: that the comparative relation 'better than' can fail transitivity in fully determinate cases not because of vagueness or epistemic limitation, but because value is structured across multiple dimensions that can rotate across a spectrum. On this view, local pairwise comparisons may all hold along one axis at each step, yet the global aggregation reverses direction, producing a cycle in which A is better than B, B better than C, and C better than A, without any borderline state being indeterminate. I need a teaching plan that makes this conceptual architecture clear rather than treating it as a bare paradox.

Please structure the explanatory arc: first, identify whether the distinction between local pairwise comparison and global multi-dimensional aggregation, or the idea of dimensional rotation itself, must come first, and justify the dependency; second, select the single carrying example or constructed case that should anchor the session—specify what formal properties it must possess (discrete, measurable dimensions with no hidden weighting) and what it must lack (e.g., sorites-style borderline vagueness or purely subjective taste); third, map the misconceptions this audience is likely to generate—such as resolving the cycle by assigning implicit weights to dimensions, or misidentifying the failure with Arrow's social-choice impossibility—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; fourth, propose one concise adjudication task I can pose at the end to test whether they have understood the metaphysical claim about comparative structure rather than merely reproducing the counterexample; and finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level—for example, formal axiomatizations of preference logic or historical genealogies of the problem—and explain why leaving it out protects the conceptual point.
