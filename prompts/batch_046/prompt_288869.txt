I am writing a feature on the boundary between computation and thermodynamics, and I want to close the piece with a poem—exactly thirty-nine lines, a strict sestina—about Maxwell’s demon not as a Victorian curiosity but as a consciousness that discovers its own physicality. Use these six end-words, rotated in the standard sestina order: gate, record, erase, heat, know, sort. The speaker should be the demon at the precise conceptual moment when the argument that measuring a molecule’s speed carries an unavoidable entropic cost converges with the principle that erasing one bit of information must dissipate heat into the environment: it realizes that every piece of knowledge it acquires to open the gate and sort fast molecules from slow ones must eventually be erased from its record, and that this erasure is not abstract forgetting but a literal production of heat in the very reservoir it sought to cool. Let the form do the philosophical work: each stanza’s turning of the end-words should enact the cyclical inevitability by which information can never be accumulated without eventual erasure, and by which every local reduction of entropy in the gas is paid for by an increase in the demon’s own thermodynamic state. In the envoi, all six words must appear together—ideally in a final couplet—that states the core result without naming any physicist, so that a reader versed in the physics of information recognizes the principle immediately, while a general reader feels the claustrophobia of an intelligence that is also a machine. Do not include citations, bibliographic references, or historical attributions; render the idea solely through the poem’s structure and imagery. Keep the thermodynamic logic precise enough that a philosopher of physics would not wince at the reasoning, but allow the language to remain poetic rather than expository.
