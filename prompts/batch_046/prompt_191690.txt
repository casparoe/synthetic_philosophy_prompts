I am preparing a presentation for my advanced high school philosophy course on the ethics of market mechanisms. I have been thinking about a scenario that is not often discussed in standard textbooks: during a large-scale public-health emergency, an autonomous predictive-pricing algorithm—not a human manager—sets the cost of essential medicines based on real-time supply scarcity and demand forecasting. Some argue this is a modern form of price gouging; others claim that because no human intentionally raises prices, the moral concept of gouging does not apply.

Please write a dialogue between two well-informed students, Mira and Leo, who are debating exactly this issue. Mira should defend the view that algorithmic pricing removes moral culpability and therefore should not be classified as gouging, emphasizing that responsibility requires an intentional agent. Leo should argue that outsourcing pricing to an algorithm creates a repugnant market in which human suffering is monetized through an automated mechanism, and that calling it "not gouging" is merely a linguistic evasion of responsibility. The exchange should involve genuine back-and-forth: each should address the specific reasoning the other just offered, not speak past one another. Neither character should be a strawman. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or lists of further reading, and please keep the discussion accessible to someone who has not studied economics formally but is familiar with basic moral philosophy.
