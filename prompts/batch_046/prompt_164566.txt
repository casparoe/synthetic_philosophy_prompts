I am preparing an essay for a course in philosophy of mind, and I have become preoccupied with a particular subset of split-brain findings that receives less attention than the canonical cases of complete corpus callosum sectioning. These involve patients with partial callosal agenesis in whom the anterior commissure remains functionally intact, permitting the interhemispheric transmission of emotional and motivational signals whilst leaving propositional and semantic information largely confined to the originating hemisphere. The result is a peculiar dissociation: a stimulus presented exclusively to the right hemisphere may elicit a clear aversive behavioural response—pupil dilation, withdrawal, or autonomic arousal—without the left hemisphere being able to articulate any reason for the reaction, and indeed whilst explicitly denying awareness of the stimulus altogether. A colleague argued that this simply demonstrates the existence of two independent streams of consciousness within one cranium, yet I worry that conclusion assumes precisely what needs to be shown, namely that behavioural dissociation entails experiential division rather than merely a fragmentation within a unified field.

With this in mind, I have been trying to reconstruct how William James, who died in the early twentieth century well before these neurosurgical discoveries, might have interpreted such phenomena. Given his pragmatic insistence that consciousness is not a substantial entity but a continuous, functionally integrated stream—characterised by his notion of the specious present and by the felt continuity of successive mental states—would he have seen these hemispheric dissociations as evidence for genuinely divided minds? Or would he have appealed to the underlying functional and emotional integration mediated by subcortical and commissural pathways to maintain that there remains a single centre of experience, albeit one that has been partially disarticulated? I should be interested to know whether you think his resistance to localising mental functions into isolated cerebral modules, combined with his emphasis on the unity of the knowing subject, would have led him to reject the dual-consciousness interpretation in favour of a more holistic, if disrupted, account of personal identity.
