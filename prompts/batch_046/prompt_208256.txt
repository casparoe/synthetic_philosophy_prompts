I've been hanging around rationalist spaces for years and lately I've gotten dragged into this endless argument with friends about procreative benficence and liberal eugenics — you know, the Savulescu-style claim that if you're going to have a kid anyway, you have a reason to select for the best expected life, and that this might extend into broader genetic enhancement duties. The debate always stalls in the exact same spot: one side treats it as just ordinary beneficence applied to reproductive choice, which makes them nervous that it slides into a duty to have as many happy children as possible (the repugnant conclusion is right there in the background, totally unaddressed). The other side says reproductve autonomy is a side-constraint that happens to align with beneficence in the embryo-selection case but doesn't scale, so there's no obligation to reproduce more. We've been going in circles for months with the same hypotheticals and nobody moves an inch.

I'm trying to diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a hidden population-ethics fight about whether merely possible people have moral standing? Is it verbal — maybe one camp uses 'reason' as pro tanto and the other as all-things-considered? Or is it bedrock intuitions about whether natural reproduction carries default value that enhancement corrupts? I want you to engage with what each side actually commits to, not just name-drop positions, and tell me whether there's a conceptual distinction or formal result that could unstick it — maybe something about act-type versus act-token beneficence, or whether liberal eugenics presupposes comparative rather than absolute axiology. And be honest: is there actually something that could move this debate, or are we just stuck with incompatible starting points that no argument will bridge?
