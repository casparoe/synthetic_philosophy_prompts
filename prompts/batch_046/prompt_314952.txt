Having recently adopted a rescue dog and begun studying Kant's moral philosophy seriously, I find myself wondering whether his ethics can accommodate direct concern for non-rational beings. Please compare Kant's canonical doctrine of indirect duties—where harming animals is wrong only because it cultivates cruelty in human agents and violates self-regarding duties to rational beings—with a revisionist analogical interpretation that grants animals direct moral patienthood. On the revisionist view, an animal's capacity for suffering and goal-directed behavior functions analogously to rational agency not by conferring dignity (Würde), but by grounding a direct duty of non-harm that operates within Kant's distinction between dignity and price (Preis). Assess whether this analogical extension genuinely modifies Kant's framework or merely restates the indirect-duty position in different terms, and identify precisely where the two views diverge on whether rational nature is the exclusive source of unconditional moral consideration.
