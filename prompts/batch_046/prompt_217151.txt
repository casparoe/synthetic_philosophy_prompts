Consider the following exam question and student response.

Question: In Sartre's account of radical freedom, he claims that human reality is condemned to be free and that this freedom produces anguish we often flee through bad faith (mauvaise foi). Explain what Sartre means by bad faith in this context, illustrate it with his café waiter example, and evaluate whether bad faith is best understood as analogous to Freudian repression or as requiring a different ontological structure.

Student answer: Sartre's thesis that existence precedes essence means humans have no predetermined nature; we exist first and then constitute our essence through free choices. This radical freedom causes anguish, which we evade through bad faith. Bad faith is self-deception in which we deny our freedom and treat ourselves as pure facticity—as if we were objects (en-soi) with a fixed identity rather than conscious beings (pour-soi) that transcend every given situation. In the café waiter example, the waiter moves with mechanical precision and seems to identify wholly with his role, denying that he could choose otherwise. This illustrates bad faith because he confuses his social facticity with his essence, acting as though being a waiter were an in-itself that defines him. Although this resembles Freudian repression—both involve hiding an uncomfortable truth—Sartre rejects the Freudian unconscious. For Sartre, consciousness is unitary and transparent; there are no hidden mental compartments. Instead, bad faith is a single conscious project in which the same consciousness both hides and knows its freedom. The waiter does not unconsciously repress his transcendence; he knowingly plays the part while subtly acknowledging, at a pre-reflective level, that he is not essentially a waiter. Sartre resolves the paradox of lying to oneself by positing a split between the reflective ego and the pre-reflective cogito, allowing one part to deceive the other without contradiction. Consequently, bad faith is best read as an ontological strategy rather than a psychological defense mechanism, and the waiter's error lies in treating his facticity—his body, past, and social role—as if it were an in-itself that cancels his transcendence.
