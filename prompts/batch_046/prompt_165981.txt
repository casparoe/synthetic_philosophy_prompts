I just spent three hours arguing with a colleague about whether modern statistical machine translation—particularly large transformer-based systems—actually understands language in any meaningful sense, or whether Douglas Hofstadter’s old critique of shallow, symbol-manipulating translation still applies unchanged. My friend thinks that because these models can handle idiom, metaphor, and context-sensitive disambiguation across dozens of languages, they must have acquired something like interlingual conceptual structures; I think they are still doing sophisticated pattern matching over token distributions without semantic anchoring, and that Hofstadter’s core point about the absence of genuine comprehension survives every benchmark improvement. Rather than continuing to talk past each other, I want us to imagine what a rigorous adversarial collaboration between these two positions—call them the Semantic-Anchoring Skeptics and the Functional-Comprehension Defenders—would actually produce.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims: about what statistical MT actually does (attention mechanisms, parallel corpora, emergent cross-lingual representations), about what “understanding” would require (intentionality, world-modeling, error correction based on meaning rather than probability), and about the specific phenomena that are genuinely puzzling—such as translating self-referential sentences, texts with deliberate grammatical violations meant to signal psychological breakdown, or metaphors rooted in embodied practices that are largely absent from digital corpora. Then present a disagreements table: the Skeptics’ precise formulation of why cross-lingual alignment is not comprehension, phrased so they would accept it; and the Defenders’ precise formulation of why high-dimensional representational convergence constitutes a functional form of understanding, phrased so they would accept it. Identify at least three cruxes. I suspect one is whether successful translation of culturally embedded silence and pragmatic implicature requires a theory of mind about the speaker, another is whether error-correction patterns in ambiguous passages track semantic coherence or merely statistical coherence, and a third is whether the absence of embodied experience in training data creates an in-principle boundary for certain classes of metaphor. For each crux, describe a concrete translation task or empirical observation both sides would agree is relevant—perhaps a specific type of poetic text with embodied metaphor, or a controlled study of how the system handles novel compound metaphors never seen in training—together with each side’s explicit prediction about what the system will do and why. End by stating whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal—say, if “understanding” is being used with different extension conditions—and if so, which part. Do not include reading lists, citations, or bibliographic references; I want this to stand on conceptual clarity alone.
