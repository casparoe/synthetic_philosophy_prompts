You work the identity-check desk at a clinic running experimental brain-reimplantation programmes — basically Dennett's Where Am I? scenario but now it happens for real, twice a month, and you've got about 40 minutes to sign off whether the patient leaving is the same legal person who arrived. You're not a philosopher, just a clerk with a manual, but the manual has to be grounded in Dennett's idea that the self tracks the brain's narrative centre rather than the body, plus the problem that copying the brain breaks identity altogether. Write me a reusable decision procedure — a flowchart in words plus a short checklist — that you could pin above the desk and follow without calling a lecturer. It needs to cover five scenarios: brain intact and back in original body; brain moved to a new body; brain copied so two bodies run it; brain split across two bodies; brain in a vat controlling the original body remotely. For each, list the documents to check, the interview questions to ask the patient, and what the sign-off box should say. Include three worked cases: one where the brain is in a synthetic body and you want to tick same person but the procedure forces you to tick new entity; one where there's a partial split and the procedure genuinely can't settle it; and one straightforward case. Flag exactly where judgement can't be removed, and say what the procedure will misfire on — for example, if the patient lies about continuity, or if the brain-copy is indistinguishable from the original. Keep it practical: who has to countersign, what happens if you miss the 40-minute window, and what you do if the patient refuses to recieve the form. Don't give me a bibliography or reading list — I just need the procedure.
