I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and applied ethics and am drafting the final examination. I want a single essay question—together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a detailed marking scheme—on the ethical implications of panpsychism when coupled with a quantitative, field-theoretic measure of consciousness. To keep the problem from being answered by generic summary, frame it through the following constructed scenario: assume that every localized excitation in a quantum field carries a minimal, non-derivative form of sentience whose magnitude scales with integrated information (in the formal sense associated with Giulio Tononi), and that suffering is the negatively valenced component of such sentience. On this premise, high-energy astrophysical processes—specifically, core-collapse supernovae—would generate suffering distributed across enormous numbers of field excitations, producing an aggregate moral catastrophe that dwarfs all biological suffering in the observable universe.

The exam question must require students to address three interlocking issues. First, explain how the combination problem for panpsychism interacts with the quantitative scaling of consciousness in this scenario: does macro-level suffering emerge from micro-level sentiences, and if so, by what mechanism of phenomenal binding or causal integration? Second, assess whether applying the concept of suffering—ordinarily anchored to unified macro-subjects—to sub-physical field excitations involves a category error, or whether the panpsychist is genuinely committed to such distributed, non-aggregated suffering. Third, evaluate one specific escape route in detail: either imposing a complexity threshold below which integrated information yields no valence, or adopting a no-subjects account of micro-experience that denies aggregation into macro-subjects altogether. For the chosen route, students must argue whether it successfully avoids the moral-catastrophe conclusion or instead collapses into eliminativism about macro-consciousness.

The model answer should advance a clear, defensible thesis; employ precise distinctions (e.g., constitutive versus derivative panpsychism, intrinsic versus relational properties of fields, phenomenal binding versus causal integration); and address the residue—namely, whether any moral obligation survives if macro-subjects are the only genuine loci of suffering, or whether the panpsychist must accept cosmological-scale moral duties toward field excitations. The marking scheme should allocate 30 marks total, specify the moves that separate an A answer from a B answer, and identify common errors that should cost marks. An A answer must integrate all three parts with original reasoning, avoid conflating integrated information theory with panpsychist ontology, and recognize that the quantitative scaling premise is the load-bearing assumption of the moral argument. A B answer may accurately describe panpsychism and the combination problem but should be penalized if it treats the scenario as a mere reductio ad absurdum without engaging the specific escape route, or if it neglects the residue question about moral obligation. Allow 60 minutes and require approximately 700 words.
