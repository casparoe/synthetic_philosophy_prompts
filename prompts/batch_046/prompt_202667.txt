After thirty years in condensed-matter physics I have recently begun studying philosophy of language and mind, and I find myself returning repeatedly to Douglas Hofstadter’s three-tiered distinction in Gödel, Escher, Bach between the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message. In physics we are trained to treat information as invariant under changes of substrate: a quantum state can be encoded in superconducting qubits or in photonic lattices without altering its logical content, and in molecular biology the genotype can be recovered from degraded tissue or even, in the case of prions, transmitted through conformational templating that survives the original polypeptide chain. Given this background, I am confused by Hofstadter’s insistence that the frame—defined not as the physical paper or vinyl but as the implicit signal that one is dealing with an encoded message—is itself part of the message rather than a neutral boundary condition. If the frame is genuinely part of the meaning-structure, then destroying the physical bearer should seem to destroy a portion of the meaning itself, not merely its accessibility; yet science routinely copies information across substrates while preserving what we take to be the essential content. So my question is whether Hofstadter’s framework commits him to a kind of substrate-dependence of meaning that conflicts with the information-theoretic view that meaning survives isomorphic encoding, or whether the frame can instead be understood as an invariant structural signature—present in any substrate that supports decoding—without making the specific physical instantiation semantically active. Specifically, if I digitize a painting or synthesize a gene in a novel chemical backbone, have I necessarily altered part of the message’s frame and therefore changed part of its meaning, even when the outer and inner messages appear fully preserved?
