I've been working through some material on epistemic rationality for a forecasting discussion group I'm part of, and recently my co-organizer offered a summary of Aumann's agreement result as a kind of knock-down argument against persistent disagreement in prediction markets. He put it like this: "Aumann proved that if two rational agents share the same prior and each knows the other's posterior belief about some proposition, then those posteriors must be identical—so if they're still disagreeing, at least one of them is either irrational or hiding information. This means that in a well-designed prediction market with transparent positions and automated resolution oracles, any remaining spread in prices must come from either bad faith or cognitive bias, not from legitimate differences in reasoning; therefore, we should treat persistent disagreement as a market failure rather than as reasonable pluralism, and design mechanisms that force convergence."

Something about this feels too neat, especially given what I recall about the conditions involving common knowledge being stricter than mere mutual awareness, and about the common prior assumption doing heavy lifting that real forecasters rarely satisfy. But I'm also unsure whether the leap from "posteriors converge" to "disagreement is always a failure" is doing extra work that the result itself doesn't license, particularly once noisy resolution mechanisms and heterogeneous priors enter the picture. Could you audit this summary carefully? Specifically: which parts accurately capture the classical agreement result, which parts conflate common knowledge with simple transparency of beliefs, whether the framework genuinely applies to prediction markets with noisy resolution oracles, and whether the normative conclusion—that persistent disagreement implies irrationality, hidden information, or market failure—actually follows once the technical conditions are stated correctly? I'd especially appreciate it if you separated what is a standard popularization, what is a subtle overstatement of the theorem's scope, and whether there is any explicit sense in which the result actually denies the possibility of reasonable disagreement under its own premises.
