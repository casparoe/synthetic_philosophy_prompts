The Peirce-James disagreement over truth—Peirce linking it to the ideal limit of inquiry, James linking it to practical success in experience—has been rehearsed for over a century without resolution, and in contemporary philosophy of science it often stalls when one side cites long-run predictive convergence and the other cites immediate practical cash-value. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a substantive clash about truth itself but a hidden divergence over the computational resources required for justification: Peirce's account implicitly demands unbounded, trans-generational belief revision, while James's demands only locally tractable verification, so they are answering different questions—one about the asymptotic norm of inquiry, the other about the epistemic authority of present experience—under the single heading pragmatist truth. Is that right, or is there a real, non-verbal conflict about whether truth is essentially normative or essentially descriptive? Diagnose the stall using what Peirce and James actually say about inquiry and verification, and tell me whether anything—a formal result about the intractability of convergence under resource bounds, or a conceptual distinction between asymptotic and local justification—could move the debate, or whether the impasse is permanent because the two sides optimize different epistemic values with no common metric. Give a committed answer with reasons, not a balanced survey.
