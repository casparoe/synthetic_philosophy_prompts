I have been teaching a unit on methodological issues in the science of consciousness, and we spent several sessions on Dennett's heterophenomenology — specifically, the idea that first-person reports should be treated as descriptions of a heterophenomenological world whose ontology remains neutral with respect to whether an inner theater or raw qualia actually exist. One of my doctoral students, who has recently been reading work on predictive processing and auto-noetic reporting, pressed me with a challenge I find conceptually slippery. She argued that because heterophenomenology deliberately brackets the reality of phenomenal states and treats reports as texts about a world that may not match neural reality, the method must implicitly regard every first-person claim — whether about the vividness of an afterimage or the location of a headache — as belonging to a kind of methodological fiction, comparable to the narrative world of a novel rather than to any real subjective state. From this she concluded that the approach cannot genuinely allow that a subject is mistaken about her own heterophenomenological world; the only errors it can recognize are behavioral inconsistencies or conflicts with third-person neuroscience, which means it has effectively replaced the traditional problem of other minds with a hermeneutics of fiction and collapsed into a sophisticated behaviorism.

I believe this conflates Dennett's claim that reports are authoritative descriptions of the heterophenomenological world with the claim that this world is unreal or purely invented, but I want to be precise about where exactly the reasoning breaks down. Could you help me pinpoint the specific false presupposition in her argument — especially her slide from not being direct neural data to being literally fictional — and explain what this misunderstanding implies for how heterophenomenology actually treats confabulation, first-person error, and the boundary between accurate description and invention within the subject's own report?
