I have been observing a recurring structural feature in debates about mental-state functionalism that does not seem to map cleanly onto the standard taxonomy of positions. The familiar version of multiple realizability concerns cross-substrate variation: the claim that a state such as pain might be realized in human neural tissue, in the nervous system of an octopus, or in a silicon architecture, provided each implements the appropriate causal-functional profile. But I keep encountering a more layered pattern. Take a specific cognitive function—say, the maintenance of working-memory representations. In biological systems, this is realized differently across individuals, across brain regions within the same individual due to plasticity, and even across time in the same region as synaptic weights shift. In artificial systems, the same high-level functional profile is implemented differently across conventional processors, specialized accelerators, and distributed architectures, and within each architecture across different parameter configurations and optimization states. So the multiplicity is not merely inter-substrate; it is intra-substrate, recursive, and present at every level of granularity, from gross anatomy down to specific circuit configurations. The functional kind remains constant, yet there appears to be no single canonical implementation even within one substrate class.

This seems distinct from the classic multiple-realizability objection to type-identity theory, which focuses on the possibility of radically different physical kinds performing the same causal role. It also differs from mere implementation variability, because the point is not that details differ while a core mechanism stays fixed; rather, there appears to be no core mechanism at all, only a stable functional description that floats free of every specific physical arrangement. I have wondered whether this layered or fractal form of realizability has an established name—perhaps in discussions of neural degeneracy, substrate independence, or heterogeneity objections to functionalist taxonomies. If there is a recognized concept, how exactly does it differ from standard multiple realizability and from claims that functional kinds are too disjunct to be explanatory? If there is no recognized concept, what are its precise boundaries: does it apply to all functional kinds or only to high-level cognitive ones, and does it pose a distinct challenge to functionalism beyond the familiar ones? I am not looking for a bare label, but for a clear carving of the phenomenon—what it is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
