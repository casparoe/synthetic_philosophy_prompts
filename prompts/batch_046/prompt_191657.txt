The pattern unfolds like this. A practice results in the death or prolonged nociceptive distress of billions of individual insects, many of which exhibit stress responses and nociceptive behaviors that, in other contexts—say, in discussions of crustacean or cephalopod welfare—are taken as evidence of welfare-relevant states. Rather than denying that insects possess any capacity for suffering, defenders of the practice typically shift the grounds of exclusion across several axes in succession. First they invoke neural architecture: these organisms lack the centralized processing that defines "real" sentience. When it is noted that decentralized systems still generate welfare-relevant states, and that some protected invertebrates share this architecture, the criterion shifts to social complexity or evolutionary distance. When eusocial insects are then raised as counterexamples, the ground shifts again to functional role within a managed system: these organisms are classified as "pests," and the classification is treated not as a biological designation but as a normative one, such that harm to them does not register in the same moral calculus as harm to "non-target" or "beneficial" species. What strikes me is not merely that the criteria are plural, but that they are applied selectively. The same feature—decentralized nociception, for instance—is treated as disqualifying in a crop pest but as insufficient for protection in a pollinator, and the boundary between "pest," "non-target," and "beneficial" tracks economic utility with remarkable precision, even as it is presented as a natural or ecological taxonomy.
