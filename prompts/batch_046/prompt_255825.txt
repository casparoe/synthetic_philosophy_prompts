I'm preparing a diagnostic instrument for a philosophy of science reading group focused on the metaphysics of physical dynamics, and I want it to work as a reasoning exercise rather than a recall test. The specific problem is deterministic chaos—exponential divergence in nonlinear systems like the Lorenz equations—not quantum indeterminacy. Most introductory treatments reduce the philosophical landscape to a single binary: either determinism survives despite practical unpredictability, or chaos reveals hidden randomness. I think that collapses several genuinely distinct commitments, so I need something that sorts people by what they actually believe.

Could you construct a branching taxonomy—presented either as a decision tree with explicit separation questions or as a flowchart of commitments leading to named positions—that classifies philosophical stances on the relationship between deterministic chaos, predictability, and determinism itself? The entry point should be a single shared commitment: the respondent accepts that the governing dynamics are classically deterministic (unique evolution from exact initial conditions, no quantum or hidden-variable interference), yet also accepts that the system displays sensitive dependence and a finite horizon of practical prediction. From there, build branches that ask concrete, label-independent questions about: whether practical unpredictability undermines determinism in any philosophically interesting sense; whether determinism conceptually requires in-principle predictability by an idealized observer, or whether it is satisfied purely by uniqueness of trajectory; whether deterministic chaos should generate epistemic humility about long-range planning, scientific explanation, or moral responsibility; and whether algorithmic incompressibility—when no program shorter than running the dynamics itself can generate the trajectory—introduces an ontological opacity that is functionally indistinguishable from indeterminism, or whether it remains firmly within determinism.

Please include at least these leaves: a strict Laplacian determinist for whom chaos has zero metaphysical significance; a separationist who distinguishes determinism from predictability but retains skepticism about free will; an epistemic fatalist who takes chaos to undermine rational long-range agency; an ontological-opacity theorist who treats algorithmic incompressibility as a distinct intermediate category; and a hidden-indeterminist who reads chaos as evidence that deterministic modeling has reached its explanatory limit. Every node should be phrased as a claim or case judgment the respondent could answer without knowing the label it produces. Where your tree genuinely cannot separate two leaves—because they share answers across all branches—flag the pair and say whether the overlap is conceptual or merely practical. Also identify any logically possible combination of answers that maps to no named position, and briefly diagnose whether that gap is incoherent, unstable, or simply unexplored. Do not include bibliographies, named scholars, publication dates, or citation requests; the instrument should classify commitments, not recall credentials.
