I am designing a 90-minute session for a third-year undergraduate moral philosophy seminar of twelve students. The topic is the argument—associated with John Taurek—that in rescue cases the mere fact that five lives are at stake rather than one does not automatically generate a stronger obligation to save the five, because moral claims may run separately to each individual and resist aggregation. I want an activity that makes the students feel the force of this view before they know its name, and that lets them construct the aggregation problem themselves rather than receiving it in a lecture or standard trolley framing.

Please design a runnable, structured deliberation or role-play with real but small stakes—using physical tokens, named roles, and explicit rules—so that participants experience both the pull of aggregation and its resistance. To move beyond the familiar textbook formulation, build in the following mechanic: each student holds physical claim cards representing independent, non-aggregable obligations to named individuals in distinct stranded groups; any collective decision to pool limited rescue capacity and save the larger group must involve physically setting aside the unfulfilled claim cards for those not rescued, leaving the decision-makers holding tangible representations of moral remainder. Arrange the rules so that a coordination paradox emerges—perhaps by changing the sizes of the stranded parties unexpectedly between rounds, or by allowing individual rescuers to act unilaterally with lower success rates—so that participants discover that consistent pairwise fairness can produce a group outcome they find unacceptable, or that unilateral aggregation undermines the fairness they just endorsed.

Specify the exact setting (group size divided into rescuers and representatives of stranded parties, materials needed, time allocation for each round and for silent individual choice versus open deliberation), the incentive or payoff structure that makes the stakes feel real without being coercive, and the step-by-step sequence. Then provide a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto parts of the argument—particularly the move from pairwise comparison to the denial that numbers count, and the idea that saving more people does not cancel the wrong to the one left behind—while being frank about which feature of the philosophical view the exercise cannot reproduce (for example, the absence of actual mortal risk, or the artificial discreteness imposed by the cards) and how you, as facilitator, will supply that missing element. Finally, identify one predictable failure mode—such as students implicitly adopting an aggregation rule they do not recognize—and explain how to handle it without dissolving the experiential tension that the activity is meant to generate.
