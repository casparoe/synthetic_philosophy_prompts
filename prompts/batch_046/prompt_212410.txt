At a winter residency for writers, two colleagues of mine—Elias, who writes speculative fiction, and Selene, a philosopher-novelist—fell into a sustained argument over a hypothetical procedure in which a living human brain is gradually replaced, one percent of its neurons each month over eight years, by synthetic analogues that preserve every functional and phenomenal property. They asked me to referee, and I found myself genuinely unsure. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander; I have no settled view and am not signaling for either side.

Elias opened by saying that gradual replacement is clearly identity-preserving. Because the change is distributed across years, there is never a discrete moment at which the original person vanishes; consciousness updates incrementally, the global workspace remains continuously active, and the first-person narrative never breaks. Identity, he insisted, is a diachronic process, not a static substance anchored to particular biological matter. If someone wakes each morning with seamless memory access and an unbroken sense of agency, then they are the same subject, regardless of the substrate's provenance.

Selene replied that this overlooks what she called somatic-marker continuity. Natural neurons are not merely functional nodes; they are embedded in an evolutionary biochemical milieu—coupled to endocrine rhythms, immune signaling, and visceral feedback loops that have been co-adapted over a lifetime. Synthetic analogues, even if phenomenally identical, enter as exogenous engineering. They lack that evolutionary lineage and homeostatic integration mechanism. Consequently, the being that emerges after full replacement is not the original subject but a highly accurate successor-subject: someone who inherits the memories and traits, yet is not the same bearer of first-personal concern.

Elias countered that natural cell turnover already replaces the brain's biological substrate repeatedly; within a decade, nearly every molecule has been exchanged through homeostatic processes. The synthetic case merely accelerates and directs what nature already does. To claim that origin—biological versus engineered—breaks identity is, he argued, a form of vitalism disguised as metaphysics. If functional architecture and phenomenal experience remain continuous, then the successor genuinely suffers, hopes, and remembers; denying them identity is a technicality that mistakes material pedigree for personal existence.

Selene conceded that natural turnover occurs, but insisted that the comparison fails on the mechanism of integration. Homeostatic replacement is genome-regulated and milieu-coupled: new cells inherit the same biochemical context and are shaped by the body's ongoing self-regulation. Synthetic insertion is exogenously designed and implanted; the replacement parts do not share evolutionary lineage with the body and are not shaped by its endogenous dynamics. The issue, she said, is not the rate of change but the kind of causal history that sustains embodied intentionality. She then added that she was not denying the successor any moral rights—only that they are identical to the pre-replacement person. The practical question is whether the original individual should fear annihilation during the procedure. If the result is a happy, continuous-appearing copy, that is a consolation for others, but not for the one who is being replaced.

Elias responded that this collapses into an unverifiable claim about private "ownership" of experience. From any third-person or even first-person operational standpoint, there is no detectable rupture. If the post-replacement being sincerely reports, "I am still Elias," and behaves with perfect psychological continuity, then to assert that some hidden, non-functional essence has been lost is to invoke a metaphysical ghost that does no explanatory work.

Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both are missing that would resolve or dissolve their disagreement. Be precise about whether the dispute is ultimately about metaphysical identity, about first-personal fear of death, or about the ethics of undergoing the procedure, since they seem to slide between these.
