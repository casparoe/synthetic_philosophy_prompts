Take the 2016 United Kingdom referendum on European Union membership as a public dispute structured by competing moral framings. Analyze it through moral foundations theory, treating the Leave and Remain campaigns as coalitions that activated different subsets of Haidt's six foundations. Specifically: map which foundations each side prominently invoked—care and harm, fairness and cheating, loyalty and betrayal, authority and subversion, sanctity and degradation, liberty and oppression—whether Remain's relative silence on loyalty, authority, and sanctity was a strategic omission or a genuine absence in its supporters' moral cognition, and whether the Leave coalition's success in mobilizing the binding foundations explains its victory better than standard economic or identity-based accounts. Then reverse the analysis: the domain asks whether the psychology of left and right tells us anything about who is right. Applying that to Brexit, does the fact that Leave activated a broader range of foundations indicate that its position had greater normative legitimacy, or only greater motivational reach? What would moral foundations theory have to say, if anything, about whether the binding foundations should count in a normative evaluation of sovereignty versus openness? And where does the theory fall silent—for instance, on whether the asymmetry in foundation use is a cognitive limitation of progressive moral psychology or a rational narrowing of morally relevant considerations? Keep the mapping disciplined: identify the actors, the options available to them, and what counts as evidence for or against each framing, and say plainly where the theory offers no verdict rather than forcing one.
