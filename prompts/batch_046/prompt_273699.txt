I got into a long argument with my coworker about whether those synthetic protocell things people build in labs count as alive — he thinks if something replicates its own chemical parts then definately yes, I think that's way too broad. While I was reading up to argue back, I kept seeing the same pattern in every debate about life: someone proposes a hard rule like "it reproduces" or "it metabolizes energy", then someone else finds a case that hits the rule but clearly isn't alive — computer viruses reproduce, crystals grow and exchange heat, my old laptop runs constant processes. So they patch the rule: reproduction yes, but only with metabolism; metabolism yes, but only inside a self-maintaining boundary; and on it goes. Each patch feels reasonable by itself, but the whole thing becomes this bloated checklist that seems tailored to include bacteria and trees and exclude laptops, rather than predicting anything. I see this with viruses, with people asking if AI is alive, and with these synthetic replicators.

Is there an established name for this specific kind of definitional bloat — where a concept keeps accumulating rescue conditions to cover counterexamples without the definition ever converging? I know about family resemblance and ordinary ad hoc fixes, but this feels different: the people doing it think they're being rigorous, yet the definition just tracks our pre-existing intuitions about what's alive instead of explaining them. If there's no standard term in philosophy of biology, can you name and define it, say exactly where its edges are, and tell me how it's distinct from just being vague or from a motte-and-bailey with definitions?
