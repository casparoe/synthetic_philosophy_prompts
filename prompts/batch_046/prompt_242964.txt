I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of social science, and in my advanced seminar on social ontology we have reached a frustrating stalemate regarding the ontological status of generational cohorts—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so forth. On one side, several colleagues defend what we might call cohort essentialism: they hold that these groupings constitute genuine social kinds because shared macro-historical conditions—economic recessions, technological adoption curves, geopolitical events—produce causally unified dispositions, values, and life-course patterns that persist across contexts. On the other side, a different faction insists that these labels are merely marketing artifacts or statistical conveniences: age-graded populations are aggregated by census bureaus and advertisers, but there is no underlying causal mechanism that binds a 28-year-old in Seoul with one in São Paulo into a single kind, and treating them as such commits a reification fallacy. We have spent the better part of a term rehearsing empirical disputes about voting behavior, mental-health statistics, and labor-market entry, yet neither camp has revised its core position.

What makes the deadlock puzzling is that both sides seem to accept the same descriptive sociology and differ only in their ontological interpretation. I have begun to suspect that the disagreement is not primarily about hidden empirical facts—say, whether inter-cohort variance exceeds intra-cohort variance—but rather about what counts as a legitimate standard for social-kindhood, and possibly about whether generational identity is constituted relationally rather than intrinsically. Specifically, I wonder whether the essentialists are implicitly treating generations as diachronic kinds that maintain causal powers over time, while the anti-essentialists are evaluating them as synchronic aggregates that fail counterfactual stability tests: if the 2008 financial crisis had not occurred, the putative kind Millennial might dissolve, suggesting modal fragility incompatible with robust kindhood. Yet I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real fault line, or whether there is a deeper conceptual divergence about whether social kinds must be mind-independent to be real.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal—turning on ambiguous uses of kind, generation, or real—or substantive, involving different commitments about causal explanation, modal robustness, or the role of institutional labeling in constituting social entities? Furthermore, if your diagnosis is correct, what would genuinely count as progress? Would it require a formal framework for testing the counterfactual stability of cohort properties, a conceptual distinction between demographic cohorts and historically constituted generations, or an acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed because generational membership is indexical rather than essential? Please engage with the positions as I have described them, and indicate whether either side is missing something that would dissolve or sharpen the dispute.
