Question: Explain Marx's argument about machinery, automation, and the extraction of surplus value, especially in the Fragment on Machines and related writings. How does the distinction between constant and variable capital create a tension between the individual capitalist's incentive to automate and the system's collective need for living labor? And what does Marx mean by describing machinery as dead labor?

Student answer: Marx argues in the Fragment on Machines in Capital that machinery is dead labor dominating living labor. A single capitalist who introduces automation can produce goods with less labor time than the social average, sell them at the old value, and pocket extra surplus value—this is relative surplus value. Once the technology spreads across the industry, the social value falls and that temporary advantage disappears.

The deeper issue is that machinery belongs to constant capital, which only transfers its existing value into products gradually. Only variable capital—the wages for living workers—creates new surplus value. So when capitalists replace workers with machines, they reduce the very pool of living labor that generates profit. This is the falling rate of profit tendency: competition forces every capitalist to automate, but collectively they erode the source of surplus value.

Marx also develops the idea of the general intellect, where collective knowledge embedded in machines becomes the direct productive force. Once automation reaches this stage, living labor is pushed to the margins and surplus value extraction becomes impossible. Marx believed this contradiction means capitalism will eventually collapse automatically into socialism, because the forces of production will outgrow the relations of production once technology is advanced enough, without requiring organized class struggle.
