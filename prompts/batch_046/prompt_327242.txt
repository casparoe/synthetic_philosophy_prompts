Essay notes — Why metric absence misses the point of hard choices

The standard account treats incommensurability as a failure of common measurement: we cannot translate artistic fulfillment and medical service into a shared cardinal scale, so rational comparison is blocked. I think this misdiagnoses the difficulty. In hard choices — say, between a life as a musician and a life as a physician — we can often rank outcomes ordinally within each domain and sometimes even across them. The problem is not the absence of a metric but what I call structural incommensurability: values belong to distinct evaluative frameworks, each supplying internal reasons, and no higher-order framework integrates them without distorting one side. Choosing music does not mean weighing less on a universal scale; it activates a framework in which healing others becomes an external cost rather than an internal reason. So all-things-considered judgment is not merely difficult but conceptually misplaced. Rational agency should involve framework-sensitive commitment: selecting one normative architecture and letting its internal reasons be decisive, treating the foregone framework's reasons as genuine but non-aggregable losses. This is not relativism — frameworks can be critically assessed — but it denies that assessment must take the form of cross-framework weighing.
