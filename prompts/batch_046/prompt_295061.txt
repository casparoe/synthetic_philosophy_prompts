I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of mind and metaphysics seminar. Please write one essay question that students could answer in 90 minutes, targeting the intersection of mind uploading, gradual replacement, and the special composition question.

To prevent answers that merely summarize standard literature, inject the following specific conceptual framing: the replacement procedure is not continuous. It includes mandatory intermittent suspension intervals—periods lasting several months during which the biological brain is completely deactivated and the synthetic functional architecture operates in isolation on geographically distributed remote servers, after which it is re-integrated with whatever biological tissue remains. By the end of the ten-year process, cognition is entirely synthetic and distributed across non-local nodes with no central biological hub.

The essay question should ask students to address whether, under these conditions, there is a determinate metaphysical fact about when the mental parts compose one continuing diachronic mind rather than a mere sequence of temporary minds. Specifically, they should evaluate whether standard psychological-continuity accounts can accommodate these multi-month suspension gaps without either allowing that a single self can temporarily cease to exist and then resume, or covertly relying on a substance-based or essentialist criterion that the gradual-replacement framework was explicitly designed to eliminate. The answer must engage directly with at least one formulation of the special composition question—e.g., unrestricted versus restricted universalism, or a functional-unity criterion for mental parts—and apply it to cognitive processes or mental states, not merely to physical objects.

Please provide:
- The exam question, clearly stating the time available (90 minutes) and expected length (approximately 1,000–1,200 words).
- A model answer of roughly 600–800 words that demonstrates the required conceptual moves.
- A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks assigned to each (total 25), and a one-line note for each point describing the specific misconception or omission that typically costs candidates those marks.

The question should be unanswerable by reciting summaries of well-known thought experiments without suspension gaps. It should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should avoid invoking highly technical or obscure recent theories that are not part of standard philosophical training.
