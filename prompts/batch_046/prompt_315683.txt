You hold, as in the Guide for the Perplexed, that the Torah’s sacrificial commandments are a divine concession to an Israelite psychology shaped by idolatrous rituals of slaughter—not an expression of the ideal law—and that animals suffer and possess natural ends, but lack rational souls, so they do not stand in moral equality with human beings. Here is the modern premise and challenge. Bioengineers have developed cultivated animal tissue that can be ritually consumed without the death of any sentient being, and some contemporary ethicists argue that this does not merely replace sacrifice but completes your concession—gradually redirecting the same psychological impulse toward harm reduction until the impulse itself dissolves. They claim that if your concession theory is correct, this technology should be welcomed as the logical endpoint of the law. I contend, however, that your view actually requires the permanence of the concession: divine wisdom accommodates a fixed feature of human attachment rather than engineering its disappearance, and a command calibrated to a specific historical psychology cannot be “fulfilled” by technological change without undermining your claim that the law is immutable and that human weakness in this domain is enduring. Does your account allow for a technological endpoint that eliminates the need for the concession, or does that endpoint contradict your view that the command was tailored to a psychology that does not progress away?
