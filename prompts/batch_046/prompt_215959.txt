I am preparing materials for an advanced undergraduate seminar on distributive justice, and I want a diagnostic instrument that sorts students by what they actually believe about Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument, rather than by which label they prefer. The problem with standard quizzes is that students can simply declare themselves libertarians or egalitarians without confronting the precise tension between historical entitlement and pattern-based outcomes. So I need a decision tree—phrased as a sequence of concrete questions a student could answer yes or no without already knowing which philosophical position each answer leads to—that begins from the Chamberlain scenario itself: a large audience voluntarily pays a small fee to watch Chamberlain play basketball, producing a highly unequal distribution of wealth, and then moves through the logical space of commitments about that scenario.

Please build the tree so that each branch node is one precise claim about the moral relevance of the exchange: whether the baseline distribution was just, whether each individual payment was fully informed and uncoerced, whether the macro-level pattern that results can retroactively undermine the justice of the micro-transactions, and whether there exists a non-arbitrary threshold at which cumulative small exchanges trigger a collective obligation to correct. The leaves should include the familiar positions—strict Nozickian entitlement, Rawlsian pattern-correction, and a standard luck-egalitarian response—but also two combinations that become available only through a particular injected distinction. Define that distinction in the tree itself: call it transactional transparency, which holds that parties to the exchange understand the full distributive trajectory of their collective choices; and call it pattern-sensitive entitlement, which holds that historical justice is preserved only if the resulting distribution remains within a bounded range of inequality. For each leaf, provide the name of the position (or mark it 'unoccupied') and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—for example, having to maintain that Chamberlain's fans bear collective responsibility for the inequality they caused, or having to deny that any meaningful moral difference exists between one large voluntary transfer and thousands of identical small ones.

Finally, identify any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate given these questions, and briefly diagnose why: whether the distinction collapses under conceptual analysis, whether the two positions are observationally equivalent on the Chamberlain case, or whether the injected distinction proves unstable when applied consistently.
