ok so me and jake got into it for like two hours after this philosophy meetup and im still kinda annoyed about it. he was defending schefflers afterlife conjecture -- not the religious kind, but the idea that human extinction is bad in a special way that goes way beyond counting deaths. basically he thinks so much of what makes our lives meaningful depends on there being future generations after us, like climate activism or writing books or even just wanting kids, and if we knew for sure humanity ends in 200 years those projects would feel hollow even if we live out our full lives. jake insists this isnt just psychology but actual value-structure: our commitments are partly constituted by an implicit belief in a kind of collective afterlife.

anyway i pushed back with four moves and he answered each one, and now i need an outside brain to tell me who was being dumb where.

(1) i said scheffler is just dressing up standard population ethics in poetic language. the real badness is that extinction prevents billions of potential good lives; talking about "hollowed present values" is just describing the emotional side-effect of knowing those lives wont exist. jake said no, the harm is direct and present-tense: our values genuinely depend on continuation, so extinction damages our current well-being immediately, not through some indirect chain.

(2) then i threw in this niche scenario i made up: suppose humanity builds a perfect "value-archive" -- not just stored data, but a self-sustaining system that genuinely continues human culture, art, and inquiry, evolving and creating new things, just without biological humans running it. if that existed and we knew extinction was coming but the archive carried everything forward, would schefflers catastrophic badness still apply? jake said yes, because our actual values are directed at biological human successors specifically, not abstract continuation. the archive is a consolation prize, not the real thing our projects aim at.

(3) i said that sounds like biological essentialism disguised as value-theory. if the archive replicates everything that matters, insisting it "doesnt count" is like saying only carbon-based computation is real computation. jake replied its not essentialism about carbon; its about the interpersonal and social nature of our commitments. we care about our actual children and their children living real lives, not about a simulation or archive "carrying on" in our place. the dependancy is relational, not substrate-dependent.

(4) my last move: if our values are really that fragile -- if they collapse just because there wont be people in 250 years -- then maybe those values were irrational to begin with, like tying your self-worth to a lottery you cant control. jake said thats a category error: values dont need to be instrumentally rational. recognizing that we depend on something outside our control isnt a cognitive bug; its part of having commitments that reach beyond ourselves. comparing it to a lottery misses that dependence is a feature, not a mistake.
