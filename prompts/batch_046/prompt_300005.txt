Two colleagues, Maya and Leo, argued after a climate-policy seminar about whether present generations genuinely wrong distant future people by emitting carbon. Maya claimed that future sufferers will be real victims of our legacy and that this suffering demands justification regardless of temporal distance; Leo replied that moral wronging requires an existing bearer who can claim redress, so talk of “intergenerational obligation” is at best metaphorical. Maya then invoked the non-identity problem—our emissions will determine which specific people are born, so no identifiable individual can say “you harmed me”—but argued this misses the real wrong: it is not done to a particular person but to the “modal space” of possible human lives, degrading the baseline conditions of flourishing for whoever eventually occupies the future. Leo countered that without an identifiable victim there can be no duty-bearer relation, and proposed “institutional fidelity” instead: we preserve climate stability not as repayment to strangers ahead, but because enduring social institutions require maintaining the shared conditions that make our own long-term projects coherent. Maya objected that this makes climate action depend on present people’s sentimental attachment to continuity, which fails if they are genuinely indifferent, whereas a true obligation should bind us even without that attachment. Leo answered that every obligation operates through some present framework—reciprocity, contract, or shared membership—and that inventing a duty to an abstract modal space merely dresses present anxiety in moral language. Please adjudicate point by point: assess Maya’s modal-space move, Leo’s reciprocity objection, the institutional-fidelity alternative, Maya’s contingency critique, and what both overlook about whether framing climate ethics as cross-generational debt is the right approach at all. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
