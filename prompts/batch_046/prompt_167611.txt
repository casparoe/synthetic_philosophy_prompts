For my seminar on comparative metaphysics and bioethics, I am trying to work through a conceptual problem that I have not seen addressed in the standard secondary literature. Zhuangzi treats death not as the termination of a fixed subject but as one transformation (wuhua) among continuous changes of form, where no particular configuration—living, dead, or otherwise—carries ontological priority. Modern clinical practice, however, defines brain death as the irreversible cessation of all integrated functions of the entire brain, which presupposes that there is a singular, identifiable subject whose biological coherence can definitively fail. Could you explain the precise conceptual tension here? In particular, does Zhuangzi's refusal to anchor personal identity in any stable substrate make the clinical boundary of brain death appear arbitrary from his metaphysical standpoint, or can one reconstruct a non-substantialist criterion for the end of a particular form without contradicting his core commitment to constant transformation? I am looking for an intuitive but rigorous conceptual mapping rather than an historical survey or bibliographic guidance.
