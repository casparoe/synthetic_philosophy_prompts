I am facilitating a ninety-minute seminar for sociology graduate students who have encountered basic social theory but have no background in metaphysics or philosophy of language. I want them to understand a precise, non-obvious distinction within “social construction”: the difference between institutional kinds that exist by collective assignment of a status function—money being the paradigmatic case—and interactive kinds whose causal properties are altered by the very classifications applied to them, with race and gender serving respectively as the relatively pure and the hybrid cases. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. In what order must the conceptual pieces be introduced—should collective intentionality come before looping effects, or vice versa, and which dependency determines that sequence? Identify the single carrying example or scenario that must bear the weight of all three categories (money, race, gender) without letting them collapse into one another, and specify exactly what that example must contain and what it must lack so the distinction remains visible. Map the misconceptions the students will likely arrive with or form during the session—I am expecting “if it is constructed then it is not real,” “construction means individuals consciously choose it,” and the tendency either to treat gender as identical to money or to erase its institutional dimension entirely—and indicate precisely where in the arc each misconception should be headed off. Provide one concrete check I can use at the end to determine whether they have grasped the dependency structure rather than merely memorized labels, and explicitly state which philosophical machinery—perhaps the full formal structure of status-function assignment, or disputes about biological natural kinds—I should openly refuse to explain at this level and simply bracket. Keep the plan concrete enough that I could run the session directly from it.
