I sit on the board of our university's Future Generations Fund — it's a modest endowment, but we have discretion over long-range grants in ethics and public policy. Last week a working group circulated a proposal that we should restructure our grant-making around strict expected-value maximisation, but with a specific twist: they want to weight potential welfare of future beings quadratically with the number of affected individuals, so that astronomical futures completely swamp present concerns. They're urging us to put nearly all our capital into one highly speculative biosecurity initaive that has perhaps a one-in-ten-thousand chance of success, but if it works it allegedly produces enormous expected value across vast future populations. Half the board thinks this is just rational decision theory applied consistantly; the other half says it's Pascal's mugging dressed up in spredsheets, and that we face deep ambiguity about whether those futures exist, whether our intervention actually reaches them, and whether welfare even scales that way.

I'm meant to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting recommending a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with clear reasoning that takes the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it out of hand. Could you draft that memo for me? Please write it in British English, and keep it to continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just like a letter or an email. I don't want any bibliography, reading list, or citation list; the argument should stand on its own. Also, in our coffee-break discussion we kept returning to the idea that ambiguity aversion — the kind of thing you see in the Ellsberg setup — might be rational here rather than a bias, since we genuinely don't have probabilities for these far-future scenarios, we just have ignorance. I'd like you to weave that in: should genuine ambiguity about outcomes put a brake on how far we let expected-value fanaticism run in institutional decision-making? Don't quote anyone verbatim unless you can check the source, and avoid asking me for further references — I need something I can actually circulate.
