The debate over how philosophical theories of truth should respond to the Liar paradox—exemplified by the self-referential claim that it itself is false—has persisted for well over a century without decisive resolution. Today the disagreement is usually mapped onto three camps: revisionists, who hold that classical bivalence must be restricted or abandoned and that some contradictions can be genuinely true; contextualists or hierarchicalists, who argue that the word true behaves indexically or operates at different semantic levels, so the paradox dissolves once hidden contextual parameters are made explicit; and deflationists, who deny that truth names a substantive property and treat the paradox as a boundary failure of an otherwise useful disquotational device. Despite extensive formal machinery—paraconsistent calculi, Tarskian hierarchies, dynamic semantic frameworks—each camp remains largely unmoved by the others, recycling familiar objections: revisionists are accused of altering the very subject of logic, contextualists of inserting syntactic restrictions that feel ad hoc, and deflationists of being unable to explain why the paradox strikes us as profound rather than grammatically malformed.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not mainly about logic or semantics, but about what explanatory labor a theory of truth is required to perform—whether it must preserve every pre-theoretic intuition about ordinary-language use, whether it must deliver a consistent formal calculus at any cost, or whether its job is merely to explain our practical reliance on truth-talk. I want a sharper diagnosis, however. Is the disagreement substantive—are there hidden empirical claims about how ordinary speakers actually deploy self-referential truth-attributions, or hidden formal claims about which calculi preserve the right inferential properties? Is it verbal—do the camps assign different meanings to truth, logical consequence, or explanation, so they are answering different questions under a single heading? Or is it a methodological impasse, where all relevant formal results are shared and the only remaining divergence concerns which costs—giving up unrestricted bivalence, giving up semantic closure, giving up deep explanatory ambitions—are intellectually acceptable?

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement by engaging concretely with what each side actually says about the Liar and about the purpose of a truth-theory, not merely by listing positions. In particular, address whether a hidden distinction between semantic closure (whether a language can contain a complete predicate for its own truths) and logical closure (whether its inference rules remain sound under self-application) is doing any real work, or whether it is just a relabeling of the same commitments. Then say what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a specific formal result, an empirical finding about language use, a conceptual distinction that forces one camp to concede ground, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the disagreement tracks irreconcilable methodological priorities rather than resolvable questions.
