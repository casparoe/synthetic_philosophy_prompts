Construct a thought experiment that isolates conceptual slippage in a microdomain modeled on fluid analogical-reasoning spaces—specifically, a small, self-contained conceptual system analogous to letter-string analogy tasks, but embedded in an everyday manual practice rather than an abstract symbol game. Design a realistic workshop scenario, in one paragraph, in which craftspeople classify ceramic vessels according to a single initially sharp criterion: whether a hairline fracture propagates across a defined glaze boundary. Then introduce an analogical repair-mapping task—adapting a patch pattern from one vessel form to a structurally similar but differently proportioned form—that exerts subtle pressure on the criterion without anyone revising the explicit rule. The slippage must occur below the threshold of ambiguity, vagueness, or polysemy: in both contexts the boundary remains sharp, yet the extension of the concept shifts for one or two specific cases that every participant consistently judges differently, with no awareness that the concept itself has moved.

After presenting the scenario, do the following: state the concept’s initial extension and the explicit rule that fixes it; identify the analogical pressure point—such as the proportional scaling of the fracture path relative to the vessel’s curvature—that causes the shift; show why a rigid necessary-and-sufficient-conditions account either overgenerates or undergenerates in the mapped context; and show how a fluid, context-sensitive account accommodates the shift without contradiction. Crucially, include one structural feature that, if removed, collapses the case into ordinary ambiguity rather than true conceptual slippage, and explain exactly why its presence is load-bearing. Finish by stating how a defender of classical conceptual analysis would try to dissolve the case—for instance by appealing to hidden ambiguity or user error—and why that dissolution fails given the feature you isolated. The setting must be non-technological and non-scientific-fictional; do not cite authors, texts, or specific theories by name; and do not ask for bibliographies or reading lists. Work entirely from the conceptual structure of the scenario you invent.
