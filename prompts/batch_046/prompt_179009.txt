I'm working through whether biological functions are determined by evolutionary selection history or by present causal contribution, and a friend has pressed an argument that seems to show the causal-role view collapses into absurdity, yet I can't locate the exact fault. Here is the chain: (1) On a causal-role account, a trait's function is whatever it causally contributes to the organism's current capacities, with no normative privilege for effects that were historically selected. (2) Consequently, if a heart's contraction also emits vibrations that happen to repel a specific parasite, deterring parasites is as much a function of the heart as pumping blood is—there is no principled distinction within the view between proper function and accidental by-product, since both are live causal contributions to system performance. (3) Apply this consistently to evolutionary novelties: imagine a synthetic kidney engineered de novo and implanted in an organism; it filters blood exactly as a natural kidney does, sustaining life without any selection history. Because its causal contribution is identical, the causal-role theorist must attribute to it the same functional status and the same potential for malfunction. (4) From (2) and (3), evolutionary origin is functionally inert: history cannot constrain what counts as malfunction, because malfunction is defined solely relative to present contribution, not to ancestral selection. (5) But then consider vestigial structures—say, an appendix that has lost digestive capacity but now contributes to immune surveillance. If its only function is its present immune role, it is not malfunctioning with respect to digestion; it has simply changed jobs, and there is no normative standard left against which to judge it a failed digestive organ. (6) Generalize: on this view, every evolutionary transition that repurposes a trait eliminates the possibility of ancestral malfunction, rendering concepts like evolutionary vestigiality, atavism, and functional mismatch conceptually incoherent. (7) Therefore the causal-role view proves too much—it preserves functional attribution for synthetic organs only by destroying the evolutionary framework in which malfunction and vestigiality are meaningful. My friend takes this to show we must reject (1) for an etiological, selected-effects account. I think the error may be a hidden equivocation in (2) between contribution to fitness and contribution to any arbitrary system capacity, or perhaps a scope shift in (6) that assumes all historical roles must remain live normative standards. Which step is actually unsound, and what does rejecting it commit us to—does saving evolutionary malfunction require abandoning functional attribution for bioengineered organs, or is there a middle path?
