I am preparing a seventy-five minute workshop for eight graduate students in moral philosophy focused on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism and the related charge that certain forms of moral reasoning allow one thought too many. Rather than revisiting the familiar George and Jim scenarios that the students have already encountered in survey courses, I want you to embed the philosophical pressure inside an unfamiliar institutional setting: a small custodial collective responsible for an irreplaceable archive of oral histories, where each member holds a non-transferable, identity-constituting fiduciary bond to a specific community's records that defines their professional and personal ground project. The exercise should take the form of a structured role-play with hidden ground-project cards, explicit phases of deliberation, and symbolic stakes—loss of custodial status, accumulation of moral residue markers, or exclusion from collective memory-work—rather than monetary payoffs, so that the rules make aggregate welfare calculation structurally available and apparently rational while rendering it personally destructive in a way that mirrors Williams's point. Please include a precise timeline with assigned roles, the mechanism by which the group reaches or fails to reach a decision, and a debrief that maps each critical moment—particularly when one participant introduces a utilitarian calculus and another rejects it on grounds that resist translation into a common currency—onto Williams's distinction between having a reason and having a justification, and onto the phenomenon of introducing an extraneous calculative step where direct attachment should operate. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run: students may conflate integrity with mere stubbornness or self-interest, the participants assigned utilitarian roles may experience genuine social pressure that obscures the philosophical point, and the collective decision mechanism may collapse into negotiation rather than deliberation. Also be frank about which feature of Williams's account the simulation cannot reproduce—likely the deep, first-personal, long-standing attachment to a ground project that is not genuinely adoptable as a temporary role—and advise how I should supply that missing element in the closing discussion. Present your answer entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write in a formal letter or email.
