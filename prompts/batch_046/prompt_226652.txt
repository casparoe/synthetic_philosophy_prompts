Exam question: Explain how H.L.A. Hart’s distinction between primary and secondary rules bears on the possibility of legal self-amendment. Use Peter Suber’s game Nomic to illustrate how a rule system might amend its own rules, and evaluate whether the paradox of omnipotence for legal rules is genuinely analogous to the liar paradox. Distinguish syntactic from semantic self-reference in normative systems, and discuss the conditions under which a self-amending framework might achieve what we might call reflexive stability.

Student answer:

Hart divides legal rules into primary rules of obligation and secondary rules of recognition, change, and adjudication. The rule of recognition is the central mechanism for self-amendment, because it identifies which rules are valid, including the very rules that specify amendment procedures. In Suber’s Nomic, players propose changes to the rules, and the current rules determine whether each proposal passes; this demonstrates that a system can embed its own amendment procedure within itself without external authority.

The paradox of omnipotence arises when a legal system contains a rule capable of amending any rule, including itself. This is structurally identical to the liar paradox, in which a statement asserts its own falsehood—both generate contradiction through direct self-reference. Suber resolved this by showing that if rules are ordered lexicographically by number, then any rule may amend only rules with lower numbers, which prevents a rule from amending itself. Consequently, self-amendment is always consistent provided the system adopts such a hierarchical numbering scheme.

Within Hart’s framework, the rule of recognition avoids paradox because it is social and conventional rather than normative in the same sense as rules of change; therefore it cannot apply to itself, and only rules of change can perform self-amendment. This means syntactic self-reference is blocked by the social nature of recognition, whereas semantic self-reference—where a norm refers to its own content—remains possible but harmless. Because Hart separates recognition from change and because Suber’s lexical ordering eliminates circular amendment, the liar-paradox analogy fails for law. A system achieves reflexive stability precisely when it combines Hart’s separation of rule-types with a hierarchical indexing of amendable rules.
