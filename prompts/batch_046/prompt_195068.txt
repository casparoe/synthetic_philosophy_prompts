I am a retired metrologist — my career was in precision measurement, instrument calibration, and the management of reference-frame drift — and I have only recently begun reading in philosophy of mind and comparative metaphysics. I would like you to take the Buddhist doctrine of anatta (the denial of a permanent, unitary, substantial self) and the three principal analytic theories of personal identity — psychological-continuity theory, animalism, and the narrative view — and restate each one, as rigorously and charitably as possible, in the vocabulary of experimental metrology and physical measurement: calibration protocols, reference-frame invariance and its failures, noise floors and signal extraction, the operational distinction between the object being measured and the apparatus that registers it, and the concept of a system boundary that persists only through continuous exchange with an environment. For each of the four positions, show what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit this framework, and what has no expression whatsoever — paying particular attention to claims about moral responsibility, first-person authority, and diachronic accountability. Then provide a concise residue report: do the elements that resist translation indicate a real limitation in treating personal identity as a measurable physical system, or do they suggest that the original philosophical distinctions depend on categories that are not empirically well-formed? Please answer in formal American English, without offering bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and include an honest account of the strongest consideration that favors the conclusion opposite to your own.
