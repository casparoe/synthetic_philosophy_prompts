Below are draft notes for a short essay. I examine whether the well-documented divergence between WEIRD and non-WEIRD folk moral psychologies—particularly the varying weight given to care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity—undermines moral objectivity, or whether it can be accommodated by what I term double-indexed moral realism. On this view, moral predicates possess both a descriptive index (the culturally specific conditions of application) and an evaluative index (the stable functional conditions that warrant the evaluation). I propose that terms such as harm or impurity function as observational perspectives on a single underlying social-coordination mechanism: in one cultural setting the mechanism is breached through autonomy violations, in another through ritual contamination, yet the evaluative index tracks an identical functional failure. I then argue that this avoids relativism without requiring transcultural transparency—the demand that moral content be recognisably identical across cultures—since divergence in descriptive index is compatible with shared reference, comparable to the way colour concepts may differ in their sensory conditions of application while tracking the same physical properties. I am especially concerned that the analogy to perceptual concepts may be strained, that the framework collapses into an implausible moral functionalism, and that I have misrepresented the empirical moral-foundations literature.

Please comment on these notes. I should particularly welcome remarks on whether the double-indexed framing is philosophically coherent, whether it genuinely evades relativism without smuggling in transcultural transparency, and whether the perceptual analogy does useful work. Please say how confident you are in your assessment and identify the point about which you are least sure.
