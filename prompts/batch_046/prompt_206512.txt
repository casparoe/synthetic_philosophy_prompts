There is a growing institutional practice — visible in some civic education curricula and in recent design choices for value-alignment datasets — of constructing biographical corpora as paired moral exemplars rather than as historical records. In this format, one life is edited to highlight a specific virtue, another is arranged to display its corresponding vice or failure, and the pair is presented for direct comparative judgement, echoing the Plutarchan synkrisis. The practice knowingly accepts hagiographic compression, suppresses biographical noise, and excludes statistically ordinary lives that show no clear moral arc. At the same time, it treats these narratives as authoritative for moral learning, even while admitting they are not accurate histories, and it privileges exemplar comparison over abstract rules or probabilistic reasoning.

This seems to presuppose a specific view of how people learn to be good, of what virtue is, and of what biography is for. But it also looks inconsistent: if the narratives are distorted, why trust them for moral knowledge? If ordinary lives are excluded, how can the virtues be claimed as universal? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that makes this practice coherent — what view of moral learning, of the ontology of virtue, and of the epistemic status of biography does it require? Say whether the practice strictly needs that theory or whether it could be justified on purely pragmatic grounds compatible with rival views. Then tell me what would have to change in curriculum or dataset design if we replaced this exemplar-pair format with statistically representative life histories, or with abstract moral dilemmas, and identify which of the practice's commitments would be hardest to abandon.
