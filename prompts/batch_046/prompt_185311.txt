I have been observing a recurring pattern in arguments over whether particular nations are genuinely ancient or essentially modern inventions, and I am trying to determine whether this specific configuration has an established label. A speaker will invoke a medieval polity—the Capetian kingdom in the tenth century, or the early Piast realm—to establish that the corresponding modern nation has existed for over a millennium. When it is pointed out that the inhabitants of those territories lacked anything resembling modern national consciousness, and that political continuity was repeatedly broken by foreign rule, dynastic unions, and territorial fragmentation, the speaker replies that the name, the geographic core, and certain cultural practices have persisted continuously, so that the nation has merely evolved rather than been created. At the same time, however, the speaker treats distinctly modern nation-building instruments—standardized vernacular education, mass-produced national anthems, flag ceremonies, and conscription—as natural expressions of that same ancient identity, even though these instruments were largely constructed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries precisely to generate the feeling of timeless belonging. The result is a circular temporal structure: the ancient name and territory are used to authorize modern identity-forming institutions, while those very institutions are then cited as proof that the pre-modern population was already animated by the national identity that only the modern institutions could have produced.

This seems related to discussions of invented traditions and of nations as imagined communities, but those frameworks appear to address somewhat different problems—either the deliberate fabrication of rituals that pretend to antiquity, or the modern conditions that make large-scale anonymous solidarity possible. The pattern I am describing is narrower and more argumentative: it is the use of nominal and territorial continuity across centuries of discontinuous collective self-understanding to license a back-projection of modern institutional identity onto pre-modern societies. Is there a specific term for this move—perhaps in historiography, sociology, or political theory—and if so, how precisely does it differ from the broader claim that all nations are modern constructs? If there is no precise term, what are the closest established concepts, and where exactly do they fail to capture the circular justification between ancient nomenclature and modern nation-building practice?
