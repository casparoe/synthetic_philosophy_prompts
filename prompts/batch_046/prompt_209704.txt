After a long argument with a friend about Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life, I'm trying to figure out whether a comparison he kept returning to actually holds water. Schlick, as my friend read him, claims that life is meaningless when treated as work aimed at some external purpose—achievement, salvation, legacy—but becomes meaningful precisely when it is lived as play, an activity valued for its own execution rather than for any result it produces. To defend this, my friend said Schlick's view is essentially like a sandbox video game: there is no win state, no score to maximize, no narrative endpoint the player is meant to reach, yet people willingly devote enormous energy to building elaborate structures inside it, and nobody asks what the sandbox is "for" because the constructing itself is the reward. I objected that the analogy might be smuggling in assumptions Schlick never endorsed. Can you audit it systematically? First, lay out the correspondence table—what in Schlick's account plays the role of the player, the sandbox rules, the absence of a win condition, and the intrinsic satisfaction of building? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for Schlick's argument: does the sandbox presuppose a designer who set the parameters, and if so, does that reintroduce the very teleology Schlick rejects? Does the analogy depend on there being a conscious subject who experiences enjoyment, and does that make Schlick's claim that meaning resides in play rather circular? Third, point out any features of the source domain—such as the ability to exit the game or change the rules—that have no counterpart in Schlick's metaphysics of human life. Finally, assess whether the conclusion that life needs no transcendent purpose survives once these breaks are visible, or whether the sandbox was doing all the argumentative work.
