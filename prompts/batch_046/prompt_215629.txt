The topic became personally urgent for me last month: my sister emerged from a minimally conscious state after a brain injury, and her neurologist used the phrase “access consciousness without phenomenal consciousness.” That evening, I reported this to my roommate and my cousin, and they fell into a sharp disagreement I could not referee. They are not present to speak for themselves, so please adjudicate their exchange exactly as I recount it below, without asking for further sources.

Roommate (cognitive-science graduate student): The neurologist’s framing confirms Block’s distinction is real and that global workspace theory is wrong to identify access with phenomenal consciousness. She cited anesthesia-awareness cases: patients who follow commands and form implicit memories but later report no subjective experience. If information guides behavior and memory formation without being experienced, then access and phenomenal consciousness have come apart.

Cousin (philosophy student): Global workspace theory does not identify them in that crude way. On the standard broadcasting model, phenomenal consciousness requires global integration with specific sensory and self-monitoring workspaces, whereas access is merely information being available to some cognitive processes. In anesthesia awareness, the information reaches motor and implicit-memory modules but never enters the global workspace that supports reportable experience. So Block’s distinction survives, but the theory explains it structurally—there is no counterexample.

Roommate: Split-brain research supports her view further. The right hemisphere processes visual information and guides behavior, yet the left, language-dominant hemisphere denies seeing anything. This shows one hemisphere can possess access—behavioral guidance, preference expression—without phenomenal consciousness, while the other possesses phenomenal consciousness with verbal report. If one brain can host both states separately, then a unified global workspace is false.

Cousin: Split-brain cases do not show two independent workspaces. They show a single workspace fractured by severed callosal connections. The right hemisphere’s processing is not “access consciousness” in the philosophical sense because it is not available for rational deliberation across the whole agent. It is closer to unconscious expert processing—like blindsight—than to genuine access.

Roommate: She then proposed a hybrid she called “modular access”: information can be globally available within a subsystem—motor, affective, or implicit-memory networks—without reaching the narrative-verbal workspace. She claimed anesthesia awareness exemplifies this genuinely intermediate state, which both Block’s binary and standard global-workspace accounts miss.

Cousin: This merely renames what the theory already calls local or pre-conscious broadcasting. If modular access lacks reportability and cross-system rational integration, it is not consciousness at all but sophisticated unconsciousness. The roommate has not defended Block’s distinction; she has only muddied the taxonomy by inventing a category for high-level unconscious processing.
