hey, i've been noticing a weird argumentative loop in special-composition debates and i'm not sure if it has a real name. people defend restricted composition by saying parts only form a whole when there's some genuine unifier - causal integration, functional unity, whatever - so random scattered objects like "the moon plus my left shoe" don't exist. then you push on the borders: a swarm of bees slowly dispersing, two strangers standing close versus holding hands, a glacier that's half melted. suddenly they say the boundary is vague, there's no sharp line, so restricted composition avoids arbitrary cutoffs. but then if you flip the argument and ask why we shouldn't just accept unrestricted composition and treat *all* scattered sums as existing with vague boundaries, they switch back: vagueness is semantic, not ontic, and unrestricted composition is ontologically bloated. so the unifier criterion is acting like a sharp ontological gate in one direction and a fuzzy conceptual heuristic in the other. is there an established concept for this specific oscillation? i know it's near the special composition question and vagueness debates, but it's definately not motte-and-bailey - the two versions aren't a defensible core plus an indefensible extension, they're more like the same idea wearing different precision-clothing depending on which counterexample is being dodged. i want to know if anyone's carved this out precisely: how it differs from plain inconsistency, whether it's a failure mode of restricted views specifically, and what its boundary conditions are. if there's no name, can someone coin one with a clear definition that distinguishes it from standard semantic/ontic vagueness disputes?
