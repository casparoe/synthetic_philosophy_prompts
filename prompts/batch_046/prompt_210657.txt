So I've been chewing on the inefficacy objection for collective harms — climate, meat, voting, the usual suspects. The standard version is purely causal: one flight doesn't change the climate, one burger doesn't shift farm output, one vote rarely flips an election. So your action is causally impotent, hence not morally required, right?

But there's this other framing I keep running into in rationalist forums, and I can't tell if it's a real named concept or just a confused intuition. The idea is that even if your causal contribution is literally zero, you're still locked into a bad equilibrium where everyone expects everyone else to keep participating, and opting out sends a coordination signal. Let's call it 'equilibrium complicty' — you're not causing the harm directly, but you're sustaining the expectation structure that lets the harm scale. So the moral problem isn't your carbon atoms; it's that you're failing to be the break in a self-reinforcing loop.

Can you compare these two? Is equilibrium complicty actually a distinct objection, or just the inefficacy objection dressed up in game-theory language? And does it survive the voting case, where your vote literally is part of the collective mechanism, not just an aggregation of private actions? I feel like people conflate 'causally useless' with 'morally irrelevant' all the time, and I want to know if there's a clean conceptual separation or if I'm inventing distintions that don't hold up.

If this second thing has an established name in social choice or collective action theory, tell me, but I'm mostly after whether the comparison holds water. No need for a bibliography, just want the map sorted out.
