I am trying to determine what I actually think about policing and the state's monopoly on violence, and my reactions to concrete institutional arrangements do not line up neatly. Here are my verdicts on six cases.

Case 1. A rapid-response armed unit, publicly accountable and operating under strict rules of engagement, intercepts an active mass-casualty attack and uses lethal force to stop the attacker. Verdict: this is legitimate; a society that lacked this capacity would be deficient, and the state's monopoly is justified here.

Case 2. A uniformed officer employs a prolonged chokehold to subdue an unarmed person suspected of a minor economic offense, and the person dies. Verdict: illegitimate; the state's coercive apparatus has exceeded its proper scope, and this suggests the problem is structural rather than fixable by minor reform.

Case 3. After a police withdrawal, a neighborhood collective—unaffiliated with the state, democratically organized locally, and trained in de-escalation—organizes armed patrols to stop a wave of violent robberies. Verdict: acceptable, perhaps even admirable. This seems to show that the state's exclusive claim is not necessary for legitimacy.

Case 4. A jurisdiction fully abolishes all coercive enforcement, leaving no institutional capacity to detain a violent offender, interrupt an active attack, or enforce a protective order. Verdict: wrong; pure abolition is unacceptable because some reservoir of legitimate coercive capacity must remain.

Case 5. A city retains an armed unit only for active, grave threats, strips police of weapons and arrest powers for non-violent and mental-health calls, and redirects those to unarmed specialists. Verdict: closer to right than either the status quo or full abolition.

Case 6. The same city contracts the armed unit's operations to a private security firm compensated per incident, with minimal democratic oversight and no public transparency. Verdict: worse than the unreformed public police, despite preserving the formal state monopoly.

These judgments pull in different directions. I want a principle—or a small set of competing candidates—that captures the pattern, explains which feature of each case drives the verdict, predicts outcomes for cases I have not listed, and admits which of my six verdicts it cannot save.

Specifically, the principle should address: whether legitimacy depends on immediacy of threat, on democratic accountability of the agent, on the distinction between lethal and non-lethal domains, or on something like a 'differentiated reserve'—the idea that coercive capacity must be held in public, democratically accountable reserve, deployed only against immediate grave harm, and never delegated or monetized?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate the clause that motivates each of the six verdicts, apply it to two further cases—(a) a community that maintains an unarmed but physically capable intervention team with local democratic oversight but no state authorization; (b) a state that retains full monopoly but legally prohibits officers from using lethal force even to stop an active shooter—and then say honestly which of my original verdicts the best principle must overturn, and why.
