In a discussion I observed between two friends — call them A and B — who were reasoning from first principles without citing any literature, the question was whether origin essentialism allows that you could have come from a different sperm and egg. They went through several rounds and asked me to referee, since I stayed neutral. I want a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange.

A opened by arguing that origin essentialism about specific gametes is implausible because the sperm and egg are destroyed in fertilization and replaced by a zygote genome formed through recombination and mutation. The only essential feature, if any, is having genome G, and since different gametes could in principle yield the same genome — or a synthetic zygote could be constructed with it — the particular sperm and egg are causally relevant but not essentially constitutive of personal identity. A also noted that identical twins share the same fertilization event yet are distinct persons, which shows the origin event alone cannot be the essence; once divergence via splitting is permitted, identity must track the subsequent cell lineage, which makes the specific gametes redundant.

B responded with the standard origin-essentialist view applied to organisms: you are essentially the product of that particular egg being fertilized by that particular sperm, not essentially that sperm cell, which is indeed destroyed. The origin event — that specific zygote — is essential. Change the gametes and you get a different zygote, hence a different person. B said the genome is a consequence of the origin, not its substitute: a clone with genome G lacks your causal history, so the origin does independent work. On twins, B argued they share an origin but diverge at the split, so each twin's origin includes the split event that produced them; artificial synthesis might replicate the genome but not the causal chain, producing at best a duplicate, not you.

A countered that if causal history is constitutive, identifying that particular sperm is biologically vague: meiosis involves random chromatid segregation and mutation, so there is no precise fact of the matter about exactly which chromatid contributed each allele. If origin essentialism requires a determinately specified gamete pair, it demands a precision that biology does not provide. A added that B's clone reply actually supports A's point: if a synthetic zygote with your genome and induced development is not you, then what distinguishes you is not the gametes per se but the natural developmental trajectory, which could conceptually have been initiated differently.

B replied that vagueness in internal chromatid assignment does not erase the actual sperm and egg that participated; we can identify the cells even if their internal parts are arbitrarily assigned. B maintained that the natural versus artificial distinction tracks the actual causal origin, not merely the trajectory pattern, and that this is enough to block synthetic duplicates.

Both feel they are missing whether origin essentialism collapses into genetic essentialism, whether the twin case genuinely supports one side, and whether gamete vagueness is fatal or a red herring. They want to know who is right at each step, what both are overlooking — especially any hidden assumption about organism identity conditions that might render the dispute partly verbal — and an overall verdict on who is more right.
