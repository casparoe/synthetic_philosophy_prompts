I'm preparing a feature article for a science-and-ideas publication on the present state of the language-of-thought debate, and I need a rigorous conceptual document rather than a conventional survey. Specifically, I want the joint statement that could emerge from a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two camps: (A) classical syntactic compositionalists, who maintain that cognition requires a language-like mental format with discrete, structurally combinable symbols and explicit rules of combination; and (B) distributed representationalists, who hold that cognitive architecture operates over continuous, high-dimensional vector spaces and that apparent productivity and systematicity are emergent properties of learned dynamics rather than manifestations of token-level syntax.

Please draft this joint statement in formal, polished prose suitable for an educated but non-specialist readership. Structure it in three parts.

Part One: A numbered section of agreed claims. List the empirical and formal points both sides accept—such as the reality of productivity and systematicity in human reasoning, the necessity for any adequate architecture to support genuinely novel combinations, the relevance of working-memory constraints to representational format, and the fact that contemporary artificial neural networks can now perform certain compositional-generalization tasks. Phrase each claim with enough precision that neither camp would reject it, and avoid framing that presupposes one side's interpretation.

Part Two: A disagreements table. For each camp, present in wording that its own proponents would accept: (i) the core claim about representational format; (ii) the interpretation of the agreed phenomena listed in Part One; and (iii) the assessment of whether current neural-network successes in compositional tasks constitute genuine implementation of compositional rules or instead represent sophisticated interpolation over high-dimensional training distributions.

Part Three: Cruxes and predictive tests. Identify at least two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. Focus on: (1) whether working-memory capacity limits reflect discrete symbol-slot constraints or continuous resource-allocation dynamics; and (2) whether out-of-distribution compositional generalization in trained systems demonstrates implemented syntactic rules or advanced pattern completion. For each crux, specify a concrete, established empirical paradigm—such as novel compound-noun interpretation in cognitive development, or controlled generalization benchmarks in artificial architectures—that both sides agree is genuinely relevant, and record each camp's explicit prediction about what that paradigm should reveal if its view is correct.

Conclude with a brief, reasoned judgment about whether any portion of the dispute is purely verbal rather than substantive: specify which disagreements concern real differences in expected empirical outcomes and which reduce to terminological preferences about the word "representation" or "rule."

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific works by title or author. The statement should stand entirely on its reasoning and be formatted as a document both camps could attach their names to. Write from the voice of a journalist commissioning this text for publication.
