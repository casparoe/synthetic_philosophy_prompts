Below is the outline and central argument of an essay I am drafting on Scanlon’s contractualism and the aggregation problem. I want your critical comments on three points: first, whether my reconstruction of the anti-aggregation reasoning is accurate; second, whether the distinction I draw between cardinal and modal proximity is philosophically fruitful or merely obscurantist; and third, whether the application in the illustrative case succeeds or collapses into a disguised form of utilitarian summation.

The essay begins with the familiar contractualist claim that an act is wrong if permitted by a principle that someone could reasonably reject, and that this framework resists straightforward aggregation because the person who bears a severe burden can reject any principle that sacrifices her for smaller benefits distributed elsewhere. Rather than treating this resistance as a bare intuition, I propose that it tracks what I call modal proximity—the structural nearness, in terms of feasible institutional pathways, of a world in which a particular claim is satisfied—rather than cardinal proximity, which measures only the magnitude of welfare at stake.

My novel framing represents obligation structures as a “contractual network”: agents are nodes, admissible principles are edges, and the modal distance of a claim is the shortest path length to institutional resolution. I argue that when the network is symmetric and fully connected, aggregating claims across nodes can be contractually permissible, because the collective modal proximity of many distant claims may match that of a single direct claim, without asking anyone to bear a burden they could reasonably reject.

In the illustrative case, ten agents each face an independent 10 percent risk of catastrophic harm, while one agent faces a certain moderate harm. Standard contractualist reasoning rejects imposing the certain moderate harm on the single agent to avert the ten low-probability catastrophes. I contend, however, that if the obligation network linking these agents is uniform, the ten distant catastrophic claims collectively achieve a modal proximity comparable to the single direct claim, thereby allowing a principled, non-utilitarian aggregation.

Please address especially whether this “contractual network” machinery genuinely extends Scanlon’s view or illegitimately imports formal apparatus that destroys its normative core. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citation verification—only conceptual assessment.

Please finish with a single concluding line, standing alone, that gives a one-sentence summary of your overall evaluation.
