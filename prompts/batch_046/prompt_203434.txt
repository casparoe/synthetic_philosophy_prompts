Please describe the most important implications of Epicurus's argument that death is not harmful to the one who dies—specifically the version that denies harm in the absence of a living subject—if we take it seriously in the context of rational long-horizon planning. I am writing this for a philosophy of death seminar, and I want to move beyond the standard discussions of fear and grief. Instead, consider an agent whose well-being is partly constituted by long-term projects, such as scientific research, artistic work, or institutional reform, that are explicitly designed to extend beyond her lifespan. If being dead means there is no subject present to experience the deprivation of an unfinished project, does accepting Epicurus's reasoning dissolve the agent's specific reason to preserve her own life for the sake of completing those projects? Or can those reasons be reconstructed solely in terms of present experience and obligations to living collaborators? In particular, address whether the argument forces an asymmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence that undermines project-based rationality, and whether any remaining justification for risk-averse self-preservation must abandon narrative or achievement-based goods and rely only on hedonistic or present-centered values.
