In my philosophy of character reading group we've hit a wall with the situationist critique and nobody's moved in months. We all agree on the basic empirical picture: cross-situational correlations for traits like honesty or courage are low, people act differently at work than with thier family, and you can't reliably predict behavior from a global personality score. The situationists say this kills virtue ethics because virtues are supposed to be stable, cross-contextual dispositions. The virtue ethicists don't deny the data but say virtues were never meant to be robot-like global traits; they're more like local skills or context-sensitive patterns that can be honest-in-business and honest-with-family in different ways.

But then we just loop. The situationists say that's moving the goalposts: if the 'same' virtue behaves totally differently across domains, you've basically replaced one trait with a cluster of local norms and slapped the word 'virtue' on it. The virtue side says the situationists are secretly committed to a behaviorist standard of explanation that rules out any mental state that isn't perfectly consistent. I think part of the problem is that we're mixing up two things: how often a trait manifests, and whether it would still guide action if the context shifted slightly. Maybe that's a hidden distinction doing the work — something like manifest frequency versus counterfactual stability — but I'm not sure if that's a real philosophical move or just me importing machinery from somewhere else.

So what's actually going on here? Is this a verbal dispute about what 'virtue' has to mean? Is it a substantive disagreement about what counts as a good psychological explanation — citing a stable disposition versus citing local norms? Or are we answering different questions altogether, with one side asking 'can we predict behavior?' and the other asking 'how should we educate character?' And if you had to say, is there anything that could break the stall — a formal result, a conceptual distinction that separates these two standards, or just accepting the question is ill-posed?
