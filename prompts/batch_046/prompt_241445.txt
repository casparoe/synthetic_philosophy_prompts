Hey — journalist here covering science and labor stuff. I've been notcing a weird pattern in my reporting on automatoin and I don't know if there's already a name for it.

Take these two cases. In some fully automated warehouses, human staff stay on payroll not to do the picking — robots do that — but to sit at monitors, sign off on robot logs, and attend safety briefings about machines they never touch. The goods move without them, but if you removed them the company would still have to invent a human "oversight" layer for regulatory and union reasons. Second case: writers using AI to draft articles now spend half their day "curating" outputs and tweaking prompts, even though the text generation is entirely model-driven. They still call it writing, and they defend the profession's value, but the causal chain from thier keystrokes to the published words is basically gone.

So here's the phenomenon I'm trying to pin down: automation eats the productive core of a job, but instead of the job vanishing, the human part gets redefined as a kind of ceremonial wrapper — monitoring, certifying, or curating — that keeps the social category of "worker" intact even though the worker is no longer in the causal loop of production. It's not exactly deskilling, since the skills are different (dashboard literacy, prompt tweaking), and it's not the bullshit-jobs idea either, because the wrapper serves real legal or institutional functions. Is there an established concept in philosophy of work for this specific thing — labor that survives its own automation by becoming a ritual preservation of the worker's social role? And if not, how would you draw its edges: does it cover teachers who now mainly manage AI tutoring logs, or is that a different beast? I don't need a bibliography, just want to know if I'm describing something real that has a name, and where it stops being this and starts being something else.
