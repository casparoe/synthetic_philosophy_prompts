I have been reflecting on a problem that seems to appear in both archaeological interpretation and the philosophy of science. When scholars study artifacts from a culture whose language, rituals, and social structures are permanently lost, they often lack the contextual information that would allow them to identify the ontology of that culture's beliefs—what entities, powers, or relations its members actually took to exist. From this, some theorists derive a general principle: ontology identification is only legitimate when the investigator enjoys complete or near-complete access to the relevant domain; wherever such access is absent, any claim about what exists is not a discovery but a projection of the investigator's own conceptual scheme. If this principle holds, it appears to threaten not only archaeological interpretation but also scientific realism about entities such as quark flavors or dark matter structures, since physicists also lack direct observational access to these posits. I want to understand whether this principle is defensible. Does the inaccessibility of contextual information genuinely block all ontology identification, or is there a coherent distinction between legitimate inferential identification and arbitrary conceptual projection? Furthermore, if the principle collapses, what exactly distinguishes an inference that genuinely identifies an ontology—whether concerning ancestral rituals or subatomic structures—from one that merely invents it?
