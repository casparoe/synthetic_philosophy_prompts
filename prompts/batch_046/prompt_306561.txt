ok so im drafting this novel about a ghostwriter who learns ethics by forging biographies, and i realized i have strong gut verdicts on which real lives actually teach you something vs just sit there. plutarhs cato—insufferable guy, but his integrity sticks with you, so that counts as instructive. a hagiography where every flaw is airbrushed out feels like eating air, zero lesson. then this corrupt mayor biography where the writer clearly adores him; i came away understanding ambition better but feeling dirty, so thats a yes but a different kind. a genuinely good but boring person? feels like homework, not real moral learning. but an obscure 19th-century reformer who kept failing at virtue and kept trying feels more useful than any perfect saint. heres the one i keep flipping on: a revolutionary who killed people for liberation—sometimes i think it teaches about moral cost, sometimes it feels like an excuse for atrocity. is there a single principle behind what makes a life morally instructive—maybe flaw visibility plus narrative distance—or do i have to drop one of these verdicts? and what does whatever principle you land on predict for a biography of a charming liar who never gets caught, written by an author who admires the lying?
