Consider the widespread practice in biopics and historical fiction of inventing private emotional reactions, intimate conversations, and internal conflicts for real deceased individuals, particularly when no primary source records those mental states, whilst simultaneously marketing the work as being “based on a true story” and including disclaimers that “some events have been dramatised for narrative effect”. By contrast, documentary ethics usually requires that reconstructions of unrecorded moments be clearly marked as speculative, and legal protections against defamation often extend weakly or not at all to the dead. What theory of moral obligation to the dead, and what conception of representational fidelity, does this differential treatment presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it assume that the dead lack interests that can be harmed by misrepresentation of their psychology; that artistic licence is ethically weightier than epistemic accuracy regarding inner life; or that there is a principled boundary between “public record” (which must be respected) and “private consciousness” (which may be freely invented)? Assess whether these commitments are consistent with the same culture’s norms around grave desecration, posthumous privacy, or the preservation of personal archives. Finally, say what would have to change in dramatization norms if we replaced the implicit view with one that treats misrepresenting a deceased person’s mental life as a harm comparable to misrepresenting a living person’s character. Write your answer in British English.
