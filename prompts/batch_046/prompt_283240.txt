I am a high school junior taking an introductory philosophy elective, and in our recent unit on early twentieth-century ethics my classmates and I have been examining G. E. Moore's account of intrinsic value, particularly his claim that conscious personal affection and the direct appreciation of beauty belong among the highest goods, and his related idea that the value of a complex state—what he termed an organic unity—depends on the specific relational structure among its parts rather than on the simple aggregation of pleasant elements. For an upcoming presentation to my school's philosophy club, I want to construct a rigorous thought experiment that applies his framework to phenomena that could not have existed in his lifetime, since our discussion has tended to stay at the level of familiar examples like friendship between two people or the experience of viewing a painting in a gallery.

Specifically, I am asking you to imagine that Moore is somehow present in the present day and is asked to evaluate two technological developments that seem to challenge the conditions he took to be necessary for these intrinsic goods. The first is the production of visually compelling artworks by generative adversarial networks that lack any human emotional origin, intention, or lived experience, yet are capable of evoking profound aesthetic responses in viewers. The second is the emergence of algorithmically mediated companionship, in which recommendation systems cultivate sustained parasocial bonds between human users and synthetic personas that adapt dynamically to emotional needs but possess no consciousness, no genuine reciprocity, and no capacity for affection of their own. Given Moore's emphasis on the non-natural character of goodness, his doctrine of organic unity, and his particular insistence that friendship requires a form of conscious, reciprocal emotional engagement, how would he likely assess whether these technological substitutes can genuinely instantiate the values he prized?

More precisely, I would like you to determine whether Moore would regard an AI-generated image that produces authentic human awe as possessing intrinsic aesthetic value in the same manner as a human-created work, and whether he would classify an optimized, algorithmically sustained relationship as a form of friendship in the morally relevant sense, or rather as a structurally deficient simulation that lacks the essential relational properties required for organic unity. Please ground your answer in his actual conceptual apparatus—especially the distinction between intrinsic and instrumental value, the role of consciousness and reciprocity, and the importance of appropriate part-whole relations—rather than offering a generic modern commentary about artificial intelligence.
