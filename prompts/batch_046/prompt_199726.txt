I'm a high school senior taking an introductory philosophy course, and after our unit on philosophy of mind I tried to write out my own position before next week's seminar on artificial consciousness. In conversations with friends I keep finding myself defending claims that feel compatible in isolation, but I want to check whether the whole package actually holds together.

Here is where I have landed: (1) I am a functionalist about mental states in general—what makes a belief or desire mental is its causal role, not the physical stuff that realizes it. (2) I accept multiple realizability, so in principle the same cognitive architecture could run on silicon, photonic circuits, or a sufficiently organized distributed network. (3) At the same time, I think phenomenal consciousness—what it is actually like to feel pain or see red—requires continuous biological integration; I imagine a gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement with silicon chips, and I suspect that if the process finished with a fully synthetic brain, subjective experience would have faded out or vanished, even though behavior and cognition remained perfect. (4) I also hold that the explanatory gap between functional descriptions and subjective feel is real, but I treat it as an epistemic limit rather than proof that consciousness is non-functional. (5) From this I draw an ethical conclusion: if a system is functionally isomorphic to a conscious human, we should grant it moral consideration regardless of substrate, because refusing to do so based on an unobservable inner life risks a kind of moral solipsism. (6) Finally, I reject the Chinese Room argument; I think understanding is a system-level property, not something possessed by the individual symbol-manipulator inside it.

Can you audit this set for consistency? I am especially unsure whether my biological restriction on consciousness in (3) conflicts with my functionalism in (1) and my multiple-realizability claim in (2), or whether those three can be separated cleanly. I also worry that (3) and (5) sit badly together: if silicon duplicates lack phenomenal consciousness, why should functional isomorphism alone generate moral obligations? Meanwhile, I wonder if (4) and (6) only seem to clash—perhaps the explanatory gap looks like it gives the Chinese Room its force, but if the gap is purely epistemic it might actually undermine that argument rather than support it. Please go through the pairs, rank the tensions by severity, distinguish the genuine conflicts from the merely apparent ones, and tell me what the least costly revision would be in each case. I am not signaling which commitment I would most want to preserve; I just want to know where the pressure points are and whether the package can be saved with a single strategic adjustment or whether it requires giving up more than one piece.
