I am drafting a novel in which a character defending performance legitimacy—citing the sustained prosperity and social order of regimes such as China and Singapore—deploys the familiar analogy of the aircraft: citizens are passengers, the authoritarian state is the pilot, democratic demands are attempts to seize the controls, and the smooth arrival at the destination (wealth, stability) proves the pilot’s right to command the cockpit without consultation. The analogy is meant to licence the claim that prosperity and order alone justify suppressing democratic mechanisms, because the passenger who accepts the flight implicitly accepts the pilot’s authority. I wish you to audit this comparison with precision. First, provide an explicit correspondence table—passenger, pilot, flight path, cockpit, arrival, turbulence, and so forth mapped onto citizen, regime, policy trajectory, governance institutions, economic outcomes, dissent. Second, isolate the disanalogies that bear on the argument: can a citizen ‘disembark’ as a passenger can? Is prosperity a single, agreed-upon destination, or is it distributed unevenly like legroom? Does the analogy falsely import voluntarism—passengers choose the airline, whereas citizens inherit their state? Third, determine whether the conclusion that authoritarian rule is justified by performance survives these fractures, or whether the analogy was carrying the moral weight all along. Please answer in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to works I might not have to hand; rely instead on conceptual analysis.
