I'm taking a course on rational choise theory and my freind and I got stuck on Pascal's mugging last night. We tried to extend it into belief formation and now I can't find where the argument falls apart. Here is the chain that keeps bugging me:

(1) A rational agent should maximize expected utility when picking actions. (2) In Pascal's mugging, a stranger offers me a trillion dollars if I hand over my wallet, with a credance of 10^-25 that they're actually a benevolent AI testing me. (3) Because the payout is enormous, the expected value is still huge even with that tiny probability, so I should hand over the wallet. (4) Now apply the same logic to belief formation: imagine there's a 10^-30 chance that a hidden cosmic evaluator rewards infinite utility if I sincerely adopt belief P, regardless of the evidence. (5) The expected utility of believing P then dominates disbelieving, no matter what the evidence says. (6) So rational belief-formation is just Pascal's mugging applied to doxastic states—any claim can be made rational to believe if you attach a big enough payoff and a nonzero credance. (7) But then rationality demands I believe contradictory claims whenever different reward structures are proposed, which is absurd. (8) Therefore, either expected-utility maximization is broken for belief, or there's a hidden restriction I'm missing.

My freind says the mistake is between (3) and (4), that belief isn't voluntary like handing over a wallet. I think that's too quick—James and others treat belief as partly voluntary, and we do have epistemic goals like accuracy. So which step actually slips, and what does rejecting it cost? Do we have to cap utility, treat sub-threshold probabilities as literally zero, or deny that belief has expected utility at all? I'd especially like to know if fixing it forces us to change how we think about action too, or if belief and action can be separated cleanly.
