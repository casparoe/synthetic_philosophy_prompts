i've been working out this hybrid view on biological function and i want to stress-test it. my take is that straight etiological accounts are right about origin but wrong to ignore when a trait gets totally co-opted — exaptation should overwrite function if the trait is now locked into a developmentally necessary feedback loop. but pure causal-role views are too permissive, letting accidental benefits count. so here's my rule: a trait has proper function F if either (1) selection history explains its presence, or (2) its current contribution is structurally irreplaceable in the organism's regulatory architecture — but not both. if both apply, the etiological story takes precedance and the causal-role is just gravy. that means exaptations can earn new functions only when the old selection story is genuinely dead and replaced.

what would actually count against this? not just the heart-pumping-blood case or the heart-murmur accidental-benefit case. i want the specific conceptual moves and biological examples that break my "not both at once" clause, or show that "structurally irreplaceable" is just something i can always claim after the fact. also, what do committed etiological theorists themselves admit is a weak spot that my hybrid fixes? and what do causal-role defenders concede looks like a real problem for them? rank the defeaters: which ones are fatal (make the hybrid collapse into one side or become circular), which are merely awkward (need a patch but don't kill the view), and is there any risk this is too well-insulated — that every counterexample can be redescribed as "the old function is dead" or "it's not really irreplaceable"? i'm especially curious whether functional redundancy in gene networks is fatal or just awkward for the irreplaceability condition.
