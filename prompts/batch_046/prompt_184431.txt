I'm taking this course on formal epistemology and we got into a debate about higher-order evidence that I can't shake. Me and a friend were arguing over coffee about whether epistemic akrasia is ever rational, and he walked me into this argument that now feels like it should work but ends up destroying belief altogether. Here it is, numbered so I don't lose track:

(1) If you have first-order evidence E for p, but also higher-order evidence H that your reasoning about p is currently unreliable, then rationality requires you to discount E by however much H undermines it.
(2) Any first-order belief can be paired with some H — fatigue, cognitive bias, unknown unknowns about your own calibration — so there's always *some* availible underminer in your evidental set.
(3) But rationality doesn't let you ignore available evidence. So if H is present, you must apply the discount.
(4) Once you discount E by H, your confidence in p drops. But now you have a new first-order belief: that p is less likely. Call this p'.
(5) The same H applies to your reasoning about p', since you're the same unreliable agent evaluating the discount.
(6) So you must apply H again to p', lowering confidence further.
(7) This creates an infinite regress of discounting — call it "calibration collapse" — driving every belief to zero confidence unless you can block it.
(8) The only blocker is refusing to apply H to the meta-level, but that's just special pleading: you're treating your self-assessment as imune from the very unreliability it reports.
(9) Therefore either you accept universal epistemic bankruptcy, or you reject (1) and allow akratic belief — holding p firmly while knowing you're probably wrong about it.
(10) But universal bankruptcy is unliveable, and akrasia is supposed to be irrational. So we're stuck.

My friend says this proves higher-order evidence is either useless or destructive, and we should be "first-order fundamentalists." I want to keep (1) — it feels right when I'm tired and still think I'm right about some math proof — but I also want to avoid (9). Where does the slide happen? Is there an equivocation between "apply H" in (3) and "apply H again" in (5)? Or is the real problem that (2) smuggles in an availability heuristic about unknown biases? I feel like there's a scope shift with "available" but I can't pin it down. What's the least costly premise to drop, and what do I pay?
