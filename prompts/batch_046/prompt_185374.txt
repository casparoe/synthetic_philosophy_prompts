I'm designing a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy graduate students who have completed a first course in modal logic—they are comfortable with accessibility relations, the distinction between K and T, and the modal statement of Löb's theorem—but who have never studied AI alignment or self-referential reasoning in artificial agents. I want them to understand the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents: specifically, why an agent that can represent and reason about its own future reasoning processes cannot, within a consistent logical framework, assign high internal credence to the claim that its future reasoning will be correct, unless it already assigns high credence to that claim now. Do not explain the obstacle to me; instead, plan how I should explain it.

The plan must specify the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces have to be introduced—moving from familiar modal belief logic, to an explicit agent model with internal representations, to the conditions of Löb's theorem, to the formulation of self-trust—justifying why each layer depends on the previous one and cannot be rearranged. It must identify the single carrying example or scenario that should anchor the session: build this around a graded "trust gradient" in which an agent assigns internal credibility scores to its own submodules and attempts to update those scores using only internal reasoning, not external validation; describe exactly what this example must include (e.g., the agent evaluating its own evaluation function) and what it must avoid (e.g., conflating epistemic trust with practical commitment or collapsing into a binary belief state). It must map the predictable misconceptions—such as conflating the obstacle with Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, assuming probabilistic credence escapes it, interpreting the result as "agents can never be rational about themselves," or treating the self-reference as a paradox rather than a consistency constraint—together with the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted and how. It must include one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the obstacle as a structural constraint on self-modeling rather than as general skepticism about self-knowledge. Finally, it must state explicitly what should be left out at this level—such as formal arithmetic formulations of Löb's conditions, treatments of logical induction, or reflective oracles—and acknowledge that these are being omitted. The prompt should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should not depend on highly specific technical results that a small language model would not have reliably internalized.
