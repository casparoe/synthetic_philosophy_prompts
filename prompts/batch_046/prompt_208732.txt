As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, I have been working through Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation—the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life—and attempting to apply its tripartite distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history to practices in the history and pedagogy of science. Before asking my question, I will lay out four judgments that I believe the framework yields, since my concern is consistency rather than exposition.

First, a university physics curriculum that teaches the history of the discipline solely through the biographies of a handful of revolutionary figures—Galileo, Faraday, Curie, Einstein—treating them as aspirational models for present students to emulate. I take this to be monumental history: it is highly selective, oriented toward inspiring present action, and valuable only insofar as it stimulates life rather than producing paralytic comparison.

Second, an institutional archive that preserves every laboratory notebook, failed apparatus, and rejected manuscript from a research center across two centuries, catalogued with exhaustive precision but never interpreted in relation to current problems. I take this to be antiquarian history: it honors and maintains continuity with the past as an end in itself, risking the suffocation of present creativity under the weight of dead material.

Third, a policy decision to remove all references to eugenics-era studies from university syllabi and to destroy the associated primary records, on the grounds that the past is an unbearable moral burden that must be expelled for present scientific life to flourish. I take this to be critical history: it judges, condemns, and excises part of the past to liberate the present, though it risks leaving current practice rootless and ignorant of its own dangers.

Fourth, a digital humanities project that algorithmically ingests every version of every scientific article—including retracted papers, peer reviews, and supplementary data—making them permanently searchable but never synthesizing them into a narrative or using them for present instruction. I initially read this as antiquarian, because its defining feature is comprehensive preservation without service to present life; yet one might argue it is critical in making hidden failures visible, or monumental in providing raw material for future heroic reconstructions.

These four judgments appear jointly consistent with more than one organizing principle:

- Rule by attitude toward the past: admiration and emulation (monumental), reverence and preservation (antiquarian), judgment and rejection (critical).
- Rule by effect on present vitality: stimulating action and confidence (monumental), maintaining roots and continuity (antiquarian), liberating from an oppressive burden (critical).
- Rule by method of selection: heroic and highly selective (monumental), exhaustive and archival (antiquarian), destructive or suppressive (critical).

Once the pedagogical exercise is complete, the group discards all archival materials related to the theory and forbids its further study in their department, arguing that the lesson has been fully absorbed and that retaining the material risks confusion.
