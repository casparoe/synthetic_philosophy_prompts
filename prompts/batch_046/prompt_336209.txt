Since I started mentoring researchers across a wide age range, I have become personally invested in whether generational labels—Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Gen Z—track anything ontologically real or are merely marketing artifacts with statistical veneers. The debate is well known and apparently frozen: sociologists point to cohort-level differences in risk preference, institutional trust, and digital socialization; skeptics reply that within-cohort variance exceeds between-cohort variance, that birth-year boundaries are administratively arbitrary, and that aging and period effects make pure cohort attribution impossible. Both camps accept the same regression tables, both concede the confounding, and neither has moved in years. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but categorical: one side treats a generation as an attributive kind, requiring shared synchronic properties, while the other treats it as an indexical kind, constituted not by shared traits but by a shared causal-historical position relative to a technological or economic shock—say, the widespread adoption of broadband internet or the 2008 financial crisis—making the cohort diachronically scattered, like a metaphysical object distributed across time and biography rather than across space. Is this diagnosis correct, or is the disagreement verbal—one side using "generation" for predictive clustering in organizational analytics, the other for metaphysical natural-kind status—without anyone noticing? Could the sides be answering different questions, one about explanatory utility and one about ontological grounding, under the single heading "are generations real"? Or is there a hidden formal disagreement about what conditions license social-kind membership that neither side has made explicit? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide your reasoning with no preamble and no restating of my question; in particular, tell me whether introducing a distinction between indexical cohorts (defined by shared causal origin) and attributive cohorts (defined by shared traits) would actually unlock progress, or whether the impasse reflects bedrock intuitions about naturalness in the social domain that no conceptual distinction can resolve.
