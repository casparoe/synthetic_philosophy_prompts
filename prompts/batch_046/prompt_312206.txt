I'm a philosophy professor who serves on an advisory panel for a regional hospital consortium's clinical-ethics review board. The board meets monthly for ninety minutes to approve updates to AI-driven triage and resource-allocation protocols; its members are physicians, administrators, and a patient advocate, none with formal philosophical training, and they must sign off on whether an algorithm's embedded ethical reasoning is acceptable for deployment. Recently I have observed that they either rubber-stamp vendor documentation or reject any system producing unequal outcomes, lacking any disciplined method to ask whether observed disparity tracks legitimate clinical priority or unexamined bias embedded in training data.

Please design a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, explicit pass-or-stop thresholds, and clearly marked points where judgment must replace computation—that operationalizes one specific philosophical constraint. The constraint is this: a permissible clinical algorithm may aggregate welfare across patients, for instance by optimizing quality-adjusted life years, only if it simultaneously maintains a separate register for what I will call moral residue—the non-compensable ethical cost borne by each individual who is deprioritized, even when the aggregate choice is correct. The system need not always be blocked by such residue, but an architecture that dissolves every individual cost into a single utility score fails a necessary condition of ethical reasoning, regardless of its efficiency.

The procedure must be executable by a board member within the meeting's time constraints. For each step, specify the question in plain language; the evidence or documentation required; whether the answer is factual, interpretive, or normative; the conditions for a hard stop that yields rejection; and the precise location where the procedure hands the decision back to the board because the issue is one of values rather than verifiable design. The procedure should distinguish three things: statistical disparity that reflects legitimate clinical priority versus disparity that reflects historical data bias; transparent, auditable trade-off rules versus opaque weightings concealed in model architecture; and acknowledged, tracked moral residue versus residue that is suppressed by aggregation.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals. First, a sepsis-prediction model that allocates ICU beds by predicted survival probability with no separate reporting of treatable patients who are deprioritized. Second, a vaccination-priority algorithm that weights age and comorbidity but also logs every delayed healthier patient, flagging these instances for post-hoc human review. Third, a palliative-care triage tool that refuses to rank patients at all, distributing resources by lottery to avoid aggregate comparison—an approach the board will likely welcome, but which the procedure should evaluate critically and reject if warranted, giving an answer the board is likely to resist.

Finally, be explicit about the limits of the procedure: where it will misfire, which philosophical disagreements it obscures—particularly whether moral residue is genuinely distinct from ordinary utilitarian regret—and whether the requirement to register residue represents a settled principle in machine ethics or a contested theoretical commitment drawn from broader moral philosophy. Separate what is widely accepted among specialists from what depends on the particular framework I have outlined, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the board needs an instrument for the meeting room, not a syllabus.
