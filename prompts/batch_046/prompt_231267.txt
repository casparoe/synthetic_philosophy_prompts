I am writing a dissertation chapter on the architecture of non-Western normative theories, and I need to work through a conceptual tension in Buddhist ethics that introductory treatments usually flatten into the question of whether Buddhism is consequentialist. The tradition assigns primary moral weight to cetanā—intention or volition—so that the karmic quality of an act depends on the mental state that accompanies it, which points toward an agent-centered, deontic structure. At the same time, the Mahāyāna bodhisattva ideal is defined by karuṇā and the commitment to reduce suffering for all sentient beings, which seems to require outcome-sensitive reasoning that aggregates welfare across beings in a way structurally similar to consequentialist calculation. Rather than asking for a simple classification, I want to examine whether Buddhist ethical reasoning operates with what I am calling a double-register evaluation: one register tracking the purity or defilement of the agent’s mental continuum (the karmic register), and another tracking the actual change in sentient beings’ welfare (the karuṇā register). Please explain the conceptual relationship between these two registers. In particular, can they be integrated without one collapsing into the other? If a bodhisattva faces a situation—such as the classic upāya-kauśalya cases involving deliberate deception—where an action promises substantial welfare gains but is accompanied by a mental state that is technically defiled, does the karuṇā register ever have lexical or even threshold priority over the karmic register, or does Buddhist ethics maintain a strict lexical priority of mental purity? Furthermore, is it coherent to model this interaction as a form of embedded consequentialism, in which consequentialist reasoning is permitted only within boundaries fixed by the deontic register, or does that framing misrepresent the metaphysical relationship between karma and compassion? I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I need a conceptual reconstruction that distinguishes what the practice of Buddhist ethical reasoning strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and that identifies precisely where the framework is philosophically underdetermined.
