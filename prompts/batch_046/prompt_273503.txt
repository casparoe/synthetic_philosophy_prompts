I'm writing a long-form piece on the tacit epistemology embedded in scientific institutions, and in conversations with a philosopher of science colleague I kept returning to a tension I hadn't fully articulated. In most disciplines, peer-reviewed journals employ double-blind review specifically to suppress information about authors' identities, institutional affiliations, and prior reputations, treating those factors as contaminating influences on the evaluation of evidence and argument. Yet the very same fields organize hiring, promotion, funding allocation, and invited speaking around metrics—citation counts, institutional prestige, prior grant success—that are almost entirely reputation-based. The bifurcation is not accidental; it is routinely defended as protecting the integrity of belief-formation about specific claims, while allowing reputation to serve as a practical heuristic for distributing scarce resources and predicting future productivity.

What philosophical commitments about the right and wrong kinds of reasons for belief does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does double-blind review presuppose that an author's identity and track record supply reasons of the wrong kind for accepting a particular scientific claim—analogous to the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem in ethics and epistemology, where non-epistemic incentives corrupt the authority of a judgment? Or can the split be explained without any deep view about epistemic normativity—say, as a pragmatic division of labor between belief-formation and resource-allocation? Say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a reliabilist about testimony consistently defend both the suppression of identity in manuscript review and its celebration in tenure committees? And tell me what would have to change in institutional practice if we replaced the implicit theory with a straightforwardly probabilistic view, in which an author's prior reliability is always treated as a legitimate Bayesian prior for evaluating their new claims, with no special firewall between evaluation and prediction.

In your response, please separate what is widely settled—about how double-blind review actually operates, or about the standard formulation of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem—from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and sociologists of science, and do not present a contested interpretation of either peer-review norms or epistemic normativity as though it were the consensus.
