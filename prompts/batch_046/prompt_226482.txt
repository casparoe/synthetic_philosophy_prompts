I sit on the clinical ethics comittee at Riverside General. We have a case thats kind of stuck in the weeds: patient M.R., 62, wrote an advance directive in 2019 saying that if she ever lost the narrative thread that makes her herself she should not receive artificial nutrition. Now she has moderate Alzheimer's but gets these long lucid windows where she insists she is still the same person, just slower, and she wants the feeding tube if it becomes necessary. Her daughter argues the 2019 document is a binding decision made by her real self; her partner argues that forcing the present M.R. to honor an ontology of selfhood she now explicitly rejects amounts to metaphysical coercion.

What complicates this further is that our hospital recently adopted an internal decision framework — barely used anywhere else — that weights advance directives using diachronic coherence scores rather than treating them as absolute. The score is meant to measure consistency between the directive's underlying theory of personal identity and the patient's current expressed values, but the scoring manual is vague and nobody on the comittee knows how to apply it without just importing our own philosophical commitments.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursdays meeting. It should reccomend a concrete course of action with a clear time frame — approve the tube, deny it, or approve with conditions — and it needs to engage the daughter's contract-like view seriously rather than dismissing it. At the same time, address whether this coherence-score framework actually resolves the disagreement, or if it just disguises our metaphysical differences behind pseudomath. The memo should be something the board can vote on, not a literature review, and it should avoid pretending the question of personal identity is settled.
