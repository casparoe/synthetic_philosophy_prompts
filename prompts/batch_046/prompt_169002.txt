As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been trying to understand why the debate over whether death is bad for you — the classic Epicurean argument that death cannot harm the dead because there is no subject present to suffer it, set against the deprivation view that death robs one of future goods — seems to have reached a complete impasse. In discussions I have followed, both sides concede that posthumous non-existence involves no phenomenal experience, and both accept that we can speak coherently about the lives one might have led; yet one camp treats harm as necessarily requiring an instantiated subject (as though a fault can only be logged by a running process), while the other treats harm as a relational deficit between an actual life-trajectory and its counterfactual continuation (as though we can evaluate a terminated process against its uncompleted output). My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical disagreement about mortality, nor merely a clash of intuitions about naturalness, but a hidden conceptual dispute about the ontology of harm: whether it is a state-property tied to present existence or a comparative relation that can hold across the boundary of non-existence. Is that diagnosis correct, or are the sides actually answering different questions under the single word "bad"? Given your analysis, is there any distinction, formal model, or reconceptualisation that could genuinely move the debate, or does the stall indicate that the question is ill-posed — applying evaluative predicates outside the domain in which they have definitional conditions?
