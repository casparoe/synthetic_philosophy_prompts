Please argue that the standard critique of grand historical narratives—exemplified by Jared Diamond's geographic determinism in Guns, Germs, and Steel, Yuval Noah Harari's cognitive-revolution framing in Sapiens, and David Graeber and David Wengrow's counter-narrative of seasonal political experimentation in The Dawn of Everything—misses a deeper structural problem. Specifically, defend the view that these accounts all implicitly treat social scale as an exogenous variable that explains organizational complexity, when in fact scale is an endogenous emergent property of what might be called infrastructural literacy: the capacity of a society to encode organizational protocols in durable, non-biological media, whether ritual calendars, kinship notation, or material storage systems, without requiring a centralized state apparatus. Use this framework to show why Graeber and Wengrow's evidence for flexible, non-hierarchical foraging societies does not refute Diamond's ecological constraints but rather reveals that infrastructural literacy—not agriculture or a singular cognitive leap—was the actual bottleneck determining whether a society could maintain large-scale coordination. Your argument should address the strongest objection: that introducing infrastructural literacy merely renames the agricultural revolution rather than explaining it.
