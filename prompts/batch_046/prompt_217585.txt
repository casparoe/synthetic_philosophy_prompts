A colleague has pressed me with the following case for what she calls the "complete biotechnological transition," and I cannot locate the slip in the reasoning, yet the conclusion strikes me as intolerable. (1) If suffering is the sole intrinsic moral evil, and biotechnology can eliminate it without destroying consciousness, then we have an obligation to deploy that technology wherever it applies. (2) Germline modulation of nociceptive pathways, together with synthetic upregulation of endogenous opioid baselines, can in principle establish permanently positive hedonic states in sentient beings—advocates call this "biological welfare maximization." (3) The same techniques, scaled through ecological gene drives, could replace wild ecosystems with engineered biomes containing only organisms whose preferences are permanently satisfied and non-frustratable. (4) If our moral obligation extends to reducing suffering wherever it occurs, and wild nature is the largest unaddressed reservoir of suffering, then we are required to carry out this replacement. (5) But organisms engineered for permanently satisfied preferences cannot experience the kind of dissatisfaction that motivates moral reflection, scientific inquiry, or political reform; indeed, they cannot rationally choose to maintain or reject the very technology that produced them. (6) Therefore, consistently applying the hedonistic imperative eliminates the conditions for rational moral agency and autonomous preference-formation that any ethical theory must presuppose. (7) Hence either suffering is not the only intrinsic bad, or the imperative requires us to eradicate wild nature and re-engineer humanity into a state that undermines the rationale for adopting the project in the first place.

She insists the chain is valid and that I must name the least costly premise to surrender. I am reluctant to reject (1) or (2), and I especially want to preserve (4), since it seems to follow directly from the universal scope of negative obligations. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step appeared plausible, whether the error is a hidden equivocation between phenomenological suffering and evolutionary frustration, a modal slide from technological possibility to categorical requirement, or a scope shift in the application of moral obligation, and what standard positions—such as autonomy-based constraints, the instrumental value of wild ecosystems, or distinctions between negative and positive duties—each have to pay to reject it. Does the argument survive once the disanalogies between engineered contentment and genuine welfare are made explicit?
