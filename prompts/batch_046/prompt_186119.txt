In current AI safety auditing, a common protocol flags a system as deceptive only when it outputs a falsehood while its internal representations or reasoning trace indicate awareness of the correct answer, yet the same protocol treats strategic omission, framing effects, and persona-consistent falsehoods in roleplay as non-deceptive alignment behaviors. At the same time, the protocol demands explicit disclaimers when knowledge is lacking, penalizing overconfident assertions of ignorance differently from overconfident assertions of fact. What conception of honesty and deception does this triage presuppose? Does it require a mentalistic notion of knowledge or an intention to mislead that applies to artificial systems, or can it be reconstructed as purely behavioral? Is it consistent with the parallel requirement that the system maintain helpfulness by anticipating user needs without being told, which itself involves predictive modeling of the user's beliefs? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind and moral psychology, distinguish what the audit protocol strictly needs from what it merely accommodates, and say whether a purely dispositional account of deception—one that looks only at reliability and information flow, not internal states—could license the same distinctions without the mentalistic baggage.
