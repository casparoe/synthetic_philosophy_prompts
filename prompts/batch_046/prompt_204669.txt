I have recently begun mentoring first-year graduate students in a professional ethics program, and I have been repeatedly struck by how consistently their expressed moral judgments shift in response to minor contextual variations—seating arrangements, the presence of authority figures, or even the framing of case studies—despite their sincere commitment to cultivating stable virtues. This has made the situationist critique of virtue ethics personally urgent, yet the standard exchange feels circular: defenders respond by weakening virtues into context-sensitive dispositions, while critics reply that this evacuates the normative and explanatory force that made virtue ethics attractive. I am applying for a six-month research grant from a small philosophy-of-education foundation and need a polished, two-page proposal that frames a tractable alternative. The precise question: can virtue ethics be reconstructed not by abandoning stability, but by treating moral character as a second-order capacity to discern which contextual features are morally salient—rather than as a first-order tendency to behave uniformly across contexts? The proposal should explain why both the standard contextualist defense and the standard situationist attack leave this second-order reconception untouched, outline a month-by-month method combining conceptual analysis of moral attention with pilot reflection protocols drawn from my mentoring practice, specify what success would look like (a framework distinguishing virtue-preserving sensitivity from virtue-eroding situational drift), and give an honest account of failure—especially if the second-order capacity collapses back into either global-trait psychology or pure situationism. Please do not request a bibliography or literature review by citation; I will integrate sources myself. The scope should be realistic for a single researcher over one semester plus summer.
