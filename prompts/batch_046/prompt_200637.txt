I'm a journalist covering science and philosophy of mind, and I need a short story to illustrate Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of cognitive architectures—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—for readers familiar with the names but not with their structural differences. Write a narrative about a single engineered organism constructed to move through all four stages in sequence: first acting on hardwired evolutionary responses, then learning only through direct reinforcement, then developing an internal predictive model that lets it test actions in simulation rather than in reality, and finally acquiring language and external symbolic tools that restructure its cognition. The conceptual twist should be that each transition permanently disables the prior capacity—when Popperian simulation emerges, the Skinnerian reward pathways atrophy beyond recovery; when Gregorian language and culture are acquired, direct experiential memory is replaced entirely by mediated records—so that the final form can articulate its lost capacities but can no longer inhabit them. Tell the story from the perspective of the designer-observer who built these stages and now studies the finished organism, letting the philosophical distinctions emerge through the creature's structural impossibilities and silences rather than through exposition. Keep the four categories precise but embedded wholly in plot, image, and the observer's failed attempts to communicate with earlier versions of the being it once was.
