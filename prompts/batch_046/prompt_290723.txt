I teach a philosophy seminar for about 14 upper-level undergrads, one 75-minute session. I want them to actually feel the problem with dispositions, finks, and masks — not just hear about it secondhand. Specifically, they should come away seeing that trying to check whether a group has some cooperative disposition can itself be the thing that introduces a protective block, so you're left arguing about whether the disposition was ever there in the first place.

Design a classroom game or structured role-play. Seperate the class into small groups with assigned roles: "members" who share a hidden cooperative norm, "testers" who try to trigger that cooperation, and one hidden "reverse-mask" player — a protective factor that only activates when people think they're being observed, meaning the group behaves one way under direct testing and another when they think no one's watching. Use point-based stakes (no real money) so they actually care about the outcome. The rules should be arranged so the testers run a "trigger" task, hit the block, and then have to debate whether the failure proves there's no disposition or just proves there's a mask. Don't use the usual poison-and-vase example from the textbooks; I want this to feel like a fresh case they haven't seen in every intro metaphysics course.
