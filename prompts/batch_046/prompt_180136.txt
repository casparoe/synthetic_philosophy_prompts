Design a taxonomy—formatted as a branching decision tree—for a metaphysics seminar on whether entities or structures about which we have in principle inaccessible information should be included in our ontology. Root the tree with the question: “Should ontology commit us to entities (or structures) that we can never, even in principle, come to observe or measure?” From there, build branch nodes from concrete diagnostic commitments rather than from position-names: (i) Is the inaccessibility causal and permanent (e.g., beyond an event horizon or in causally disconnected regions) or merely practical/temporary? (ii) If causal, does the best overall theory require those entities for explanatory coherence, or only for predictive adequacy? (iii) Does ontological identification require token-level identifiability of particulars, or is type-level structural identification sufficient? (iv) Does the inaccessibility concern concrete entities, or only relational/structural information? Each leaf should be a named view—e.g., scientific realism about unobservables, constructive empiricism, ontic structural realism, a deflationary “pragmatic ontology” position—or marked “unnamed” if the combination is unoccupied, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). Specifically include leaves that separate strong realists who accept token commitments beyond horizons from structural realists who reject token commitments but keep structure, and from empiricists who reject both. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate (for instance, if two positions share all answers at every node). Keep the tree to about six to eight leaves, phrase every node as a question answerable without knowing the labels, and note which logical combinations remain empty. The audience is graduate students; do not ask for bibliographies or citations.
