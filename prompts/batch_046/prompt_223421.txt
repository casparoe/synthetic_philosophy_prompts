I'm auditing a graduate seminar on the political economy of development, and a recurring argument broke out between two students, Mira and Leo, over whether we can ever genuinely know why some nations are rich. The debate quickly moved past the familiar triad of institutions, geography, and culture to a deeper disagreement about the limits of causal inference from historical evidence. Mira holds that because history never runs experiments twice, any claim to have isolated institutional causes is epistemically hollow; Leo maintains that comparative methods and long-run persistence arguments let us recover stable causal structures despite the absence of true counterfactuals. They asked me to referee, and since I genuinely cannot tell where the burden of proof should lie, I am asking you to adjudicate.

Mira opened by attacking the canonical Korea comparison: the division was not randomly assigned but selected by great-power bargaining, so treating North and South Korea as an institutional natural experiment violates the exclusion restriction. Leo conceded the selection problem but replied that researchers do not rely on single cases; they combine instrumental strategies linking colonial settlement patterns to present institutional quality—with process tracing through legal origins—thereby identifying persistent mechanisms rather than momentary correlations. Mira countered that these instruments capture local average treatment effects embedded in specific technological and imperial regimes; the causal pathway from colonial conditions to modern governance operates through intermediate institutions that may not be stable once global markets and state capacities change, so one is explaining historical variance, not modern richness. Leo answered that this objection misses the point of path dependence: if legal and political institutions are genuinely sticky, then identifying their origin is sufficient to explain current outcomes, because the mechanism is precisely that past structures constrain present possibilities. Mira then pressed that this assumes mechanism stability across centuries without being able to verify it—there is no observable counterfactual in which Britain industrialized under extractive colonial institutions—so the argument collapses into a just-so story dressed in regression tables. Leo retorted that by this standard all historical social science is impossible, and suggested that we can at least test institutional explanations out of sample by examining cases where geographic endowments shift without corresponding institutional change, or vice versa; but Mira closed by noting that such tests are circular, since the capacity to exploit geographic endowments is itself institutionally mediated, meaning the sample selection already embeds the hypothesis being tested. Leo finally proposed that perhaps the disagreement is not about identification at all, but about the definition of knowledge: maybe we should abandon the aspiration to uncover transhistorical causal laws and instead treat well-supported institutional narratives as explanatory models whose value lies in coherence and predictive breadth rather than in counterfactual necessity. Mira agreed that this reframing might resolve their stalemate, but insisted that then scholars should stop claiming they have demonstrated that institutions dominate geography, and should instead be explicit that they have constructed plausible, selection-sensitive narratives.

Please work through their exchange round by round, identifying who is right about each specific point—the exclusion restriction in the Korea case, the relevance of local average treatment effects across technological regimes, the legitimacy of path-dependence arguments, the circularity of out-of-sample geographic tests, and the proposed shift from causal-law to explanatory-model standards. Say who is more right overall, and specify what both parties are missing, particularly regarding whether their dispute is ultimately about causal identification, mechanism stability, or the normative standards of proof in historical explanation. In your response, please state how confident you are in this adjudication overall, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
