I'm hosting a colloquium podcast next month and am drafting the interview script for an episode on the gender-equality paradox — the well-documented cross-national pattern in which societies with stronger egalitarian norms and policies frequently exhibit more pronounced occupational gender segregation, especially between STEM fields and caregiving professions. Rather than booking a public commentator, I've invited a philosopher who recently advanced a specific interpretation she calls the reflexive-architecture thesis: she argues that in highly egalitarian contexts, the removal of economic and legal coercion does not expose pre-social biological preferences, as evolutionary-psychology accounts claim, nor does it dissolve cultural stereotypes, as social-constructionist accounts predict. Instead, she contends that gender becomes an object of conscious self-fashioning, and individuals select gender-typed careers not despite expanded autonomy but through it, using occupational choice as a medium for constructing coherent gendered identities precisely because other structural markers have been flattened. I need a sequence of interview questions that forces this position to do real philosophical work rather than letting her retreat into abstraction. Specifically, please draft: first, a question that compels her to distinguish reflexive architecture from the standard preference-revelation model, followed by a sharp follow-up keyed to her likely reply that her preferences are socially mediated rather than biologically hardwired; second, a question pressing how her framework explains the asymmetry between STEM segregation and caregiving segregation across countries without implicitly relying on the very gender stereotypes she claims egalitarianism dissolves, with a follow-up anticipating her move to invoke what she terms representational plasticity; third, a question about the normative stakes — if occupational segregation in egalitarian societies is an autonomous expression rather than a structural injustice, does that weaken the case for policy intervention, and a follow-up that challenges her should she defend the outcome by appealing to the authenticity of choice; and finally, a closing question asking what specific empirical result — for instance, a reversal of the paradox in a particular demographic cohort, or a longitudinal study tracking career choice before and after major egalitarian reform — would genuinely falsify her thesis, given that she currently seems able to accommodate both high and low segregation within her framework. The tone should be collegial but sharp, suitable for an audience familiar with the basic literature but not with her particular papers, and the questions should build on one another so that answers given in the first half constrain what she can plausibly claim in the second.
