Read Rashomon — the 1950 Kurosawa film, not the short storys — through Nyāya epistemology, especially the four pramāṇas and the taxonomy of illusion (khyāti). The plot gives four contradictory eyewitness acounts of a bandit attack and rape: some witnesses claim direct sight (pratyakṣa), others fill in motives through inference (anumāna) or comparison to past crimes (upamāna), and the woodcutter offers secondhand report (śabda).

Here's the niche framing I want injected: apply the Nyāya distinction between anyathākhyāti — misapprehending a real thing as something else that's also real, like seeing a rope as a snake — and viparītakhyāti, where you cognize the exact opposite of what's present. Are the four testimonies just different anyathākhyāti misplacements of one underlying event, or is at least one a viparītakhyāti reversal? Also, Nyāya holds that testimony (śabda) works only when the speaker is an āpta, a trustworthy authority. Is the woodcutter an āpta, or does the film collapse direct perception and hearsay?

Then there's the resistance: Nyāya thinks errors are correctible by applying more pramāṇas, but Rashomon never resolves the contradiction — there's no authoritative version. Does that make the film skeptical in a Buddhist akhyāti-adjacent way, or just dramatize practical limits?

I'm prepping this for a philosophy of perception seminar. Don't reduce the movie to an illustration, and don't twist Nyāya to fit the plot. End with what Rashomon adds to debates about testimonial knowledge that pure argument can't capture.
