The debate over coherent extrapolated volition and idealized preference aggregation for AI alignment has circled the same moves for well over a decade without anyone visibly conceding ground. One camp treats CEV as a procedural recipe: extrapolate each agent’s volition under conditions of logical consistency, full information, and reflective stability, then aggregate, yielding a single target for alignment. Critics reply that those “extrapolation conditions” already smuggle in a normative framework—usually some variant of liberal rationalism—so the result is circular rather than neutrally derived. A third group insists the whole apparatus confuses descriptive decision theory with normative ethics, noting that even perfectly extrapolated preferences could be morally monstrous. All three sides have standard rebuttals, and none seem to move.

Is this stall substantive—perhaps an empirical disagreement about whether reflection actually converges across agents—or is it verbal, with “coherent” meaning procedural consistency to one side and normative justification to the other? Or are there really three different questions collapsed under one heading: what an aligned system should technically optimize, what value-learning requires, and what democratic legitimacy demands? Diagnose the structure using the actual commitments each side makes, including whether hidden work is being done by an unexamined assumption about interpersonal preference aggregation under logical omniscience. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result showing convergence or divergence of idealized agents, a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates the procedural from the normative, or an argument that no single extrapolation procedure can satisfy all three questions simultaneously.
