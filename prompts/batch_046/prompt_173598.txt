You are a volunteer in a clinical study on episodic memory and decision-making under amnesia. The protocol works as follows. On Monday you are sedated and your recent memories are pharmacologically cleared. The researchers then flip a fair coin exactly once. If the result is heads, you are awakened once on Tuesday, interviewed briefly, and released. If the result is tails, you are awakened on Tuesday, interviewed, and then—after your Tuesday memories are cleared—the identical physical and cognitive state is reproduced on Wednesday for a second, indistinguishable interview; in both Tuesday and Wednesday awakenings you have no recollection of any prior awakening and subjectively feel as though you have just woken up for the first time. At each awakening you must assign a numerical credence to the proposition that the coin came up heads, and on the basis of that credence you must select one of two payout structures: Option A rewards you if heads is true, Option B rewards you if tails is true, with the payoff scaled so that your choice is genuinely consequential.

Two fellow participants have offered competing frameworks. One argues that because the coin was physically fair and flipped exactly once, your credence should remain 1/2 regardless of repetition; your subjective feeling of being in a repeated trial is epistemically irrelevant, like learning after the fact that there are two identical rooms with people like you in them. The other argues that from your first-person, indexical perspective there are three equally likely awakening-events—Tuesday-heads, Tuesday-tails, Wednesday-tails—so your credence in heads should be 1/3, which implies you should choose Option B.

A further complication is built into the study design: because the Wednesday replay under tails uses the same brain-state profile as Tuesday, some researchers in the study maintain that Tuesday-tails and Wednesday-tails are not two distinct observers but two temporal stages of a single observer interrupted by amnesia, which raises the question of whether your reasoning should count them as separate "centers" at all.

You need to decide not merely what number to report, but what principle governs rational belief when an agent is uncertain about which temporal position—or which stage of a possibly continuous consciousness—they occupy within an experimentally repeated structure. Is there a correct answer between the 1/2 and 1/3 positions, or does the case show that standard Bayesian updating needs revision when applied to self-locating beliefs? And if you must place a real bet upon awakening, which framework should govern your choice?
