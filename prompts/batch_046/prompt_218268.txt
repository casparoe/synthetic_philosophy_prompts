I am drafting a chapter for my thesis on Pascal's political fragments, and I have been trying to systematize his implicit jurisprudence by testing how he would rule on a sequence of hypothetical conflicts between equity and coercion. The track record I have reconstructed from the Pensées runs as follows. Where a sovereign issues a decree that is morally arbitrary yet is rigorously enforced by the state's apparatus, Pascal treats obedience as mandatory, since justice lacking institutional force is powerless and organized strength alone prevents chaos. Where an ancient custom governs inheritance or rank with no rational foundation but enjoys universal acceptance, he treats it as binding equity, because custom supplies the stability that abstract reason cannot provide. Where a philosopher proposes a scheme of perfect distributive equality with no mechanism for enforcement, he dismisses it as politically irrelevant, holding that force is a necessary condition for any practical justice. Where a statute is genuinely just in content but is applied only against the weak while the powerful escape it, he denies that justice exists there at all, arguing that selective enforcement reveals private interest rather than public right. Finally, where a revolution destroys a tyrannical regime but produces no stable authority, leaving society in disorder, he judges the disorder worse than the original tyranny, because unorganized violence is more destructive than organized, if imperfect, rule.

The case I must now address does not fit neatly among these. Consider a transnational algorithmic arbitration protocol that enforces contracts with perfect consistency across borders, applies its rules without human bias or national exception, and derives its coercive power solely from economic exclusion and cryptographic locking rather than from state violence or democratic authorization. The protocol's rules are morally defensible—they protect vulnerable parties in cross-border disputes—but they were originally drafted by a small, unaccountable technical elite and now override local democratic legislation wherever the two conflict. Predict what Pascal would say about this system, and recommend whether treating it as legitimate justice is consistent with the precedents above. Identify the closest analog in my list, state whether the implicit rule is force-necessity, conventional sovereignty, or universal equity-with-force, and tell me what further hypothetical case would distinguish these three rules from one another. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give your reasoning with no preamble and no restating of the question.
