I've been drafting an essay for a course on early modern cosmology that argues we should read Kepler's astrological writings—especially De fundamentis astrologiae certioribus—not as a reluctant concession to patronage, but as a rigorous application of his metaphysics of world harmony. The core claim is that Kepler's well-known reduction of astrology to planetary aspects (angular separations) and his dismissal of houses, signs, and other judicial apparatus follow directly from his conviction that only quantitative, relational structures possess genuine ontological and causal status. On this reading, zodiacal divisions are conventional impositions, comparable to grid lines on a map, whereas aspects are real geometric relations that can serve as the causal channels through which the anima mundi exerts influence on sublunary matter. I further suggest that this makes Kepler a kind of metaphysical minimalist about astrology: he retains the practice not despite his science, but because his cosmology demands that any celestial influence must be mediated by mathematically precise relations, and aspects alone satisfy that constraint.

I'm particularly interested in whether this interpretation over-intellectualizes Kepler's practical motivations—he did cast horoscopes for income, after all—and whether the distinction he draws between natural and judicial astrology actually supports a metaphysical reading or instead reveals a more pragmatic, empirical skepticism. Could you comment on the accuracy of this framing, especially regarding the role of harmonia mundi in his astrological theory? I'd also welcome suggestions on whether the comparison between zodiacal conventions and cartographic grid lines is illuminating or anachronistic, and on any specific parts of the argument—particularly the move from geometric realism to causal efficacy—that seem weakest or most in need of qualification.
