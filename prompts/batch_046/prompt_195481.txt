question: in the theory of moral sentiments smith describes sympathy as entering into another person's situation and feeling something analogous. he also introduces the imparial spectator as an internal judge. some recent philosophers propose the spectator works like a social thermostat — a homeostatic regulator comparing internal states to an external reference, rather than through imaginative projection. explain smith's actual account of how sympathy and the spectator relate, evaluate whether the thermostat model captures smith or imports something foreign, and point out one place in smith's text that resists reducing the spectator to sympathy.

student answer:
smith says sympathy happens when we imagine ourselves in another person's shoes and feel a copy of their emotion, just weaker. the imparial spectator is basically sympathy turned around: instead of feeling with the other guy, i imagine what he would feel about me, so it's the same imaginative mechanism running backward. the social thermostat idea says we don't actually imagine anything — we just have a built-in regulator like body temperature that adjusts behavior automatically. that can't be right because smith keeps talking about changing places in fancy, which is clearly imagination. but the thermostat people are onto something because smith does say the spectator corrects our self-love, like a feedback loop. so the spectator is continuous with sympathy — it's not a separate faculty, just sympathy applied to the self with the volume turned down. the only problem is that smith sometimes sounds like the spectator can approve of actions even when nobody around actually feels sympathy for them, which the thermostat model explains better than pure sympathy does.
