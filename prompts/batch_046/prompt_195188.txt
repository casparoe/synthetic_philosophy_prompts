I have been thinking about pain asymbolia — the condition in which people report that they feel pain, yet say it is not unpleasant or bad for them. In my own reading, philosophers often treat these cases as proof that the badness of pain is only a contingent feature: pain is essentially just a sensory signal about bodily damage, and the negative affect is an extra layer that can fail to attach. I want to challenge this interpretation, but I need help constructing a rigorous argument. Please argue for the following position: that pain asymbolia is not evidence that badness is separable from pain, but rather reveals a specific kind of dissociation between two normally integrated layers — a representational layer that encodes damage as something that ought not to be happening, and an affective layer that registers this normative status as felt unpleasantness. On this view, the asymbolic patient still possesses the representational content that damage is occurring and that it is undesirable, but lacks the emotional mechanism that translates that content into suffering. Therefore, the absence of felt badness is a malfunction of integration, not a revelation that pain can exist in a purely neutral form. Please develop this argument in detail, and address the objection that if the badness can disappear while the sensation remains, then badness was never part of the essence of pain to begin with.
