For a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science—ten participants, one two-hour session—design a structured, low-tech simulation that lets the group experience the instability of treating the first-person pronoun I as a self-referential strange loop rather than as a fixed label for an inner substance. Invent and frame the exercise around a protocol you call the Nested Indexical Exchange: assign students to successive representational levels—sensory registration, conceptual categorization, meta-cognitive monitoring, and symbolic labeling—so that each may only use I to refer to the representational state immediately below them. The loop closes only when the top-level symbol points back to the bottom-level input, and no individual student may claim I for themselves outside their assigned level.

Specify the turn-taking rules; the physical token or written card that circulates to stabilize the loop temporarily; the collective payoff scheme—small but real, such as shared access to a snack break or a graded discussion point—that collapses if anyone asserts an unmediated, self-anchoring reference; and the engineered breakdown point, such as the moment when a meta-level student must either deny their own first-person authority or generate an infinite regress of I think that I think. Before naming the breakdown, the rules should force participants to feel the tension themselves.

Include a twenty-minute debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto Hofstadter’s conception of the self as an epiphenomenal symbol that arises from circular causation across levels rather than from a core agent, distinguishing the symbol that refers from the symbol that refers to itself. Be explicit about which feature of the strange-loop theory the session cannot reproduce—specifically, the long-term temporal stability and narrative continuity of the loop across days or years, which depends on memory and biography that a single meeting cannot supply—and instruct the facilitator on how to supply that missing dimension verbally without resolving the paradox for the group.

Finally, predict the failure mode that will occur on the first run—most likely, participants will attempt to break the regress by appealing to their biological bodies or to a private mental theater as an extra-systemic anchor—and tell the facilitator exactly how to block that move within the rules without lecturing. Keep all materials to paper and a single circulating token; do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; and ensure that every element of the idea emerges from what the participants do under the rules, not from what the facilitator explains afterward.
