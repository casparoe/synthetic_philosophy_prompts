I run a 90-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of action with 12 students. Design a live, in-class deliberation that makes participants feel the Knobe-effect asymmetry in their own reasoning rather than observing it in a survey vignette — specifically, the pull between the normative account (that moral evaluation is constitutive of judgments about intentional action) and the explanatory-salience alternative (that harmful outcomes are simply more cognitively prominent and thus explained differently). Avoid the standard corporate-CEO case; instead, stage a simulated institutional review board in which pairs receive identical study protocols except that one subgroup is told the foreseeable side-effect is harmful and the other that it is beneficial. Each pair must reach a unanimous verdict on whether the principal investigator acted “intentionally” with respect to that side-effect, write a brief justification, and then defend it in a structured cross-group exchange. Include explicit rules (no appeals to prior coursework, no naming the effect), a minimal payoff scheme (e.g., the subgroup whose justification survives cross-examination earns a symbolic concession such as choosing the break music), a debrief that maps each deliberation stage onto the competing explanations, a prediction of what will predictably fail the first time you run it (participants smuggling in the familiar vignette or collapsing into meta-philosophy), and an honest admission of which feature of the asymmetry — its cross-cultural stability or its fine-grained sensitivity to linguistic framing — this embodied version cannot reproduce.
