I am preparing a contribution for a philosophy-and-literature seminar, and in discussion with fellow students I found that Hamlet’s paralysis is usually dismissed as mere weakness of will or melancholy. I should like a stricter, more philosophically informed reading: take the play’s central moments of deferral — particularly the soliloquy weighing existence against self-slaughter and the repeated postponement of revenge upon Claudius — and analyse them through Buridan’s ass and modern accounts of choice without preference. Rather than treating the drama as a straightforward illustration, I want you to ask whether Hamlet’s situation is genuinely a Buridan case, in which the values of action and inaction are exactly equipollent, or whether it instead involves incommensurable goods — honour, filial obligation, moral revulsion at murder, and bare survival — that resist being placed on a single scale. Does Buridan’s logic, in which rationality demands arbitrary selection when preferences are equal, illuminate Hamlet’s sustained suspension, or does the play dramatise a failure that formal decision theory cannot capture: a refusal to rank values that are not equal but incomparable?

Consider, too, whether the Ghost’s injunction functions as an external tie-breaker that Hamlet actively suppresses, thereby transforming a potential decision into prolonged stasis, and examine precisely where Shakespeare’s text resists this philosophical framing — for instance, in Hamlet’s self-reproach, which implies he recognises a preference he is failing to enact. I am writing for an audience that takes both dramatic criticism and philosophy of action seriously, and I should be grateful if you could present your analysis in British English, avoiding any reduction of the play to a mere case study and saying explicitly what Hamlet’s resistance adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not.
