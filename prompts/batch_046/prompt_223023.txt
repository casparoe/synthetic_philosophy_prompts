You are preparing for a seminar defense at an interdisciplinary program in historical sociology and philosophy of science. Your thesis is that non-human forces—specifically epidemic pathogens, volcanic climate perturbations, and rapid climate shifts—should be reconceptualized not as deterministic "historical actors" with autonomous agency, but as "fragility-mediated co-agents" whose causal efficacy is entirely contingent upon pre-existing institutional failure modes. You argue that attributing independent historical agency to germs or volcanoes without mapping the specific social and infrastructural fragilities they exploit commits a double error: it naturalizes outcomes that were politically contingent, and it obscures human responsibility by displacing causal weight onto non-human entities.
