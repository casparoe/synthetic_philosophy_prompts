I'm drafting a novel about a protaganist who runs these "memory clinics" in a near-future city — people pay to have specific griefs extracted, not erased but sealed in state vaults, so they can live without the weight. In my writng group last tuesday someone said my premise was immoral because "if you don't remember them, you never really loved them." They threw it out like it was self-evident, probably gesturing at Margalit or Rieff or whoever writes about the ethics of memory vs forgetting, and used it to shut down my idea that voluntary forgetting can be a kind of care. But I feel like that one sentence is smuggling in half a dozen different arguments. Can you lay out the precise theses it could actually be making? I mean: is it claiming love requires continuous mental recall, or that love generates an obligation to commemorate, or that forgetting equals moral abandonment of the dead? I want a menu — which readings are just platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are flat-out false, and especially which one my friend was invoking when they dismissed state-archived grief. Don't just pick the best version; list them, say what kind of "remember" and "love" each needs, and tell me for each whether it actually supports the conclusion that forgetting is always a betrayal. I'm not asking for a bibliography, just the conceptual map so I can defend my clinic.
