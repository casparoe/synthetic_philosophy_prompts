I'm designing a writing exercise for an advanced introductory philosophy module that treats Plato's theory of Forms and the Cave allegory not as museum pieces but as live institutional blueprints. For this exercise, I need a model-generated example of sustained bureaucratic satire: specifically, a deadpan Frequently Asked Questions page for a fictional state agency—the Department of Epistemic Ascent—tasked with administering daily life in a society that has fully implemented Platonic metaphysics as public policy. The piece should parody the genre of municipal government FAQs (numbered questions, flat administrative diction, references to forms and appeals processes) while using the allegory's details—the prisoners, the shadows, the ascent, the Sun as the Form of the Good—to expose the practical absurdities of treating perceptual hierarchy as governance.

The satire should target the theory itself rather than any individual: exaggerate the unobservable ontological ladder (Forms behind phenomena), the political authority granted to those who claim direct access to the Forms, and the implicit dismissal of ordinary sensory experience as degraded projection. To keep the prompt from collapsing into generic anti-Platonism, please inject one invented, niche administrative concept—"shadow-taxation," defined as the ontological debt incurred whenever a citizen views a projected shadow of a true Form, reconcilable only through assigned civic service—and treat it throughout the answers as unremarkable, routine policy. The voice must remain consistently deadpan; the humor should come from the mismatch between transcendent metaphysics and banal bureaucracy, not from winking asides or anachronistic slang.

The deliverable should run about 600 words, with at least six numbered Q&A pairs covering practical citizen concerns: taxation of shadows, the appeals process for citizens who dispute their demotion back to shadow-viewing after an alleged ascent, licensing exams for philosopher-guides, the national holiday marking the first direct sighting of the Sun, and the refund policy for failed enlightenment programs. Avoid bibliographies, citations, or references to specific modern scholars; the piece should be readable without specialist knowledge yet precise enough that a reader familiar with the Cave recognizes exactly which features of Plato's view are being distorted. Do not use verbatim quotations from Plato's texts; paraphrase any references to the allegory in modern administrative language.
