In discussions of moral status across species, commentators frequently reach for a “dimmer-switch” analogy: consciousness is treated as a continuous variable like the brightness of a bulb, with thermostats and inanimate objects near zero, insects somewhere in a low-middle range, and humans near the top. From this image, they derive a proportionalist conclusion—that if an insect registers at roughly one-twentieth the “brightness” of a human, its interests deserve about one-twentieth the moral weight. A recent interlocutor, however, has pressed a different calibration: she claims moral status actually depends on two independent dimensions—first, the capacity for valenced experience (pleasure and pain), and second, the structural integration required for a unified point of view—so that an organism might rank high on one axis and negligible on the other. She charges that the dimmer-switch picture illicitly collapses these into a single scale, thereby licensing a false arithmetic of moral weight. Can you lay out precisely what in the dimmer-switch analogy maps onto what in the target domain of moral status; identify which properties of electrical dimming—continuity, linear scaling, reversibility, absence of sharp thresholds—are being quietly imported and whether any of them genuinely belong; and determine whether the proportional-allocation conclusion survives once the two-dimensional calibration is made explicit? If the analogy breaks in a way that undermines the argument, propose a better analogy that respects both dimensions without reverting to a simple on-off switch.
