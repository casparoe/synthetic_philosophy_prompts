I was at coffee with my study group and we got into this weirdly intense argument about whether Western individualism or East Asian collectivism actually gets the relation between person and community right. Maya was arguing the Western side, but in a kind of sharp way. She said the whole idea of an "atomic" self is necessary for moral responsibility -- if you're just a product of your roles, then praising or blaming you makes no sense, it's like blaming the weather. She said that when people talk about East Asian harmony or "face" they're really just describing social pressure that supresses dissent, and that concepts like filial piety are basically just internalized obedience with a nice name. Jonas, who's taken some anthro courses, came back with this idea he called "recursive role-constitution" -- not just that people have social roles, but that the self is literally made and remade through a feedback loop between performing a role and getting social recognition for it. So you can't extract an individual "kernel" because there's nothing there prior to the loop. Maya said fine, maybe that's descriptively true, but then you've lost the normative ground to criticize the loop itself -- if I'm fully constituted by recognition patterns, then where do I stand to say those patterns are oppressive? Jonas said that's the Western mistake right there: assuming critique needs an "outside" viewpoint. In his view, you change the loop from inside by altering recognition, not by stepping out of it. They went back and forth for like forty minutes and neither budged. Can you walk through their moves and say who's right at each step? Like, is Maya correct that "face" and filial piety reduce to social control? Does Jonas's recursive idea actually add anything beyond normal social constructivism? Is her worry about lacking a critical standpoint a real problem for his view? And who is closer to being right overall -- or are they both stuck in a false dichotomy?
