I'm preparing a ninety-minute philosophy-of-science seminar for second-year graduate students who have read Darwin's Origin in an undergraduate survey but have no formal training in evolutionary biology. The session must introduce Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique not as a blanket denial that natural selection shapes traits, but specifically as a methodological argument about the burden of proof in adaptationist reasoning—that consistency with an adaptive story is not sufficient to establish that a trait was selected for its present function. I want a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself: the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the architectural metaphor actually carries the logical point, and why the distinction between origin and current utility has to precede the metaphor rather than follow it.

For the carrying example, specify a single biological case that should anchor the entire session and state explicitly what properties it must have and lack: it should be intuitively readable as an adaptive solution, structurally implausible as an independent target of selection, and not one of the overused textbook illustrations like the panda's thumb or the human chin; it should also lack an obvious exaptation pathway that lets an adaptationist rescue the account too easily. Map out the misconceptions the students will form at each stage—I anticipate they will conflate spandrels with pure evolutionary accidents, or interpret the critique as politically motivated anti-Darwinism—and identify the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted before it hardens. Include the check-for-understanding question I should pose at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the burden-of-proof inversion rather than merely memorized the spandrel image; and state clearly what I should explicitly set aside and say so for this audience, including quantitative-genetics debates and the socio-political context of Gould's broader writing.

Finally, inject one framing that makes the session feel contemporary and specific rather than a repetition of a well-worn polemic: treat the spandrel logic as continuous with constraint-based explanations in evolutionary developmental biology, so that pluralism about mechanisms—not just negation of adaptation—becomes the live conclusion, without requiring the students to master evo-devo terminology in advance. The plan should present the spandrel as a structural feature of evolutionary explanation that forces a shift in explanatory standards, not merely as a negative counterexample.
