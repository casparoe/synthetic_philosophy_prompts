I've been trying to apply the cause-prioritization framework—importance multiplied by tractability multiplied by neglectedness—not just to charity but to how I actually live, and I have built an argument that feels like a closed loop I cannot exit. Here are the steps as I see them.

(1) If I want to do the most good I can, I should direct my scarce resources—money, time, attention, emotional energy, and social influence—toward causes with the highest product of importance, tractability, and neglectedness.

(2) This applies continuously, not only at the moment of donation: every hour I spend on anything is an hour not spent on whatever currently scores highest, and every unit of concern I feel for one problem is concern I withhold from another.

(3) I should also account for recursive leverage: some work increases my future capacity for high-impact work, so its true tractability includes spillover effects on other dominant causes rather than being evaluated in isolation.

(4) On this four-factor ranking, preventing large-scale technological catastrophes and certain high-leverage biomedical risks dominate; local mutual aid, personal relationships, cultural preservation, and neighborhood improvement score orders of magnitude lower, often approaching zero.

(5) So if I spend an evening with a friend, donate to a local food bank, or feel deep emotional concern for a community issue, I am actively allocating scarce resources away from the globally dominant causes.

(6) Choosing the lower-scored option is not morally neutral; it is allowing the preventable harm associated with the higher-scored option to continue, weighted by the tractability of that harm and discounted only by my small share of responsibility, which does not disappear just because others also fail to act.

(7) Therefore, to avoid being complicit in that continuing harm, I should restructure not only my budget but my emotional life and social bonds so that they serve primarily, if not exclusively, the top-ranked causes.

(8) But an agent who actually eliminated all non-instrumental attachment to family, place, or local community would likely become unstable, alienated, and eventually useless for sustained work on any cause, including the globally dominant ones.

(9) So the framework either proves that sustained moral agency is impossible, or there is a hidden error somewhere between (2) and (7).

Which step is the weakest link? Is it the continuous application in (2), the inclusion of emotional resources and social bonds in (5), or the assumption in (6) that every non-optimal choice amounts to complicity? And if I reject one of them in order to preserve my sanity and my relationships, what exactly do I have to give up about the framework's authority over ordinary life? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (4) intact, but I want to know what that choice costs me.
