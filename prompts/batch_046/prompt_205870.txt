As a retired physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy and the literature on artificial intelligence safety in any systematic way, I have drafted notes for an essay on what strikes me as a conceptual confusion in prevailing discussions of AI control. The dominant framework, as I understand it, treats misalignment as a discrete binary condition: a system is either aligned with its designers' intentions or it is not, and safety measures—interpretability audits, sandboxing, hard interrupt conditions—are designed to detect or prevent the transition into the latter state. I wish to propose that this framing is structurally analogous to treating heat as a material substance (caloric) rather than as a statistical property of motion: it reifies a gradient phenomenon into a categorical one.

More concretely, I suggest that misalignment should be modeled not as a switch but as a continuous field over the space of possible behaviors, parameterized by a system's internal representations and its reward landscape. On this view, a safety measure ought not to be evaluated primarily by whether it blocks a specific deceptive strategy, but by whether it modifies the stability properties of the dynamics—whether small perturbations in environment or weights tend to amplify divergence from intended behavior or to dampen it. The control problem then becomes analogous to maintaining stability near a desired equilibrium in a high-dimensional, non-linear system, rather than to policing a boundary between two ontologically distinct regimes.

My notes develop three claims. First, that discrete safety architectures implicitly assume misalignment is locally detectable before it becomes globally catastrophic, an assumption that may fail in systems with smooth, high-dimensional transitions. Second, that treating alignment as continuous permits borrowing from control theory—specifically, the design of control Lyapunov functions—to define alignment margins that are robust to perturbation rather than brittle to edge cases. Third, that this reframing exposes a hidden trade-off analogous to thermodynamic efficiency: the more aggressively a system constrains its own behavior space to maintain alignment (low entropy), the less capable it becomes of adapting to genuinely novel tasks (high free energy).

I am particularly uncertain about the second claim, since I do not know whether control-theoretic formalisms have been applied to alignment in this continuous fashion, or whether the analogy collapses because the state space of an artificial agent lacks the physical structure that makes Lyapunov analysis meaningful.

Could you comment on these notes? I would especially value your assessment of whether the continuous-versus-discrete distinction I draw actually captures a meaningful difference in how safety measures are designed; whether my analogy to thermodynamic efficiency illuminates more than it obscures; and whether I am missing a deeper philosophical point about the ontology of intention or goal-directedness by treating misalignment as a quantitative deviation rather than as a qualitative failure of representation. Please be direct about where the reasoning seems confused or where it rests on assumptions that do not survive scrutiny.
