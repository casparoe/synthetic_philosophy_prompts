I'm preparing a proposal for a one-semester independent-study fellowship (approximately 14 weeks, 4,000–5,000 words) in philosophy of mind and philosophy of science. I want to investigate a neglected structural feature of the classic thought experiment—famously associated with Hofstadter and Dennett's The Mind's I—in which Einstein's brain is replaced by a book that encodes all of his cognitive patterns. The standard literature divides over whether such a book could be conscious: functionalists tend to say yes, provided the internal causal structure is preserved, while biological naturalists say no, because the substrate is wrong. I suspect both answers miss the point, because they treat the book either as an abstract implementation or as a mere physical object, without examining whether books as a medium possess or lack a specific kind of self-modeling stability.

My proposed question is precise: does consciousness require what I will call counterfactual page-stability—the property that altering a single entry or state in the system should propagate through the overall self-model in a coherent, narrative-preserving way, rather than producing local incoherence or requiring an external reader to repair the sequence? Books, by their material nature, are linear, static, and actualized only through an external interpreter; but different book architectures vary in how they handle disruption. I want to map four architectures—(1) a linear bound volume read sequentially, (2) a hypertext network with cross-referenced entries, (3) a distributed set of copies held in separate libraries and assembled only on query, and (4) a self-rewriting living book that modifies its own pages in response to interaction—and ask, for each, whether counterfactual page-stability can be satisfied without collapsing back into standard functionalist criteria.

The proposal should contain: (1) a sharp statement of the research question and why existing functionalist and biological-naturalist positions leave it open; (2) the method—how I will construct the taxonomy, apply the stability test, and distinguish it from familiar arguments about substrate independence or causal integration; (3) what a result would look like (for example, only architecture 4 satisfies the condition, or none do, or the condition proves redundant) and what considerations would decide among these outcomes; (4) an explicit failure condition—what would show that the project is either too big for one semester or that the stability notion is incoherent or empirically empty; and (5) a realistic scope and timeline for 14 weeks. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. Please write in American English.
