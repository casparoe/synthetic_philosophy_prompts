I serve on my university’s Research Integrity Council, and we must vote Thursday on a formal complaint against Dr. Elena Voss, a climate-economist who published a regional tipping-point forecast. Two peer reviewers—matched in expertise, publication record, and institutional rank—have issued opposing assessments: Reviewer A argues that Voss’s Bayesian prior on albedo-feedback strength is unsupported by the observational record, while Reviewer B holds that it sits within the defensible range given structural uncertainty in coupled climate-economic models. Voss has refused to issue a correction or lower her confidence, contending that when peer disagreement traces to incommensurable methodological priors rather than shared empirical evidence, conciliation is rationally optional and steadfastness is permitted. Our policy manual is silent on what it calls “prior-level disagreement.”

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. Recommend either (a) that the Council reprimand Voss for failing to conciliate with an epistemic peer, or (b) that the Council dismiss the complaint and recognize a narrowly defined “domain-partitioned steadfastness” exception for model-dependent sciences—whichever you defend, justify it with explicit reasoning. The memo should: explain what counts as an epistemic peer when likelihood functions are underdetermined by data; weigh the institutional risk of false consensus against the risk of unwarranted dogmatism; and honestly treat the strongest argument for the position you reject. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Keep the memo to a single tightly argued page—no more than 400 words—with a clear bottom line the Council can act on.
