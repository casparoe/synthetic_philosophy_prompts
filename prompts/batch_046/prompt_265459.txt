I've been mulling over something since a course on personal identity where we touched on Hofstadter's idea that an 'I' isn't confined to one brain — that the patterns making up a self can persist or be shared across different skulls. But I've noticed a related phenomenon in everyday life that doesn't seem to match his framework, and I can't find the right label for it. I was chatting with my flatmate about it after a seminar, and we both realised we'd experianced it with long-term collaborators or close partners: after years of working together, you face a decision — say, whether to relocate for a job or drop a shared project — and you don't just imagine what they'd advise. Instead, the weighing-up feels like it's being done by a single deliberative subject that just happens to have access to both your memories and preferences and thier's. It's not empathy, not internalised values, and not two people negotiating; it's experienced as one 'I' distributed across two living brains, reaching a conclusion that draws on both sets of reasons without feeling like a dialogue.

Is there an established concept for this? I know about shared intentionality and about Hofstadter's symbolic survival of the self in others, but this feels different — it's simultaneous rather than posthumous, and it's felt as one agent rather than a collective 'we'. If philosophers haven't named it, I'd like to understand exactly how it should be distinguished from we-mode collective intentionality, from identification with another, and from ordinary influence. The carving matters more than the label: what are the boundary cases, and where does this phenomenon stop being philosophical curiosity and start being just a psychological quirk? Any guidance on whether this has been discussed, or how one might define it precisely, would be really useful.
