Okay so I've been going down a rabbit hole on Berkeley's master argument — the bit where he claims you can't form an idea of something existing unperceived because the moment you try, you're perceiving the idea itself. In my philosphy of mind reading group this has turned into a total stalemate that feels eerily like the free will debate: one side says it's a straight modal fallacy (conceivability doesn't entail possibility), another says Berkeley's 'conceive' is doing special phenomenological work tied to the transparency of experience, and a third camp — mostly people who've read too much Brentano — insists the whole thing is really about intentional inexistence and that Berkeley accidentally stumbled on the claim that mental content is always directed at something present-to-consciousness. Nobody budges, and the same three moves get replayed every session.

I'm wondering if this is actually one disagreement or three running under the same name. Is the modal-fallacy crowd answering a metaphysical question about whether mind-independent matter is possible, while the transparency crowd is answering a structural question about perceptual representation, and the Brentano crowd answering a question about intentionality? Or is there a hidden empirical premise — maybe about mental imagery or whether 'empty' representations are psychologically possible — that's doing all the work without anyone admitting it? I'd like a diagnosis of the stall: verbal, substantive, parallel-questions, or ill-posed, with reference to what each camp actually commits to. And tell me what progress would even look like — a formal model of conceiving, an experiment on mental presence, or just a change of question? Also, I keep flipping on a test case: does the argument still bite if the unperceived object is a quantum field that no human has ever observed, or does the scale and nature of the object break the symmetry? I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just me moving the goalposts.
