I am a high school student writing a paper for my philosophy of science elective. In my study group, two classmates—Alex and Jordan—got stuck in a sustained disagreement about the common prior assumption, and I want to understand exactly where each of them stands. I have reconstructed their exchange below as faithfully as I can; I am not taking sides.

Here are the eight moves in order:

1. Alex said that the common prior assumption essentially means that if two people are fully rational and begin with identical beliefs, then once they share all of their evidence they must eventually agree on every relevant proposition.

2. Jordan replied that Alex is conflating the common prior assumption with Aumann's agreement theorem, which specifically requires common knowledge of each agent's posterior beliefs—not merely shared priors and shared evidence.

3. Alex conceded that formal common knowledge involves an infinite recursion, but insisted that in ordinary life people do converge when they talk things through, so treating shared priors as a baseline remains a useful idealization of rational disagreement.

4. Jordan then offered a concrete counterexample: two physicians with exactly the same prior probabilities over possible diagnoses examine the same blurry radiological scan. One physician treats the image as yielding a precise likelihood—this is probably a fracture—and updates by standard conditioning. The other physician, viewing the image as genuinely ambiguous, applies Jeffrey conditionalization, distributing her updated belief across a range of possible interpretations. Despite identical priors and the same physical evidence, their posteriors diverge sharply.

5. Alex responded that this only demonstrates that their likelihood functions or update rules differ, not that the prior assumption itself fails. The prior can remain common; the divergence is located in the interpretation of evidence, which is a separate layer.

6. Jordan countered that if rational belief formation includes not just the prior but also how one interprets ambiguous evidence and which update rule one applies, then "common prior" is too thin to do any explanatory work. To guarantee agreement you would need common priors, common likelihood assessments, and common framing—at which point you are assuming convergence rather than explaining it.

7. Alex said that even so, formal models in economics and epistemology require the common prior assumption to derive agreement theorems; without it the mathematics collapses, so dismissing the assumption makes an important theoretical tool useless.

8. Jordan replied that a modeling benchmark is acceptable, but the danger is treating that benchmark as a normative claim about real people—that rational agents "ought" to share priors. Given that background knowledge, conceptual framing, and evidential ambiguity are inseparable from belief formation, insisting on common priors actually obscures the sources of persistent disagreement rather than illuminating them.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the eight moves above, rate it as clearly mistaken, partially right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then state, for the exchange as a whole, who is right about which specific points, and identify anything important that both Alex and Jordan are missing—particularly regarding whether their dispute depends on treating the common prior assumption as descriptive, normative, or purely formal, and whether they have adequately distinguished disagreement that stems from the prior itself from disagreement that stems from the interpretation of evidence or the choice of update rule.
