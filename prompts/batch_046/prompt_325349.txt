I am trying to determine whether there is an established concept for a particular pattern I keep noticing in discussions of moral agency—one that seems adjacent to Sartre’s account of radical freedom and bad faith, yet differs in a specific way. Here are two instances. First, a scholar who sincerely maintains that she is not defined by her academic role and could leave it tomorrow, but who has built such an intricate lattice of commitments—grant deadlines, mentoring obligations, disciplinary norms—that she experiences each day as navigating an external structure rather than making continuous choices; when pressed, she does not deny her freedom but appeals to “the system I have designed,” treating her own architecture as if it were a given. Second, an activist who accepts that he could abandon his cause at any moment, yet maintains such a granular ethical accounting—tracking supply chains, carbon footprints, labour conditions—that every consumption decision feels like executing a rule rather than exercising judgement; his freedom is not rejected but distributed across so many micro-administrative acts that no single act feels like the decisive choice Sartre describes. In both cases, freedom is acknowledged in principle but fragmented in practice through self-imposed bureaucratic mediation. Is there a recognised term for this exact configuration—radical freedom preserved at the theoretical level but dissolved through deliberate, self-constructed procedural fragmentation? If the concept exists, how does it relate precisely to Sartrean bad faith and to ordinary habit? If it does not exist, can it be named and defined with clear boundary conditions—distinguishing it from outright denial of freedom, from mere preference for routine, and from external social conditioning? I want the carving rather than the label: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. Please respond in British English.
