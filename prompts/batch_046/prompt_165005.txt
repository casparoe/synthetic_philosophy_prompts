ok so i've been trying to map out when self-deception is actually possible vs when it's just motivated reasoning with a fancy name. here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases, and i keep going back and forth on the pattern.

case 1: a researcher has data that kills her theory. she doesn't falsify it; she just stops running that analysis and within a month genuinly can't recall the contradiction. if you remind her she's surprised. self-deceived?

case 2: a guy thinks he's generous. he skips donations and tells himself "next month," but he never feels guilty and never forms the belief that he's cheap. he just... doesn't update. is that self-deception or just failure to reflect?

case 3: this one's weird — call it "mental escrow." someone predicts thier startup will fail, seals that belief away in a mental box they never open, and then acts with total optimism for years. they don't suppress it actively; they've just structured attention so it's never retrieved. five years later they open the box and see they were right. were they deceiving themselves the whole time?

case 4: a patient understands she has six months to live, but her emotional system down-weights it so she plans vacations with genuine excitement. she isn't denying it; it's just not "live" for planning. self-deception?

case 5: a politician repeats a false stat because it helps thier cause. over time he sincerely believes it. he never decided to lie; repetition just rewired his credence. self-deception?

my verdicts: 1 is yes but barely — more like motivated forgetting. 2 is no, just laziness. 3 is... maybe yes? but only if "escrow" counts as hiding from yourself, which i'm not sure about. 4 is no, that's emotional buffering, not deception. 5 is yes, though there's no intentional hiding.

so what's the principle? is it about "epistemic insulation" — building barriers between beliefs? but then 4 should be yes and i said no. or maybe it's about "simultaneous accessibility" — you only count if both beliefs are available at once? but in 3 they're never together. i want a principle that saves 1, 3, and 5 while leaving 2 and 4 out, but i can't see one that doesn't feel ad-hoc.

also, what's the deal with the escrow case — is that a real mental category or am i inventing a distinction that collapses? and what should my principle predict about a case i haven't listed: someone who uses a decision framework to assign a belief a "low precision weight" so it never updates other beliefs, but can still report it if asked directly? is that self-deception or just disciplined compartmentalization?
