I am a retired physical chemist who, since leaving active laboratory work, have devoted sustained study to philosophy of logic and the foundations of rational agency. I currently serve on my university's Committee for Interdisciplinary Research Governance, and we face a decision that sits uncomfortably between engineering and conceptual philosophy. The mathematics and philosophy departments have jointly proposed funding and ethical clearance to deploy an autonomous reasoning assistant—designed to support formal peer review in mathematics—that implements what they call a "self-certifying modal architecture." Their central claim is that they have circumvented the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents not by altering the logic, but by embedding the system's belief-revision dynamics within a physically dissipative substrate governed by Landauer's principle: they argue that the entropic cost of self-referential computation supplies an external, thermodynamic boundary condition that pure modal logic cannot provide, thereby allowing the agent to consistently endorse its own soundness without triggering the fixed-point collapse that Löb's theorem would otherwise entail.

The committee is divided along lines that mirror the philosophical tension precisely. One faction treats the proposal as a routine engineering adjustment—simply bounding the modal operator by a physical constraint—while another insists that this strategy misunderstands the philosophical core of the obstacle: that any reasoning system capable of recognizing its own inferential patterns must navigate an irreducible tension between epistemic humility and instrumental self-trust, and that relocating that tension into a thermodynamic boundary condition may be technically ingenious yet conceptually evasive. A third concern, which I share, is institutional: if we approve funding for a system that claims to have resolved a foundational problem in rational self-reference, we implicitly adopt a philosophical position on the limits of self-knowledge, with downstream implications for how this university positions itself in broader debates about autonomous epistemic agents.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve outright, reject, or approve with clearly specified conditions and an explicit time frame for compliance—and it should provide reasoning that faculty outside modal logic can evaluate and act upon. The memo must treat the researchers' thermodynamic-boundary argument with genuine seriousness, acknowledging why architectural restriction coupled to physical dissipation might appear to dissolve the obstacle, and it must equally honor the opposing view that such restriction does not resolve the philosophical problem but merely displaces it into a different register. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for circulation among interdisciplinary colleagues; include no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; and frame the recommendation so that it respects both the scientific plausibility of the engineering proposal and the conceptual weight of the Löbian challenge.
