Plan a fifty-minute undergraduate philosophy seminar on causation by omission for students who have encountered basic counterfactual theories of causation but have not yet worked through the specific asymmetry between acting and failing to act, or the problem of whether omissions can be causally efficacious without being grounded in prior mental states. The session should turn on a single carrying example—preferably a case of institutional or collective inaction rather than individual negligence—capable of illustrating both the causal claim and its structural limits. Specify the exact conceptual dependency that must be established before omissions can be presented as more than mere descriptions of absence, so that students do not collapse the distinction into negative event-causation. Then map the predictable misconceptions—such as the assumption that omission requires agency, the belief that negative causation is a perfect mirror of positive causation, or the view that counterfactual dependence is by itself sufficient for omissive causation—and indicate the precise point in the explanatory sequence where each should be intercepted and defused. Propose one closing diagnostic question that reveals whether a student has grasped the structural asymmetry between omission and action, rather than having merely memorized the distinction. The plan should be self-contained: do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation, and do not rely on highly specialized technical apparatus that would be unfamiliar to a student who has only read introductory counterfactual analyses.
