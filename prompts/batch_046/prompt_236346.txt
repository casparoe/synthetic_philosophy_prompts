I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science studying reasoning about causal and probabilistic independence, and I've encountered a recurring argumentative pattern I can't place precisely. A researcher treats two variables or domains as independent not because separation has been demonstrated, but by default — a null hypothesis of non-interaction that does heavy explanatory lifting. When critics produce correlations or interaction effects, the researcher doesn't revise the independence framework globally. Instead, they append localized adjustments — an interference term, a boundary condition, or a coupling parameter — that absorb the anomaly while preserving independence as the baseline. Each adjustment is individually reasonable, but cumulatively they yield a patchwork model far less parsimonious than the original, yet the independence claim retains privileged status: violations are treated as deviations from it rather than as evidence against it. I see connections to heuristic reasoning and to attempts to formalize a presumption of independence, but I'm asking specifically about this protective dynamic. Is there an established term for the asymmetry between the ease of invoking independence by default and the resistance of that default to global revision via correlation? How exactly does this differ from a standard auxiliary hypothesis, from an immunizing stratagem, or from a degenerating problemshift? If there's no precise name, what features distinguish a defensible formalization of independence-presumption from the pathologically protected version I'm describing?
