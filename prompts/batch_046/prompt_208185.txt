As a novelist, I have been trying to formalize my moral intuitions about bringing new lives into being, treating each potential person as a kind of unwritten narrative for which I might bear responsibility. Here are my verdicts on specific scenarios.

If I know that conceiving now would produce a child with a severe, lifelong condition that causes constant suffering, then conceiving is wrong; that suffering is a decisive reason against creation. If, however, I could conceive a child who would experience nothing but profound happiness and flourishing, I feel no corresponding obligation to do so; failing to write that narrative is not a moral failure. This asymmetry holds even though both cases involve the same initially non-existent subject.

But the pattern complicates in further cases. If an existing child suffers, I have a powerful reason to relieve that suffering. So suffering commands action for those already within the story, yet the absence of happiness commands nothing for those still outside it.

Consider a harder pair. I could conceive twins: one bound for intense suffering, the other for extraordinary flourishing. The suffering twin is biologically inseparable from the flourishing one; I cannot have the second without the first. I judge that I should not conceive them both, even though the flourishing twin would never exist otherwise. Yet if I could conceive only the flourishing twin, that seems permissible, and still not obligatory. The presence of the suffering twin seems to contaminate the act, but the absence of the flourishing twin, taken alone, does not.

Another tension: I might conceive a child whose life is, on balance, good—mostly joyful but containing one catastrophic, unavoidable tragedy. I feel it is permissible to create such a life, provided I did not deliberately engineer the tragedy. However, if I possess a simple intervention that could eliminate the tragedy without diminishing the joy, then failing to use it makes conception wrong. The wrongness seems to lie not in the net value of the life, but in the avoidable harm embedded within it.

Finally, I sometimes write fiction in which characters endure great suffering, and I feel no guilt, since they are not real. Yet I treat potential real people differently, even though both are, in a sense, products of my decision to bring something into being—one on the page, one in the world. The distinction feels robust, but I cannot articulate why the ontology of fiction absolves me while the ontology of procreation does not.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the directionality of harm, the nature of narrative obligation, or the metaphysics of possibility—that captures all of these verdicts? Or must I abandon one? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my cases motivate each clause, predict its verdict on a case I have not listed—namely, a child whose life is precisely neutral, neither beneficial nor harmful to live—and be explicit about which of my intuitions, if any, the principle forces me to reject.
