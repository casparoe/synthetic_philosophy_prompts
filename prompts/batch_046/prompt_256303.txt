I've been preparing for a graduate seminar on Enlightenment practical philosophy and have been trying to fix my interpretation of Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness—specifically the commitments implied by her essay on the subject. I want to lay out the profile I think best captures her position and ask whether it is jointly tenable. Here are my five commitments. First, happiness is essentially hedonistic: it consists in sustained, high-quality pleasure rather than in virtue, honor, or religious salvation. Second, the most durable pleasures come from sustained intellectual study, especially in mathematics and natural philosophy, which she treats as a kind of therapeutic occupation for the mind. Third, happiness requires what she calls necessary illusions—cultivated self-deceptions, particularly about one's own mortality and about the permanence of one's present affections, without which anxiety undermines pleasure. Fourth, conventional moral virtue is neither necessary nor sufficient for happiness; in fact, the restraints and self-denials that virtue demands often diminish pleasure without compensation. Fifth, romantic love is indispensable, but only if it is accompanied by a rational, clear-eyed acceptance of its contingent and transient nature.

I am concerned that these commitments do not sit comfortably together, but I also suspect some tensions are only apparent. The rational clarity demanded by both study and the sober view of love seems to conflict with the willful opacity required by necessary illusion; and if study demands a discipline that looks like virtue, then my denial that virtue is necessary seems threatened. At the same time, perhaps intellectual discipline is merely instrumental to pleasure and therefore not genuinely moral, and perhaps the illusions are compartmentalized so they do not corrupt the rational parts of the mind. Could you audit this set? Order the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether it is genuine or dissolves on closer inspection, say what the cheapest revision would be in each case, and tell me which single change—abandoning hedonism, weakening the rationalist conditions on study or love, redefining virtue, or dropping the necessity of illusion—would resolve the largest number of conflicts at once. Please also state how confident you are in your audit and single out the specific point about which you are least sure, whether that concerns the compatibility of rational study with self-deception or the classification of intellectual discipline as non-moral.
