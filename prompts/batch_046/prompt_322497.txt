You are delivering the opening remarks at a workshop titled "Collective Responsibility and Historical Judgment." The audience is divided evenly: half are diplomatic historians deeply familiar with the Fischer controversy—the debate over whether Imperial Germany pursued a premeditated program of expansion that turned the First World War into a calculated risk rather than a defensive reaction—while the other half are philosophers of collective action who understand group intentionality but are not specialists in the archival history of 1914. A smaller contingent of non-specialists, including family members of the organizers, is present as well. Your address should run about ten minutes and must treat the Fischer controversy not as historical decoration but as the central structural test case for a philosophical problem: what does it mean, exactly, to hold a nation responsible for a war?

To do this, you must carefully distinguish three layers that public discourse usually collapses: causal efficacy of state institutions, unified intentional planning by a collective agent, and retrospective moral blame assigned to the nation as a moral subject. Then inject one precise conceptual distinction—between blame directed at a distributed bureaucratic network, where responsibility is fragmented across competing ministries and personal ambitions, and blame directed at a singular collective subject capable of bearing guilt analogously to an individual—to show why the Fischer thesis, even if correct about German war aims and the continuity of expansionist policy, does not by itself settle the moral question of national guilt.

The speech must be rigorous enough that the historians recognize you are not caricaturing the debate over the September Program or the respective roles of civilian and military leadership in 1914, and philosophically sharp enough that the collective-action specialists see you engaging the real problem of whether state-level blame requires a unified intentional agent. At the same time, it must remain fully intelligible to listeners with no background in either field: avoid unexplained jargon, do not assume familiarity with technical terms in collective intentionality, and never allow the philosophical distinction to serve as an excuse to avoid historical specificity.

You may not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and you should not ask the audience to consult particular texts or scholars. Conclude by articulating what practical difference this conceptual clarification makes for contemporary talk about national responsibility—whether in memorial culture, diplomatic rhetoric, or the logic of reparative claims—without declaring a final verdict on Fischer's historical arguments. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to an academic opening address, but keep the core argument accessible to the non-specialists in the room.
