Below are working notes for a short essay I am drafting, tentatively titled "Structured Accuracy and the Illusion of Singularity in Epistemic Utility Theory." I would welcome comments and suggestions focused especially on three things: first, whether the central argument misrepresents the accuracy-first position or the formal machinery of proper scoring rules; second, whether the case study comparing hierarchical and linear models is philosophically interesting or merely a technical artifact; and third, whether the conclusion—that accuracy-first epistemology relocates rather than eliminates pluralism—actually follows, or whether there is a standard reply from proponents of the view that I have overlooked.

The notes run as follows. Accuracy-first epistemology takes the sole fundamental epistemic value to be accuracy, usually formalized via a proper scoring rule applied to an agent's credences. For atomic propositions this yields a straightforward prescription: calibrate credence to the evidential probabilities. The position is often presented as avoiding the pluralism and pragmatic contamination of broader epistemic utility theories, on the grounds that accuracy supplies a single, unambiguous metric.

But once we move from atomic propositions to structured belief spaces—hierarchical causal models, taxonomic classifications, or interdependent propositions—accuracy becomes decomposable in more than one dimension. We might measure pointwise accuracy across observed variables (for instance, Brier scores on individual nodes), global coherence accuracy over the joint distribution (a multivariate Brier score), or structural accuracy, which rewards correctly representing the dependency graph or causal architecture even when marginal predictions remain noisy. These dimensions can come apart: a simpler linear model may achieve a superior pointwise score on observed data, while a more complex hierarchical model correctly captures unobserved dependencies and therefore scores better on structural or counterfactual dimensions of the joint distribution.

The argument I want to press is that accuracy-first epistemology must either arbitrarily privilege one of these decompositions—thereby reintroducing pluralism about which accuracy metric is epistemically primary—or allow trade-offs between dimensions that are formally indistinguishable from the pragmatic and instrumental trade-offs the view was meant to exclude. Specifically, the decision whether to include an unobserved variable in one's hypothesis space involves a judgment about which parts of the world are to count in the accuracy assessment, and that judgment is not itself fixed by the available evidence or by the formal properties of scoring rules alone.

Please keep your response focused on philosophical coherence and conceptual structure rather than on empirical literature review or bibliographic recommendations. In particular, clearly separate what is settled or widely accepted in discussions of epistemic utility theory from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed interpretation—such as the claim that structural accuracy is irreducible to pointwise accuracy—as though it were the consensus position.
