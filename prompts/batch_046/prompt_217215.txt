Design an in-class simulation for a graduate epistemology seminar—eight participants, ninety minutes, no prior lecture on the distinction—to make the group feel the tension between Nozick’s tracking conditions (sensitivity and adherence) and the principle of epistemic closure under known entailment. The rules, roles, and payoff structure (using symbolic course-credit stakes, never real money) must be arranged so that participants discover for themselves that a belief can satisfy tracking conditions while a closely entailed belief fails them, even though they explicitly endorsed the entailment, and they must hit this wall before the facilitator names it.

Set up two roles: Observers hold belief-cards, and Validators award “knowledge-points.” Give each Observer direct perceptual cards about a hidden object in a sealed container—singular observable properties such as “this token is crimson”—plus inference-rule cards they hold in writing (e.g., “if crimson, then not azure”). From these they may derive closure-beliefs. The payoff scheme initially rewards both direct defense and long entailment chains: each successfully defended direct belief earns two points, and each additional link in a deductive chain earns one bonus point, so chain-building is attractive.

The hidden constraint: Validators probe every belief with a counterfactual question—“If this belief were false, would you still hold it?”—and the game mechanics contain a modal boundary rule. Once an entailment crosses from actual-world observation into a skeptical alternative or into a distributive statistical aggregate, the counterfactual probe becomes mechanically unanswerable within the simulation: the Observer must forfeit all points accumulated in that chain. Crucially, include one “closure-resistant” derived belief that is entailed by tracked singular observations but concerns a statistical aggregate rather than a skeptical world—e.g., deriving “fewer than ten tokens are black” from direct observation of each visible token—so participants encounter closure failure arising not from brain-in-vat scenarios but from distributive epistemic risk across multiple items.

Provide the exact card sets and inference rules, the turn structure and how Validators conduct probes, the payoff matrix that makes chain-building seem rational, and the debrief that maps each game phase onto Nozick’s sensitivity and adherence conditions and onto closure. The debrief should also say frankly which feature of tracking—actual modal proximity—cannot be reproduced by card-game mechanics and must be supplied by the facilitator verbally.

Finally, specify the setting details (group size, time, materials), what participants should come away seeing, what will predictably go wrong the first run—specifically, Observers conflating the game’s modal boundary with mere logical complexity, and Validators being too generous with counterfactual judgments—and exactly how to correct each failure without breaking the experiential force of the simulation.
