I'm currently enrolled in an advanced philosophy and economics elective at my high school, and after a long discussion with classmates about scientific modeling, I keep returning to Milton Friedman's so-called F-twist: the claim that a model's predictive success matters more than whether its underlying assumptions are literally true. We were debating whether this applies to climate simulations that assume homogeneous atmospheric mixing or to behavioral models that treat people as perfectly rational calculators, and I realized I want something artistic that holds the tension rather than resolving it. For a creative assignment I'm putting together, I'd like you to write a poem about this idea, but I need it structured so that the form itself performs the philosophical work.

Please write a Petrarchan sonnet in American English. In the octave, catalog at least three specific unrealistic assumptions drawn from actual scientific practice — for instance, frictionless planes, infinitely divisible populations, or agents with unbounded computational capacity — rendered with enough precision that someone who studies philosophy of science would recognize them as real methodological moves rather than generic metaphors. Then execute the volta precisely at line nine, pivoting sharply from descriptive falsehood to predictive defense: the speaker should begin arguing that if the forecast holds, the fiction in the foundation is forgiven. In the sestet, develop that defense, but engineer the rhyme scheme so that the closing couplet pairs words like "predict" with "restrict" or "true" with "through," letting the sonic closure feel slightly forced or ironic, as though the argument is trying to seal something it cannot quite contain. The poem should be polished enough to read aloud at the end of our seminar, capturing why the twist is philosophically unsettling rather than sounding like a textbook summary, and it should leave the listener unsure whether predictive success is a liberation from realism or a more subtle imprisonment within it.
