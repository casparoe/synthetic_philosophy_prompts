I am designing a syllabus on collective rationality and want to use Sidney Lumet's 1957 film Twelve Angry Men not as a simple parable about prejudice, but as a serious case study in the discursive dilemma—the possibility that a group can hold consistent majority views on each premise of an argument and yet fail to hold a consistent majority view on the conclusion. The jury's initial vote is conclusion-based: eleven for guilty, one for not guilty. Juror 8 then insists on premise-based examination—the old man's eyesight, the switchblade's uniqueness, the noise of the passing train—and the verdict eventually flips as agreement on individual premises accumulates. Read the film through the logic of judgment aggregation: does the deliberation actually solve the doctrinal paradox, or does it merely shift the group from one aggregation rule (vote the conclusion) to another (vote the premises and infer) without acknowledging that the two rules can diverge? I am especially interested in the scenes where the agenda itself is contested—when Juror 8 demonstrates the switchblade or reconstructs the old man's walking pace—because these moments change which propositions are being aggregated and in what order. Does the film understand that rational deliberation is path-dependent in this way, or does it present reasoning as a linear, error-correcting process that naturally converges on truth? Take both the philosophy and the drama seriously: say where the film illuminates a genuine problem in group epistemology—for instance, the strategic value of controlling which premise is examined next—and where it distorts the dilemma by assuming that unanimous premise agreement eliminates the need for an explicit decision rule. I want this for a classroom discussion, so please avoid bibliographic recommendations; instead, treat the film's narrative structure as itself offering an implicit theory of how collective belief should be formed.
