Here are my verdicts on concrete hypothetical cases.

Case 1 — The Karelia household altars: In a settlement of about thirty households, each domestic structure contains a small limestone altar in its northeast corner, consistently oriented toward a specific mountain ridge, with identical burnt botanical remains. Verdict: I think this justifies inferring a widely shared household-level ritual with standardized orientation and practice.

Case 2 — The obsidian elite altars: The same limestone altars appear in only the three largest houses, each of which also contains imported obsidian tools and exotic shell beads. Verdict: Here I do not think we can infer a shared communal belief; the altars are better explained as elite status markers or patronage displays.

Case 3 — The circular bench structure: A large public building with a central fire pit and stone benches arranged in a circle is dated roughly eight centuries before any written mention of councils in the broader region. Verdict: I think it is reasonable to infer some form of communal deliberative assembly, but not specific procedural rules, voting mechanisms, or legal codes.

Case 4 — The distant circular benches: A structurally similar circular bench building is found four hundred kilometers away in a region with no shared pottery styles, burial practices, or settlement patterns. Verdict: I do not think this supports inferring the same institution; it is more plausibly convergent architecture addressing a generic need for collective gathering space.

Case 5 — The gendered burial ground: Over fifty adult graves in one cemetery show a consistent pattern: biologically male skeletons are interred with a spear and a hunting toolkit; biologically female skeletons are interred with a spindle and processing tools. Verdict: I think this supports inferring a gendered division of labor or social role, though not necessarily a rigid, legally enforced, or universal system.

Case 6 — The anomalous grave: In that same cemetery, one grave contains both spear and spindle in association with a biologically male skeleton, alongside other goods that mix both categories, with no other grave showing this pattern. Verdict: I am genuinely torn. One part of me wants to say this reveals a recognized exception—perhaps a ritual specialist or high-status individual who transcended ordinary gender roles. Another part suspects it undermines Case 5, suggesting the grave goods mark occupation or age rather than gender.
