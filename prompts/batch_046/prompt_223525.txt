Consider an argument that extends the classic symmetry problem into moral psychology and preference theory in a way that seems to eliminate genuine dilemmas. (1) If a rational agent faces two options that are exactly equal in value, and the agent lacks any preference between them, then selecting one without a further reason is arbitrary and therefore not rationally justified. (2) Any attempt to break the tie by an arbitrary mechanism—randomization, an act of bare will, or a default rule—faces the same problem one level up: if the agent is indifferent between mechanism M and mechanism N, then choosing M is equally arbitrary. (3) Therefore rational resolution of symmetric choice requires an infinite hierarchy of justifying reasons, which no finite agent possesses; hence rational choice in genuinely symmetric cases is impossible. (4) Apply this to moral choice: when two strangers face identical peril and I can save only one, if I have no reason to prefer saving A over B, then saving A is arbitrary. (5) But if arbitrary selection is not rationally permissible, then I am either not required to save either—which violates the intuition that I must do something—or there must be a hidden asymmetry I have missed. (6) Generalizing: if all rational choice requires a non-arbitrary ground, and symmetric situations lack one, then apparently symmetric situations must always conceal a hidden preference or fact. (7) This eliminates the possibility of genuine moral dilemmas—cases where moral reasons genuinely balance—because any balance would render choice arbitrary and hence irrational. (8) Furthermore, applying the reasoning to preference formation implies that every preference must be grounded in a prior preference, yielding either an infinite regress or brute, unjustified preferences at the base, which undermines the idea that rational agents author their own values. (9) Yet we routinely make choices in apparently symmetric conditions—accepting one of two identical job offers, breaking a tie vote, selecting among medically indistinguishable patients—without judging ourselves irrational. (10) So either we accept that rationality permits arbitrary resolution at some point (giving up (1)), or we accept that there are no genuine symmetries in practical life (giving up (9)), with the further consequence that moral dilemmas are illusory and all preferences are ultimately brute. Where does this argument fail? Is the flaw in (1), in the regress in (3), or in the move from (4) to (6) that collapses practical symmetry into hidden asymmetry? If forced to choose, I would keep (1) and allow that rationality can include a primitive executive capacity to resolve ties without further justification, but I want to know what that concession costs—does it save moral dilemmas, or does it merely relocate the arbitrariness into the structure of the will?
