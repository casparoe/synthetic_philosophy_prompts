I'm preparing a handout for an advanced comparative ethics seminar on moral desert, and I need a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—that sorts philosophical positions on karma specifically in its role as a theory of moral desert, not merely as a causal law of action and consequence. The audience is graduate students who understand the basic Buddhist and Jain frameworks but tend to conflate them with secular naturalized analogues; the instrument should force them to see where those analogues actually diverge. I want the root node to be a concrete commitment: "There is such a thing as moral desert, and its satisfaction does not require an intentional distributor like a deity or judge." From there, each branch must split on a diagnostic case or claim that positions genuinely answer differently, phrased so that someone could respond without already knowing the label—no questions of the form "Are you a Theravada Buddhist?" The branches should cover: whether desert-satisfaction requires phenomenal consciousness in the affected subject; whether desert can be interpersonally distributed or must remain strictly individual (here I want you to inject and include a deliberately underexplored position—call it "distributed karmic debt"—in which one agent's virtuous act can offset another agent's undeserved suffering despite no causal connection between them); whether the temporal structure is strictly past-to-future or permits atemporal correlations; and whether the scope extends beyond human agents to non-human animals or sufficiently complex artificial agents. Each leaf should carry either a named position (classical Theravada individual accumulation, Jain pluralist karma with its multiple soul-types, secular naturalized desert, eliminativist retributivism, and the injected distributed-debt view) or be marked "unnamed," with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where the logical space contains combinations that nobody occupies—such as accepting distributed debt while denying that consciousness is ever required for desert, or accepting atemporal correlation but restricting scope exclusively to humans—note them and briefly diagnose whether the gap seems incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, particularly if they both reject distributive agency but differ only in metaphysical framing rather than in practical verdicts on cases. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the goal is to map the conceptual space, not to document scholarship. Keep every node concrete enough that a student answers by judging a scenario, not by picking a team.
