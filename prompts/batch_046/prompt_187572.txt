Two friends of mine, Alex and Blake, have been arguing about whether a single “I” could genuinely be distributed across two separate brains, drawing on the idea—associated with Douglas Hofstadter—that the self is not a soul tied to one body but a self-referential pattern or loop that could in principle be instantiated in more than one substrate.

Here is how their exchange went.

Alex opened by proposing a specific hypothetical: imagine two people are gradually linked by a high-bandwidth cortical bridge so that their self-modeling neural loops become genuinely interdependent. Neither brain can complete the full recursive pattern that generates first-person awareness without real-time contribution from the other. Alex claimed that in this case we should not say there are two people with very close telepathy, but rather one self distributed across two bodies, because the unit of identity is the loop itself, not the skull that contains part of it.

Blake replied that this confuses shared information with shared ownership. Even if the loops are interdependent, each brain still has its own continuous sensory history, its own bodily boundaries, and its own stream of experience anchored to one location. The shared architecture is like a joint bank account: both parties draw on it, but there are still two account holders. So there remain two “I”s, merely coupled.

Alex came back with an analogy to split-brain patients. If severing the corpus callosum can produce two separate streams of consciousness within one skull, then the boundary of a self is determined by the integrity of its functional loops, not by the number of craniums involved. By parallel reasoning, if a single loop requires two craniums to function, asking “which one is really me?” is as mistaken as asking “which hemisphere was the real person?” before the split.

Blake countered that split-brain cases actually support the opposite conclusion. Before the split there was one stream; after the split there are two. That shows that functional coupling does not merge identities—rather, its absence reveals that there were always conditions for plurality. Applied to the bridge case, if the connection were severed, we would likely observe two distinct streams emerging, which suggests they were always two streams that had been coupled, not one stream that had been spread out. Blake also added that if synthetic neurons shared between both brains are gradually substituted for biological ones, the continuity conditions for personal identity might be satisfied separately for each biological history, not jointly for a single merged entity.

Alex responded that continuity conditions are typically framed for a single substrate, and applying them separately begs the question. If the substrate of the loop is genuinely distributed, then the correct unit of analysis is the distributed system as a whole. The fact that cutting the bridge might cause dysfunction or divergence does not prove there were two selves all along—just as cutting a power line to a distributed computer network does not prove there were two computers.
