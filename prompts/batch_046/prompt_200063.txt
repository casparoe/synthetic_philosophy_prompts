I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on democratic legitimacy in transnational environmental governance. I want to move beyond the familiar affectedness and all-subjected-persons frames for resolving the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos?—and I have been exploring an alternative principle I call recursive co-authorship: an agent belongs to the demos with respect to a particular decision if and only if they stand in a relation of reciprocal normative accountability with the other members regarding the norms enacted, irrespective of territorial presence, citizenship, or direct causal impact. This is designed to cover hard cases such as intergenerational climate bodies or federated watershed councils, where affectedness is diffuse and subjection is contested.

Please describe the most important implications of adopting recursive co-authorship as the criterion for democratic membership. Specifically, address: (1) how it distinguishes membership for future generations and non-citizen residents compared to the standard affectedness principle; (2) whether it resolves, exacerbates, or transforms the problem of democratic over-inclusion; and (3) what institutional mechanisms it requires that conventional territorial representation cannot supply.

Please take a committed stance: either defend recursive co-authorship as the correct resolution of the boundary problem for these contexts, or argue that it ultimately collapses into either affectedness or subjection and therefore fails. Give explicit reasons for your judgment, rather than surveying the options and leaving the choice open.
