In a philosophy-of-history seminar on contingency and determinism, a classmate has taken to invoking the nursery rhyme about the lost nail—"for want of a nail the shoe was lost, for want of a shoe the horse was lost..."—as a conversation-stopper in three different contexts. First, she used it to reject my suggestion that historians ought to weight structural economic forces more heavily than individual actions, implying that a single missing nail can topple a kingdom and therefore micro-events always swamp macro-structures. Second, she cited it to claim that historical prediction is impossible in principle, since any initial perturbation amplifies without bound. Third, she appealed to it to argue that counterfactual reruns of history with one tiny alteration would diverge radically at every scale. I need to bring a rigorous disambiguation to our next meeting. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this proverb could be expressing—distinguishing, for instance, claims about counterfactual dependency of outcomes on specific events, about non-linear causal amplification, about epistemic limits on prediction due to sensitive dependence, about the ontological irreducibility of historical explanation to structural laws, and about scale-invariance in explanatory relevance—ranking them from the trivially true to the genuinely controversial? For each reading, please say whether it actually supports the particular argumentative move she made, and whether there is any substantive reading on which the rhyme remains compatible with the view that structural explanations are primary and that macro-causes are not merely epiphenomenal aggregates of micro-fluctuations. Deliver this as a menu of interpretations with verdicts rather than as a defense of a single analysis.
