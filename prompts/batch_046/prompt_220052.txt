I have come to defend what I call the inversion thesis in wild-animal suffering ethics: once field mortality, pest control, and habitat conversion are fully integrated into the accounting, beef produced on well-managed rotational pasture likely carries a lower wild-suffering footprint per kilocalorie than either chicken or plant-based staples, so that the standard recommendation to avoid beef in favor of grains or poultry actually reverses under a suffering-minimization framework. The view rests on three specific commitments—that monoculture grain and soy production have substantially higher mortality densities per nutritional unit than low-intensity ruminant grazing; that the suffering of rodents, birds, and insects killed during harvest, storage, and feed production is morally relevant but can be weighted without collapsing into full interspecies equality; and that pasture-based systems can replace more destructive land uses rather than merely supplement them, yielding a positive “replacement coefficient” of wild lives spared per beef calorie consumed.

What I want is a precise map of what would count against this position—not the familiar objection that farmed cattle suffer immensely, which I treat as a separate problem, but the defeaters that specifically threaten the inversion claim. Which empirical findings about field mortality rates, which structural results in population ethics or interspecies moral weighting, and which supply-chain realities would be fatal rather than merely awkward? For example, if chicken feed conversion turns out to be so efficient that total wild deaths per edible calorie remain lower for poultry despite high feed-crop mortality, does that collapse the view, or can a defender absorb it by adjusting the replacement coefficient? If suffering by small vertebrates and invertebrates is shown to be negligible relative to mammalian suffering in some well-grounded framework, does that undermine the comparison or only shift the arithmetic? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to awkward, distinguish what a committed defender should concede as genuinely damaging from what they can dismiss by refining scope conditions—such as restricting the claim to specific pastoral regimes or excluding farmed-animal suffering altogether—and say whether these qualifications risk insulating the thesis to the point of unfalsifiability. I also want to know what distinctive predictions the inversion thesis makes that rival suffering-minimization positions do not, and whether any foreseeable development in agricultural ecology or moral philosophy would make the view well-insulated in a way that should worry its defenders rather than comfort them.
