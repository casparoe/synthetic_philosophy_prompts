I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who has developed a view she calls "anchored abstractionism," intended as a direct response to Benacerraf's epistemological dilemma for mathematical platonism. On her account, mathematical objects remain fully abstract and causally inert, but human knowledge of them is mediated by what she terms "structural calibration": a non-causal, empirically disciplined process in which stable physical instantiations of mathematical structures allow cognition to lock onto abstract relations without direct causal contact. She argues that this dissolves Benacerraf's challenge—we can know numbers without causal interaction—without retreating to formalism or accepting a uniformly causal theory of knowledge across all domains.

I'd like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid generic biography or summary prompts. Specifically, I want at least one question asking why she regards the causal theory of knowledge as not merely false but misapplied specifically to mathematics, paired with a follow-up anticipating her likely response that causal requirements are domain-variable. I also want a question that presses the mechanics of structural calibration: if the process is genuinely non-causal, what distinguishes it from mere reliable belief-formation, and how does she prevent the objection that she is secretly relying on causal neural processing? Another question should explore whether her view renders mathematical error impossible in principle, with a follow-up keyed to her probable answer that error occurs at the calibration stage rather than in the abstract domain itself. Finally, include a question asking what kind of evidence—empirical, conceptual, or structural—could genuinely count against anchored abstractionism, given that she rejects both causal-access conditions and pure a priori intuition as necessary for mathematical knowledge.
