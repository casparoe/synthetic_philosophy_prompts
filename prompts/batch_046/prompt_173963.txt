I am preparing the second session of a small graduate reading group—six students drawn from philosophy, sociology, and law—on the idea that moral cultures are partly defined by which emotional architecture they privilege. We have already worked through basic action-theory, so they understand intentionality and social norms, but they carry no formal training in moral psychology. For next week I want to teach the distinction between guilt and shame not as a clinical taxonomy but as a structural choice: guilt as an obligation-to-repair logic anchored to specific deeds, shame as a visibility-and-belonging logic anchored to global self-assessment. From that structural base I want to raise the normative question—which is the “better” moral emotion?—and then push further into an institutional-design question that is rarely asked in this form: can a culture deliberately engineer its practices (legal transparency, rituals of public accountability, anonymous versus named restitution, the architecture of confession) to favor one emotional regime over the other, and if so, does the choice remain stable?

Plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering the explanation itself. Begin with the dependency structure: which component must come first—the phenomenological contrast between deed-focused and self-focused distress, the analysis of social conditions that make each emotion available, or the normative comparison—and explain why introducing the institutional-choice question too early causes the arc to collapse. Then choose one carrying example or tightly paired scenario that can bear the full weight of both the conceptual distinction and the design question: it should clearly separate reparable obligation from global exposure, and it should make visible how altering the audience-structure (witnessed versus anonymous, named versus masked) transforms the emotional logic without changing the underlying wrong. Next, map the misconceptions in the order they will predictably arise: I expect they will reduce shame to mere embarrassment, treat guilt as automatically more autonomous and therefore more “civilized,” and imagine that a culture “chooses” an emotional architecture the way it chooses a tax code. For each, specify exactly where in the sequence it should be intercepted and what brief counter-move will dissolve it without derailing the dependency chain. Design one check—an exercise or a single targeted question—to be used at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the structural point (that the choice concerns visibility and obligation-structures, not individual mental health) or have merely adopted a new slogan that shame is toxic. Finally, state explicitly what must be left out at this level—perhaps evolutionary accounts of these emotions, clinical therapeutic frameworks, or dense ethnographic case studies—so the arc does not branch into unsupportable detail, and indicate whether the institutional-choice thesis should be presented as settled, contested, or deliberately left open.
