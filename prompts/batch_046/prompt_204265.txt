I am preparing a module for my advanced seminar on rational belief revision, and I would like you to construct a philosophical dialogue that my students can use to test whether they understand the normative architecture of self-trust rather than merely its standard formulations. Write a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors—Dr. Aisha Rahman, who defends a modified Reflection Principle, and Dr. Samuel Okonkwo, who denies it—debating Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle in light of what I call epistemic labor asymmetry: the thesis that a future self's credence in a proposition is often the product of conceptual work—new distinctions, restructured categories, or revised background theories—that is not available as mere additional evidence to the present self. Rahman should argue that Reflection can be preserved if we treat the future self not as a black-box oracle but as a specialized expert whose reasoning, though conceptually transformed, remains accountable to shared norms of coherence; she should specifically address Okonkwo's worry that this smuggles incommensurability through the back door. Okonkwo, in turn, should press the point that treating future credence as expert testimony ignores the fact that conceptual innovation can change the very content of the propositions being assigned credence, so that Reflection demands identity conditions across temporal stages that rationality cannot guarantee. The exchange should be genuinely dialectical—each speaker must engage with the other's previous claim rather than delivering parallel monologues—and neither should appeal to bibliographic authority or invoke obscure formalisms that require outside reference. Keep the tone rigorous but accessible to graduate students who have read van Fraassen's original discussion but have not specialized in formal learning theory.
