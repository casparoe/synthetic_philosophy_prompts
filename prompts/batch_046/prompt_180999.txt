I have spent the last several months thinking about democracy outside any formal training, mainly through Spinoza's political writings. The idea that has stayed with me is that democracy is not merely one regime among others but the form in which the power of the multitude is least alienated — in which collective strength and collective reason coincide. Spinoza seems to tie this to the formation of common notions: shared understandings that arise when people are affected together and reason together about what is useful in common. I find this compelling, but I also live in an environment where the conditions for being affected together are structured by predictive systems — recommendation architectures, ranking algorithms, and filtered information flows that shape which voices, events, and interpretations reach collective awareness. No one person controls these filters, yet they determine the field within which any common notion can form.

This raises a cluster of conceptual questions I want to work through seriously, but I have only limited time and no institutional support. So I need a defended prioritization, not a bibliography or survey. If I can devote roughly one sustained year of conceptual work to this intersection — Spinoza's democratic theory and the mediated formation of collective reason — which open questions or lines of implication deserve my attention first, and which should I treat as downstream or overrated?

In particular, I see three lines that seem important, though I may be missing others:

First, whether the non-alienation of the multitude's power — Spinoza's core democratic criterion — can still be satisfied when the shared affective and cognitive environment is shaped by predictive mediation that operates below the level of explicit collective choice. Second, whether common notions genuinely require direct intersubjective encounter and mutual affect, or whether algorithmically curated shared experience can generate the same rational collective power without the same direct interaction. Third, if the first line resolves negatively, what conceptual work is required to rethink democratic legitimacy: does it point toward structural redesign of information architecture, toward a modified account of representation, or toward accepting that mediated democracy is a different kind of regime altogether?

Please rank these lines, or substitute better ones, according to what should come first for someone in my position — a non-specialist trying to build a coherent framework for evaluating democratic institutions today. For each item, explain your criteria: tractability for conceptual rather than empirical work, how many other answers depend on resolving it, and whether settling it would actually change practical or institutional judgment. Show me the dependency structure: which questions are upstream, which collapse if an upstream answer goes one way, and which are largely independent. Also, tell me which line sounds important but is likely overrated — a question that absorbs attention without shifting the overall framework. Finally, indicate what kind of reasoning, comparison, or hypothetical case over the coming year would cause you to reorder the priorities.
