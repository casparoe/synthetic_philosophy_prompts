I'm writing an essay for my metaphysics module on Leibnitz's law and those two identical spheres Black wrote about — the ones in a totally symetrical universe where you can't tell them apart. It made me wonder about a weird split in institutional practice. Art museums and auction houses will pay tens of millions for an original painting, but if you make a perfect molecular copy in a lab — same atoms, same brushstroke patterns, literally every qualitative feature identical — it's treated as basically worthless junk. Meanwhile in forensic science, if two fingerprints match every ridge and whorl, the system assumes they came from the exact same finger; there's no institutional room for saying these are numerically distinct prints that just happen to be qualitatively identical. So you've got the art world treating perfect duplicates as genuinely different objects, and forensics treating them as the same object. What philosophical commitments about identity and individuation does this split actually presuppose? Is the art market implicitly accepting that objects have non-qualitative properties — like causal origin or primitive thisness — which means they're quietly rejecting the identity of indiscernibles? And does forensics secretly depend on hidden relational properties to do the individuating work? Are these two practices philosophically consistent with each other, or do they just show that institutions apply Leibnitz's law selectively depending on whether money or prison sentences are involved? And if we actually tried to apply one consistent view of identity across both fields, what would have to change in museum practice or in courtroom forensics?
