I'm running a workshop for about 18 grad students in this sociology-philosophy crossover course I TA for, 90 minutes, and I'm tired of the usual Weber lecture followed by a slide about Japan. I was talking with my roommate about "rest debt" — this idea I made up that every work-ethic system borrows leisure from the future and either pays it back or defaults — and I want them to feel that tension instead of just hearing about it. Design a role-play or simulation where they sit on a labor board for a fictional small country across three rounds. Round one: they write rules about work hours and leisure rights with no outside guidance, definately letting them construct norms themselves. Round two: a crisis hits and they vote whether to extend shifts or protect rest time, with hidden payoff cards I only reveal after. Round three: they learn whether anyone in their system "collapsed" from overwork and have to defend their round-two vote without knowing in advance which rules cause the collapse. They should build the Protestant work ethic logic, the 35-hour week as institutional repayment, and karoshi as debt-collection themselves, and only hear those three names in the debrief.

The debrief should map each round onto the three frameworks, tell me what will predictably go wrong — like students treating the collapse card as just a game mechanic rather than a moral failure, or one group gaming the crisis to prove their system is best — and be honest about which feature of karoshi the activity can never reproduce (the actual physical death and family grief, not just the symbolic failure) and that I have to supply verbally. No lecture up front, please don't ask for a bibliography or reading list since I don't need that, and include a frank note on how to handle it if the group never reaches a crisis decision in round two because they avoid conflict too politely.
