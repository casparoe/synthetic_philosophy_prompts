I therefore ask for your actual credence, decomposed with explicit reasoning, regarding the following precise claim: in a functioning liberal democracy, a bureaucrat whose routine, non-violent administrative actions structurally perpetuate an unjust policy through diffuse mediation has stronger moral reason to pursue internal, disclosure-based resistance (for instance, selective non-compliance or protected whistleblowing) than to maintain obedience out of respect for democratic legitimacy, provided that formal democratic mechanisms for reform remain technically available but are rendered practically inert by institutional inertia and the absence of identifiable, immediate victims. Please break your reasoning into four terms: first, the baseline probability that democratic legitimacy generates any pro tanto obligation to obey substantively unjust laws; second, the conditional probability that diffuse bureaucratic causation weakens that obligation more severely than direct participation in visible injustice; third, the probability that internal resistance is morally superior to traditional public civil disobedience in cases of structurally mediated harm; fourth, the probability that democratic channels should be treated as effectively closed when they are technically open but institutionally blocked. Assign each term a rough numerical value with a single line of justification, combine them into an overall credence expressed as a specific number between zero and one, and indicate which term you are least confident in and what kind of conceptual argument or empirical observation would most substantially revise it. I do not seek bibliographic references; I am interested solely in the architecture of your reasoning.
