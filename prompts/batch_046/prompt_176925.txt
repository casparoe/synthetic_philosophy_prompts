So I was at a bar last week with two old friends, Maya and Leo, and the conversation got weird because I've been lowkey obsessed lately with whether I should actually switch from marketing into longtermist work or if I'm just overthinking it. Anyway they started arguing about anthropic reasoning — specifically self-sampling vs self-indication, and Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher thing — and I was the one who brought it up so I feel like I need to understand who's right before I make any big career moves.

Maya went first. She said if you take self-indication seriously, meaning your own existence is actual evidence for theories that predict more observers, then the fact that I'm here and conscious should push me toward thinking there will be tons of future people, or tons of observers across possible worlds, because those theories make my existence more probable. So she thinks SIA actually strengthens the case for caring about the far future: we should update upward on the total number of beings, which makes actions that affect them more morally weighty.

Leo said that's basically the presumptuous philosopher fallacy. Under self-sampling you treat yourself as randomly drawn from your referance class, so finding yourself existing tells you nothing about total population size — it's like being surprised you're not alone in a huge stadium. He argued SIA lets you reject a small-universe cosmological model just because you personally exist, which is absurd, and that SSA blocks that mistake cleanly.

Maya pushed back and said Leo's painting SIA as "more observers always win," which is a strawman. She said it's about updating on the likelihood of your specific indexical position, not raw headcount. Then she pointed out SSA has its own weirdness: if you apply it consistently, shouldn't you be surprised to find yourself living in a big city instead of a tiny village? So maybe the referance class selection is doing all the real work in SSA anyway, and if you're honest about restricting the class to reflective agents capable of asking this, SIA and SSA converge — but SIA still gives you reason to expect more of those agents exist, which feeds back into her far-future point.

Leo countered with this hybrid idea he called "weighted self-sampling," where your sampling weight is proportional to cognitive complexity — so finding yourself as a philosophically reflective agent updates you differently than finding yourself as a simple organism. But he said that's still smuggling in self-indication through the back door. He also said Maya's argument collapses either way: if you apply SSA with future people in the reference class, you actually get a doomsday-style update that humanity is likely near the middle of its timeline, which undermines longtermism more than SIA supports it. So he thinks both assumptions point away from her conclusion, just for different reasons.

Maya said Leo's doomsday point only works if you illegally include not-yet-existent people in your present reference class, which she refuses to do, and that his weighted hybrid is interesting but he hasn't shown it's incoherent — just that it isn't pure SSA. They kept going but I zoned out.
