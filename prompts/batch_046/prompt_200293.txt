I am drafting notes for a philosophy of mind seminar essay on insect consciousness and moral aggregation. My central claim is that the standard scale argument—multiplying a low per-insect probability of sentience by an enormous population—faces what I want to call an "epistemic dilution problem." If our warrant for attributing phenomenal states to any particular insect remains below the threshold required for direct moral patienthood, then aggregating across billions of individuals produces only a statistical expectation, not a distributed justification for treating the class as a collective bearer of interests. I further suggest that this creates tension with precautionary reasoning in animal ethics, since the same probabilistic structure that amplifies expected suffering also undermines the epistemic basis for attributing that suffering to concrete organisms rather than to an abstract population parameter. I am not defending a definitive denial of insect sentience, but I want to examine whether the scale argument can survive once we require that moral consideration be anchored in warranted attributions to particular beings rather than in aggregate risk calculations alone.

Please comment on the coherence and accuracy of this framing, with particular attention to the "epistemic dilution problem" idea. In your response, please separate what is relatively settled in discussions of insect consciousness and aggregation-based moral reasoning from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation—especially regarding the connection between statistical expectation and moral justification—as if it were the consensus view. I would also appreciate feedback on whether this line risks mischaracterizing standard precautionary arguments in the literature.
