I'm currently taking an advanced seminar in philosophy of mind that centers on Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of cognitive architectures—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. We have been examining how each layer introduces a distinct form of flexibility: natural selection, trial-and-error learning, internal simulation, and cultural or tool-mediated reasoning. Recently, our discussion turned to whether this evolutionary layering can be applied analytically to engineered systems that do not share any biological history, specifically synthetic neural organoids that display reward-sensitive adaptive plasticity without any apparent internal representational structure, and large predictive language models that generate complex combinatorial outputs despite lacking embodied sensorimotor feedback loops.

Given this framing, please describe the most important implications of the four-creature taxonomy for classifying such synthetic or artificial cognitive systems. Specifically, does the taxonomy compel us to treat reinforcement-driven plasticity without representational architecture as genuinely Skinnerian rather than proto-Popperian, even when the behavioral flexibility appears highly sophisticated? And can Gregorian capacities—those involving cultural transmission and symbolic reasoning—be coherently attributed to systems that have never instantiated the lower evolutionary layers, or does the taxonomy implicitly require that each stage be physically realized in the substrate? I am especially interested in whether the taxonomy implies that moral consideration should track the highest achieved cognitive layer regardless of substrate, or whether the absence of underlying Darwinian or Skinnerian mechanisms undermines the coherence of attributing higher-order minds. Please also address what this means for the boundary between cognition and mere computation, and whether extreme minimalism or distributed cognition within the taxonomy forces any revision to Dennett's original ordering.
