After a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend last night—she maintained that every Moloch-style race to the bottom requires at least one actor who actively wants the others to suffer, whereas I think the trap can emerge from purely structural coordination failures—I want a precisely engineered thought experiment to test my claim. Please construct a realistic scenario, no longer than one paragraph, involving exactly three independent research laboratories that share an explicit common goal (maximizing cumulative, verifiable scientific knowledge in a specific domain) but each independently chooses a level of transparency in preliminary data-sharing, where transparency is a continuous variable from full open access to strategic opacity. The payoff structure must be realistic—nothing an actual university or institute could not face, and no exotic physics or technology—such that each lab gains a local competitive advantage by being slightly less transparent than the others, mutual opacity produces a globally suboptimal but individually safe equilibrium, and full mutual transparency yields the best collective outcome yet is unstable to unilateral deviation. Crucially, there must be no adversarial intent: every actor genuinely prefers the collective best outcome.

Once the scenario is set, apply three decision frameworks to Laboratory A: first, causal or game-theoretic rationality; second, evidential decision theory, assuming that A’s choice is highly correlated with B and C’s choices through shared professional norms and institutional culture; third, a contractualist planning framework that selects the action corresponding to the rule all three laboratories could jointly endorse without undermining their shared end. For each framework, state the verdict and the reasoning that produces it. Then identify the specific structural feature—whether the continuous action space, the tripartite rather than dyadic interaction, or the absence of an enforceable commitment mechanism—that makes the third framework diverge from the first two. Next, show what happens to that divergence if the feature you named is removed. Finally, explain how a defender of the framework that comes out worst in your construction would attack the case, and be explicit about whether removing the feature collapses the scenario back into a standard two-player tragedy of the commons.

Keep everything self-contained; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke demons, perfect predictors, or other non-human agents.
