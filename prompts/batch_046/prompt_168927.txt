In a recent philosophy of value seminar, a colleague kept returning to the maxim: 'If there is no craving and no suffering, then nothing is lacking.' She used it to dismiss the charge that tranquilism — the view that only the absence of desire and pain carries normative weight — renders a fulfilled life indistinguishable from an empty one. I want to understand precisely what that slogan could be claiming, because its persuasive force seems to depend on hidden premises about what counts as absence. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express — separating phenomenological readings (about felt deprivation), normative readings (about what harms or goods are real), metaphysical readings (about whether absent states are genuine absences), and semantic readings (about the conditions for correctly applying 'lacking') — and for each indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is defensible, and whether it genuinely answers the emptiness objection or merely redefines 'lacking' so that the objection cannot be formulated? Please present the options as a structured menu with verdicts rather than selecting one, since I need to take the list back to argue about which interpretation she actually needs for her defence of tranquilism.
