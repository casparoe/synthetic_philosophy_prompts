Since List and Pettit formalized the discursive dilemma in the early 2000s, debate over judgment aggregation has settled into a familiar stalemate. One camp treats collective inconsistency—where majority judgments on premises and conclusion diverge—as a failure of democratic rationality that demands premise-based aggregation, strategic voting, or institutional redesign. The other camp argues that consistency is an individual-level norm inappropriately projected onto groups, and that suppressing divergence in the name of coherence merely masks genuine pluralism. Meanwhile, a smaller line of work asks whether the dilemma applies at all once we reject the assumption that groups hold propositional attitudes like single rational agents—suggesting the problem might dissolve if collective judgment is framed instead as distributed cognition rather than unified belief. Is the long-running impasse substantive, verbal, or a case of different questions—normative design versus descriptive social choice, rational agency versus distributed cognition—pursued under one heading? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, particularly their appeals to group agency and to pluralism, and specify what would count as progress: a formal result, an empirical study of real deliberation, a reconceptualization of collective intentionality, or an argument that no progress should be expected? Finally, say what would change your mind—what finding or argument would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
