You serve as the senior provenance auditor for a national museum that acquires large private collections—typically 200 to 500 objects—through bequest. For each object, you review an independent file of ownership history, export licenses, and expert authentication. You have high confidence in every individual file, yet institutional experience shows that in any collection of this scale at least one object carries a significant documentation error or concealed provenance gap, and you must sign a single attestation covering the entire batch. Your board asks you to operationalize the logic of the lottery paradox and the preface paradox—specifically, the tension between rational acceptance of each individual claim and rational acceptance that not all claims in the set hold—into a reusable decision procedure for these batch certifications.

Write the procedure as a step-by-step protocol with explicit tests an auditor can apply in order, including: (1) how to treat high-confidence individual judgments when they are conjoined in one institutional statement; (2) a threshold or rule for when collection size or statistical error-rate expectation should block blanket attestation; (3) worked applications to three cases—one where every file is strong but the collection exceeds the statistical error threshold, one where a single file is weak but the rest are impeccable, and one where the auditor’s own prior experience suggests a hidden gap that the documentation does not reveal; and (4) explicit flag points where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the procedure will necessarily yield an answer the auditor dislikes. Keep the protocol usable by auditors without philosophical training, preserve the conceptual distinction between failure of belief closure and rational aggregation across sets, and state clearly what the procedure cannot do.
