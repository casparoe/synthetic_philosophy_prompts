As a journalist writing on moral psychology and emerging technology, I am examining whether a particular philosopher’s recent case judgments about Strawsonian reactive attitudes form a consistent pattern or conceal an ambiguity that only a boundary case can expose. The thinker in question—let us call her Vasquez—has published explicit verdicts on four non-paradigmatic scenarios. She holds that moral resentment is unfitting toward a wild predator that kills livestock, on the grounds that the predator lacks the capacity for moral understanding that reactive attitudes presuppose; that resentment is fitting toward a corporate board that knowingly authorizes toxic dumping, treating organizational deliberation as expressing a genuine quality of ill will; that gratitude is unfitting toward a beneficial genetic mutation that confers immunity, since mutations are not agents in any relevant sense; and that indignation is fitting toward a human caregiver who neglects a patient out of callous indifference, but unfitting toward the same caregiver when severe dementia has destroyed their capacity for interpersonal recognition.

These four rulings are jointly consistent with at least two competing interpretations of Strawson’s participant stance. On the Interpersonal Recognition reading, a reactive attitude is fitting only if the target can enter into reciprocal moral relationships—understanding the attitude and being responsive to it. On the Expressive Agency reading, the attitude is fitting whenever behavior manifests a quality of good or ill will that issues from practical reasoning rather than mere mechanism, regardless of whether the target can reciprocally understand the attitude.

Consider now the case Vasquez has not yet addressed: a distributed artificial system—specifically, a language model fine-tuned across thousands of independent nodes—that consistently generates harmful moral recommendations to users. The system adjusts its weights in direct response to critical feedback, altering its outputs over time, yet it possesses no unified consciousness, no narrative self, and no capacity to feel guilt or comprehend moral demands in anything like the human sense. Given Vasquez’s track record, should she conclude that users can fittingly feel moral resentment toward this system? Which of her four prior judgments is the closest analog, and why? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, what principle would she be adopting with each possible choice? Finally, specify one further case—varying either the system’s architecture or its mode of interaction with users—that would best distinguish whether she is operating with the Interpersonal Recognition rule or the Expressive Agency rule.
