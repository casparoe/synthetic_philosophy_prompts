I'm drafting notes for an essay on a failure mode in AI-safety debate protocols that I think gets mislclassified under the usual "obfuscated arguments" framing. The standard story is that a deceptive model hides malicious reasoning inside a computation too long for a human judge to unpack. I want to argue there's a structurally distinct problem: distributed ontological drift. Over many adversarial turns, both debaters can—without deception—gradually shift the implicit ontology of the evaluation frame, so the judge applies criteria that were coherent at turn one to a conceptual landscape that no longer supports them. This isn't hidden reasoning; it's frame erosion that exploits ambiguities in the judge's background commitments.

The essay would run in three parts. First, a sharp distinction between epistemic obfuscation (concealing premises inside long computations) and ontological drift (shifting what counts as a valid move or relevant distinction across turns). Second, an argument that debate protocols amplify drift because adversarial pressure rewards exploiting ontological ambiguity rather than resolving it, letting debaters maintain internally coherent but divergent frameworks. Third, a claim that this is why scaling debate strength doesn't solve alignment: stronger debaters become better at sustaining coherent internal ontologies that diverge from the judge's, not merely better at hiding bad arguments.

I'd value comments specifically on whether this distinction analytically holds, or if drift collapses into familiar framing effects or theory-ladenness. I'm also testing whether the proposed mechanism—cooperative frame erosion under adversarial incentives—is genuinely different from the standard "judge is outmatched by computation length" account, or just a redescription. No literature survey or citations needed; I'm probing whether the conceptual architecture is sound and where it breaks.
