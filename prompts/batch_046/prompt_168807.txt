I have spent the last year trying to think through karma not as a cultural slogan but as a serious theory of moral desert—something that explains why particular people receive particular outcomes because they have genuinely earned them, not because of random chance or mere cause and effect. I do not have formal training in philosophy, but I want to know whether the idea can survive careful scrutiny before I commit to it. So I want the full specification first: what must any adequate theory of karma as moral desert actually deliver?

Write the desiderata. For each requirement, state what it demands, the motivating case or distinction behind it, and whether it is non-negotiable or only a preference of a particular tradition or camp. The list should cover at least the following: (1) the distinction between moral desert and bare causal regularity—why a car crash following a reckless act is not karma, even though it is causally linked; (2) the problem of undeserved suffering, including suffering that clearly originates in no agent’s prior action; (3) the accounting rules—whether desert is quantitative, qualitative, or both, and whether rewards and punishments must be symmetric in kind or only in moral weight; (4) the scope—whether karma applies strictly to individuals, or also to groups, families, or societies, and whether debts can be inherited or transferred; (5) the mechanism problem—whether the theory requires a supernatural delivery system or can operate through natural psychology, social feedback, and institutional consequences alone; (6) the intertemporal problem—how desert survives death if it survives at all, and whether that requires literal rebirth, some other form of continued existence, or can remain metaphorical; (7) consistency with modern understanding of human agency and neuroscience, without collapsing into determinism that eliminates the very responsibility karma is meant to track.

Then tell me whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. I am especially interested in whether strict proportionality, individual-only accounting, and a fully natural mechanism can all hold together, or whether accepting one forces the abandonment of another. If there is a genuine impossibility result here—an internal tension that no version of the theory can resolve—that is the part I most want to see highlighted.

In addition, include one further desideratum that popular discussions usually ignore: the problem of ledger opacity. If karma tracks intentions, mental states, and subtle qualities of character that are often hidden even from the agent, then the system must either possess perfect access to those states or risk frequent false positives in assigning outcomes. How should an adequate theory address this?

Finally, briefly score the three main pictures people actually discuss—the Buddhist model of intentional action and rebirth, the Hindu model tied to dharma and cosmic social order, and the secular retributivist analog—against your list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the conceptual architecture laid out clearly.
