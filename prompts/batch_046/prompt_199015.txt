I am in the middle of drafting a historical novel that moves between Florence circa 1480, Oxford circa 1680, and a present-day fictional archive, and to ground the narrative's temporal texture I have composed a short argumentative essay—intended for private circulation rather than academic publication—that I now want to test for conceptual coherence before I build scenes around it. Rather than rehearsing the familiar debate over whether the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are real epochs or later inventions, I have tried to push the argument into a more granular, almost material register. I propose that these three labels should be understood not as chronological containers but as competing regimes of temporal legibility—systems that determine which artifacts survive as evidence and which are rendered invisible—so that the Renaissance becomes real not as an epoch but as a nineteenth-century curatorial technology that retroactively sorted paintings and texts into a narrative of rebirth; the Scientific Revolution acquires reality only through the material standardization of instruments—precision balances, air pumps, standardized lenses—that made experiments comparable across different courts and cities; and the Middle Ages functions as an administrative fiction invented by humanist scholars to justify the redirection of ecclesiastical patronage away from monastic scriptoria and toward secular art and civic architecture. The essay concludes with a somewhat audacious claim: that a novelist possesses a special, non-epistemic license to treat these categories as simultaneously false in historiographical terms and structurally necessary for narrative form, because fiction can hold contradictory temporal regimes in suspension without resolving them.

I am writing this without access to a research library, and I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want to see whether the argument can stand on its own conceptual legs. I would be grateful for specific comments on whether the distinction between curatorial technology and material standardization actually holds up, whether the claim about humanist patronage redirection sounds like a defensible historical mechanism or an overreaching just-so story, and whether the conclusion about fiction's license to maintain contradictory regimes feels earned by the preceding premises or reads like an aesthetic escape hatch I have smuggled in to avoid a more rigorous ending. Please also tell me whether the overall framework risks collapsing into a mere nominalism about history, and whether there is any hidden inconsistency between treating the Scientific Revolution as instrumentally real and the Renaissance as purely curatorial.
