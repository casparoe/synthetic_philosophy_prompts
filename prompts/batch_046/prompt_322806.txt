You are serving as the grader for an advanced undergraduate examination in comparative constitutional design. The exam asks students to work with an analytical framework—the "structural separation thesis"—which holds that a constitutional monarchy can stabilize democratic legitimacy only if there is a clear institutional boundary between the symbolic locus of state continuity (the Crown) and the operational locus of democratic authority (the legislature and cabinet), with the former being entirely non-partisan and convention-bound.

The exam question was as follows:

Using the structural separation thesis, compare how the separation between symbolic and operational authority operates in the United Kingdom and Japan, and evaluate whether Sweden's Instrument of Government of 1974 resolves the residual tensions more effectively than either arrangement. In your answer, address: (a) the legal versus conventional status of the monarch's remaining formal powers in the UK and Japan; (b) whether Bagehot's distinction between the dignified and efficient parts of government helps explain the Japanese case, or whether it is inapplicable because Japan's separation is codified rather than conventional; and (c) whether the absence of a democratic mandate for the monarch undermines the democratic legitimacy of the state as a whole, or whether the separation thesis demonstrates that it does not.

The student submitted the following answer (approximately 450 words):

The structural separation thesis holds that constitutional monarchy survives in democracies only when there is an institutional boundary between the symbolic source of state continuity—the Crown—and the operational exercise of democratic authority, with the former entirely non-partisan and convention-bound.

In the United Kingdom, this separation is conventional rather than statutory. The Crown retains legal reserve powers—such as dissolving Parliament and appointing the Prime Minister—but these are exercised only on the advice of ministers, enforced by political practice rather than by judicial review. Bagehot's distinction between the dignified and efficient parts of government captures this precisely: the monarchy provides ceremonial continuity, while Parliament and Cabinet perform the actual work of governance. Because the rules are unwritten, they depend on political self-restraint.

Japan presents a sharper separation because it is codified in the 1947 Constitution. Article 1 defines the Emperor as the symbol of the State and of the unity of the people, deriving his position from the will of the people with whom sovereign power resides. The Emperor performs acts in matters of state only with the advice and approval of the Cabinet, and possesses no reserve powers whatsoever. One might be tempted to apply Bagehot's framework here as well—treating the Emperor as dignified and the Cabinet as efficient—but this would be a category error. Bagehot was describing an organically evolved British arrangement, not a constitutionally engineered division of labor imposed after 1945. In Japan, the separation is not conventional but textual, so the dignified/efficient distinction fails to explain the legal architecture.

Sweden's Instrument of Government of 1974 resolves the remaining ambiguities more thoroughly than either the British or Japanese models. The reform formally abolished every political function of the monarchy. The King of Sweden no longer participates in government formation in any capacity—not even as a formal signatory to the commission—because the Speaker of the Riksdag alone nominates the Prime Minister and the Riksdag votes on the appointment. By removing even ceremonial involvement in the selection of the government, Sweden eliminates any residual democratic deficit and makes the separation total.

This also answers the legitimacy objection. A hereditary office lacks an electoral mandate, but democratic legitimacy is not unitary. The democratic institutions derive authority from popular consent, while the monarch derives authority from constitutional definition and historical continuity. Because these two sources are institutionally separated, the state remains fully democratic despite the hereditary element. Sweden demonstrates this most clearly: once the monarch has literally no governmental function, the democratic mandate of the legislature and cabinet is entirely unambiguous.

Therefore, the separation thesis confirms that monarchy and democracy are compatible, provided the boundary is absolute. The Swedish case achieves this absoluteness through statutory total separation, while the UK and Japan retain minor conventional or ceremonial overlaps that create unnecessary theoretical tension.
