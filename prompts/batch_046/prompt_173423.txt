A sociologist of generations holds that a cohort is a real ontological kind—not merely a demographic label—only when a discrete birth-range shares formative conditions powerful enough to produce a distinct social type. Her recorded verdicts: Baby Boomers (c. 1946–1964) are real, formed by post-war economic expansion and mass institutional growth; the Silent Generation (c. 1928–1945) is not, being only an interstitial demographic trough; Millennials (c. 1981–1996) are real, shaped by the Great Recession and the transition to social media during adolescence; Generation X (c. 1965–1980) is not, lacking both a unified macro-level shock and a discrete communication environment; Gen Z (c. 1997–2012) is real, formed by smartphone-native adolescence and pandemic-era institutional disruption; the so-called "9/11 Generation" (those aged 10–25 in 2001) is not, since the event was too brief and geographically uneven; and "Digital Natives" defined solely by technology access are not, because adoption is continuous rather than cohort-bound.

Now consider the proposed "Neuroadaptive Cohort": children born roughly 2030–2045 who, from age six, undergo standardized non-invasive brain-computer interface training in public education, yielding measurably different cognitive architectures, yet who otherwise inhabit the same social-media ecosystems and enter labor markets structurally resembling Gen Z's.

Predict her verdict.
