Read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies through Blaise Pascal’s reflections in the Pensées on the necessary union of justice and force: the idea that justice without coercive power is impotent, that power without justice is tyrannical, and that what a society calls “justice” often collapses into nothing more than established custom backed by violence. Focus on the conch not merely as a symbol of democratic order but as an attempted institution that binds legitimate speech to physical presence and the implicit threat of exclusion. Trace how Ralph’s authority depends on the boys’ willingness to treat the shell as binding, and ask whether Golding presents the collapse of that authority—through the hunt’s first killing, Piggy’s death, and Jack’s final dominance—as a confirmation of Pascal (justice was always powerless once force withdrew) or as a deeper challenge to Pascal’s framework, namely that force and justice were never genuinely separable capacities on the island, since even Ralph’s rules required the boys’ fear of ostracism and punishment to function. Identify specific scenes where a Pascalian reading illuminates a character’s choice, and point to where the novel resists being reduced to illustration: for instance, whether Jack’s tribe generates its own normative justification in a way that blurs Pascal’s distinction between legitimate union and pure tyranny. Conclude by saying what Golding’s narrative adds to the philosophical debate that Pascal’s fragments alone do not—perhaps a psychological account of how quickly a justice-force compound dissolves when the external state vanishes, or an argument that the two are conceptually fused rather than alignable by reason. Keep the reading faithful to both the text and the philosophy: do not treat the boys as mere allegorical figures, and do not distort Pascal’s claim to make it fit the plot.
