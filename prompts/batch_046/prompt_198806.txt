As a retired physicist who has recently begun reading in New Testament studies, I have noticed a recurring methodological pattern that I cannot place precisely within the philosophy of science or historiography, and I would like help identifying whether it has an established name and, if not, how best to delineate it.

The setting is the use of criteria of authenticity in the quest for the historical Jesus—principally dissimilarity to early Christian practice, embarrassment to the evangelists, coherence with other established sayings, and multiple independent attestation. In practice, these criteria do not seem to function as independent tests that can falsify a scholar’s overall reconstruction. Instead, they behave like tunable parameters. When a saying passes the criterion of embarrassment but conflicts with a scholar’s preferred portrait of Jesus, the criterion is not abandoned; rather, an auxiliary qualification is introduced—the embarrassment was rhetorical rather than historical, or it was manufactured by a later redactor who wished to heighten the drama. Conversely, when a saying fails multiple attestation but fits the reconstruction well, the failure is explained away as an artifact of source preservation, without any corresponding reduction in the weight assigned to attestation elsewhere. Over successive studies, each criterion accumulates individually reasonable exceptions that collectively insulate the core reconstruction from refutation, yet no single exception is counted as a strike against the criterion itself.

This looks like the slow accumulation of auxiliary hypotheses that philosophers of science have criticized, but it is more specific: the adjustments are not made to save a single predictive theory, but to preserve the epistemic authority of a family of diagnostic rules while allowing those rules to be overridden case by case. It is not quite the protective belt surrounding a hard core in a research program, because here the criteria themselves are being protected, not the historical portrait they serve. Nor is it ordinary confirmation bias, because the adjustments are often explicitly theorized and defended in the literature rather than being hidden. I am looking for the precise concept, if one exists, for this specific pattern: the immunization of evaluative criteria through individually defensible, case-specific qualifications that prevent the criteria from ever delivering a verdict that threatens the reconstruction they are meant to support.

Please organize your response as follows:

1. Characterization of the pattern (approximately 350 words): Describe the phenomenon in your own terms, using concrete hypothetical or real examples drawn from historical-Jesus scholarship, and state exactly what makes it distinct from generic theory-protection.
2. Mapping onto existing concepts (approximately 300 words): Identify the nearest established ideas—ad hoc adjustments in the Popperian sense, Lakatosian protective belts, Duhem-Quine underdetermination, confirmation bias, or others—explain how the described pattern relates to each, and specify where the fit is imperfect.
3. Boundary conditions and distinctions (approximately 250 words): Clarify what is not part of the pattern—legitimate refinement of criteria, transparent weighting adjustments, or ordinary scholarly disagreement—and state the conditions under which a qualification counts as part of the phenomenon rather than as sound methodological revision.
4. Terminological proposal (approximately 200 words): If no established name fits precisely, propose a term and define it, including its necessary and sufficient conditions and at least two boundary cases that fall just outside it.
5. Conclusion (approximately 100 words): Summarize whether the pattern has an established home, what that home is or is not, and what practical difference it makes to treat it as a named phenomenon.

Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further literature; I am interested in the conceptual carving, not in citations.
