Here is an argument I have been turning over about multiverse explanations in fundamental physics, and I am convinced it proves too much, but I cannot locate the exit. (1) The string landscape together with eternal inflation generates a multiverse spanning an enormous range of physical constants and effective laws. (2) Within this multiverse, observers can exist only in regions where constants permit complex structures, so the observed values are explained anthropically rather than by a single dynamical mechanism. (3) Once anthropic reasoning is licensed for one constant—say, the cosmological constant—it applies to every observed feature: if our universe had a different electron mass or a different nuclear force strength, we would not be here to observe it, so those values are also "explained" in the same way. (4) A framework that can accommodate any observed value of any parameter makes no risky predictions about what we should actually see. (5) Science requires theories to take predictive risks; a theory compatible with all possible data explains none of it. (6) Therefore, either multiverse cosmology is unscientific, or we must abandon the idea that empirical risk is necessary for science—both conclusions seem to undermine standard philosophy of science. (7) The standard reply is that multiverse theories do make statistical predictions about distributions, but for any actually observed value one can always adjust the prior over the landscape post hoc, so the risk remains illusory. Where does the argument slip? If I want to keep (2) and reject (4) through (6), what exactly do I have to pay—do I need a principled boundary between legitimate and illegitimate uses of anthropic reasoning, or do I need to reject the claim that science requires predictive risk? And if the flaw is in (3), what distinguishes the cosmological constant case from the electron-mass case in a way that does not look ad hoc?
