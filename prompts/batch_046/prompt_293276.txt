I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last five years studying philosophy of mind and value theory. Next month I will present a seminar paper to a philosophy of well-being reading group at a research university. The audience consists of analytic philosophers: one specialist in desire-fulfillment theory, one defender of an objective-list account that emphasizes knowledge and achievement, and several others who are broadly mental-state internalists but deeply skeptical of arguments that rely on intuition pumps rather than on conceptual analysis. My thesis is that the familiar experience-machine scenario reveals a structural condition on well-being that standard treatments overlook. I call this condition open-loop causal sustenance: for a mental state to contribute genuinely to well-being, it must be causally sustained by the very external state of affairs it represents, rather than being maintained within a closed feedback loop of simulation. I argue that this condition undermines not only hedonism but also unrevised desire-fulfillment and knowledge-based objective-list theories, unless they are amended to include it.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face in the thirty-minute question period. Order them by the damage each could do to my position, phrased in the vocabulary these philosophers would actually use—distinguishing in particular the lines of attack the desire-fulfillment specialist and the knowledge-theorist are most likely to pursue. For each objection, indicate what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm, and precisely where my reply is weakest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks: the challenge that distinguishes my open-loop condition from a purely epistemic or relational condition that I have not yet clearly separated from it. I do not need a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need a speaker’s prep sheet.
