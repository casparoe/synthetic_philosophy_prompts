My late grandfather’s estate has recently been settled, and I find myself holding substantial assets I did not earn. The experience has made inheritance and dynastic wealth personally urgent for me, and I want to understand the philosophical landscape precisely—not through party labels, but through the normative commitments that actually divide people on whether and how such transfers should be taxed or restricted.

Please construct a decision tree (or taxonomy) of positions on inheritance taxation and dynastic wealth, designed to sort a respondent by what they actually believe rather than by which slogan they prefer. Begin from a concrete starting commitment—say, that unearned advantages should not determine life prospects—and branch through yes/no or either/or nodes that isolate genuinely divergent commitments. The branching questions should include at least the following distinctions:

- Whether the primary wrong (if any) lies in the unequal opportunity created for the heir, or in the intergenerational social power and stratification that dynastic wealth perpetuates regardless of the heir’s individual prospects;
- Whether family autonomy or the continuity of intimate projects (for example, preserving a family enterprise or a particular mode of life) carries independent normative weight that can justify transmitting assets to descendants;
- Whether equality of opportunity is satisfied merely by leveling the playing field at adulthood (say, via estate taxes that fund public goods) or requires active suppression of accumulated advantage across generations;
- Whether the morally relevant comparison is between heirs and non-heirs within one society, or between generations within a family lineage, such that limiting inheritance might violate an intergenerational social contract;
- Whether the scale and form of wealth matter: is there a principled distinction between assets sufficient for basic security and assets large enough to confer political influence, and does that distinction determine permissibility?

Each leaf should carry the name of a position if one is standard (for instance, a libertarian family-autonomy view, a luck-egalitarian suppression view, a radical egalitarian abolitionist view) or be marked “unnamed” if the combination is defensible but rarely articulated. For each leaf, provide one sentence describing its most awkward practical commitment—for example, that a view permitting family continuity must accept vastly unequal starting social capital, while a view suppressing all large transfers must deny parents the ability to fund a child’s specific vocation.

Also flag any pairs of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate—positions that converge on every practical branch but differ only in rhetorical framing or in metaphysical commitments that do not affect the tax policy they support. And identify any unoccupied answer patterns: combinations that seem logically available but are apparently unheld, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect in the literature.

The instrument should be usable without specialized training: every node must be phrased as a concrete case judgment (for example, “If a parent could leave ten million to a child or donate it to a fund that improves educational access for strangers, is the parent permitted to choose the child?”) rather than asking “Are you a luck egalitarian?” Avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not depend on verification of specific scholars’ texts.
