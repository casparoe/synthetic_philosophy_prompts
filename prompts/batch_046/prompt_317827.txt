I'm a doctoral student in historical sociology, and at a recent workshop on causal explanation in the social sciences, two senior colleagues—one in economic history and one in the philosophy of social science—became locked in a sustained disagreement about whether Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis genuinely establishes that culture can function as an independent cause, or whether it ultimately collapses into circular hermeneutics.

The economic historian, Dr Reeves, argued that Weber succeeds precisely because he operates with a configurational, rather than mechanistic, model of causation. She claimed that the elective affinity between inner-worldly asceticism and rational capitalist bookkeeping cannot be reduced to material incentives, since Weber's comparative analysis—particularly the absence of comparable capitalist rationalisation in societies such as China and India despite materially favourable conditions—supplies genuine counterfactual structure. For Reeves, values act as formal causes that configure the meaningful orientation of action rather than as efficient causes that push behaviour like physical levers. She insisted that critics who demand a transmission mechanism linking theological doctrine directly to accounting practice are misapplying a physicalist criterion that Weber explicitly rejected.

The philosopher, Dr Halloway, replied that this defence saves the thesis only by rendering it unfalsifiable. He argued that if values are formal rather than efficient causes, they do no explanatory work distinct from redescribing observed patterns; they become post-hoc narrative coherence rather than causal explanation. Halloway noted that Weber identifies the Protestant ethic largely through the very behavioural patterns he seeks to explain—systematic labour, reinvestment of surplus, abstention from conspicuous consumption—so the argument risks circularity: the value-complex is inferred from economic behaviour and then invoked to account for it. He conceded that comparative cases provide some structure, but maintained that selecting societies on the basis of the presence or absence of the value-complex already assumes its causal relevance.

Reeves countered that Halloway confuses the identification of a value-complex with its causal efficacy. She observed that Weber draws on independent theological and pastoral sources—sermons, catechisms, devotional literature—to establish the presence of ascetic values prior to their economic effects, thereby breaking any circularity. She added that the problem of explaining with values arises only if one assumes that all explanation must be law-governed and predictive; Weber's aim was causal adequacy in the sense of rendering an outcome historically intelligible, not predicting it with nomological regularity.

Halloway responded that causal adequacy without predictive or robust counterfactual bite is merely narrative consistency. He granted that theological texts establish the presence of beliefs, but argued that presence is not efficacy: one must still demonstrate that those beliefs altered economic behaviour in ways that institutional or material conditions alone could not have produced. Without specifying how beliefs overcome collective-action barriers, resource constraints, or institutional inertia, invoking values functions as an explanatory stop-gap rather than a genuine cause.

They then asked me to adjudicate. Please evaluate their exchange move by move: rate each of Reeves's and Halloway's principal arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification for each rating, then state who is more right overall. In addition, identify what both parties appear to miss about the specific problem of explaining with values—particularly whether there is a coherent middle position between treating culture as an autonomous formal cause and dismissing it as an epiphenomenal redescription.
