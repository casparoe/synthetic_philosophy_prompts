Here is an argument that extends the standard inefficacy objection to an apparently absurd conclusion. Identify where, if anywhere, it goes wrong; say which premise is least costly to reject and what rejecting it costs—especially whether it forces us to accept obligations to perform causally inefficacious acts. If you think the argument is valid and we must accept its conclusion, defend that position.

(1) If an agent knows that her individual act is neither necessary nor sufficient for producing a collective harm, then the act is not wrong in virtue of contributing to that harm.

(2) In large-scale collective harms—anthropogenic climate change, industrial animal agriculture, and democratic elections—each single act (one flight, one meat purchase, one vote) is neither necessary nor sufficient for the harmful or beneficial outcome.

(3) So none of these individual acts are wrong (or obligatory) in virtue of their contribution to the collective result.

(4) If an act is not wrong in virtue of its collective contribution, and there is no other independent moral reason bearing on it, then the act is morally permissible (and not required).

(5) For emissions, meat consumption, and voting, there are no other independent moral reasons—ecological virtue, direct animal welfare, civic duty—that apply independently of the collective contribution.

(6) Therefore, individuals have no moral reason to reduce emissions, avoid meat, or vote; and symmetrically, no one has a reason to maintain collective goods like taxation or public infrastructure, since each contribution there is also neither necessary nor sufficient.

(7) But the conclusion that democratic participation and public contribution are never obligatory is absurd.

(8) Therefore, at least one of premises (1) through (5) must fail.
