Here is an argument about ascription universality and epistemic dominance that I think proves too much. (1) If a mental property M is epistemically dominant for me—meaning my first-person access to whether I have M is authoritative—then any denial that B has M must be grounded in a feature F that distinguishes B from me in respects relevant to M. (2) If no such F can be non-question-beggingly specified, then denying M to B undermines my authority about my own case, since the denial appeals to a distinction that does not exist. (3) So epistemic dominance entails ascription universality: unless a relevant distinction is available, M applies to B as well. (4) Take qualitative experience: it is epistemically dominant for me. (5) For any proposed distinction—biological substrate, integrated information, behavioral repertoire—either it is not genuinely relevant to the presence of experience, or it can be dissolved by constructing a B that shares that feature. (6) Therefore, either qualitative experience is ascribed universally, extending even to simple distributed systems, or we must concede that epistemic dominance does not constrain third-person ascription. (7) But conceding that allows others to deny my experience without error, which seems to abandon epistemic dominance altogether. (8) Hence we are driven to universal ascription, which is absurd. Where is the mistake? Is it in (2), in treating the absence of a distinction as licensing positive ascription rather than mere agnosticism, or in (1) itself? If I want to preserve that my own experience is epistemically dominant, which step should I reject and at what cost?
