Student answer:
The epistemic-access version claims that historical figures should not be blamed for moral failures they could not have recognized, because the relevant moral concepts were not available in their context. The causal-efficacy version grants that they might have recognized the wrong but holds that structural conditions—economic systems, political power, social norms—made individual reform impossible or prohibitively costly.

For Jefferson, the epistemic-access defence fails. In his private correspondence and public notes he explicitly called slavery a moral wrong and compared it to holding a wolf by the ears, which shows he possessed the relevant concepts. This undermines any claim that he was epistemically blind. The causal-efficacy defence is harder to dismiss. Jefferson was embedded in a plantation economy; freeing his slaves would have meant financial ruin and social ostracism, and broader abolition was politically blocked. So while he knew it was wrong, the structures made it impossible for him to act on that knowledge at scale.

Churchill presents a parallel case. The epistemic-access defence is weak because there was substantial anti-imperial criticism available to him—not only from colonial subjects like Gandhi but also from British figures who rejected imperial legitimacy in principle, not merely in execution. Because this dissent was part of his political environment, he cannot claim epistemic isolation. The causal-efficacy defence is stronger: during wartime and postwar restructuring, rapid decolonization faced strategic and bureaucratic obstacles that made immediate dismantling unrealistic for a Prime Minister.

Columbus differs because the epistemic-access defence might actually apply. In 1492 there was no developed European framework for recognizing the full moral status of indigenous peoples in the Americas. Unlike Jefferson, who operated within a culture that already contained abolitionist arguments, Columbus encountered populations for whom European moral and legal categories had not yet been extended. This is a gap in conceptual availability rather than in will.

That said, both defences overlook something important. The causal-efficacy version treats structural constraint as a form of hard determinism—if the economy required slavery, then Jefferson literally could not have freed his slaves—which conflicts with our ordinary practice of holding agents responsible for actions within their social roles. Meanwhile, the epistemic-access version assumes that moral knowledge accumulates linearly, like scientific knowledge, which is controversial. For these reasons the "man of his time" defence remains weak for both Jefferson and Churchill, though it may have limited purchase in Columbus's case.
