Write a short story about a legal archivist in a society that has adopted anatta—the Buddhist denial of a permanent, unitary self—as the foundation of its civil and moral law. The archivist is responsible for adjudicating disputes over "continuity claims" made by citizens whose biological brains have been progressively replaced by synthetic substrates. Because the law denies that any enduring subject persists through these replacements, personal identity is treated as a conventional designation sustained by institutional records rather than an intrinsic property. The archivist encounters a case in which two separate, incompatible emotional and moral histories have become embedded within the same bodily and legal identity, with neither history able to assert priority over the other. Through this case, explore whether treating identity as a fiction is liberating—freeing individuals from attachment—or violently reductive, erasing genuine patterns of suffering and obligation that seem to demand a single bearer. Do not resolve the tension through either a mystical recovery of true selfhood or a nihilistic dissolution of all responsibility; instead, depict personal identity as a practical construction that becomes ethically binding precisely because there is no underlying owner to be bound.
