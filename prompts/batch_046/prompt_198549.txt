Design an exam question for an upper-level seminar in decision theory and philosophy of science that applies the theorem on the non-negative value of free information—commonly associated with I. J. Good—to a deliberately non-standard scenario. The scenario should involve a research team that can acquire a preliminary measurement before choosing between two mutually exclusive experimental architectures, but with two complications that do not appear in textbook presentations: first, the measurement instrument suffers from a calibration drift that is not independent of the unknown parameter being estimated, and second, the team operates under a binding institutional pre-commitment that requires them to disregard the measurement and default to the safer architecture if the signal falls below a regulatory threshold. Write the exam prompt itself, specifying that students have forty-five minutes, that the essay is worth forty percent of the final grade, and that a strong answer should run approximately eight hundred words. Then compose a model answer of about six hundred words that does not merely recite the theorem statement but instead derives or explains the conditional expectation argument step by step, explicitly identifies the independence assumption that fails under calibration drift, and shows how the regulatory pre-commitment collapses part of the decision tree so that the expected utility comparison is no longer governed by the standard non-negativity result. Finally, write a marking scheme in continuous prose—without headings, bullet points, bold type, tables, or any other formatting—that distinguishes an A-level response from a B-level response by naming the precise analytical moves required: correctly separating the value of information from the value of experimentation, locating the branch where correlation reverses the sign of the expected value, and recognizing that pre-commitment transforms an information-gathering problem into a constrained optimization problem. The entire response must be presented as continuous prose only, like a formal letter, and must not request bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to obscure theories that would require outside lookup.
