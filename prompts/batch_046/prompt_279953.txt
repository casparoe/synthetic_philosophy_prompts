As a novelist accustomed to tracking how characters justify risk, I have noticed a recurring argumentative move for which I lack a precise name. Consider a decision-maker who sizes commitments in strict proportion to present capital—betting a fixed fraction, never exceeding it, so that long-run growth is maximized and total ruin is structurally avoided. I observe this not only in gamblers and investors but also in fictional figures who scale emotional or strategic exposure by an internal sense of bankroll. Now imagine a threat that is not merely a large loss but a qualitative alteration: the rules shift, or a single failure eliminates not just resources but the very option space that made proportional scaling meaningful. Rather than abandoning the fractional principle, the reasoner retains it globally yet inserts local adjustments—exceptions for broken correlation, for collapsed option spaces, for systemic thresholds—each defensible in isolation, yet together they bend the straight geometric line into a curved form that is harder to test, without anyone counting that curvature as a theoretical cost. I have seen this pattern in discussions of growth-optimal investment, in serial commitments under existential risk, and in arguments about long-term rationality where catastrophic downside is treated as a dimensional collapse rather than a point on a utility curve. Does this slow accumulation of individually reasonable curvature-corrections to an otherwise linear geometric scaling rule have an established concept? How does it differ structurally from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, from simple risk aversion, or from ad hoc immunization—what distinguishes a dimension-collapsing exception from a merely convenient exemption? If no precise term exists, what boundary conditions would define it: when does bending the geometric line preserve rational coherence, and when does it become unfalsifiable? I seek not a bibliography but the carving itself—what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
