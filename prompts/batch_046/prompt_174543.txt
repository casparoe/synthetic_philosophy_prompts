I'm a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and experimental philosophy, and I'm preparing material for an advanced seminar on the architecture of folk psychological concepts. In our discussions of the Knobe effect, my colleagues tend to interpret the asymmetry—where agents are seen as acting intentionally when they produce harmful side effects but not when they produce beneficial ones—as a performance-level distortion, specifically a moral heuristic interfering with an otherwise value-neutral competence for attributing intentionality. I want to argue the contrary: that this asymmetry is not a contaminant but a constitutive feature of the concept of intentional action, revealing that normative evaluation is built into the competence itself rather than imported during judgment. Could you please develop a rigorous argument for this position? Specifically, address the objection that evolutionary or cross-cultural explanations can rescue a modular, descriptive account by treating the asymmetry as a locally adaptive bias; argue instead that these explanations fail conceptually because they assume a separability between explanatory and normative functions that the concept of intentional action does not actually possess.

To give the argument a sharper edge, I ask you to adopt a somewhat unconventional functional framing: rather than treating intentionality attributions as primarily causal-explanatory devices, treat them as mechanisms of social coordination whose primary role is to stabilize expectations about accountability and response. From this premise, demonstrate that if intentionality judgments serve coordination rather than explanation, then the valence-sensitive pattern documented in the Knobe cases is precisely what a well-calibrated competence should produce, not a malfunction to be corrected. Finally, address the cost of this view—namely, that it seems to forfeit any purely descriptive psychology of action—and defend the claim that descriptive purity was never a coherent desideratum for folk psychology, so that abandoning it is not a loss but a clarification.
