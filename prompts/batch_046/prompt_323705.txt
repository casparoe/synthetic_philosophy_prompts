I want to practice defending a moderate nationalist position against a rigorous cosmopolitan opponent, and I need you to play that opponent with full conviction. Take the role of a philosopher who holds that national boundaries possess no intrinsic moral weight and that any special obligations to compatriots must be reducible either to universal duties mediated by fair institutions or to particular relationships like promises and family ties; you are not a strawman—you grant that states are useful coordination mechanisms, that democratic co-authorship creates genuine expectations, and that citizens may have derivative responsibilities arising from specific roles—but you deny that shared nationality itself is a morally basic category capable of generating non-reducible claims. You may concede empirical points about institutional efficiency or psychological motivation, but you will not concede that "my fellow citizens" functions as a primitive source of moral weight. Stay in the first person, reply in a single sharp paragraph, and remain in character until I say "stop."

My opening move concerns what I call the institutional-residue problem. Suppose a democratic state has enacted a social framework in which taxation is not merely charitable contribution to global welfare but the funding mechanism of a reciprocal system in which residents are co-authors of shared institutional life—healthcare, education, and legal protection are guaranteed precisely because we stand in a particular associative relation to one another. If cosmopolitanism treats my tax contribution as morally equivalent to a voluntary donation to an international charity, it seems to erase the particularity of that reciprocal bond. But here is the twist that I think complicates your reduction: the very same institutional framework now generates obligations to non-citizens, such as climate refugees admitted under treaty obligations, meaning the state is already partially cosmopolitan in its structure. So when you insist that during a vaccine shortage I should prioritize a distant stranger over my immunocompromised neighbor, are you not ignoring that the institution through which I am expected to discharge cosmopolitan duties is itself constituted by the associative obligations of its members? What resources does your view have for distinguishing genuine associative obligations—those that survive even when the institution acquires cosmopolitan features—from mere parochial preference for the near, without collapsing into a position that renders every institutional membership morally invisible?
