I've been tracking a recurring pattern in reasoning about the special composition question that I haven't seen clearly named, and I want to know whether it corresponds to an established distinction or requires a new one.

Consider two configurations. First, an artifact case: parts are arranged in a warehouse so that each stands in the spatial and causal relations characteristic of a functioning bicycle, yet they are interspersed with parts arranged according to the design of a completely different machine—say, a loom—such that no part belongs exclusively to one organizational scheme. Intuitions do not merely split over whether there are two objects, one, or none; rather, the presence of the second scheme seems actively to suppress the compositional claim for the first, not just by competing for parts but by undermining the very judgment that the conditions for bicycle-composition have been satisfied. If we gradually remove the loom-parts, leaving only the bicycle-parts in place, people reliably say a bicycle now exists—suggesting that the earlier absence was not merely vagueness but a kind of destabilization.

Second, a biological case: a cluster of cells realizes the functional profile of a heart, yet the same cells, at a different organizational level, realize the profile of a neoplasm. Here people are often willing to allow both descriptions, or to resolve the tension by appeal to integration with a larger living system. But apply the same reasoning to artifacts—ask whether a heart-shaped arrangement of metal parts embedded in a larger functioning robot counts as an organ—and the willingness to tolerate dual composition seems to diminish.

The phenomenon I am trying to isolate is this: in certain configurations, the satisfaction of compositional conditions for one object is destabilized not by physical impossibility but by the simultaneous satisfaction of incompatible compositional conditions for another, and the strength of this destabilization varies with the ontological category involved. This is not the standard problem of coincident objects, which usually assumes that if conditions for A and B are both met then both exist, leaving only the puzzle of shared parts. Here the puzzle is that meeting the conditions for B appears to prevent meeting them for A in the first place, and the prevention is stronger for artifacts than for organisms.

Does this have an established name—as a variant of competitive exclusion in mereology, or as a specific failure mode of functionalist composition? Or does it fall between existing categories, requiring a new term that distinguishes it precisely from coincidence, from vagueness, and from context-sensitive ontology? I will not be satisfied with a bare label. I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is and is not, where its boundaries lie—whether, for instance, it requires that the two schemes be physically co-realizable but conceptually incompatible, or whether it extends to cases where only one scheme is actually realized but an alternative is merely salient. If no established concept fits, please coin one with a precise definition and specify its boundary conditions, including how it behaves across artifact, biological, and social domains.
