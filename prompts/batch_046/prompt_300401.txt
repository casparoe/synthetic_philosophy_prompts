I'm developing a research program that tries to bridge Buddhist normative ethics—specifically the centrality of cetanā and the kusala/akusal root-analysis of action—with consequentialist evaluation as it is actually used in global health and climate governance. Rather than a survey, I want a defended prioritization: among the conceptual tensions below, which two or three open questions should occupy the center of the agenda, and which should be treated as downstream distractions or overrated?

The tensions are these.

First, whether the Buddhist claim that an act's moral quality is fixed by its mental roots—greed, hatred, and delusion versus non-attachment, goodwill, and wisdom—can be reconciled with outcome-sensitive evaluation without collapsing into a disguised form of rule-utilitarianism.

Second, whether upāya, where an advanced practitioner might perform an act that conventionally violates precepts if motivated by compassion and wisdom, provides a genuinely non-consequentialist exception to moral rules, or whether it is better reconstructed as a hidden consequentialist calculus about long-term spiritual welfare.

Third, how to model karmic weight—the idea that some actions carry heavier ethical consequence because of the conjunction of object, intention, and context—within a quantitative moral framework. Specifically, does karmic weight function like a scalar multiplier on aggregate welfare, like a lexical constraint that blocks certain trade-offs, or like a feature that resists aggregation altogether?

Fourth, whether the Buddhist distinction between conventional and ultimate truth implies that consequentialist reasoning belongs only to the conventional level, and if so whether that renders consequentialism instrumentally useful but ultimately false, which would undermine its deployment in policy design.

Please rank these in terms of which deserve sustained philosophical effort over the next few years. Fix the audience and the resource constraint clearly: this is for analytic philosophers working without deep Pali philology, with about three years of focused work, aimed at producing conceptual tools that could actually shift how Buddhist-informed ethics operates in applied domains like public-health allocation or environmental governance. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability with current conceptual tools, how many other debates depend on resolving this one first, and whether settling it would change downstream practice or theory. Identify which questions are upstream, which are tractable now, and which you would deprioritize because their resolution would not alter much either philosophically or practically.

Please separate what is broadly settled among scholars working at the intersection of Buddhist ethics and consequentialism from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as whether upāya genuinely licenses precept-violation or whether consequentialist reasoning is confined to conventional truth—as if it were the consensus view. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I want reasoning I can evaluate on its own merits without access to a specialized library database.
