I've been trying to work out a decision rule for social and tech policy and I keep coming back to this: the only changes we should ever allow are strict safe Pareto improvements. By that I mean not just Kaldor-Hicks efficiency or compensation in principle, but literally that in every world compatble with our current evidence, every affected party — including future people and maybe even nonhuman agents with standing — is made no worse off according to their own welfare ranking, and there's zero risk of catastrophic downside for anyone. I think this avoids a lot of the usual objections to cost-benefit analysis because it refuses to trade away anyone's wellbeing.

But I want to know where this actually breaks. What formal results or conceptual arguments show that non-trivial safe Pareto improvements are either impossible or vacuous in realistic choice spaces? How do endogenous preferences mess with the 'no one worse off' clause — if adopting the change alters what people value, does that make the view circular or sneaky permissive? And what about the non-identity problem: if a policy brings different future people into existence, in what sense can they be 'not made worse off' by a change that was necessary for them to exist at all? I'm looking for an honest exposure map: which objections defenders of this view would concede as genuinely damaging, which are just misunderstandings, and whether the view risks being insulated into unfalsifiability by defining 'safe' too broadly. Also — does it collapse into status-quo bias in a way that's a feature rather than a bug?
