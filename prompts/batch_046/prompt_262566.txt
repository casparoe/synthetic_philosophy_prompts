You serve as the procedural-ethics officer for a regional network of palliative and psychiatric-acute-care units. Roughly twice each week, interdisciplinary committees submit intervention proposals—revised sedation protocols, involuntary-hold criteria, screening expansions, or budget reallocations—that pit the reduction of severe suffering against autonomy preservation, life extension, staff moral integrity, or distributive fairness. You are committed to a suffering-focused ethical framework, but you need it translated into a reusable, stepwise decision procedure that committees without philosophical training can run consistently, with sign-off required within forty-eight hours by both a clinical lead and a non-clinical community representative.

Turn negative utilitarianism, together with the specific conceptual distinctions it forces upon practice—the asymmetry between harm and benefit, the difference between eliminating currently instantiated suffering and preventing its occurrence, and the problem of suffering generated indirectly through institutional feedback loops—into an operational checklist. The procedure should be strictly ordered: specify the exact questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the conditions under which the procedure terminates with a rejection, the conditions for approval, and the precise nodes where the question is irreducibly one of values rather than facts and must be handed back to the committee for judgment. It must explicitly handle hard cases: interventions that reduce one patient’s suffering but predictably increase another’s; proposals that prevent future suffering only through structural analogues to preventing future existence (for example, redesigning admission criteria so that high-risk populations never enter the system); and cases where suffering reduction conflicts with an explicit autonomous refusal.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals. First, a protocol allowing deeper palliative sedation for terminal patients who request it but whose families object on religious grounds. Second, a mandatory expanded adolescent-trauma screening program that would identify more at-risk individuals but requires involuntary reporting mechanisms that themselves generate distress and stigma. Third, a budget shift that moves resources from long-term rehabilitative care to acute crisis intervention, designed so that the procedure yields an answer the committee members who proposed it will find unwelcome. For each case, trace the path through the checklist, mark every point where judgment replaces procedure, and state the final determination together with its reasoning.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure is incapable of doing—whether in resolving quantitative trade-offs without suffering metrics, in adjudicating conflicts between suffering-reduction and non-suffering goods that the framework treats as subordinate but which institutional practice treats as hard side-constraints, or in addressing suffering that is purely structural and lacks a direct phenomenal bearer. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; ground the procedure only in distinctions that can be applied by someone without access to external sources.
