I've been convinced by a version of the Epicurean line that death can't be a harm to the dead, but not for the usual you-are-not-there-when-dead reason. The version I like says harm has to be experienced or at least borne by a subject at the time it occurs, and since death eliminates the subject, there's literally no one to bear the harm. But it also accepts that prenatal non-existence wasn't harmful, not just because of symmetry, but because there was never a subject who could have been deprived. So the argument isn't just no-subject-at-time-of-death but deprivation requires an actual subject with a continuing interest, and death terminates that interest permanently rather than frustrating it.

What would count against this? I want the real defeaters, not just but my grandma died and I feel sad. Which parts are doing the work: the metaphysics of harm, the asymmetry claim, or the idea that interests can be permanently terminated without being violated? What evidence or argument should actualy move someone holding this view? And is there a risk it's been qualified into unfalsifiability—like if I just define harm as requiring a present subject, then of course death can't harm? I'd like an honest assessment of whether this version is well-insulated or genuinely exposed.
