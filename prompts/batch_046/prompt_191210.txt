I'm noticing a pattern in practical reasoning with imprecise probabilities, and I don't know if theres a name for it. Someone says the risk of failure is somewhere between 5% and 25%. When they're arguing for a cautious policy they treat the 25% as the number that matters; when they're arguing against intervention they suddenly say "well it's probably closer to 5%". The range isn't being updated consistently — it's being mined for whichever endpoint fits the conclusion they already wanted. I know about ambiguity aversion and those classic urn experiments, but this feels different: it's not about preferring known over unknown risks, it's about using the bounds of a range as a kind of rhetorical reservoir. A second example: climate damage estimates given as "2 to 6 degrees by 2100" get collapsed to the low end in economic models that want to justify inaction, and to the high end in advocacy pieces that want urgency, with nobody explaining why one bound is operative in each case. Is there an established concept for this selective endpoint-mining? If not, how should I distinguish it from ordinary ambiguity aversion, and where does it sit in imprecise-probability theory?
