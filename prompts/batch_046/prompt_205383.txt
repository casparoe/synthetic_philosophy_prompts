Here is an argument extending Davidsonian radical interpretation that seems to eliminate false belief altogether. (1) In radical interpretation, we assign meanings and beliefs jointly so as to maximize agreement between interpreter and interpreted, holding shared environmental causes constant. (2) The same charity constraint applies diachronically: to recover the content of my own earlier mental states, I must interpret my past self using my present beliefs as the fixed point. (3) Yesterday I sincerely took the conference to be in Lyon, but today I know it is in Marseille. By (1) and (2), charitable self-interpretation requires attributing to my past self the belief that it is in Marseille—reinterpreting my use of "Lyon" as referring to Marseille or to some other true feature—since attributing false belief violates charity unless syntax and behavior overwhelmingly resist. (4) But the behavioral and syntactic evidence (booking trains, repeating "Lyon") can always be accommodated by a vocabulary reinterpretation, just as in cross-linguistic cases. (5) Therefore I never genuinely believed the conference was in Lyon; my apparent error was only a misinterpretation of my own past mental content. (6) Generalizing, retrospective self-interpretation corrects every apparent false empirical belief, so false belief is impossible. (7) That is absurd, so something must fail. Which step should I reject? If I defend (2), must I deny privileged access to my own past thoughts? If I defend (4) and allow syntax to block reinterpretation for self-cases, do I break the symmetry between self- and other-interpretation that Davidson's framework requires?
