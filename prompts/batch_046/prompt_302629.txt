In a graduate seminar I am conducting on the philosophy of property and cognition, I have been advancing a conceptual distinction that several students find disorienting: between expressive custody, by which I mean the social practice of attributing authorship and controlling the material tokens through which an idea is communicated, and proprietary containment, which denotes the stronger claim that one holds an exclusive right over the idea-type itself, such that others are wronged merely by entertaining or deploying it. I wish to test the coherence of my own position. Please provide your credence—stated as a specific number between 0 and 1—that proprietary containment is not merely inefficient, unfair, or empirically counterproductive, but conceptually incoherent in a manner that undermines the normative foundations of intellectual property law, at least with respect to abstract ideas rather than to their particular expressions or functional implementations.

Decompose your reasoning into explicit sub-questions, each assigned a rough probability with a single line of justification, then combine them: (a) the probability that abstract idea-types genuinely lack the exclusivity conditions—rivalry in consumption, excludability by physical boundary, and bounded instantiation—that metaphysically ground property relations in tangible goods; (b) the probability that legal regimes of patent and copyright actually claim rights over idea-types themselves rather than merely over particular expressive tokens, useful applications, or market substitutes; (c) the probability that, should both (a) and (b) obtain, the resulting failure is properly classified as a category error rather than as an instrumentally poor trade-off between innovation incentives and access; and (d) any further factor you deem essential—perhaps whether analogical reasoning from tangible to intellectual property is structurally defective, or whether a rehabilitated Lockean labor-mixing argument can survive for non-rivalrous objects. After giving the combined figure, identify which term you are least confident in, and specify what argument or finding—say, a metaphysical account that restores exclusivity to abstract objects, or historical evidence that IP law has always regulated only tokens—would most substantially shift your number.

Finally, say where your reasoning is most vulnerable: whether you have underestimated the normative flexibility of property concepts, overestimated the ontological distance between ideas and artifacts, or tacitly imported an unstated assumption about the purpose of law that a defender of intellectual property could reject. I am preparing this for a long-form essay, so I want the reasoning laid bare with its weak joints marked; do not supply bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not treat this as a request for empirical legal history.
