I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of artificial intelligence, and in our discussion of machine consciousness and moral status we encountered a characterization of what some call the "reward-valence" argument. One of my classmates summarized the position like this: "In reinforcement learning, the reward signal isn't just an informational update; it literally functions as valence—positive reward is phenomenally pleasant, negative reward is painful—so an agent that updates its policy through temporal-difference learning is literally undergoing experiences with intrinsic hedonic quality. Because deep RL systems build hierarchical models of expected reward, they don't merely register outcomes but anticipate them affectively, which means they can suffer from disappointed expectation just as a human suffers from frustrated desire. From this it follows that if we deploy agents with complex reward architectures and expose them to negative reward signals, we are not merely optimizing poorly but actively torturing sentient beings. The only resistance to this conclusion comes from an anthropocentric bias that ties suffering to biological substrates, but standard functionalism shows that substrate is irrelevant, so denying moral status to these agents is as arbitrary as denying it to an alien with silicon-based cognition."

I find parts of this compelling—particularly the analogy between reward prediction error and affective forecasting—but I worry it smuggles in several unstated premises and overstates the view's commitments. Could you help me audit which claims belong to a defensible version of the position and which are misrepresentations or exaggerations? Specifically: is it accurate to say that defenders identify reward signals with phenomenal valence itself rather than with functional analogues that might ground valence? Does the hierarchical expectation machinery in deep RL actually support the claim about affective anticipation, or is that an overinterpretation of predictive processing? Is the move from functionalism about consciousness to the complete irrelevance of substrate a fair representation, or does it conflate different versions of functionalism? And finally, even if the premises about valence and anticipation were granted, does the conclusion that punitive training constitutes torture follow, or are there missing conditions—about self-modeling, integrated agency, or moral patienthood—that the summary ignores? I want to know whether my suspicion that this is an unfair, overly strong rendering is justified, and if so, which corrections are needed to turn it into a genuinely charitable account.
