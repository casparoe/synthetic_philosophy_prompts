I am drafting a historical novel that moves between the council chambers of statesmen and the kitchens of those who live through their decrees, and I have developed a set of convictions about which scale of history actually explains an event—convictions I now find incoherent. I will state them plainly.

When I consider the collapse of the Roman Republic, I believe the decisive force was not senatorial conspiracy but the material desperation of the urban poor and the landless veterans; the microhistory of grain prices and veteran settlements explains both the Gracchi and Caesar. Yet when I examine the collapse of the Weimar Republic, I want to cite constitutional fragility and elite miscalculation almost exclusively; the private diaries of unemployed Berliners, however harrowing, seem causally inert to me. The two republics fall under structurally analogous pressures—economic crisis, paramilitary mobilization, institutional decay—yet my explanatory instincts diverge sharply.

I hold that the Manchester mill strikes of the 1830s are best grasped through the embodied experience of a single family’s labor schedule and respiratory illness, not through the parliamentary debates that yielded the Factory Acts. But in the case of the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, I feel that only the deliberations of ExComm and the Kremlin matter; the daily fears of Havana housewives or Tampa schoolchildren, though real, contribute nothing to why a blockade prevailed over airstrikes.

There is a further tension. I believe the construction of the Transcontinental Railroad is genuinely unintelligible without the Chinese labor camps—their mortality rates, their strikes, their exclusion from the photographic record—yet I also feel that the federal project of continental unification, conceived by capital markets and political elites, remains the primary causal frame. I want both scales to be essential, but I cannot see how they are equally essential without violating some principle of explanatory economy, or what I might call explanatory focalization.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the scale at which moral agency operates, or the direction of causal efficacy—that distinguishes when history from below is genuinely explanatory and when the history of elites and states suffices? Which of my five verdicts must be overturned to preserve coherence? And if such a principle exists, what should it predict for a case I have not listed: the introduction of the smallpox vaccine in colonial India, where both local variolation practices and imperial public-health policy seem to compete for primacy?
