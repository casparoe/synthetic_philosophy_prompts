I’ve been discussing decision theory with two friends, Jordan and Riley, after we came across descriptions of the Platonia dilemma and the related Luring Lottery—both introduced in Douglas Hofstadter’s Scientific American columns, though none of us has the original articles in front of us. The disagreement got fairly technical, and I’d like an outside adjudication rather than my own vague sense that both have points.

Here is the exchange. (1) Jordan argued that in the Platonia dilemma—where twenty people each may send a telegram to win a billion dollars, but if more than one does so everyone gets nothing—the superrational agent should send with probability exactly 1/20. The reasoning, Jordan said, is not that your telegram causes others to send theirs—cooperation is forbidden—but that if your reasoning process leads you to send with certainty, then by logical symmetry all twenty identical agents reach the same conclusion, guaranteeing failure. So you should choose the policy you’d want every identical reasoner to follow, weighted by the chance you’re the unique exception. Jordan applied the same logic to the Luring Lottery, in which readers could submit postcards bearing any number of entries; submitting one entry is cooperative, submitting many is defecting. Jordan said each contestant should submit exactly one, treating the decision as legislating for all identically reasoning agents. Jordan then added a twist: suppose the magazine allowed contestants to write a conditional entry rule, such that you submit many entries only if you can identify yourself as being in the bottom decile of rationality among participants. Jordan claimed that even then, a genuinely superrational agent should refuse the asymmetry, because there’s no principled, non-circular reason you’d be in that bottom decile rather than anyone else, so exploiting the condition violates the symmetry that makes superrationality work.

(2) Riley responded that Jordan confuses epistemic correlation with practical constraint. In Platonia, your decision has zero causal influence on the other nineteen; sending strictly dominates not sending, since if you send and they don’t you win, and if you don’t send you can never win. The symmetry observation tells you something about what others will likely do, but it doesn’t change the payoff structure of your own action. (3) Jordan came back with the Luring Lottery’s actual structure: the prize is one million dollars divided by total entries, so your choice affects not only your share but the pool itself, and universal defection collapses the prize to near zero. Jordan insisted that this interaction is mediated precisely through identical reasoning: if you reason that defection is best for you, that same reasoning applies to everyone, so you must choose as if you’re setting the universal strategy, not best-responding to an independent population. (4) Riley then attacked Jordan’s conditional-entry twist, arguing that if genuine epistemic asymmetry existed—say, you received private information placing you in that bottom decile—then conditioning on it wouldn’t violate superrationality; it would be rational belief-updating. More broadly, Riley said Jordan’s universal-policy framing ignores the actual strategic interaction in the lottery. The correct analysis, Riley noted, is standard best-response: you should submit a number of entries roughly equal to the expected total submitted by others, which maximizes your average payout given beliefs about their behavior. This treats others as causally independent agents whose actions you don’t control, which is exactly what the rules allow. Riley concluded that Jordan’s view demands you pretend you’re legislating for others when in fact you’re playing a strategic game against them, and that the actual 1980s results—where contestants submitted astronomically large expressions and destroyed the prize—show that people don’t reason superrationally precisely because they recognize they’re in a competitive interaction, not a logical mirror.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of Jordan’s and Riley’s four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and specifically address whether Jordan’s conditional-entry twist strengthens or weakens the superrational position. Please take a committed position—don’t just lay out both options and leave me to choose—giving definite reasons for your verdict.
