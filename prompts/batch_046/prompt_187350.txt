The paradox of voting asks why rational agents participate in large elections when the ex ante probability of being pivotal is negligible. For decades, the debate has split between instrumental accounts, which evaluate voting by its causal contribution to outcomes, and expressive accounts, which evaluate it by its symbolic value for preference declaration or identity affirmation. Yet the disagreement persists despite shared agreement on the arithmetic of pivotal probabilities, the empirical regularities of turnout, and the basic structure of expected-utility reasoning. My hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical or formal but conceptual: the two sides are implicitly operating with different conceptions of what rationality requires — specifically, whether it must be outcome-sensitive, or whether it can also be satisfied by actions that are "identity-rational," meaning they generate direct utility by aligning behavior with an agent's self-conception, irrespective of causal efficacy. Is this diagnosis accurate? Or does the impasse conceal a substantive disagreement about the correct probability framework (objective versus subjective pivotal probabilities), about whether expressive benefits belong inside or outside the utility calculus, or about what counts as an admissible reason for action? Please provide a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure — verbal, normative, conceptual, or hidden empirical — supported by what each side actually commits to. Then specify what kind of progress — a formal distinction, an empirical result, a reconceptualization of rationality, or a change of question — could resolve or dissolve the debate, or argue that none should be expected.
