As a novelist accustomed to manipulating point of view, unreliable narration, and the architecture of consciousness in fiction, I want to understand Daniel Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater and his alternative model of consciousness as multiple drafts. Restate both positions—the theater model he attacks and his own multiple drafts account—entirely in the vocabulary of narrative craft: the narrating voice, editorial revision, stream-of-consciousness technique, the implied reader's reconstruction, and the distinction between manuscript and published text. For each philosophical claim, indicate precisely what corresponds to what. Then provide the residue report: which elements translate without distortion, which must be twisted to fit narrative terms, and which have no counterpart in how fiction actually works. In particular, address whether Dennett's notion of a final draft of experience is better analogized to a published novel fixed by the author or to an interpretation reconstructed by the reader from disparate textual cues, and whether a genuinely central-less first-person narrative is structurally achievable or whether the form itself smuggles in an organizing consciousness. Finally, tell me whether the residue reveals a genuine limitation of the narrative framework for describing mind, or whether it exposes an ambiguity in Dennett's own account that the philosophical vocabulary was masking.
