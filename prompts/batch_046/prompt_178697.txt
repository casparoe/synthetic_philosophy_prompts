As a novelist drafting an application for a three-month interdisciplinary fellowship, I want a proposal that treats the historiography of late Rome not as settled empirical narrative but as a problem of explanatory framing. The precise question is this: when historians describe the Western Empire between roughly 200 and 600 CE as undergoing a "fall," do they implicitly commit to a tragic narrative structure—reversal, hubris, inevitable catastrophe—that distorts the causal weight we should assign to climate stress, barbarian migration, institutional adaptation, and economic network reconfiguration? Or does the "transformation rather than fall" thesis genuinely eliminate that narrative scaffolding, leaving a different causal picture? The proposal should state why the standard alternatives—barbarian invasion, climate collapse, lead poisoning, Christian pacifism, and the transformation school—leave this framing question open rather than resolving it. It should then outline an approach that works only with the established evidentiary record: comparing how each explanatory element (say, the withdrawal of legions from the Rhine frontier, or the shifting patterns of Mediterranean trade) is causally weighted under a tragic-collapse model versus under a distributed-reconfiguration model, without requiring new excavation or archival discovery. Explain why this conceptual comparison rather than fresh empirical research is the right method for a short project, and specify what a usable result would look like—perhaps a taxonomy of which causal claims depend on narrative form and which survive its removal. Include an honest assessment of failure: if removing the "fall" frame changes no causal attributions, then the framing is decorative and the project collapses. Finally, justify the three-month scope—sufficient for disciplined conceptual analysis and comparative case study, but explicitly not a comprehensive historiographical survey or an empirical data-gathering exercise—and note that citations and source lists will be added separately. Write the proposal in formal, polished prose suitable for a fellowship panel that includes both historians and literary scholars.
