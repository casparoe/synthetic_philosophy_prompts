I am a political philosopher advising a municipal charter-reform commission, and I need a reusable decision procedure that translates a principle I am calling “stratified random selection weighted by structural exposure” — a refinement of lottocratic theory — into an operational protocol for convening citizens’ assemblies. The principle holds that democratic legitimacy requires random selection within demographic strata, but with selection probabilities adjusted by the intensity and irreversibility with which a proposed policy will restructure the daily practices of different subgroups, rather than by pure demographic mirroring or self-selection.

Please design a step-by-step procedure that a non-philosopher staff officer could run under time pressure. Specify: (a) three ordered tests for measuring “structural exposure” without relying on citizens’ subjective self-assessment; (b) the weighting formula and the explicit normative judgments embedded in its coefficients; (c) the precise gate where quantitative analysis ends and normative judgment must take over, with a clear hand-off criterion; (d) how to handle incommensurable exposure across groups (e.g., temporal versus spatial restructuring); and (e) two failure modes — one where the procedure yields a technically correct but publicly illegitimate assembly, and one where it systematically excludes a group whose exposure is real but hard to quantify.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) siting a waste-transfer facility; (2) revising police patrol protocols in residential neighborhoods; and (3) a climate-adaptation zoning change that benefits long-term residents but displaces recent renters. For the third case, show explicitly where the procedure produces an assembly composition that I, as the advisor, will find normatively troubling despite its technical correctness, and state exactly what the procedure cannot resolve.

Structure your response as follows:
1. The Operationalized Principle (approximately 150 words): restate the idea in non-technical language and flag the simplifications made for usability.
2. The Procedure (approximately 350 words): numbered steps with decision gates, marking every point where judgment is unavoidable.
3. Application to Three Cases (approximately 450 words): one paragraph per case, with case three’s troubling outcome clearly flagged.
4. Limits and Misfires (approximately 150 words): where the procedure breaks down, what it cannot do, and the specific judgment call the staff officer must sign rather than compute.
