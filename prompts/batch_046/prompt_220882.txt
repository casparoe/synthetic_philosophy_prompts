i've been chewing over this aggregation thing — dust speck in every eye vs one person tortured — and my study group built this chain that feels like it proves way too much. (1) if harm is the same in kind, it's additive across people, full stop. (2) a dust speck is not literally zero harm; call its weight epsilon, where epsilon > 0 but epsilon is tiny. (3) with ~8 billion people, universal dust-specking is 8bn × epsilon. (4) for any epsilon > 0 there's a population size N where N epsilon exceeds torture-harm, just by ordinary arithmetic on real numbers. (5) so if aggregation holds, there must be a hidden threshold below which epsilon doesn't count, otherwise sneezing globally is worse than torture. (6) but a sharp threshold means 10^30 people each at epsilon/2 + delta suffer more than torture, while 10^40 people each at epsilon/2 - delta suffer literally nothing; that violates moral continuty in a pretty brutal way. (7) therefore aggregation of tiny harms must be an illusion, and 10^40 dust specks equal zero harm. (8) but then climate damage, factory farming, and walking on grass — all composed of epsilon/1000 harms spread over billions — are also morally weightless, which is suspiciously convienient. (9) so we're stuck: either global sneezing justifies torture, or we deny additivity and lose the intuitive reason the dust-speck comparison ever worked. i feel like something slips between (4) and (6), maybe treating moral weight like a physical density field that can be integrated over populations, but i can't name the error. which premise do you reject? i want to keep (1) and (6) if i can, but i'm willing to drop (5). what does giving up each option actually cost?
