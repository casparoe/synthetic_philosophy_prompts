hey, i'm a phd student in philosophy of mind working on percetion and i'm writing part of my diss on disjunctivism. i've come to accept a pretty strong version: not just that hallucination lacks the object-relational character of seeing, but that there's literally no shared mental kind between veridical seeing and a matching hallucination, and that the argument from illusion only gets going if you smuggle in a representationalist picture of content that i think is wrong. i also think this should definately extend to cognitive penetration cases — if expectation makes a neutral face look angry, that's still a direct relation to the face mediated by affect, not a shared inner image.

so what would actually count against this? i don't want the standard "but they feel the same" line; i want the deeper defeaters. for instance, if predictive processing is right and there's a common prediction-error signal that's neurally identical in veridical and illusory cases, does that undercut disjunctivism at the personal level, or can i dismiss it as subpersonal? and what about the recent relationalist turn — the idea that perceptual content is already object-dependent in a way that makes disjunctivism redundant? is that a rival theory or just a reformulation?

please rank these kinds of problems: which are fatal, which are merely awkward, and which ones a committed disjunctivist should honestly concede hurt. also, has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — like defining hallucinations as only "indistinguishable" in some weak phenomenological sense — and if so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind? i just want the arguments sorted by how decisive they'd be, including whether anything that has arguably already occured in the literature counts. no bibliographies or reading lists, please.
