I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy, and I've been asked to advise the governance assembly of a cooperative housing project that I study as part of my research on institutional pluralism. The community operates under what its founders call a "nested tolerance protocol": residents agree to accommodate any belief or practice that does not actively dismantle the shared infrastructure of collective decision-making, but the charter also includes a "recursion clause" permitting members to propose formal restrictions on viewpoints that explicitly reject the protocol itself. The situation has become acute. One resident has used our open-discussion forums to argue—repeatedly and with growing support—that residents adhering to certain religious and cultural practices should be barred from voting in collective matters, on the grounds that those practices are fundamentally incompatible with cooperative living. A faction of residents insists that we must tolerate his speech, since suppressing critical viewpoints would violate the charter's foundational commitment to openness. Another faction contends that protecting this argument is structurally self-defeating: if the view prevails, the inclusive mechanism that protects all residents will dissolve from within, leaving the cooperative with no pluralist framework to defend. I am genuinely unsure how to apply the recursion clause without either betraying our commitment to tolerance or inadvertently licensing the collapse of the very conditions that make tolerance sustainable. Could you advise me on how to navigate this? I would be grateful if you could reply in British English, and in particular I want to understand whether there is a principled way to distinguish between tolerating a person and tolerating the institutional damage that their argument, if enacted, would cause—without simply assuming the answer is obvious.
