Two friends of mine, Maya and Leo, have been arguing about Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self is a strange loop — a symbol, I, that loops back to refer to the system that produces it. Maya thinks this genuinely explains subjective selfhood: the loop creates a stable, self-referential pattern that functions as an observer embedded in the machinery. Leo says that is either trivial or wrong. He distinguishes, borrowing from computer architecture, between a meta-circular interpreter — in which a program interprets its own code at the same logical level — and a reflective tower, in which each layer observes the one beneath it. He argues Hofstadter's I is only the former: a single symbol pointing back to its source, lacking the hierarchical level-crossing that makes self-reference genuinely strange. Without that vertical separation, Leo claims, the brain is just running causal feedback, no different in principle from a thermostat monitoring its own state, and calling it an I is merely relabeling homeostasis.

Maya replies that in neural tissue there is no separate hardware running the loop; the symbol and its substrate are the same physical dynamics, so the software-hardware distinction Leo needs collapses. She says the thermostat fails as an analogy because its feedback concerns temperature, not its own existence as an entity. For Maya, the loop's strangeness is epistemic rather than ontological: the system represents itself as a unified agent, and that representation causally feeds back into the dynamics. Leo counters that if the levels are truly collapsed, then there is no level-crossing at all — just flat physical causation — and Hofstadter either smuggles in hidden hierarchies he denies or uses strange loop as ornate language for ordinary feedback. They asked me to adjudicate and I honestly could not.

Please evaluate point by point: is Leo's distinction between meta-circular interpretation and reflective towers appropriate here, or does Maya correctly show that it does not apply to biological brains? Is the thermostat analogy apt or misleading? Most importantly, does Hofstadter's account require a genuine ontological separation of levels to be non-trivial, or can the strangeness be fully epistemic? And what, if anything, are both missing about whether a self-referential symbol can explain the first-person quality of experience rather than merely describing an organizational pattern?
