I'm preparing a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a reconstructed Lockean negative answer to Molyneux's problem. Rather than resting on the familiar empiricist claim that sight and touch supply different simple ideas, she argues for what she terms a structural binding deficit: recognizing a cube by sight after knowing it only by touch requires constructing an amodal structural descriptor that neither modality delivers on its own, so the initial failure is cross-modal and conceptual rather than merely a lack of visual experience. She is particularly skilled at deflecting objections by insisting that modern clinical reports of rapid post-operative shape recognition are confounded by sub-personal predictive updating, and she claims that contemporary predictive-processing frameworks actually reinforce her view by showing that even apparently immediate visual recognition is already concept-laden inference. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond having her restate this position.

Specifically, I need questions that press her on four fronts. First, how she distinguishes her structural-binding deficit from a simpler empiricist association-gap, since both seem to predict initial failure but differ in what kind of remedy they allow. Second, what she makes of documented cases in which recently sight-restored patients do identify basic shapes within minutes, and whether her appeal to hidden rapid learning renders the thesis unfalsifiable. Third, how her reliance on amodal descriptors avoids collapsing into rationalism—if the descriptor is not extracted from either sensory stream, what prevents it from being an innate geometric concept? Fourth, what her view implies for the unity of perceptual consciousness: if every cross-modal identification requires its own binding descriptor, does that fragment consciousness into modality-specific silos? For each of these, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—especially her tendency to invoke predictive updating as a universal shield—and close with one question about what kind of experimental or conceptual evidence could genuinely count against the structural-binding thesis rather than being absorbed as yet another instance of concealed inference. The tone should be rigorous but conversational, suited to a philosophy podcast audience that knows the classic problem but not her particular reconstruction.
