I am currently employed in the public-records division of a mid-sized municipality, and three months ago I was tasked with auditing the digitization of planning-board minutes from the 1980s. In the course of that work I discovered that objections raised by local agricultural stakeholders to a watershed-redevelopment proposal had been silently dropped during the migration from analog to digital storage, leaving only the approving narrative intact. This experience has made me personally invested in Hannah Arendt’s reflections on lying in politics—not as an abstract academic concern, but as a practical problem about whether the danger of political falsehood lies chiefly in the destruction of a shared factual world or in the corruption of the narrative and imaginative capacities through which citizens make sense of collective life. I have been debating this with a senior archivist who takes the former view, and our exchange has reached an impasse that now feels genuinely philosophical rather than merely procedural.

I would like you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between our two positions might produce. The first camp—call them the Archival-Factualists—holds that Arendt’s critique of political lying is fundamentally about the erosion of factual reality and the shared evidentiary ground required for democratic action, and therefore that the integrity of bureaucratic records is not incidental but central to her concern. The second camp—call them the Narrative-Imaginationists—holds that Arendt’s deeper worry concerns the destruction of storytelling and the faculty of judgment, meaning that even technically accurate archives can participate in a kind of lying when they suppress the narrative of dissent and thereby prevent citizens from exercising the imaginative reconstruction of events that political understanding requires. Your document should begin with a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign regarding Arendt’s broader framework, formulated without reference to specific editions or page numbers and without invoking obscure secondary literature. This should be followed by a disagreements table in which each camp’s position is stated in wording its own members would accept, separating verbal disputes from substantive ones.

Please identify the precise cruxes—particularly whether preserving dissent in institutional memory serves primarily an epistemic function or a narrative-political one—and for each crux specify a concrete institutional design or historical case that both sides agree would test the matter, recording in advance what each camp would predict about its outcome. The statement should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, and citations, and should be written in a formal, polished register appropriate for a document that both parties might actually present to a municipal ethics review board concerned with record-keeping integrity.
