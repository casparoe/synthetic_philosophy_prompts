I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, currently preparing discussion materials for a seminar on methodological controversies in consciousness studies. In our reading group we recently worked through Dennett’s heterophenomenology, particularly his claim that first-person reports of conscious experience should be treated not as direct descriptions of inner phenomenal reality but as narrative texts that construct what he terms the heterophenomenological world — a fictional object that the researcher interprets with the same neutrality one brings to a literary work, rather than as a literal account of subjective states.

To help my colleagues see how conceptually strange this stance becomes when fully institutionalized, I would like you to write a satirical parody in the form of an institutional Frequently Asked Questions document, approximately 700 words, formatted as numbered questions with deadpan bureaucratic answers, purportedly released by a university-affiliated Center for Heterophenomenological Studies announcing its new clinical intake protocol. The satire must exaggerate the method’s actual commitments — the suspension of first-person authority, the treatment of patient reports as texts for hermeneutic analysis rather than windows on experience, and the clinician’s principled refusal to validate anything about subjective phenomenology — but the humor should arise from getting the epistemology right, not from misrepresenting it. To push the exercise further, please invent and embed within the document one procedural innovation that you should call the Double-Bracket Protocol: define it as a two-stage interpretive procedure in which the clinician first brackets the subject’s claim to possess an experience, then brackets her own framing of that bracket, leaving only a pure textual artifact evaluated solely for internal narrative coherence. The piece should be readable to someone unfamiliar with Dennett, who should understand that patient reports are being systematically stripped of experiential authority, and genuinely unsettling or amusing to a specialist who recognizes that the exaggeration tracks Dennett’s real methodological commitments. Maintain a consistently deadpan, administratively cheerful tone; include a brief institutional header but no footnotes, citations, references to scholars beyond Dennett, or requests for further reading.
