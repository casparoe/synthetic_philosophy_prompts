As a novelist drafting the climactic arc of a long-form narrative, I have reached a structural crisis in which the protagonist must choose between saving one fully realized character—whose consciousness the reader has inhabited for hundreds of pages—and allowing dozens of peripheral figures to perish, figures who exist in the text primarily as statistical background rather than as subjects of interior narration. I was discussing this problem with a friend who studies moral philosophy, and she mentioned John Taurek's argument that in rescue cases the numbers should not count, a view that struck me as potentially devastating to the conventional moral architecture of fiction, where audience sympathy is usually engineered to scale with the magnitude of suffering depicted.

Before I commit to how this scene resolves, I need you to describe the most important implications of taking Taurek's anti-aggregationist position seriously—not merely as an ethical curiosity, but as a formal constraint on storytelling: how does it alter the ethical status of background characters relative to protagonists; what does it imply about the legitimacy of using scale or magnitude as a dramatic and moral lever; and can a novelist coherently present the choice to save the one as anything other than a failure of reason? I am especially interested in whether the argument survives translation into narrative causality, where the author rather than nature decides whose death counts as a harm to be avoided, and I want you to identify precisely where the anti-aggregationist view is weakest when applied to fictional worlds deliberately designed to manipulate the reader's sense of who matters. Please treat this as a conceptual analysis rather than a request for literary examples, and flag the steps at which judgment about narrative value cannot be separated from judgment about moral aggregation.
