I'm working on a feature about whether history is actually locked in, and I keep running into this wall in the literature and in conversations with historians and philosphers. There's this long-running disagreement about path dependence—like, would we have ended up with roughly the same industrial outcomes if some small event in 1760 had gone differently? One side points to things like QWERTY keyboard layout or railway guage standards and says these are clear cases where an early acident got frozen in and shaped everything after. The other side looks at the same cases and says actually, the underlying functional pressures were always going to push toward something like this, so the 'path' is mostly an illusion of hindsight—what you call path dependence is just convergence with noise.

But here's what's wierd: both sides have been making these moves for decades, and nobody seems to have changed anyone's mind. I was talking with a friend who's in a philosophy of science course and she suggested maybe it's not a real disagreement at all—maybe they're just talking past each other because one group is asking 'could this specific institution have been different?' and the other is asking 'would human welfare have been different?' And I wonder if there's something even deeper: maybe the stall happens because there's no agreed standard for what counts as the 'same outcome' at different scales. Like, if you change the guage but keep the network topology, is that path dependence or not? Or if you get democracy but with different voting rules?

So I'm trying to figure out: is this actually a substantive disagreement about history, or is it a verbal dispute about the word 'locked in'? Are they fighting over hidden empirical claims—like whether we can actually run counterfactuals on complex social systems—or is it a disagreement about what kinds of explanations count as good in social science? Or are they literally answering different questions under one label? And if it's the latter, what would progress even look like—some formal model that separates the scales, or just agreeing to split the question into pieces? I'd love a diagnosis that actually engages with what both camps say, not just a verdict about who's right.
