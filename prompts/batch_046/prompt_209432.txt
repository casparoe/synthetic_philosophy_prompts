As a journalist covering debates in epistemology and decision science, I have been tracking a tension that keeps surfacing in discussions of forecasting and expert judgment. The problem concerns what some call the inside view—the granular, causal reasoning an expert constructs for a particular case—and the outside view, which treats the expert as a data point in a reference class. The standard prescription, often labeled epistemic modesty, is that when the two conflict, the outside view should usually win, because treating one's own reasoning as exceptional demands justification one rarely has.

But I recently sketched an argument that seems to show this prescription collapses into self-defeat, and I am trying to locate the error. Here is the chain.

(1) Epistemic modesty requires that, whenever my first-order reasoning conflicts with the statistical record of people with my observable credentials making analogous judgments, I should lower my credence in my reasoning and defer to the base rate.

(2) This requirement must apply uniformly: if I exempt the reasoning by which I defend modesty itself, I am arbitrarily privileging my meta-cognitive position, which is precisely the kind of special pleading modesty forbids.

(3) Consider a concrete case. I have developed an inside-view model—call it Model A—that accounts for several anomalies in a well-studied dataset. The outside view indicates that roughly ninety percent of researchers with my publication record who propose anomaly-resolving models of this structural type are eventually shown to be overfitting.

(4) By (1), I should therefore assign low credence to Model A.

(5) Now apply the same structure to my belief in modesty. I initially adopted (1) and (2) through first-person philosophical reasoning, not through statistical observation of my own reliability. The outside view on philosophers who defend deference principles shows that a large majority later abandon or substantially revise those principles.

(6) By (2), I cannot exempt this belief from the requirement. So I should apply (1) to my defense of modesty itself, lowering my credence in (1) and (2).

(7) But if my credence in modesty drops, then the obligation to defer in (4) is weakened or suspended, which means my credence in Model A should rebound.

(8) Once my credence in Model A rebounds, the conditions for applying modesty are restored—my reasoning is again in conflict with an unfavorable base rate—so modesty applies again, pushing my credence in Model A back down and my credence in modesty back up.

(9) The result is not a stable equilibrium but an oscillation: every restoration of modesty undermines Model A, and every collapse of modesty restores Model A.

(10) Rational belief should not be trapped in a perpetual loop driven by its own governing principle; therefore either modesty is false, or it carries an implicit restriction that prevents recursive application.

(11) But adding such a restriction violates (2), which forbids arbitrary exemptions.

If forced to choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (2) and reject whatever creates the loop, but I want to understand what rejecting it actually costs. Which step is the weakest link, why did it look plausible, and what do the standard positions—those who defend unconditional deference, those who restrict it to first-order domains, and those who reject base-rate deference altogether—each have to pay to reject it?
