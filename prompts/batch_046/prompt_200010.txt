I hold a position reconstructed from George Berkeley's writings on currency, banking, and national wealth, interpreted through his broader immaterialist metaphysics. On this view, money is never wealth in itself but only a relational counter or ticket for labor, industry, and service; gold and silver possess no intrinsic economic value but only a conventional value sustained by collective practice; and a properly regulated paper or credit instrument tied to real productive activity is preferable to metallic hoarding, which drains circulation. The interpretive extension is that monetary value, like the sensible qualities Berkeley analyzes, has no existence independent of the perceivers and social relations that constitute it—wealth is not a property residing in metal or paper but a status conferred within an economic community.

What would genuinely count against this composite position? I want arguments and observations that go beyond standard economic objections. Would empirical cases in which commodity money retains value during the collapse of social institutions—crisis economies, prison camps, or hyperinflationary collapses—defeat the relational claim, or could a defender absorb them by appealing to residual social structures? Are there internal tensions in Berkeley's system—perhaps between his denial of material substance and the need for durable, stable value-storage—that undermine applying immaterialism to money? Could modern state-chartalist or tax-driven theories of fiat currency either vindicate the view or reveal a hidden commitment to state authority that Berkeley, with his suspicion of centralized power, could not accept? I also want to know whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every failure of paper currency is attributed to misregulation rather than to the nature of value itself, is that insulation a vice for a theory of this kind?

Please distinguish the defeaters that a committed defender of this Berkeleyan relationalist account of money would themselves concede from those they would dismiss. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Identify the single observation or conceptual result that, if firmly established, would most clearly settle whether the immaterialist interpretation of monetary value is coherent or collapses into conventional nominalism. And say honestly whether you think the view is currently well-insulated, and whether that insulation is appropriate for a philosophical account of economic value.
