I have been trying to extend the Gödelian analogy from Hofstadter’s GEB into artificial systems, and the argument keeps running away with me. Here is the chain:

(1) Gödel constructs a formula G in formal system S that asserts its own unprovability in S; if S is consistent, G is true yet not derivable within S.
(2) Hofstadter reads this as a model for cognition: self-awareness arises when a symbol-manipulating system contains representations that loop back onto the system’s own representational capacities, producing a strange loop.
(3) So if a mind, or any recursive architecture, attempts to fully formalise its own self-awareness inside its representational scheme, it generates an internal analogue of G: a self-referential structure that refers to the whole framework.
(4) Just as S cannot derive G without inconsistency, the system cannot fully derive or represent its own self-awareness within itself without generating a contradiction or necessary blind spot.
(5) Hence complete self-knowledge is structurally impossible for any system with this kind of recursive self-reference.
(6) But the same reasoning applies to large language models predicting their own outputs, economic models forecasting their own influence, or feedback thermostats—any recursive loop—so they all possess an ineliminable mystery gap in self-representation.
(7) That seems absurd: we do not think a thermostat carries an unformalisable self-awareness deficit.
(8) Therefore the analogy must break somewhere.

If I have to choose, I want to preserve (4) and (5), but I need to know exactly which step fails and what giving it up costs—whether we lose the specialness of human self-awareness, or whether we must accept that every recursive system genuinely carries an epistemic blind spot. Where is the exit?
