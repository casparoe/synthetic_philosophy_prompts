In debates about gradual mind uploading, proponents often invoke the Ship of Theseus: if every biological neuron is replaced, one at a time, by a functionally equivalent synthetic unit, the person remains the same, just as the ship survives total plank replacement. The analogy is meant to license the claim that diachronic personal identity is substrate-independent so long as functional and causal continuity are preserved. I ask for a rigorous audit. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the ship, the planks, the shipyard, the captain’s testimony, and the criterion of sameness—then identify the precise points of fracture. In particular, address whether a synthetic neuron counts as the same kind of part as its biological predecessor, whether the intermediate hybrid state (part-biological, part-silicon cognition) possesses determinate identity conditions at all, and whether the analogy smuggles in an unargued structuralist theory of the self that neglects phenomenal or biological continuity. Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that gradual replacement preserves personal identity—survives these disanalogies intact, or whether it was carrying the argument all along. If the ship comparison fails, suggest a more apt analogy—perhaps drawn from linguistic translation or the successive replacement of musicians in an orchestra—and explain whether that replacement fares any better.
