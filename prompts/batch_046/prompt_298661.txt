I have just come out of a three-hour argument with a friend about whether James’s defense of the will to believe can be reconstructed without collapsing into the evidentialism Clifford condemned, but applied specifically to what I am calling delegated epistemic commitments—the practical, corrigible stances we adopt toward institutional testimony in law, science, or administration when we must coordinate action despite lacking direct access to the underlying reasoning. My friend insisted that either these commitments involve implicit propositional belief in institutional reliability, in which case Clifford’s prohibition applies, or they involve no belief-like assent at all, in which case they are strategically cynical rather than genuinely epistemic. I am presenting a reconstructed hybrid position at next Thursday’s social epistemology working group: Clifford’s prohibition applies strictly to direct propositional assent formed without sufficient evidence, whereas Jamesian willing is legitimate only for these delegated commitments, provided they are structured by explicit corrigibility conditions and do not function as closed beliefs about particular states of affairs. The audience will be six philosophers working on testimony and institutional knowledge, one democratic theorist concerned with epistemic justice, and a specialist in nineteenth-century American philosophy who knows the James-Clifford exchange in granular detail; they care about whether this distinction survives pressure from testimonial injustice and whether it anachronistically imposes analytic categories on a debate originally about religious psychology.
