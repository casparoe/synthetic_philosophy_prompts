I've been stuck on this for months in our ethics-of-art reading group. There's this old argument about what we owe the real dead when we turn them into characters — not just factual accuracy, but whether dramatising them at all is a kind of theft, or whether it's just storytelling with no victim because they're dead. One side says harm needs a subject, and dead people aren't subjects, so all we owe is maybe not lying malicously. The other side says reputation is a kind of legacy that survives the body, and distorting it wrongs the dead even if they don't feel it. We've been round this for ages and nobody shifts an inch.

My hunch is that it's not really about harm at all, but about whether we treat a life as an open narrative or as a closed archieve — but I'm not sure if that's just me dressing up the same deadlock.

Can you diagnose what's actually going on? Is it a verbal mess about 'harm' and 'subject', are we answering different questions (one about moral obligation, one about aesthetic licence), or is there a hidden factual disagreement about whether posthumous interests exist? And if it's the kind of disagreement that can't be settled, what would actually move it — a distinction, a thought experiment, or just accepting that the two sides start from incompatible pictures of what a person is?

Also, if I'm talking nonsense here — like if the whole framing assumes something confused about narrative identity — just say so plainly. I'd rather be corrected than humoured.
