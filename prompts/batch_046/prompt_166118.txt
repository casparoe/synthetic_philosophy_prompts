Write a one-page proposal for a ten-week solo research project (deliverable: a 6,000-word workshop paper in normative social theory) on whether a philosophically coherent concept of “achievement-status desert” can adjudicate between the American Dream’s commitment to visibly elevating exceptional achievers and the Jantelagen / tall-poppy norm’s commitment to suppressing social differentiation rooted in success. Frame the precise question as follows: do these two traditions represent incommensurable cultural preferences—individualist meritocracy versus egalitarian solidarity—or do they share an implicit normative logic about how achievement-related visibility functions as a distributive good, disagreeing only on whether status elevation is non-positional and earned or inherently zero-sum and solidarity-eroding? Argue that existing answers in cultural psychology and sociology leave the question open because they describe divergence without analyzing the underlying ontology of social recognition; they treat status as either a private reward or a cultural taste rather than as a form of recognition subject to principles of justice. Propose an approach of conceptual reconstruction rather than empirical survey: construct a normative model—call it “distributive humility”—that treats achievement-based status not as a prize but as socially conferred visibility whose legitimacy depends on whether it can be universalized without generating comparative deprivation. Defend this approach over the obvious alternatives: cross-cultural attitude studies only map disagreement without resolving it, and standard liberal theories of desert wrongly assume that earned status is a private good with no positional externalities. State exactly what a successful result would look like: either (a) a reconstructed principle showing that the American Dream and Jantelagen are consistent at different scales of social organization, with explicit conditions that determine which scale applies, or (b) a demonstration that they rest on mutually exclusive premises about whether social recognition is rivalrous, with specific considerations that decide between them. Define failure clearly: the analysis collapses into mere cultural description, or the model cannot distinguish legitimate achievement-recognition from arbitrary privilege. Assess realistic scope: ten weeks allows for the conceptual framework, one detailed case study (e.g., professional visibility in meritocratic institutions versus Scandinavian workplace norms), and the normative argument, but not for original empirical data collection or a full historical genealogy; note this limitation honestly. Finally, take a committed position: choose which of conclusions (a) or (b) the project should pursue, defend that choice with reasons grounded in the ontology of recognition, and do not present the two outcomes as equally viable options left for the reader to select.
