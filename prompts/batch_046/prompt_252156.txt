I will defend at a normative-ethics seminar the claim that consequentialist cluelessness is not symmetric across time horizons: near-term actions face parametric uncertainty (quantifiable outcome variance), whereas far-future actions face structural uncertainty (possible shifts in the evaluative framework itself across civilizational scales). From this I argue that standard robustness reasoning addresses only the first kind, so long-horizon ethics should shift from expected-value maximization to option preservation. The audience is mostly Kantian and contractualist ethicists familiar with Lenman and Greaves but skeptical of longtermism because it seems to neglect present duties; plus two decision theorists who specialize in imprecise probabilities and believe robustness rules already solve cluelessness. There are no longtermist advocates in the room. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six objections I will actually receive, ordered by likely damage, each phrased in the voice of the objector (specify whether it comes from the ethicists or the decision theorists), with what I should concede up front, my two-minute reply, and the specific weak point in that reply I must defend rather than gloss over. End with the question I hope is not asked — the one pressing whether the climate-science analogy between parametric and structural uncertainty actually maps onto moral value drift — and sketch a reply that relies only on conceptual reasoning, not on citing texts I have not quoted. Write in American English.
