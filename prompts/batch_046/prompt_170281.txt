I have been thinking about counterpart theory and transworld identity ever since my partner's early-onset dementia began altering her personality so profoundly that I catch myself asking whether the woman I married still exists in this world, or whether I am now caring for a counterpart who shares her body but not the essential properties that made her who she was. I want a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge in the final couplet—written in a voice that does not resolve the metaphysical question but lets the form do the work. Let Refrain A be a line asserting transworld identity: she is numerically the same person across worlds, however changed her properties. Let Refrain B be a line asserting counterpart theory: in this world she is only a counterpart, similar enough to be loved but not identical to the one in other worlds. The rhyme scheme should follow the villanelle's A-b-A pattern, and the rotation of the refrains should mirror how the two theories keep passing each other without synthesis—until the last two lines, where they stand side by side. The poem should render the distinction accurately enough that a specialist recognizes the Lewisian claim that individuals are world-bound and related across worlds only by intrinsic similarity, and the essentialist claim that one object can exist in multiple worlds with different accidental features, rather than merely naming them; someone unfamiliar with the debate should still find a poem about love, loss, and whether the beloved survives her own transformation.
