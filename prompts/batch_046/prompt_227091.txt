I spent three decades running precision measurements in condensed-matter physics, and for the past several years I have been working through philosophy of mind as a serious amateur rather than a dabbler. I am struck by the asymmetry between how my old field treats observation and how philosophy treats introspection: in the laboratory we calibrate instruments, model systematic drift, and quantify resolution limits, whereas in philosophy of mind introspection is either granted near-infallible authority over phenomenal states or dismissed wholesale on the strength of confabulation studies. I want to understand where limited intellectual effort should actually go.

I see four live lines of work. First, whether introspection has domain-specific reliability—perhaps accurate for affective valence but systematically poor for the causal origins of belief. Second, whether there is a genuine analog of instrument calibration for first-person methods, so that one could correct for what I will call introspective drift: the possibility that observing a mental state alters its dynamics in a predictable, correctable way rather than destroying it. Third, whether the failure modes revealed in split-brain reports and choice-blindness experiments generalize to ordinary cognition or are artifacts of extreme conditions. Fourth, whether developing formal models of introspective resolution—specifying how fine-grained a mental state must be before it exceeds first-person discriminative capacity—would resolve disputes about phenomenal concepts or merely relocate them into a different vocabulary.

Of these, which two or three deserve the greatest attention from philosophers and cognitive scientists over the coming decade, and for whose benefit: conceptual clarity in philosophy of mind, methodological reform in consciousness science, or practical improvement in clinical self-report? Please defend the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability given current empirical methods, how many other debates depend on the answer, and whether neglect produces active harm—rather than appealing to personal taste. I also want you to identify which of my four lines is overrated or rests on a false analogy with physical measurement, and whether there is a fifth question I have missed that should displace one of them.

Please treat well-known studies—such as the classic work on verbal reports and causal reasoning, or the choice-blindness paradigm—as common knowledge, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or page references. I am asking for a reasoned agenda, not a survey. Finally, say what your proposed priorities predict about a case I have not listed: if a trained meditator reports a sustained, granular awareness of subtle emotional precursors that untrained subjects deny experiencing, should we treat this as improved introspective resolution, as a reconstructed narrative, or as something else altogether?
