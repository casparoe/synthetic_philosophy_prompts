I am putting together an application for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship that sits between the history of science and philosophy, and I would like you to draft the proposal document itself. The project grows out of a recent seminar discussion in which several of us noticed that Fernand Braudel’s tripartite scheme—geographical time, social time, and the time of events—is almost never applied to conceptual history; it is reserved for economic cycles, climate patterns, and political structures. I want to ask whether this framework can illuminate the history of causal ontology without collapsing into either Whiggish progressivism or a reductive environmental determinism. Specifically, I propose to investigate whether the slow-moving structures Braudel identifies—long climate cycles, agricultural rhythms, and trans-regional trade networks—functioned not merely as background conditions but as conditions of intelligibility for particular causal ontologies, so that the conceptual shift from Aristotelian to mechanistic causation in seventeenth-century Northern Europe might be read less as a sequence of individual breakthroughs and more as an epiphenomenon of deeper material rhythms. The existing literature either treats Braudel narrowly within economic and political history, leaving intellectual change to internalist narratives, or applies sociology of knowledge without the temporal architecture, producing flat contextualism. I need the proposal to state the precise research question—whether the longue durée framework can distinguish structural conditions of conceptual possibility from mere causal background—explain why current answers genuinely leave this open, outline a method that combines rigorous conceptual analysis with targeted climate and economic archive work without requiring full professional archival training, and specify what success and failure would look like.

Please also include an honest assessment of what makes this too ambitious for the timeframe, particularly the risk of overreaching from philosophy into historical climatology, and state how the project avoids that risk. Write in a formal register suitable for a fellowship committee that is philosophically literate but not specialised in either Braudelian historiography or the philosophy of causation.
