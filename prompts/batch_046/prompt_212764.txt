I've been working through some issues in rational choice theory for a graduate seminar, and I keep returning to what I'm calling plural Buridanism: not the classic case of an ass paralyzed between two identical bales of hay, but the far more ordinary situation in which an agent faces multiple alternatives that are genuinely incomparable across distinct dimensions of value—say, a vocation that maximizes intellectual autonomy versus one that maximizes communal belonging, with no single metric that reduces both to one scale. If Buridan's original paradox is typically read as proving that rational agency requires a complete and transitive preference ordering—since without one, choice is allegedly impossible—then plural Buridanism seems to entail that any rational agent confronting incommensurable goods is necessarily stuck in an analogous paralysis, because there is no fact of the matter about which option is best.

This leaves me with a tension I can't resolve. Behavioral economists and some philosophers of practical reason maintain that rational agents routinely operate with incomplete preferences, particularly across incommensurable domains, without thereby being irrational. Yet the standard decision-theoretic remedy for Buridan's case—randomizing with equal probability when preferences are tied—feels misapplied here: flipping a coin between intellectual autonomy and communal belonging doesn't resolve the incommensurability, it merely substitutes arbitrariness for paralysis. So my question is whether plural Buridanism actually reveals that rationality must permit incomplete orderings over genuinely incomparable values, or whether it instead demonstrates that any choice among incommensurable goods is necessarily non-rational, with randomization being only a pragmatic escape hatch rather than a genuinely rational resolution. And if the latter is correct, are we forced to conclude that most significant human decisions are systematically irrational?
