I'm looking for the established term, if there is one, for a particular reflexive tension I keep encountering in discussions of Wittgenstein's family-resemblance analysis. A speaker will invoke family resemblance to block essentialist definitions of some concept—arguing, for instance, that "language" or "rule-following" has no single feature shared by every instance, only a network of overlapping similarities. Yet in deploying this move, the speaker treats "family resemblance" itself as a sharply bounded theoretical mechanism with definite conditions of application: they reject a putative counterexample by saying "that isn't family resemblance because the overlaps are too sparse," or they claim that Wittgenstein's account applies universally to all first-order concepts except the meta-concept of family resemblance itself. Meanwhile, if someone suggests applying the family-resemblance idea to "family resemblance"—proposing that Wittgenstein's own notion is itself a loose cluster of related uses without a common core—the speaker usually resists, insisting that there is a correct, determinate reading that must be preserved intact. So the pattern is using a concept designed to undermine essentialist boundaries as if it possessed essentialist boundaries, and shielding that very concept from being analyzed by itself. Is there a recognized name for this specific reflexive failure mode—something like a self-exempting application of anti-essentialism? If not, can you coin and define a term that captures exactly this asymmetry, and distinguish it carefully from plain inconsistency, from a motte-and-bailey retreat to a weaker claim, and from the ordinary philosophical difficulty of applying a theory to itself? What are its boundary cases: does it require explicit denial of reflexive application, or is implicit protection enough, and how should we classify cases where the speaker applies family resemblance to some but not all of their own theoretical tools?
