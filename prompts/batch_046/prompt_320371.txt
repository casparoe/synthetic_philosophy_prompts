If René Descartes had encountered contemporary neurobiological findings about octopuses—specifically, that these animals possess a distributed nervous system in which a substantial majority of neurons reside in the arms rather than in a central brain, permitting each arm to learn, remember, and initiate complex actions with minimal central coordination—how might he have adjusted his mechanistic view of animal consciousness? Would he have been forced to concede that such an organism hosts a unified conscious subject, or would he have interpreted the architecture as a cluster of semi-independent automata lacking a single immaterial soul? In answering, draw on his actual metaphysical commitments regarding substance dualism, the pineal gland as the designated point of soul-body interaction, and the criteria he used to distinguish reflexive machinery from beings capable of thought.
