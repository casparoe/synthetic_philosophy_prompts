I'm defending a thesis at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-action seminar next week: that hyperbolic discounting explains only one species of weakness of will—present-biased preference reversal—while leaving untouched a distinct phenomenon I call diachronic authority fragmentation, in which an agent's all-things-considered practical judgment remains stable across time but is overridden in the moment by a non-rational motivational source that does not involve any change in evaluated preferences. I argue that conflating the two under a single intertemporal-choice model erases the normative difference between merely changing one's mind and genuinely acting against one's better judgment, and I sketch a two-system framework that assigns hyperbolic dynamics to the preference-revision system while treating authority fragmentation as a failure of executive integration rather than of temporal consistency.

The audience is a mixed room: two philosophers of action who work on practical reason and are deeply skeptical that behavioral economics can capture the phenomenology of clear-eyed akrasia; one behavioral economist who treats hyperbolic discounting as a unified explanation for all observed weakness of will and who tends to reply that philosophical distinctions are post-hoc rationalizations of the same underlying curve; and several moral psychologists who care about empirical tractability and will ask whether my proposed separation can be operationalized in choice experiments. They share a commitment to preserving normative distinctions but differ on whether those distinctions need distinct mechanistic explanations.

Prepare a speaker-prep sheet for the Q&A. State my thesis precisely as above, then list the objections I will actually face—ordered by how damaging they are and from whom—phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would use. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—likely the one that forces me to specify the empirical marker distinguishing authority fragmentation from extreme present-bias in real-time decision data—and draft both a defensive reply and an honest admission of what I do not yet have. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; do not invoke named theories or results I could only guess at; and frame everything so that a respondent without web access can engage it using only the conceptual resources in the prompt. Keep the tone formal and the structure that of a prep sheet, not a general defense of the view.
