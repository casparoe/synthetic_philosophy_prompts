I am drafting a short essay for a graduate seminar on historical method in religious studies, and I would like your critical comments on its central argument before I finalize it. The essay examines the standard criteria of authenticity used in historical-Jesus research—multiple attestation, dissimilarity, coherence, and embarrassment—and asks whether they can ever yield a stable portrait of the figure behind the texts, or whether they necessarily presuppose the very conclusion they claim to establish. To frame the problem, I introduce a distinction that I have not seen articulated precisely in this form: I call the two competing meta-approaches reductive authentication and synthetic authentication. Reductive authentication treats dissimilarity and embarrassment as primary evidentiary filters, stripping away material that coheres too neatly with early Christian theology in order to isolate a minimal, resistant core. Synthetic authentication, by contrast, treats coherence and broad attestation as primary, arguing that discontinuity with the later church is neither necessary nor sufficient for historicity, since a genuinely historical figure might well have generated traditions that were later developed rather than invented. My thesis is that every application of the standard criteria already smuggles in one of these two meta-frames, so that debates about particular sayings—whether the logion about rendering to Caesar or the prediction of the temple's destruction—are not really resolved by the criteria themselves but by the prior commitment to either stripping or integrating the tradition.

I am especially concerned with whether this distinction genuinely clarifies the methodological stalemate or merely renames it. I also want to examine a specific tension I have noticed: if embarrassment is taken as a mark of authenticity because the early community would not have invented a detail that made Jesus or his followers appear weak, then we must already possess a well-defined psychological model of the community's rhetorical incentives. But if we possess such a model, then coherence with that model can be used both to defend and to dismiss any given pericope, which seems to collapse the independence of the criteria. Could you comment on whether my argument holds together, whether the distinction between reductive and synthetic authentication is philosophically useful, and whether there is an obvious counter-example—perhaps a saying that clearly satisfies both meta-frames or clearly violates both—that would undermine my claim that the criteria are always mediated by a prior hermeneutic choice? I am not looking for bibliographic suggestions, but rather for conceptual objections or refinements that might strengthen or complicate the essay's structure.
