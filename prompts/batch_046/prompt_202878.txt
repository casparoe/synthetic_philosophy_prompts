I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop next month titled "Rigidity and Agency," convening behavioral ecologists, philosophers of action, and clinicians who work with patients exhibiting highly stereotyped behavioral patterns. The theme has taken on an unexpected personal weight: six months ago my younger brother was diagnosed with a condition characterized by compulsive ritualization, and I have found myself unable to separate the clinical literature on behavioral inflexibility from the philosophical literature on mechanical action—particularly the classic discussion of sphexishness in the digger wasp, where a rigid, loop-like behavioral script seems to operate without correction by circumstance. I do not want a generic welcome. I want a speech, roughly fifteen minutes, that uses the tension between mechanical rigidity and flexible response as its structural spine: it should explain to the scientists why philosophers care about whether sphexishness is an absence of freedom or merely an absence of a particular kind of flexibility, and it should explain to the philosophers why the clinical and biological data on ritualized behavior make the standard free-will framing feel inadequate. Crucially, the speech must avoid two temptations: it must not romanticize rigidity as hidden wisdom, and it must not treat human ritual as merely sphexish writ large. It should land for experts without losing a general academic audience, and it should acknowledge—without being confessional—that my own investment in the question is no longer purely theoretical.
