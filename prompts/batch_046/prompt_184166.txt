I have been thinking on my own about the phrase people use when describing constitutional monarchy: that "the monarch reigns, but does not rule." I came across it recently in a comparison of the British Crown with Japan's postwar system, where the writer mentioned that Japan's constitution establishes the emperor solely as the symbol of the state and of the unity of the people, and then applied the same "reigns but does not rule" formula to both cases. I cannot tell whether this is a precise description or just a comforting generalization. Could you set out the different specific claims the slogan might be making—separating, for example, ceremonial presence from legal authority, symbolic representation from executive command, possession of reserve powers from their actual exercise, and absence of democratic mandate from absence of all influence—and for each say whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or genuinely illuminating? Please indicate which readings actually apply to the United Kingdom, which to Scandinavian monarchies, and which to Japan, and whether any single reading justifies treating all three under one phrase. I am particularly unsure whether the slogan is meant to deny that a monarch can ever shape policy, or only that they lack the legitimate authority to do so against an elected government, so I would like that ambiguity addressed directly.
