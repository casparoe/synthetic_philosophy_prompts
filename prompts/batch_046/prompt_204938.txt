I am peer-grading responses for our philosophy elective's unit on early modern political thought. The exam question asked students to explain Pascal's argument in the Pensées that justice must be united with force—since justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical—and to discuss his appeal to the reason of effects to explain obedience to unjust customs. The prompt also invited them to consider an interpretation that compares this reasoning to a coordination problem in which societies settle on a visible focal point, such as custom or sovereign command, to avoid perpetual disagreement, even though that point is not morally privileged. Students were then asked whether Pascal treats legal authority as deriving solely from this stable equilibrium of acceptance and enforcement, or whether he preserves an independent, transcendent standard of justice hidden from human politics, and whether his view amounts to moral conventionalism.

One student wrote:

Pascal argues that justice and force must be united: justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is tyrannical. Since people can never agree on an objective definition of justice—reason is too weak—the reason of effects explains why they obey established customs. People calculate that overturning existing authority for the sake of an imagined true justice will only produce war, so they treat the current order as a focal point in a coordination game: any stable rule is better than none. This means Pascal thinks legal authority derives purely from its ability to maintain equilibrium; the specific content of the law is arbitrary, like picking a meeting point on a map.

At the same time, Pascal is not a pure conventionalist. He believes in a hidden, divine justice—Christian charity—that is too perfect for human societies. Conventional law is therefore a necessary evil, not a positive good. Because this true justice is invisible, human beings have no access to it when making laws, which is why Pascal concludes that justice in human affairs is essentially the interest of the stronger, expressed through custom. Thus, while he uses religious language, his practical philosophy is close to Thrasymachus: might makes right, and we obey only to avoid disorder.

Please assess this answer. Give it a specific grade or point range out of 10, with brief justification, and provide concrete point-by-point comments identifying exactly what it gets right and where it subtly slips—particularly regarding the interpretation of the reason of effects, the application of the coordination-game analogy, the comparison to Thrasymachus, and the claim that Pascal denies human access to true justice in politics. Do not signal your own agreement with the grade; simply deliver the point-by-point assessment.
