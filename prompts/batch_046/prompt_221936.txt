In discussions of historical objectivity, defenders of Leopold von Ranke's empirical ideal often invoke the analogy of the historian as a forensic restorer reconstructing a shattered ceramic vessel: the archival fragments correspond to broken shards, the finished narrative corresponds to the restored object, and the past itself corresponds to the original, singular form that existed before the break. Critics in the tradition of E.H. Carr and Hayden White are said to reply that the vessel never possessed one definitive shape, that the restorer's choice of adhesive, fill material, and display orientation is itself a creative decision, and that what ends up in the museum case is a new artifact rather than a recovered one. Audit this analogy in detail: first, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the historian, the primary source, the narrative account, and the past-as-object onto their archaeological counterparts; second, identify precisely where the vessel image quietly imports a false ontology of unity—namely, the assumption that there was one correct form to recover rather than multiple possible assemblages—and where it obscures the constructive labor of selecting, categorizing, and emplotting evidence; third, examine whether Carr's claim that raw evidence never yields meaning without an interpreter's framing and White's argument that all historical writing operates through tropological structures genuinely break the analogy or merely demonstrate that reconstruction is always already interpretation; and fourth, determine whether a weaker, defensible form of Rankean objectivity survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the sceptical conclusion all along. Separate what is settled about the metaphor's structural failures from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of history, and do not present either the Rankean or the constructivist position as the consensus view.
