I've been working through the luck-egalitarian distinction between option luck and brute luck, and I think I've hit a contradiction that requires either abandoning the responsibility condition or inventing a hybrid category. Consider two people who both voluntarily enter a risky, competitive field—say, experimental theater—fully aware that success is uncertain. One thrives because audience tastes happen to shift in her favor; the other fails because tastes shift unpredictably against her. The shift was exogenous and unforeseeable, yet both made the same kind of calculated gamble. If I classify her failure as brute luck, I seem to erase her responsibility for entering the gamble. If I classify it as option luck, I seem to hold her responsible for an outcome no more foreseeable than being struck by lightning, which feels like blaming the victim. So I propose recognizing "embedded brute option luck"—a choice that is genuinely voluntary, but whose payoff structure is exogenously and unpredictably determined. But then I worry: in any domain where markets, norms, or technologies are volatile, won't there always be an exogenous component hidden inside every option-luck choice? Wouldn't that make the responsibility condition practically empty, reducing luck egalitarianism to continuous welfare equalization disguised by talk of choice? Or is there a principled internal boundary—perhaps around reasonable foreseeability—that lets the view keep both conditions intact without collapsing? I want to know whether my proposed hybrid is necessary, or whether I'm misunderstanding how the standard distinction already handles this, and what that implies for whether luck egalitarianism can survive in rapidly changing societies.
