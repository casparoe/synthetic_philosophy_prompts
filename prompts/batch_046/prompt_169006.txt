I am working through a notoriously dense passage in Kant’s Metaphysics of Morals (1797), in the Doctrine of Right section on punishment. Kant invites us to imagine a society that has agreed to dissolve itself and disperse its members across the world, leaving behind only prisoners. Even in this terminal situation, he argues, the last murderer in custody must still be executed; if he is instead released, the departing members can be seen as complicit in a public wrong—namely, allowing a grave crime to go unavenged. The reasoning is compressed, and I am struggling to reconstruct the exact moral architecture.

Specifically, I want to understand three interlocking points. First, is the obligation to carry out the execution directed at the murderer himself, as a form of respect for his rational agency that has forfeited its claim to life, or is it directed at the collective body of citizens, to preserve the moral coherence of their legal will? Second, since Kant derives the state’s punitive authority from a social contract that is about to be annulled by the society’s dissolution, what contractual basis remains for enforcing the sentence once the community no longer exists? Third, I am wondering whether a particular interpretive framing—one that reads this case as showing that retributive punishment is fundamentally about closing the institutional record rather than about inflicting proportionate suffering—actually fits Kant’s text. Does the island example require that the murderer actually suffer in a manner calibrated to his deed, or does it only require that the legal judgment not be left hanging?

Could you help me map the dependencies among these issues? In particular, I want to know whether the charge of complicity falls on each individual survivor or on the dissolving legal personality itself, and whether Kant’s anxiety about leaving the crime unavenged contains any consequentialist residue about the moral condition of the departing people. I am not looking for a survey of the literature, but for a conceptual reconstruction of how the argument is supposed to hold together.
