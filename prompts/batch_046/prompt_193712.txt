Design a 90-minute classroom simulation for an upper-level seminar of about 18 students that makes participants feel the force of Amartya Sen’s claim that famines are political entitlement collapses rather than natural food shortages. Stage a closed-economy scenario—drawing on the institutional dynamics Sen identifies in the 1943 Bengal famine, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward—in which aggregate food availability never falls below subsistence needs, yet starvation occurs because exchange entitlements disintegrate under state procurement obligations, price ceilings, and information suppression. Give specific roles: rural cultivators holding direct entitlement to harvest, urban wage laborers with only market-dependent entitlements, state procurement officers who see hidden aggregate stock data but not local failures, and traders facing a banned private-market rule. Specify exchange rules, a point-based payoff scheme with no real money, and structured rounds so that the starvation emerges from the rules themselves before anyone names it. Include a debrief that maps each phase onto Sen’s distinction between food-availability decline and entitlement decline, predicts what will go wrong the first time (for instance, students mistaking the artificially induced scarcity for a harvest shock), and frankly notes which feature of Sen’s historical analysis—such as long-run demographic effects or specific colonial administrative structures—the simulation necessarily flattens and the facilitator must supply.
