I run a philosophy-of-science seminar for about 14 graduate students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class deliberation or structured game that makes participants feel the problem of explanatory discrimination within inference to the best explanation: specifically, how two reasoners can apply the same explicit virtues—simplicity, coherence with background knowledge, predictive power, and unification—to an identical body of evidence and still converge on opposite hypotheses because they implicitly weight counterfactual stability differently, without realizing that this hidden parameter rather than the evidence is driving the divergence. Specify the setting, materials (evidence cards, hidden role or weight cards, etc.), rules of interaction, a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no real money, and the sequence of rounds. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants experience the coordination failure or hidden conflict before anyone names it. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of IBE, states what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and is frank about which feature of the actual epistemological problem—such as the absence of an objective metric for weighing explanatory virtues—the activity can only simulate and that you must supply verbally. Keep the deliverable runnable and focused.
