For an essay on how we invent the past, take the 1687 Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns—Charles Perrault’s poetic defense of Louis XIV’s age and Bernard de Fontenelle’s subsequent essays—as a case study in periodization critique. Rather than treating the quarrel as an early articulation of modern scientific and cultural progress, examine what its participants actually said about their own temporal position: did they invoke a rebirth from medieval darkness, a scientific rupture, or something closer to a cyclical, court-centered chronology of “siècles”? Map precisely which period-labels they used and which they never touched, and ask whether their silence about a “Renaissance” or “Scientific Revolution” undermines the claim that these breaks were lived realities. Then use the episode to test the invention thesis: if the actors who allegedly inhabited these epochs had no vocabulary for them, does that show that “Middle Ages” and “Scientific Revolution” are retrospective historiographical containers? Or does it merely reveal that epochal self-consciousness is unnecessary for epochal change? Conclude with what the quarrel implies about whether periodization describes real discontinuities or imposes narrative order on continuous processes, and flag any feature of the dispute—such as the role of royal patronage in framing “modernity”—that resists either reading.
