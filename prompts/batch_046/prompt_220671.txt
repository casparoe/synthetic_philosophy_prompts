I have never studied philosophy or psychology formally, but over the past two years I have kept returning to a basic question that feels deeper the more I think about it: what exactly is an emotion? Is it a kind of judgment—like deciding that a situation is dangerous—or is it more like a perception, as if the world itself comes colored with fear or joy? Or is it ultimately just a bodily feeling, like hunger or cold, that happens to have a cause?

Recently, a well-read friend tried to settle the matter for me with the following summary. He said that philosophers traditionally divide into three camps: judgment theorists, who claim that fear just is the belief or assessment that something threatens harm; perceptual theorists, who treat emotions as direct awareness of value-laden features in the world, analogous to seeing color or hearing a sound; and feeling theorists, who reduce emotions to raw internal sensations with little or no built-in mental content. My friend then introduced what he called emotional granularity—the observation that people who possess finer distinctions in their emotional vocabulary (for instance, separating shame from guilt) report measurably different bodily states than those with coarser vocabularies. From this he argued that if emotions were mere bodily feelings or direct perceptions, linguistic refinement should not reshape their physical form; therefore, emotions must be constructed judgments. He concluded that the judgment theory is correct, that perceptual theories are really just judgment theories in disguise because both involve mental content, and that feeling theories have been empirically refuted. He added that this means emotions are always open to rational correction in exactly the same way as beliefs, since altering one's conceptual judgment should directly alter the emotion itself.

I am not convinced his conclusion follows, and parts of his account feel suspiciously tidy. Could you go through his summary slowly and tell me which parts accurately represent the three positions, which parts subtly misrepresent them—for example, whether judgment theorists really identify emotions with explicit, conscious beliefs, or whether perceptual theorists accept that emotions are belief-like in the relevant sense—and whether his argument from emotional granularity actually supports the conclusion he draws? I am particularly interested in whether the claim that vocabulary reshapes bodily states truly undermines the feeling view, or whether a careful defender of that view could accept the finding without surrendering. Please separate the accurate framing from the misrepresentations, and tell me whether his final claim about rational correctability survives once the errors are fixed.
