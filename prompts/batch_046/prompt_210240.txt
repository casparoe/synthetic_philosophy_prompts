After my father's death this past winter, I have been unable to stop returning to a question that sounds abstract but feels visceral: in what sense, if any, do the moments I shared with him still exist? This has drawn me back to the philosophy of time, specifically the Rietdijk-Putnam argument that special relativity's relativity of simultaneity undermines any global "now" and supports a four-dimensional block universe. I would like you to write a dialogue—intended for my own private clarification, not for publication—between two interlocutors of equal philosophical competence. One should defend a version of presentism that accepts special relativity but rejects the move to eternalism; the other should defend the block view and press the Rietdijk-Putnam reasoning. To keep the exchange from being a recitation of textbook positions, please build the presentist's case around a specific, non-obvious retreat: she concedes that there is no frame-independent global present, but argues for a "fragmented presentism" in which each inertial worldline carries its own local present defined by its proper-time hyperplanes, so that temporal becoming is real but distributed rather than unified. The eternalist should then argue that this fragmentation collapses into eternalism by another name—since every spacetime event lies on the local present of some worldline, nothing is genuinely excluded from being present-relative-to-something, and the presentist has merely relabeled the block. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: the presentist must respond to the collapse objection by appealing to the metaphysical primacy of first-person temporal experience anchored to a single worldline, and the eternalist must answer that appeal without simply restating the original argument. Neither speaker should be a strawman; each should grant the strongest version of the opponent's point before replying. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific page numbers, and do not ask me for sources—I want the reasoning on its own terms.
