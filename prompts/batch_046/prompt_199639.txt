I'm a PhD student in philosophy working at the intersection of rational choice theory and the metaphysics of agency, and I've been reconsidering the classic Buridan's ass scenario not as a paradox about paralysis but as a misdiagnosis of what rational equilibrium demands. My working view—call it procedural asymmetry injection—is that when an agent faces two options that are perfectly symmetric in every first-order respect (value, accessibility, expected utility, evidential support), the correct rational response is neither arbitrary selection via a randomizing mechanism nor indefinite suspension, but rather an ascent to a second-order deliberative level. The agent applies a pre-committed decision-procedure—whether a temporal-priority rule, a heuristic drawn from prior practical commitments, or a structural feature of her own reasoning architecture—to restructure the choice space so that the tie is broken not by fiat but by the meta-structure of her deliberation. I take this to preserve a qualified principle of sufficient reason: there is always a reason for the choice, but it resides in the architecture of practical reasoning rather than in a first-order difference between the options. On this account, apparent cases of "pure" arbitrary choice in equilibrium are always disguised applications of hidden asymmetries, and Buridan's ass dies not from indecision but from a normative failure to maintain this structured suspension rather than collapsing into ungrounded selection.

What I want is an honest map of what would actually count against this position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Does it violate formal constraints in decision theory—such as completeness, continuity, or the independence of irrelevant alternatives—by allowing second-order restructuring to alter first-order preference orderings in ways that seem ad hoc? Would defenders of randomization, including those who appeal to objective chance, epistemic humility, or the normative permissibility of breaking ties without reason, concede that procedural injection avoids the charge of arbitrariness, or would they argue that I've merely relocated the problem upward, leaving the selection of the meta-procedure itself ungrounded? Are there intuitive or empirical cases—perhaps involving genuine moral dilemmas, perceptual equilibrium, or cognitive overload—where procedural injection seems unavailable or psychologically implausible, and if so, does my view collapse into an implausibly restricted domain claim? I also worry that the view risks insulation by definitional maneuver: if every apparent arbitrary choice can be redescribed as concealing a second-order rule, has the position been rendered unfalsifiable, and is that a genuine defect for a theory of rational agency rather than a virtue? Finally, please distinguish which of these objections a committed defender could concede as genuinely damaging without abandoning the core idea, and which they would dismiss as resting on a false picture of deliberation—particularly whether the charge of upward relocation mistakes the normative status of second-order practical identity.
