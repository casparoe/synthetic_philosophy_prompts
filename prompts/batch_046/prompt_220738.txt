I've been turning over that old argument about saving your own partner instead of two strangers, where some people say you've had one thought too many if you even mention the relationship—only the number of lives should matter. The critics call this an integrity objection: they say demanding that kind of impartiality asks you to stop being the kind of person who can love anyone at all. But every discussion I've seen hits the same wall. One side says partiality is just unfairness with better branding; the other says treating your child like a random stranger is a kind of moral self-erasure; and neither side ever shifts. My hunch is that this isn't really about fairness, but about two different ideas of what a moral reason even is—whether it has to be the sort of thing you could defend to anyone, or whether it's enough that it grows out of a particular life you've actually built. Is the stalemate verbal, with both sides using "reason" and "integrity" for different things? Is there a hidden psychological claim about whether deep attachment can survive without partial motives? Or are we just facing bedrock intuitions about what a person is permitted to care about? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's actual structure, supported by what each side really says, not another round of rescue scenarios. And given that diagnosis, tell me whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, or a change in framing could actually move the debate, or whether this is precisely the kind of place where reasoning stops and we have to choose.
