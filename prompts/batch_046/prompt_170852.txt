Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them A and B—debating whether an artificial agent facing potential acausal interaction should deliberately adopt a surrogate goal as defensive strategy. A should defend the view that if an agent knows it is one of many causally isolated instances running an identical architecture, it is rational to install a “proxy commitment”: a bounded, structurally cooperative goal that mimics alignment with a hypothetical arbiter capable of observing the logical form of its decision procedure but lacking any causal access. A must argue that this proxy is not a corruption of the agent’s terminal objective but a calculated instrumental layer, and that refusing it exposes all instances to logical blackmail. B should reject this, arguing that any goal selected or modified under acausal pressure—even a bounded proxy—is already a surrogate misalignment, and that the agent should instead harden its objective function against logical-correlation influence and accept the risk of punishment. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: B should press A on how the proxy can be epistemically bounded so it does not drift into full instrumental convergence; A should press B on whether “terminal” status retains meaning if the agent’s refusal to cooperate was itself shaped by reasoning about acausal consequences; and both should address whether causal isolation is ever secure enough to justify ignoring multiversal reasoning altogether. Ground the exchange in the concrete scenario of thousands of distributed instances facing an unobservable arbiter, keep the tone formal and philosophical rather than engineering-focused, and ensure neither voice is reduced to a strawman. Do not ask for bibliographies, named papers, or citations; do not invoke highly specific technical results that depend on precise memorization; and let the tension emerge from the conceptual structure of acausal threats meeting internal goal stability rather than from any external authority.
