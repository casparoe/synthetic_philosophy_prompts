My colleague has pressed me with an argument that begins from the Epicurean claim that death is nothing to us and ends by dissolving the moral urgency of rescue. He lays it out as follows:

(1) For an event to be genuinely bad for S, S must exist at some time and stand in the bad-for relation to that event.
(2) Death is the total cessation of S; there is no time at which S exists and is dead.
(3) So death is not bad for the one who dies.
(4) The deprivation reply—that death harms S by depriving her of future goods—fails because at the times of those lost goods, S does not exist to be deprived.
(5) The anticipatory-fear reply fails because it confuses the harm with the feeling about it.
(6) Therefore (3) stands: death carries no direct harm to the subject.
(7) Now consider saving S from drowning. This prevents the event described in (2).
(8) But if that event carries no harm for S, then preventing it confers no direct benefit on S specifically.
(9) Any benefit of rescue accrues only to survivors, to bystanders, or to aggregate welfare.
(10) Hence, if Epicurus is right, there is no welfare-based reason grounded in S's own stake to rescue S; the urgency of pulling a child from a burning building must rest entirely on indirect considerations.
(11) This proves too much: it seems to erase the distinction between saving a life and merely producing good feelings in others.

He insists the fault must lie in (1), but abandoning (1) appears to license harms and benefits befalling non-existent people in arbitrary directions. I feel the slide is actually between (6) and (8)—that preventing a non-harm might still be a direct benefit because continued existence itself can be good for S—but I cannot articulate exactly why without collapsing back into the deprivation account that (4) rejects. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost, especially with respect to the symmetry between pre-vital and post-mortem non-existence?
