At a recent philosophy seminar, two participants—Maya and Leo—debated Plato’s Meno problem in a form updated for epistemology of cognition. They asked a bystander to referee; the exchange ran as follows.

Maya opened by arguing that knowledge is superior to true belief because it is tethered: justification anchors a belief to reality, giving it stability and resistance to disturbance. A true belief arrived at by guessing is like a balloon that happens to reach the right altitude; it can be blown off course by the next piece of counter-evidence. Knowledge, because it includes reasons the agent can deploy, survives scrutiny and supports further inference.

Leo replied that “tethered” is only a metaphor for justification, and that this just relocates the puzzle. He introduced what he called an epistemic-escrow model: imagine a belief is fully justified by evidence locked in a cognitive module the agent never accesses. The belief is true and justified, yet the agent treats it exactly as she would treat a lucky guess. If such a belief lacks the extra value Maya claims, then justification must be active or accessible to matter; if it does have extra value, then inert justification is doing mysterious work. Leo said this suggests the Meno problem assumes, without argument, that justification must be psychologically available.

Maya countered that if justification is truly inert, then for the agent in question the state is functionally identical to true belief. The value of knowledge, she insisted, lies in its accessibility: the knower can defend, correct, and extend the belief precisely because the supporting reasons are within reach. Leo’s escrow case therefore supports her view rather than undermining it—hidden justification is epistemically present but practically absent.

Leo then proposed an alternative he called tracking coherence. Rather than locating value in conscious access to reasons, he suggested that knowledge is belief embedded in a larger network of representations that collectively track the world across contexts, even when the agent cannot articulate the links. He called beliefs justified only by their structural role in this network silent partners. On this view, knowledge is better than true belief not because the agent can cite her reasons, but because the belief is integrated into a reliable tracking system that sustains inference elsewhere. This, he argued, preserves the intuition that knowledge has added value without reducing that value to psychological accessibility.

Maya responded that tracking coherence is essentially reliabilism redescribed, and that it fails to address the agent-centered concern at the heart of the Meno problem. If the agent herself does not know that her belief participates in a coherent tracking network—if the coherence is subpersonal—then she still lacks the capacity to defend or correct the belief. Leo has shown that some beliefs are reliably formed, but he has not shown that inert structural properties confer on the agent the distinctive goods—authority, defensibility, extendability—that make knowledge more valuable than mere true belief.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: evaluate Maya’s tethering argument, Leo’s epistemic-escrow challenge, Maya’s accessibility reply, Leo’s tracking-coherence proposal, and Maya’s final rebuttal. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, clearly right, or somewhere in between, with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—whether it concerns the social or communicative dimension of knowledge, the distinction between personal-level and subpersonal justification, or some other consideration that neither has raised. Keep your verdict precise: do not merely declare a winner, but specify exactly on which points each side succeeds and where each falls short.
