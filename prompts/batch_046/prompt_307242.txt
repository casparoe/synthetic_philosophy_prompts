I have been trying to reconcile two commitments in Leibniz's moral philosophy, but I suspect I am conflating distinct levels of analysis. Leibniz defines justice as the charity of the wise governed by harmony, treating it as an eternal idea in the divine intellect and therefore a necessary truth of reason. Yet he also holds that God freely chooses the best world according to the principle of sufficient reason, and the particular obligations of benevolence we actually face seem to presuppose the contingent existence of sentient beings in that world. From this I have drawn what feels like a contradiction: if the beings to whom charity is owed exist only contingently, then the claim that charity is obligatory here appears to be a contingent truth of fact rather than a rationally necessary one. How can Leibniz maintain that moral norms are metaphysically necessary while allowing that their binding application depends on the contingent population of agents in the world God happens to select?
