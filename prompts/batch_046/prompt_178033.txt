Argue that the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are not independent puzzles about rational belief aggregation but twin symptoms of a single underlying error: the assumption that epistemic rationality operates under a uniform closure principle applied indiscriminately to all accepted propositions, regardless of their functional role in an agent's cognitive economy. Defend the view that rational belief-acceptance should instead be governed by what may be termed "epistemic jurisdiction"—the specific domain of reasoning for which a belief is licensed to function—such that high-confidence beliefs about one's own general fallibility (as in the preface) fall under a reflexive, meta-cognitive jurisdiction with licensing conditions distinct from those governing high-confidence beliefs about particular empirical outcomes (as in the lottery). Demonstrate how this jurisdictional distinction dissolves both paradoxes without invoking arbitrary probabilistic thresholds, without abandoning the rationality of believing highly probable propositions, and without collapsing into contextualism about knowledge ascriptions. Finally, address the objection that this framework merely relocates the tension into an unprincipled taxonomy of belief-types rather than resolving it.
