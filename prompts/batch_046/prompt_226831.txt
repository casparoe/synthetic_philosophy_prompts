You chair the ethics review board for a psychiatric research hospital, and every proposal lands with the same structural problem: a deontological constraint points one way—never deceive a subject, never withhold a known beneficial treatment—while honoring it produces severe harm, and relaxing it risks collapsing deontology into consequentialism. Write a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions with stopping conditions—that your board can run in under thirty minutes per case. Ground it in the actual paradox of deontology (absolute rules in conflict with outcomes or with each other), not in a generic “balance the principles” framework. The procedure must include: (1) a test to distinguish a genuine deontological conflict from a disguised consequentialist trade-off; (2) a hard-stop rejection rule; (3) an approval-with-conditions rule; (4) explicit flag points where judgment is unavoidable and must be documented by the chair. Apply it to two proposals: (a) a study requiring deceptive placebo administration to severely depressed patients who have failed open-label drugs; (b) a trial withholding an effective standard therapy from a control group to test a risky experimental intervention. For each, trace the procedure’s path, identify the step where it forces a decision you personally find wrong, and state what the procedure cannot do—specifically, whether it resolves the paradox or merely relocates it. Take a committed position: defend a definite view on whether deontology can survive this procedure without becoming threshold deontology in disguise, and explain why that view holds or fails.
