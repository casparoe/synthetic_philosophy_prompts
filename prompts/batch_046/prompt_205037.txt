Two colleagues in my department—I'll call them A and B—got into a prolonged disagreement about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, and they kept returning to a concrete case: a research group that had spent nearly four years developing a predictive framework that was now consistently underperforming relative to simpler baseline models. A opened with the canonical argument: rational deliberation is forward-looking, so past labor and capital should carry zero weight in the decision to continue or abandon the project; persisting solely because "we've already invested so much" is precisely the sunk-cost fallacy, and its irrationality is independent of domain, whether one is deciding about a movie ticket or a multi-year grant. B conceded the point for private consumption choices but insisted that institutional and identity-embedded commitments operate differently. B introduced the idea of "epistemic collateral": in a credibility-dependent environment like academic funding, an observable, costly trajectory functions as collateral that secures future trust. Abandoning the framework now doesn't merely waste prior effort; it realizes a reputational default that makes future commitments by the same team non-credible to reviewers and collaborators. Therefore, B argued, continuing the project can be rational not despite the sunk cost but because the sunk observable investment has structurally altered the payoff landscape—quitting now carries a future penalty that exists only in virtue of the past expenditure having been made and witnessed. A replied that B was smuggling forward-looking strategic reasoning back under the label of honoring sunk costs: if the rational agent anticipates that quitting damages future funding, then she continues for expected future benefit, not because she weights the past. The fallacy, A insisted, requires that the past cost itself figure in the evaluation of outcomes, not merely that past actions have caused present strategic conditions; B had shown only that quitting has hidden costs, not that sunk costs are ever legitimately honored. B countered that in practice one cannot cleanly partition "reasons that involve the past" from "reasons that involve the future" in embedded decision architectures. The future penalty of quitting is not an independent state of the world; it is constitutively tied to the observable history of investment. To treat them as separable, B claimed, is an artificial analytic move that assumes a god's-eye view unavailable to actual agents embedded in reputational markets. A then pressed that if B's argument dissolves the boundary between strategic persistence and fallacious persistence, the concept of the sunk-cost fallacy becomes unfalsifiable and loses all normative bite—any instance of persistence can be redescribed as protection of epistemic collateral. B acknowledged this risk but maintained that the burden lies on the normative theorist to show why protecting credibility-built-through-observable-commitment should be classified with the irrational movie-goer rather than with rational intertemporal bargaining. Neither would concede. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: where is each party correct, where do they talk past one another, and is there a specific further case—perhaps involving a non-observable investment or a domain without reputational tracking—that would best reveal whether B's "epistemic collateral" genuinely differs from the fallacy or merely renames it? Also, identify anything significant that both are missing in framing the dispute.
