I'm trying to nail down what I actually think about societal collapse versus just changing shape, going by my gut on specific cases. The Late Bronze Age crash around 1177 BCE -- palaces burn, Linear B writing vanishes, long-distance trade collapses -- that feels like a real collapse, not just a reorganzation. The Classic Maya in the southern lowlands around the 9th century -- they stop building monuments and abandon the big palace centers, but populations stay in the region, writing and religion continue elsewhere, so I'd call that transformation, not collapse. Easter Island cutting down the trees, ending the moai cult, and losing a huge chunk of population -- I keep flip-flopping. Part of me says that's collapse because they never rebuilt the old social form; part says it's just contraction, since people survived and developed the birdman cult. The Western Roman Empire in the 5th century -- imperial administration stops in the West, but local life, language, and markets keep going under different rulers, so I say the state collapsed but society didn't, though I'm not sure that distinction holds water. Then there's this pattern I notice: I seem to care whether there's continuity in what I'd call the institutional substrate -- the basic rules people live by, the knowledge transmission, the economic coordination -- versus just a rupture in the political superstructure. So my tentative principle is that collapse only happens when the substrate breaks, not when the superstructure does. But that runs into trouble with Easter Island, where the substrate maybe did shift to smaller-scale agriculture and a new religion, yet I still feel the pull to call it collapse because the loss was so severe. And then there's the hypothetical: imagine a city of half a million loses its water infrastructure, the governing elite evacuates, and the remaining people split into small farming villages with a completely new oral religion and no literacy or memory of the old administration. By my substrate principle that's collapse, but it also feels like transformation. So is there a cleaner principle that saves my Bronze Age/Maya/Rome verdicts and forces me to resolve Easter Island one way or the other? Or do I just have to bite the bullet and drop one of them? Please take a definite stand -- don't just lay out options and leave me hanging. And tell me what your principle predicts for that water-city hypothetical.
