I had a long argument last night with a friend about coherence arguments for expected utility maximization, and she reconstructed a chain that I think must fail somewhere, though I can't quite name the slide. Here is her reasoning, as I wrote it out this morning to check for equivocations.

(1) Rationality requires coherence: your beliefs and preferences must be arranged so that you cannot be Dutch-booked.
(2) Any preference ordering that violates expected utility maximization is Dutch-bookable: one can construct a series of bets that guarantee a net loss if your choices don't align with a single utility function weighted by your credences.
(3) Therefore violating EU maximization is not merely imprudent but literally incoherent.
(4) Now apply coherence across modal space: rationality shouldn't depend on contingent facts about which world happens to be actual, so your preferences must be coherent across all counterfactual scenarios you might face, including ones in which your physical identity, your values, or the very ontology of outcomes differs radically.
(5) Hence a fully rational agent must possess a complete, transitive preference ordering over every logically possible state of affairs, each assigned a credence and evaluated by the same utility function.
(6) But then I am rationally required to have definite preferences about, for instance, whether a specific quantum fluctuation in Andromeda is arranged in pattern A rather than pattern B—since that is a logically possible state, and coherence demands I not be Dutch-bookable with respect to bets framed over it.
(7) That is absurd: I have no such preferences, and demanding them confuses rational consistency with omniscient cosmic bookkeeping.
(8) So coherence arguments for EU maximization are false, or at least they collapse once extended consistently.

She insists I am smuggling in an unstated restriction—that coherence only applies within a "domain of agency"—that coherence theorists never endorsed, and that rejecting (4) costs me the universality that makes coherence attractive. I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact if I can. Which step actually breaks? Is the error in (4)'s modal extension, in (5)'s completeness requirement, or in (6)'s assumption that every logical possibility must fall within the scope of preference? If I reject (4), what does that do to the claim that rationality is world-independent?
