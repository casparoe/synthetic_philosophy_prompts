Here are my verdicts on several episodes linked to the Enlightenment, and I want to know whether one principle can sort them, or whether I must abandon one of my judgments. First, the Encyclopédie project’s publication of mechanical plates and cross-referenced articles by a dispersed network of artisans and philosophes: I take this as genuinely progressive, because it broke guild secrecy and made technical knowledge revisable by its readers rather than fixed by master craftsmen. Second, the revolutionary French imposition of a decimal calendar and metric system by central decree, abolishing Sunday and religious feast days: I take this as domination, because an abstract rational grid designed by a small administrative class replaced lived social rhythms without leaving those rhythms open to revision by the people who inhabited them. Third, the adoption of smallpox inoculation across European courts during the 1700s, including its uptake in the Russian court after observation in Ottoman territories: progressive, since it saved lives and relied on empirical observation that crossed cultural boundaries without suppressing the knowledge of the societies observed. Fourth, the Jardin des Plantes in Paris, arranged by Linnaean taxonomy and open to the public, but assembled in part through colonial expeditions in Senegal and Southeast Asia that extracted botanical specimens and rarely acknowledged local classification practices: domination, because the universal taxonomy served administrative control of colonial resources while presenting itself as neutral, democratic education.

Now a pair that looks structurally similar but feels opposite to me. The British abolitionist campaigns that used universalist natural-rights language to attack the slave trade strike me as progressive, even though they were led by elite reformers operating within imperial structures. By contrast, the universalist language of the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen strikes me as domination, because its abstract equality initially excluded enslaved people in the colonies and women, functioning as ideological cover for continued exclusion. Both invoke universal human equality and are led by elites; I want to preserve the distinction.

My tentative principle is this: an Enlightenment practice or discourse belongs to its progressive face when it structurally enables those affected by it to challenge, revise, or redirect it, and belongs to its domination face when it structurally disables such challenge among the affected while reserving revisionary power for its architects alone. Test this principle against my five cases. Does it save all my verdicts? If not, which verdict must be overturned, and what is the simplest repair that preserves the others? State the repaired principle precisely, identify the specific case that motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for an episode I have not mentioned—such as the founding of a state technical school with meritocratic entrance examinations but a primary mission of military engineering and state administration. Be honest about whether the repaired principle is too complex to be worth keeping, or whether it collapses into something simpler that forces me to change my mind about the calendar or about the Declaration.
