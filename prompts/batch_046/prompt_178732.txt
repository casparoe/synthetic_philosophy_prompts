I'm drafting an essay for my graduate seminar in historical methodology, and I'd like your critical comments before I finalize it. The central claim is that Whig history should be analyzed not primarily as presentism or anachronistic moral judgment—though those are present—but as a specific structural error I call retrospective convergence: the implicit metaphysical commitment that historical processes possess an attractor state identical to present institutions, so that earlier events are narrated as necessary approximations rather than contingent outcomes. I examine three canonical narratives through this lens: the Glorious Revolution as an inevitable constitutional settlement, the European trajectory toward religious toleration, and the historiography of medicine's departure from humoral theory. In each case I ask whether stripping away the convergence assumption collapses the explanatory architecture or merely renders it descriptively thinner. I also engage with Butterfield's classic critique, but I argue that he targeted moral projection onto the past more than this hidden directional scaffolding. My concern is that retrospective convergence might be indistinguishable from generic teleology, or that I am misreading Butterfield's actual emphasis. Could you evaluate the coherence of this framing, point out any serious misrepresentations of the historiographical literature, and tell me whether the concept actually does useful analytical work?
