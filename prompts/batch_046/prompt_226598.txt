I'm advising a small conceptual research group — three people, one year, no empirical data-collection budget — focused on institutional decision-making under deep uncertainty. Their concern is not forecasting a single event, but meta-epistemic calibration: how an agent should weigh its own inside reasoning against outside-view base rates when assessing the reliability of that very reasoning process over time.

They are particularly interested in a reflexivity problem that standard treatments tend to bracket. The reference classes used to build an outside view — past expert predictions, prior technological transitions, historical episodes of institutional failure — are not drawn from a population of naive reasoners. They are drawn from agents who themselves employed inside-view reasoning, often with high confidence, and whose successes and failures were partly shaped by that reasoning. Treating this population as an independent base rate therefore risks a systematic bias: it may underweight cases where inside reasoning correctly identified structural novelty, and overweight cases where narrative coherence masked similarity to past failures.

At the same time, pure inside-view reasoning risks overfitting to locally coherent narratives and underestimating structural similarity to past failures. The practical stakes involve how boards should weight expert dissent, how individuals should revise confidence in long-range predictions, and whether there is a principled way to recognize regime change without collapsing into special pleading.

4. What criteria you are using to rank them, such as tractability, dependency on other questions, practical leverage, or risk of being a dead end.
