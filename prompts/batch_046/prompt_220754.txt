I've been rewatching Groundhog Day for a philosophy of mind discussion group and I'm stuck on whether the loop actually dramatizes teh frame problem in a useful way, or if it just uses repetition as a moral fable. Phil Connors wakes up to the same day; at first he treats every detail as potentially changeable (he robs a bank, drives on train tracks, eats endlessly), but eventually he seems to learn which beliefs and actions are relevent to update—he learns piano, saves a child, helps townspeople—while treating the loop itself as a fixed background he can't frame away. That looks like a human version of the AI frame problem: the system has to know which representations to revise and which to hold constant without checking every belief against every new input. But I want to push the analogy further into something less obvious. There's this idea in some cognitive science literature—relevance realization, I think it's called—where framing isn't just filtering beliefs but actively constructing what's salient based on embodied interaction. If we read Phil's arc through that, does the film suggest that solving the frame problem requires not just computation but a kind of moral embodiment? Like, he only stops treating everything as equally relevent once he cares about specific people?

At the same time, I worry I'm forcing the film. Groundhog Day was made well before the frame problem became a popular philosophical reference point, and Phil's transformation is usually read as a story about virtue and love, not about representational economy. So I'd want an honest critic to say where the philosophical lens actually illuminates a scene—maybe the diner conversation with Rita where he lists her preferences, showing he's built a model of what's relevent to her—and where the film resists being reduced to an AI parable. Does the movie imply that human framing is solved through emotional attachment rather than efficient representation, and if so, does that actually help philosophers understand why the frame problem is hard for machines? Or does it just change the subject?

I'm teaching this in a class that mixes film studies with philosophy of mind, so I want a reading that takes both the movie's narrative structure and the technical problem seriously, without making Phil into a chatbot. What do you think?
