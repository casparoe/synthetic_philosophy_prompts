I was in this late-night discussion with some friends after a course on consciousness and we got stuck on Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. Like, one friend was arguing that the self is basically just the story your brain tells to keep behavior coherent — so if you've got severe dissociation or a split-brain setup where two different life-stories seem to run without talking to eachother, you've literally got multiple gravity centers, or maybe none if the story fragments too much. The other friend kept insisting there's always this bare indexical anchor, just the minimal sense of being-here-now, that survives even when the narrative totally collapses, so narrative gravity is just fancy decoration on top of something more basic.

We went in circles for like two hours. Nobody denied the clinical facts about dissociation or split-brain patients, and nobody even disagreed that stories matter for identity over time — but the debate didn't move at all. I'm trying to understand if we're actually fighting about what's real, or if self just means the narrative construct for one person and the phenomenalogical anchor for the other, so we're answering different questions with the same word. Or maybe there's a hidden crux about whether coherence requires a single author-function in the brain, or whether it can be distributed across hemispheres, and neither of us knows enough neuroscience to settle it. Could you diagnose what kind of stall this actually is? Is it verbal, substantive, about standards of explanation, or just bedrock intuition? And what would actually count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a specific brain-imaging result, or maybe accepting that the question is badly posed? I don't need a survey of every philosopher who's written about this; I want the structure of the disagreement laid bare, with reasons.
