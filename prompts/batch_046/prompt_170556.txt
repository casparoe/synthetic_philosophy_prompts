In our philosophy elective, two classmates debated a hypothetical astronomical finding: in a distant, isolated galaxy cluster, free-falling bodies accelerate at exactly half the rate our gravitational equations predict, with no detectable hidden mass or interfering field. Maya argued from a Humean view that this simply reveals a different local regularity; she said our gravitational equation was never more than a summary of constant conjunction observed near Earth, so there is no universal necessity being violated, only a pattern that fails to extend everywhere. Leo defended an anti-Humean position, insisting that if gravitation is genuinely a law it must govern all matter necessarily, which means the anomaly should drive us to search for an undiscovered local cause rather than treating the law as regionally optional; otherwise, he claimed, science collapses into a disconnected catalog of local habits with no real distinction between correlation and law. Maya replied that Leo is projecting metaphysical glue onto observed regularities, exactly the error Hume warned against when people confuse habitual expectation with enforced modal structure. Leo countered that without such governing structure, scientific explanation itself becomes impossible, since there would be no reason to prefer a unified account over an arbitrary patchwork of regional patterns. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate Maya's claim about regional regularities, Leo's argument from necessary governance, Maya's charge of metaphysical projection, and Leo's defense of explanation—indicating for each whether it is clearly mistaken, plausible but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing, especially regarding how contemporary anti-Humean views such as dispositional essentialism might address local anomalies differently from classical necessitarian accounts.
