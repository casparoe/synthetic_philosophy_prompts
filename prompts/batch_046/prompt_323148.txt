I have been working through the disagreement between Humean regularity theories and anti-Humean necessitarian accounts of natural law, and I have constructed an argument that seems to show anti-Humeanism is either circular or ontologically extravagant, yet I cannot locate the precise exit. Here are the steps as I understand them. (1) Anti-Humeans hold that laws are metaphysically necessary relations—whether among universals, essential powers, or governing structures—that explain why particular events occur as they do. (2) If laws genuinely govern instances, then they must stand in an explanatory relation to those instances; otherwise the language of governance is empty. (3) Explanation is asymmetric: if A explains B, then B does not explain A. (4) Humeans reject this explanatory direction: particular matters of fact form the Humean mosaic, and laws are merely the theorems of the best axiomatic systematization of that mosaic; the mosaic explains the laws, not the reverse. (5) Take dispositional essentialism, a leading anti-Humean position: fundamental properties carry their causal profiles essentially, so that once a property is instantiated, its behavior is fixed by its nature. (6) Which properties are actually instantiated, however, is itself contingent on the particular arrangement of the mosaic—different worlds contain different universals. (7) Thus the mosaic determines which powers are present, and the present powers, in virtue of their essential natures, determine the behavior of the mosaic. (8) This creates a vicious circularity: the mosaic selects the powers, and the powers govern the mosaic. (9) To break the circle, the anti-Humean must treat universals and their governing relations as existing necessarily and independently of any instantiation, analogous to Platonic forms. (10) But this generates what we might call a "law glut": an infinite ontology of uninstantiated necessary relations that govern nothing actual, while Humeanism avoids any such glut by tying laws strictly to instantiated regularities. (11) Consequently, either anti-Humeanism is circular or it abandons the claim that the mosaic plays any role in selecting which laws are relevant, thereby undermining its own explanatory advantage over Humeanism. (12) From this I conclude that there is no coherent realist metaphysics of lawhood that preserves both explanatory asymmetry and ontological parsimony.

I am particularly unwilling to surrender (2) or (6), and I want to preserve the intuition that circular explanation is a genuine defect. Yet holding both seems to push me toward the absurd result that all realist accounts of law fail. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? In particular, is there a way to maintain that powers explain their instances without accepting that the mosaic explains the powers? I ask this in good faith, and I want direct pushback if my framing confuses the metaphysical relation between a universal and its instances with a causal or explanatory relation: tell me plainly rather than politely reconstructing the argument to save it.
