In discussions of translation under formal constraint — particularly the kind of extreme fidelity to rhyme, metre, and tonal music that Douglas Hofstadter explores in Le Ton beau de Marot through his many renderings of Clément Marot’s brief sixteenth-century poem À une damoyselle malade — defenders of form-first translation often invoke a medical analogy: translating the poem is like performing a vascular graft, in which the translator acts as surgeon, the original rhyme scheme and syllabic metre form the patient’s irreplaceable circulatory network, and the target-language words are donor tissue that must be accepted without immune rejection. The intended conclusion is that if the formal body survives intact, the poem remains alive, even when individual semantic cells are replaced or lost.

Audit this analogy thoroughly. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the surgeon, the patient, the donor tissue, the immune system, and the rejection crisis? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the argument: for instance, a biological body possesses a single correct anatomy, whereas a poem’s form is conventional rather than organically necessary; a rejected graft kills the patient, whereas a rejected rhyme merely produces a different poem; and the surgeon’s goal is restoration of prior health, not the creation of a new functional being. Third, examine what the analogy quietly imports about the value of the whole versus the parts — does it assume that form is the essential life-support system and meaning merely replaceable tissue? Fourth, consider Hofstadter’s own practice, in which semantic drift is frequently permitted to serve musical and rhyming fidelity: does this support the graft model, or does it show that the translator is actually redesigning the anatomy rather than preserving it? Fifth, state whether the conclusion — that formal preservation alone guarantees the poem’s survival — survives these disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along.
