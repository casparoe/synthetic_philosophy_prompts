I'm drafting a long-form feature on whether "societal collapse" denotes a real analytical category or merely a dramatic narrative frame we impose retrospectively. Before committing to a thesis, I want to see whether my gut judgments about specific historical endings form a coherent pattern, or whether I'm applying the label inconsistently.

Here are my verdicts, case by case.

First, the Late Bronze Age crisis across the Eastern Mediterranean around 1177 BCE: I call this a genuine collapse. The palatial economies, Linear B administration, and interregional trade networks do not merely shift form; they vanish simultaneously across multiple independent polities, and in Greece the writing system is lost for centuries. It feels like a system-level failure rather than a local transition.

Second, the Classic Maya abandonment of the southern lowlands between roughly 800 and 900 CE: I resist calling this a collapse of "Maya civilization." Divine kingship, the Long Count calendar, and writing persist in the northern lowlands and highlands; the population does not disappear but relocates. What dies is a particular urban-political ecology, not the society's capacity to reproduce itself. So I classify it as regional transformation, not collapse.

Third, Rapa Nui (Easter Island) between the 17th and 19th centuries: here I do say collapse. The competitive chiefdom system that organized moai construction and resource distribution fragments irreversibly; the population contracts severely; and while the Rapanui language and some customs survive, the specific socio-institutional complex that defined the island's organized complexity is not regenerated. It feels categorically different from the Maya case, even though both involve demographic stress and regional cultural survival.

Fourth, the Indus Valley de-urbanization around 1900 BCE: I do not call this collapse. There is no sharp rupture, no widespread destruction layer, and no clear centralized institution whose disappearance can be tracked. The Harappan script fades, but material culture evolves gradually eastward. It looks like a slow reconfiguration of settlement patterns in response to hydrological change, not a breakdown.

The obvious tension is between the Maya and Easter Island judgments. In the Maya case, institutional structures vanish across a large region, yet I deny collapse because the culture continues elsewhere. In the Easter Island case, cultural and linguistic continuity also persists, yet I affirm collapse because the institutional order is not restored. Meanwhile, in the Bronze Age, writing and administration disappear in some regions while persisting in others, yet I call the overall network collapsed.

So my question is whether there is a coherent principle—perhaps one centered on what I might call "reproductive closure," meaning the ability, somewhere within a society's recognized boundaries, to regenerate the institutional and symbolic mechanisms that maintain its complexity. The Maya retain it; Easter Island, being geographically bounded and lacking an external reservoir, does not; the Bronze Age system loses it at the network level even if individual societies survive; and the Indus never possessed a centralized reproductive mechanism that could be lost in the first place.

But if that is the principle, I need to know: does it force me to drop any of my four verdicts? And what should it predict about a case I keep returning to—the transformation of Anglo-Saxon Britain after Roman administrative withdrawal in the 5th century, where urban life and writing disappear for centuries, yet a new political order eventually emerges? Is that collapse, transformation, or something else?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, report honestly whether the Maya/Easter Island distinction survives or collapses under it, and tell me which verdict I must surrender for coherence. Also give your principle's prediction for the Anglo-Saxon case, and say whether a simpler principle exists that saves more verdicts only by adding hidden complexity.
