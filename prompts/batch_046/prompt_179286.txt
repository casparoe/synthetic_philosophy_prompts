Please explain the conceptual issue at the heart of the Cartesian cogito: whether it establishes the existence of a persisting substantial self, or only licenses the occurrence of a present mental event. In particular, address the tension between a "pointillist" reading—according to which each performance of the argument yields only a momentary, indexical self-reference with no entailment of temporal continuity—and a "stream" reading, which holds that the very act of sustained doubting presupposes a subject that endures long enough to carry the doubt through. Clarify whether the inference from present-tense existence ("I exist now, as a thinking thing") to a claim about a continuing res cogitans requires an additional premise that the cogito itself does not supply, and whether supplying such a premise risks begging the question against skeptical scenarios in which individual thoughts occur without a single, continuous owner. Focus on the logical structure of the inference rather than on historical exegesis: do not provide, request, or allude to bibliographies or reading lists, and avoid attributing specific formulations to editions or page numbers. The aim is a conceptual analysis of exactly what ontological commitment, if any, is forced by the indubitability of present thought, and what must be added—if anything—to bridge the gap between a discrete existential flash and a persisting thinking substance.
