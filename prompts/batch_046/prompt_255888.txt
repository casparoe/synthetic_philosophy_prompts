I'm going through my aunt Hillary's old notebooks — she was a musicologist who got weirdly obsessed with ants in her last years — and she kept sketching out this thing she called the Ant Fugue. Her idea was that a colony isn't just individual bugs running local rules, but more like a Bach fugue where each ant is its own independent voice and the actual melody only exists at the level of the whole colony. She took that seriously and argued that the colony, not the ants, is the real bearer of beliefs and intentions. I mentioned it in a philosophy reading group and now we're stuck in exactly the loop I see in the literature: one side says collective cognition needs intergrated information or some kind of phenomenal unity, the other side says that's just brain-chauvinism and functional organization is enough. Nobody ever changes their mind, and I can't tell if we're arguing about ants, about the word 'mind,' or about whether consciousness is required for cognition at all.

My hunch is that it's partly verbal — 'mind' is being used for 'functional information processor' on one side and 'subject of experience' on the other — but I also think there might be a hidden empirical disagreement about whether ant colonies actually have the kind of integration that matters, except we can't check it without begging the question. So I'm asking for a real diagnosis, not a survey: is this a substantive disagreement, two different questions under one label, or just an ill-posed problem? And what, if anything, could break the stalemate — a formal result on distributed computation, a conceptual distinction that separates the two senses, or just admitting that the Ant Fugue is only a metaphor? Or should I expect this debate to stay exactly where it is, with everyone repeating the same moves forever?
