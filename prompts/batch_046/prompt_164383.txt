I am a novelist developing a long-form speculative work about governance protocols between human institutions and superintelligent advisory systems, and I have been invited to submit a two-page research proposal for a one-year interdisciplinary fellowship in the philosophy of technology. The conceptual problem I wish to frame is this: if a superintelligent system can construct arguments of such rhetorical precision and emotional calibration that human interlocutors are persuaded before critical scrutiny can be exercised, what normative architecture should govern its mode of address? Existing approaches divide sharply between transparency mandates—requiring disclosure of artificial origin and reasoning steps—and capability restrictions that limit linguistic sophistication, yet both treat persuasiveness as an intrinsic property of the message rather than as a relational dynamic shaped by the listener's epistemic vulnerability and the interaction's institutional context. I want to argue that we need a procedural framework—tentatively named an "epistemic friction protocol"—that does not merely restrict what the system can say, but structures how it must invite disagreement, surface its own uncertainty, and calibrate rhetorical force against the listener's demonstrated capacity for resistance.

The proposal should state the precise research question—under what conditions does calibrated persuasive speech by a superintelligent agent become epistemically coercive rather than legitimately clarifying?—and explain why transparency and restriction models leave this relational dimension unresolved. The method should be philosophical rather than empirical: drawing analogies from clinical informed-consent procedures and from the ethics of persuasive-technology design, I would construct a tiered taxonomy distinguishing informational, deliberative, and affective modes of AI address, specifying for each tier the conditions under which persuasion crosses into manipulation. I need the proposal to specify what success looks like—a workable decision architecture adaptable by policy bodies without collapsing into either an unworkable ban on all sophisticated communication or a vacuous appeal to benevolent intent—and to identify explicit failure conditions, such as an inability to distinguish clarification from coercion under conditions of extreme cognitive asymmetry. The scope must remain realistic for twelve months of conceptual work by a single researcher, excluding empirical testing and bibliographic compilation, which I will supply myself; please refer to relevant positions and arguments by name where needed, but do not include citations, a literature review, or a reading list.
