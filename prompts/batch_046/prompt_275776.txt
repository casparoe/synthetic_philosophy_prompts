I am working through a long-standing puzzle in population ethics—the procreation asymmetry—and I have the sense that the literature has reached a kind of stalemate that nobody quite admits. The standard formulation, roughly, is that there is a strong moral reason not to bring into existence a person who would suffer, but no corresponding reason to bring into existence a person who would be happy, merely for the sake of that happiness. Defenders tend to ground the asymmetry in a person-affecting constraint: harming requires an identifiable victim, whereas failing to confer a benefit does not wrong anyone who never exists. Critics respond that this treats non-existence as a morally privileged baseline in an ad hoc way, and that if we take impersonal value seriously, happiness should generate reasons symmetrically with suffering, only with opposite valence. Yet after decades of exchange, both camps seem to talk past each other: one side insists the asymmetry tracks a deep feature of moral reasons about particular acts, the other insists it collapses under pressure from consistency and axiological symmetry. I am not asking you to adjudicate which side is right. Rather, I want a diagnosis of why this particular debate has stalled. Is it a substantive disagreement about the nature of moral reasons—person-affecting versus impersonal—masked by shared vocabulary? Is it a verbal dispute about what counts as a “reason” or a “harm”? Or are the two sides actually answering different questions, one about the ethics of particular reproductive choices and the other about the axiological structure of possible worlds?

Some defenders of the asymmetry seem to treat both omissions as equally permissible, while critics treat both omissions as equally wrong (or both as neutral). Does this modal framing reveal that the disagreement is really about whether the baseline for comparison should be indexed to specific possible individuals or to generic welfare levels across worlds?
