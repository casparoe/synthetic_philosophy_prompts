In a budget debate at my school, I argued that we should directly compare funding an expanded mental-health counseling center against upgrading the robotics lab, using student flourishing as a shared benchmark. A classmate shut this down by saying, “You can’t put a price on well-being—comparing counseling hours to lab equipment is comparing apples and oranges; the values are incommensurable.” I think this slogan is concealing several different claims, but I cannot tell which ones are serious. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that “you can’t put a price on everything” or “these are apples and oranges” could express here—such as the absence of a common cardinal scale, the failure of ordinal ranking alone, lexical priority of one value over another, the irreducibility of plural goods, the inappropriateness of market framing, or the idea that practical reason must halt before hard choices? For each reading, please say whether it is trivially true, substantively defensible, or false; and indicate which versions actually justify refusing to compare the two budget items, versus which versions merely reject one specific comparison method while still permitting decision-making through other reasoning. I am especially interested in whether any defensible reading blocks practical choice altogether, or whether it only demands a different form of judgment about genuinely hard choices between incommensurable goods.
