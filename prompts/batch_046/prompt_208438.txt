A colleague summarized the exchange between Avishai Margalit and Philip Rieff on collective memory and forgetting like this. Margalit, she said, holds that a moral community owes an unconditional duty of remembrance to victims of injustice committed in its name, because shared memory is the constitutive bond of collective moral identity; to forget is therefore not mere neglect but a kind of collective self-deception that dissolves moral personhood. Rieff responds that this moralizes memory into a therapeutic burden, and that functioning cultures actually depend on structured forgetting—what she terms "symbolic amnesia"—to prevent past trauma from colonizing present moral authority. She then claims that the core disagreement is not ethical but jurisdictional: Margalit defends mnemonic sovereignty for victims, treating the collective archive as an inescapable epistemic debt to the dead, whereas Rieff defends mnemonic sovereignty for the living, treating the past as sovereign property the community may edit to preserve its authority. She concludes that both thinkers are therefore arguing about who holds jurisdiction over the past rather than about the value of memory itself.

Is this characterization fair? Please examine it sentence by sentence: where does it accurately reflect Margalit's distinction between shared and divided memory and his account of forgiveness; where does it misrepresent Rieff's critique of therapeutic culture and his understanding of authority and sacred order; whether "symbolic amnesia" and "mnemonic sovereignty" are concepts either thinker actually employs or are imported framing devices; and whether reducing their dispute to a jurisdictional conflict over archival sovereignty distorts the actual moral and cultural stakes. In particular, assess whether her claim that the disagreement "is not ethical but jurisdictional" survives correction of the earlier misrepresentations.
