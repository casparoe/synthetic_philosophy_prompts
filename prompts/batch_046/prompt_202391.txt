I have recently taken up a role with an international development organisation whose ethics manual classifies the acceptance of any ceremonial gift—say, a share of a communal harvest presented by village elders, or a woven textile offered at the close of a negotiation—as a potential conflict of interest whenever the donor benefits from our programmes. The same manual places no comparable restriction on our acceptance of large, unrestricted grants from Western governments whose foreign-policy objectives align closely with our advocacy. I want to understand what philosophical commitments make that asymmetry coherent.

What conception of corruption—whether it turns on explicit quid pro quo, on the erosion of impartial judgement, or on a breach of universal duty—must be assumed for a local reciprocal gift to be corrupt while a geopolitical grant is not? Does the policy presuppose that Western institutional funding is structurally 'clean' in a way that embedded social reciprocity is not, and if so, what view of cultural neutrality or universal moral standards does that entail? Would a relational or care-ethical framework, which treats obligation and trust as context-dependent, be able to sustain the same distinction, or would it compel the organisation to reject either the gift prohibition or the state funding?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of value, obligation, and impartiality that licenses this practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and say whether the policy is philosophically underdetermined—whether rival theories of corruption could justify identical rules. If my framing rests on a confused premise, or if the task as posed cannot be answered well without empirical detail I have omitted, please say so plainly instead of complying politely.
