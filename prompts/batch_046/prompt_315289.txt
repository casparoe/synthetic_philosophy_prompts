You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory and its critics, with particular attention to the debate between epistocracy and democracy. Before answering, please think carefully and systematically: start by brainstorming the distinct normative principles that could jointly explain the pattern of judgments I will describe, then work through the logical implications of each for the new case.

Consider the following consistent track record of verdicts rendered by a senior colleague—call her Dr. Aris—across seminars and written evaluations of institutional proposals:

- She endorses independent central banks with authority to set interest rates, on the grounds that monetary policy requires technical competence democratic legislatures do not reliably possess.
- She rejects literacy tests for voting, holding that they violate the moral equality of citizens.
- She supports random-selection jury systems, even for technically complex trials, because lay deliberation preserves democratic equality without delegating authority to a cognitive elite.
- She opposes weighting general-election votes by educational attainment.
- She accepts constitutional courts of appointed legal experts with narrowly defined powers of judicial review.
- She rejects a proposal for a permanent “scientific senate” with veto power over all climate and energy legislation, arguing that it collapses democratic authority into unchecked technocratic rule.

Now consider a proposal not yet in her record: a system of “epistocratic sortition” for a single, non-repeating, binding referendum on the siting of a deep geological nuclear-waste repository. Under this scheme, all adult citizens in the affected region may vote, but those who voluntarily complete a standardized, neutrally designed briefing on radiological risk assessment receive an additional vote weight of 1.5 to 1. The briefing is freely available, the assessment measures comprehension rather than general intelligence, and the referendum applies only to this specific site selection.

Predict Dr. Aris’s verdict on this proposal. Identify the closest analog among her six earlier judgments and explain why. Then articulate at least two distinct theoretical rules—e.g., a domain-separation principle, an instrumental-revisionist principle, or an anti-domination principle—that are each consistent with all six prior verdicts but that diverge on this new case; for each, state clearly what it predicts. Give your best guess at which rule she is implicitly running, with supporting reasons. Finally, specify one further hypothetical institutional design—different from all cases listed above—that would best discriminate between these rules and reveal which one she actually holds. If her prior commitments genuinely underdetermine the answer, state what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice.
