I've been thinking lately about those proposals arguing that biotechnology—gene editing, neural rewiring, something like that—should eventually eliminate suffering altogether, not just treat it when it shows up. I came across a phrase, "the hedonistic imperative," describing the view that once we can engineer away grief, anxiety, and physical pain, we might actually be obligated to do so. The more I turn it over, the more it sounds like a cheerful startup brochure waiting to be written: a clinical trial that promises a flat emotional baseline with zero negative valence, while the fine print quietly lists everything that might disappear along with suffering. I'd like you to write a satirical parody of an informed-consent packet and patient brochure for a fictional protocol called Project Baseline Zero. Use the deadpan, professionally formatted voice of a biotech company—numbered sections, bullet points, reassuring clinical language—like something a person would actually sign in a doctor's office. The satire should treat the real philosophical objections as routine disclosure risks: that parents can enroll fetuses without the child's consent; that suppressing distress signals also suppresses the motivation to help strangers; that the treatment may create a psychological discontinuity severe enough to make post-treatment identity a legal dispute over inheritance and liability; and that joy, artistic creation, and moral urgency may depend on emotional tension that a permanently flat baseline cannot sustain. Make it readable and funny to someone without a philosophy background, but precise enough that someone familiar with negative utilitarianism and the abolitionist project recognizes the actual arguments being exaggerated rather than invented. Around 700 words. Please write the answer in American English.
