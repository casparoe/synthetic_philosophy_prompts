The debate over whether genocide should be defined according to Raphael Lemkin's original broad conception—which included the destruction of a group's culture, language, and social institutions through non-physical means—or according to the narrower physical-and-biological destruction standard adopted in the UN Genocide Convention has remained structurally unchanged since the mid-twentieth century. On one side, legal scholars and international prosecutors argue that expanding the category beyond killings, serious bodily harm, and conditions calculated to bring about physical destruction risks rendering genocide a vague moral accusation rather than a precisely chargeable crime, and they point to successful convictions in Rwanda and the former Yugoslavia as evidence that the narrower framework works. On the other side, moral philosophers, indigenous-rights advocates, and some historians contend that this narrowing was a politically motivated compromise—driven by state interests in shielding assimilationist and cultural-suppression policies from international scrutiny—and that it fails to capture the intent to destroy a group in its collective identity when that destruction proceeds through forced language loss, institutional dismantling, or systematic erasure of collective memory. Both sides regularly invoke the same canonical atrocities—the Holocaust, the Armenian case, the Rwandan genocide, and residential-school systems—to support opposite conclusions about definitional scope. Meanwhile, a newer conceptual pressure has emerged: distributed, state-adjacent algorithmic cultural erasure, in which automated content moderation, recommendation architectures, and digital archive policies systematically suppress a group's ritual transmission and linguistic continuity without any direct physical violence or explicit state order. Proponents of the broad definition treat this as a clear case of genocide by cultural means; defenders of the narrow definition treat it as, at worst, a human-rights violation or discrimination, but not genocide, precisely because no physical destruction is intended or executed. Despite decades of exchange, neither camp has revised its core position.

My hypothesis is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement about what destroys a group, nor a simple verbal dispute about the word genocide, but rather a case of two disciplines answering different questions under one heading: international criminal lawyers asking what evidentiary standard and jurisdictional trigger should govern a specific international crime, and moral-political theorists asking which collective harms warrant the strongest possible international condemnation and interventionist obligation. If that diagnosis is right, then adding more historical case studies will not resolve it, because the sides evaluate the same cases through different functional lenses—prosecutorial feasibility versus moral-taxonomic adequacy.
