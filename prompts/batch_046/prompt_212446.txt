As a physicist who spent three decades working on statistical mechanics before turning to political philosophy, I have been wondering whether representative democracy suffers from an institutional analogue of model overfitting. In scientific practice, we distrust predictions that are trained and validated on the same dataset; replication requires fresh, independent samples. Career politicians, on this view, function like an overfitted model—optimized over repeated election cycles to extract signals that please the same electorate, with little guarantee of generalization to long-term welfare. Sortition, by randomly drawing citizens into deliberative bodies, introduces fresh samples from the population distribution, reducing the selection biases that professional campaigning amplifies. Term limits operate analogously to forced cross-validation: they prevent any single strategic parameter set from dominating indefinitely. I am not claiming that ancient Athenian designers intended this logic; rather, I suggest that modern defenses of sortition and term limits gain conceptual coherence when framed as epistemic safeguards against the pathologies of professionalized politics—provided we can specify who belongs to the sampling frame without collapsing into mere demographic representation.

Please comment on this essay—especially on whether the statistical analogy illuminates or obscures the normative stakes, whether I have neglected serious objections to sortition concerning competence and democratic consent, and whether the notion of a sampling frame introduces philosophical difficulties about membership in the demos that I have left unresolved. I would also value suggestions for sharpening the conceptual link without importing technical mathematics that would alienate a philosophical reader.
