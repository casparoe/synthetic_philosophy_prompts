I constructed the following argument for a seminar on projectability, and I now think it overreaches in a way I cannot locate. (1) Goodman rejects "grue" as projectable because its extension is partitioned by an explicit temporal cutoff—green before time t, blue thereafter—where that cutoff is not grounded in any underlying physical property of the emeralds themselves. (2) Any predicate that partitions its extension by reference to an unobserved parameter whose value is fixed independently of the observed properties is unprojectable for exactly the same reason: projection requires that unobserved instances share the observed property in virtue of something causally or structurally continuous with what we have seen. (3) The predicate "believes that p" partitions mental states by reference to an unobserved parameter: the internal representational structure that distinguishes genuinely holding p from a merely behaviorally equivalent state of pretense or mimicry. Just as we do not observe whether an emerald lies past the temporal cutoff, we do not observe whether a speaker's internal architecture lies past the representational "cutoff" that separates belief from its behavioral simulacrum. (4) So if explicit temporal arbitrariness blocks projection for grue, representational arbitrariness blocks projection for belief-attribution in precisely the same way. (5) Therefore, if we apply Goodman's criterion consistently, we must treat other-minds reasoning as an ungrounded inductive leap, no more justified than projecting grue onto emeralds examined after tomorrow. (6) But other-minds reasoning is unavoidable in scientific practice, moral judgment, and linguistic interpretation. (7) Hence the criterion used to reject grue must be flawed, which means grue is actually projectable after all, and Goodman's riddle dissolves not through a solution but through a reductio that undermines all induction.

Where does this break? I want to keep (6) intact, and I would prefer not to abandon the claim that mental-state attribution genuinely involves hidden representational parameters. Which step should be rejected, why did it seem plausible, and what does giving it up cost for the standard responses to Goodman's problem—the entrenchment account, the causal-structural account, and the natural-kind account? Give me a committed answer: name the step, defend your choice with reasons rather than a survey of options, and state clearly what each of the three standard accounts must pay if your diagnosis is right.
