I work as a software engineer at a mid-sized technology firm, and I have been trying to make sense of a new internal policy governing AI-assisted development and intellectual property. The policy treats any code, design notes, or generated outputs stored in company-sanctioned cloud repositories—including logs from our internal coding assistant—as automatic work product belonging to the firm, on the explicit rationale that these external digital artifacts are functional extensions of the engineer's cognitive workflow. Yet the very same document contains a privacy clause that strictly protects anything retained only in an employee's biological memory: managers may not compel disclosure of unrecorded reasoning, mental models, or internal problem-solving strategies. I find the pairing puzzling. For ownership, the boundary of the self seems to reach out into servers and notebooks; for privacy, it suddenly contracts to the skull.

What philosophical account of mind, personal identity, or knowledge does this institutional split presuppose? Must it be read as an implicit endorsement of an extended-mind view for property attribution combined with an internalist, brain-bound view for cognitive privacy? Or is there a single, coherent framework—perhaps one centered on accessibility, control, or institutional tractability—that can license both halves without treating the distinction as metaphysically deep? I am not looking for legal advice; I want to know whether this practice is philosophically consistent, or whether it quietly relies on incompatible theories about where thinking ends and the person begins.
