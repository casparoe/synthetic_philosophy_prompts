You are a political philosopher specializing in normative theories of secession and self-determination, with particular attention to how institutional-capacity conditions scale. Here is an argument I have been turning over that seems to prove far more than I want to accept.

(1) A region has a primary right to secede if it possesses (a) a stable democratic will expressed through legitimate institutions, and (b) the administrative and economic capacity to govern itself as an independent state.

(2) Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec each meet conditions (a) and (b) with respect to the states they currently inhabit.

(3) If (1) is correct, then for any sub-region R' within R that also meets (a) and (b), R' has a primary right to secede from R.

(4) The capacity conditions in (b) are relational, not absolute: what counts as sufficient tax infrastructure, legal administration, and democratic mandate scales with the size and scope of the unit in question. Therefore, if Catalonia can govern itself, so can its metropolitan core, and within that, districts with robust local budgets and participatory assemblies can also meet (a) and (b).

(5) There is no non-arbitrary institutional floor that stops this recursion without begging the question—any proposed minimum population, territory, or GDP threshold either excludes legitimate cases like Quebec or is satisfied by ever-smaller nested units once capacity is understood relationally.

(6) So accepting (1) commits us to an infinite fragmentation of sovereignty, which is institutionally incoherent: there must be a smallest unit of sovereignty, yet (1)-(5) provide none.

(7) But infinite fragmentation is unacceptable.

(8) Therefore (1) is false, and there is no primary right to secession.

This conclusion strikes me as wrong—I think there probably is some form of secession right, at least for cases like these. So I want to reject one of the premises, and I would strongly prefer to save (1) rather than (7), even if saving (1) is costly.

Tell me which step is the weakest link, explain why it looked plausible, and then price the alternatives: what does a defender of (1) have to pay to reject that step?
