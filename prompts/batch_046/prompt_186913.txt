Write a dialogue between two philosophers of equal competence debating whether Samuel Scheffler's 'afterlife conjecture' genuinely explains why human extinction is catastrophic beyond the deaths and lost pleasures involved. The first philosopher should defend Scheffler's claim that our present projects draw meaning from an implicit expectation of collective human continuity—an 'afterlife' of humanity—so that extinction would retroactively impoverish our current commitments. The second should reject this, arguing that it improperly extends conditions of personal diachronic identity to an abstract collective, and that any remaining badness of extinction can be explained without invoking a metaphysically dubious transgenerational 'we'. Have them respond directly to one another's points: the defender should address the charge of illicit collective identity, while the critic should answer the claim that meaning-structures genuinely depend on anticipated future uptake rather than being merely instrumentally valuable. Include at least one exchange examining whether the argument holds if restricted to 'thin' social practices—such as scientific inquiry or artistic tradition—rather than the full human project, and whether that restriction saves it from metaphysical objection or weakens it too much to carry the moral weight Scheffler assigns. Please present the dialogue in British English. Finally, state clearly what argument or evidence would change your verdict on the dispute, and identify the specific point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
