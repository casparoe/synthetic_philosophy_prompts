I'm preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on the ontology of art, and I'd like to begin with a poem that puts the central tension in motion rather than resolving it. Over the term we've worked through the idea that in allographic arts—poetry, literature, music—a perfect copy seems to preserve everything aesthetically relevant, yet we still treat an original manuscript or autograph score as carrying a value that no facsimile possesses, even when every observable property is identical. I want you to write a pantoum of exactly six quatrains (twenty-four lines total, following the standard repetition scheme: lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next, with the first stanza's lines 1 and 3 returning in the final stanza's lines 2 and 4). The poem should be set in a specific, somewhat narrow scenario: an unpublished poem is discovered in the archive of a dead writer, printed on paper and in ink chemically identical to the poet's known materials, with text that scholars initially celebrate—only to learn it was produced decades later by a forger using recovered formulas. The repeating lines must be identical in wording each time they return, but their grammatical function or semantic weight should shift—so that a phrase like "the hand is absent" can mean, in one occurrence, that the forger's hand is missing from the record and, in another, that the author's living hand has been erased by time—so that the form itself enacts the instability between causal origin and present appearance. The poem should leave the problem open: its circular structure should feel like an argument that keeps returning to the same point without settling whether our attachment to the original is a rational response to an invisible relation or an unavoidable illusion. Do not name specific philosophers in the text, do not ask the reader to choose sides, and do not resolve the tension with a concluding moral about authenticity being merely sentimental; let the repetition do the philosophical work. Use American spelling and a relatively formal, restrained diction appropriate to a seminar opening.
