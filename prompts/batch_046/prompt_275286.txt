Having spent my career in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science and social epistemology, I am struck by how debates over "national character" echo the shift from essentialist typologies to population-level statistical thinking in the natural sciences. I do not want a bibliography or a historical survey; I want to know which conceptual open questions in this domain are genuinely upstream, and which are distractions for a scientifically literate non-specialist. Please consider three interrelated problems. First, is the incoherence of national-character claims best explained by high intra-group variance, by the arbitrariness of national boundaries, or by causal opacity between macro-cultural conditions and individual dispositions? Second, can a deflationary, distribution-based account of cross-national behavioral patterns avoid collapsing into either methodological individualism or an unreconstructed essentialism? Third, do answers to the first two questions carry real weight for practical issues—such as the methodology of comparative social science, or the design of culturally representative AI systems—or are these applications largely independent? Rank these lines of inquiry in order of priority for someone with scientific training but no formal background in philosophy, defending your ordering with explicit criteria: dependency on other results, tractability within a few years of self-directed study, and practical leverage. I am especially interested in whether there is an unacknowledged crux—perhaps a contested philosophy of statistical explanation—that both sides of the popular debate implicitly rely on but rarely state.
