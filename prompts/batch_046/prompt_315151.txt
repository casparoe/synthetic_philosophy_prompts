I've been trying to work out my own reading of Kant's Perpetual Peace, and I'm unsure whether these commitments form a stable package or whether I'm smuggling in contradictions. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) The six preliminary articles—no secret reservations for future war, no acquisition of states by inheritance or exchange, abolition of standing armies, no national debt contracted for external conflict, no interference in the constitution or government of another state, and no perfidious means of warfare—are purely negative, prudential conditions that any form of government, including absolute monarchy, can satisfy without constitutional reform.

(2) The first definitive article requires republican constitutions (representative government with separation of powers) because citizens who bear the costs of war will withhold consent; republicanism is therefore structurally necessary for peace, not merely morally preferable.

(3) The second definitive article establishes a federation of free states (a foedus pacificum, not a world republic or civitas gentium) in which sovereignty remains inalienable, but member states voluntarily submit disputes to arbitration.

(4) This federation must grow organically by example and can initially include non-republican states that commit to its rules, since preliminary article 5 forbids interference in internal constitutions and sovereignty cannot be coerced; republican reform therefore cannot be a condition of entry.

(5) Cosmopolitan right (Weltbürgerrecht) is strictly limited to conditions of communication and commerce: it gives strangers a right not to be treated with hostility upon arrival, but it imposes no positive duty of admission, settlement, or redistribution, and it binds individuals rather than states as corporate agents.

(6) Perpetual peace is an idea of reason (Idee der Vernunft) serving as a regulative ideal; its feasibility is not an empirical prediction about actual state behavior, so the failure of actual states to achieve it does not falsify the theory.

My worry is that (2) and (4) pull against each other. If republicanism is structurally necessary for peace, then admitting non-republican states into the federation undermines the guarantee. At the same time, (3) seems to involve voluntary arbitration that does not violate sovereignty, which might dissolve the tension with (4)—but does submitting disputes to an external body really avoid interference, or does it require surrendering some sovereign authority?

There is also a possible tension between (6) and (2): if perpetual peace is merely a regulative ideal with no empirical causal mechanism, then republicanism's structural disincentive to war seems to be doing causal work that the regulative framing denies. Is that a genuine contradiction, or does the idea of reason operate precisely through institutional mechanisms like republican consent?
