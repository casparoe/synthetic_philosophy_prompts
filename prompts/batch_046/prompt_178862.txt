In the governance committee of a cooperative housing association, two members—Nadia and Marcus—disagree about whether their colleague Clara may legitimately reprimand another resident, Jonah, for repeatedly taking shared laundry supplies without replenishing them. Clara herself used those supplies without contributing for nearly three months while recovering from surgery, though she had previously championed strict enforcement of the replenishment rule and resumed her advocacy immediately after her recovery. Nadia argues that Clara has forfeited her standing to blame Jonah: by benefiting from the very practice she now condemns, Clara reveals that her commitment to the rule is conditional on her own convenience, and allowing her to reprimand Jonah would make the committee complicit in a kind of moral theater. Marcus replies that this conflates hypocrisy with the loss of standing. He grants that Clara's conduct looks inconsistent, but insists that her prior use was excused by genuine medical need, which means she never violated the principle she endorsed—only its application to herself during an exceptional period. Therefore, Marcus claims, she is not a hypocrite in the relevant sense, and her current authority as a committee member who is now in full compliance gives her all the standing she needs.

Nadia is unconvinced. She introduces a distinction between principle-level and practice-level consistency: even if Clara's medical excuse saves her from violating the principle, she still enjoyed the practical benefits of the loophole she is now closing for others. In Nadia's view, standing to blame is partly a matter of not having extracted private advantage from a shared resource failure that one now treats as blameworthy in others; Clara's past exemption, however justified, puts her in the position of having exploited the very vulnerability in the system she now polices. Marcus counters that this makes standing implausibly fragile—if any past exemption or mistake permanently disables someone from enforcing a rule, then collective governance would be paralyzed, since nearly every administrator has at some point bent a rule they later uphold. He proposes instead that standing should be indexed primarily to one's present relational position and current compliance: Clara now contributes fully, Jonah does not, and the committee's authority derives from its present representational role rather than from each member's biographical purity.

They have asked me to referee their exchange, and I find myself genuinely torn. Could you adjudicate move by move? Specifically: is Nadia right that benefiting from a justified exception undermines one's standing to blame others for the unexcused version of the same act? Is Marcus correct that standing should depend primarily on present compliance and institutional role rather than on biographical consistency? And is there something both are missing—perhaps a distinction between the standing to blame as a private moral critic versus the standing to impose institutional sanctions? Please say who is right about each specific point, what each argument gets wrong or overlooks, and who is more right overall.
