Consider a university-affiliated Digital Epistemics Review Board that has issued binding verdicts on whether particular information architectures constitute harmful echo chambers, benign epistemic bubbles, or neither. Its published precedents are as follows.

First, it ruled that a social-media feed using pure engagement-maximization ranking—where users in a political cluster rarely encounter dissent—constitutes a harmful echo chamber and required algorithmic redesign, on the ground that the architecture actively amplifies homophily.

Second, it ruled that an invitation-only forum for quantum-computing researchers, which excludes laypersons but permits vigorous internal methodological dispute, is a benign epistemic bubble requiring no intervention, because exclusion tracks expertise rather than viewpoint.

Third, it ruled that a news-aggregator allowing users to manually select ideological source-labels—displaying all headlines without algorithmic filtering—is neither an echo chamber nor a problematic bubble, since transparency and direct user choice preserve epistemic agency.

Fourth, it ruled that a corporate messaging system that automatically suppresses messages endorsing a rival project-management framework by keyword—making suppression the default, though disablement is technically possible via buried settings—constitutes a harmful echo chamber, holding that an opt-out mechanism does not neutralize default viewpoint-based structural suppression.

The Board now faces a new proposal: a professional-networking platform open to all verified users. Its feed-ranking algorithm is opaque and trained on users' past endorsement patterns. The system does not remove, flag, or block dissenting professional opinions; instead, it assigns them visibility weights so low that they rarely appear in active feeds. There is no user-level opt-out. Once monthly, the platform sends a summary notification acknowledging the existence of opposing viewpoints, but these viewpoints are never inserted into the feed. The domain is general professional knowledge, not restricted by credential.

Write an analysis that identifies at least two plausible governing rules jointly consistent with the four precedents but diverging on this new case. For each rule, state what verdict it yields about the platform. Then recommend the decision that best coheres with the Board's own track record, identifying the closest analog precedent and explaining your reasoning. Be explicit about what further principle the Board would be adopting with either choice. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—different from the four above and from the current proposal—that would distinguish between the two rules you have articulated.
