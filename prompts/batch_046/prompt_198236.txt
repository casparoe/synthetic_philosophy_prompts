You are advising a small interdisciplinary group with three years and limited funding to investigate a single conceptual problem: whether habitat destruction, taken in aggregate across ecosystems, tends to reduce or increase wild animal suffering. The team includes ecologists fluent in population dynamics and trophic cascades, and philosophers comfortable with welfare aggregation but not with field ecology. They need a defensible research agenda, not a literature survey, and they need to know where limited effort actually moves the needle.

Identify the three or four open questions, methodological cruxes, or lines of conceptual modeling you would prioritize for this team, rank them, and defend the ordering. Your defense should specify the criteria—epistemic leverage over the central question, tractability without large-scale field campaigns, and how many downstream issues depend on the answer—and should note the trade-offs between these criteria. For each priority, describe what a useful partial result would look like within the three-year window, and identify the evidence or argument that would cause you to demote it.

In framing your priorities, take seriously two tensions that conventional conservation ethics often glosses over. First, the replacement problem: should welfare assessments be replacement-sensitive, counting only the suffering and wellbeing of currently existing individuals, or should they include the counterfactual welfare of potential future individuals that destroyed habitat prevents from being born? Second, the mortality-profile problem: habitat fragmentation and edge effects alter not just population size but the age-structure and cause-structure of mortality—specifically, whether predation and starvation fall more heavily on juveniles or adults—and different weighting of suffering across life stages can reverse the sign of the net welfare effect. Do not merely list both sides; identify which specific conceptual choice—how to aggregate across taxa with radically different nervous-system architectures, whether to treat ecosystem intactness as an independent good, or how to model trophic-cascade feedbacks on suffering at lower trophic levels—would actually change the conclusion about a given habitat loss.

Also address the distinction between the descriptive question (does this particular destruction reduce net suffering?) and the prescriptive question (should anyone actively destroy habitat?). The group needs to know whether resolving the first has direct policy implications or remains purely descriptive given institutional and moral constraints.

Conclude by stating the one objection or counter-argument—whether from conservation biology, from welfare economics, or from the philosophy of interspecies comparison—that you think this agenda is most vulnerable to, and how the team should prepare to meet it rather than avoid it.
