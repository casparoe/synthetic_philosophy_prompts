I'm writing from an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar in which we've been working through Dennett's account of the self as a center of narrative gravity—treating the self not as a Cartesian substance but as an abstract, functional center analogous to a physical center of gravity. I'd like to test how robust that view is by translating its core claims into the vocabulary of corporate legal theory, specifically the three competing accounts of corporate personality: the real-entity theory, the fiction or concession theory, and the nexus-of-contracts view. For each of Dennett's central theses—that the self is a theoretical posit rather than an observed entity, that it lacks spatial location and substantial boundaries, that it persists through narrative coherence rather than substance continuity, and that its reality is fully compatible with its being invented—indicate whether it maps cleanly onto one of these legal frameworks, whether it can only be expressed there with significant distortion, or whether it has no genuine counterpart at all. I am particularly interested in whether the legal distinction between natural persons and artificial persons survives this translation intact, or whether Dennett's framework forces us to treat that boundary as itself another invented narrative. Finally, report what the untranslatable residue suggests: does it reveal that legal theory is missing a functionalist conception of personhood, or does it show that Dennett's narrative view relies on metaphysical commitments—perhaps about explanation, ontology, or persistence conditions—that he never explicitly defends?
