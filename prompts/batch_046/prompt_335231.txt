In recent discussions of institutional design and digital contracts, performative utterances are often analogized to software function calls: uttering “I promise” or “I bet” is treated as invoking a social-state-transition routine, where felicity conditions correspond to preconditions, misfires correspond to runtime errors, and institutional authority corresponds to execution privileges within a defined system. How far does this analogy actually hold? Please provide an explicit correspondence table mapping Austin’s dimensions—locutionary, illocutionary, perlocutionary—onto the computational framework, then identify the precise points of disanalogy, particularly regarding ambiguity, revocability, and the absence of a centralized execution environment. Crucially, assess whether the analogy can accommodate what might be called “asynchronous performatives”: commitments whose illocutionary force is not immediately executed but is suspended until an external verification condition is met, a structure that has no direct analog in standard procedural programming. Does recognizing this category strengthen the analogy by expanding the computational model, or does it reveal that the analogy was only carrying the conclusion that social commitments are mechanically deterministic? Give an honest judgment on whether the function-call framework illuminates Austin’s taxonomy or quietly imports assumptions—discreteness, determinism, hierarchical authorization—that distort the phenomenology of social commitment.
