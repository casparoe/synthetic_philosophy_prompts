My history department has just asked me to redesign our final-year module so that the Anthropocene is treated as a historical period rather than an environmental epilogue, which means I must now teach seventeen-year-olds not only when it might have begun but who gets to decide that question and why it is not settled like the fall of Rome. I need a pedagogical plan for one seventy-five-minute session with students whose geology stops at documentary-level familiarity.

Lay out the dependency order: which conceptual distinction—between stratigraphic boundary and historical epoch—must come first, and how should I sequence the competing start markers (the Great Acceleration, the 1610 Orbis proposal, Ruddiman’s early-agriculture hypothesis) so that the disagreement reads as a problem of periodization authority rather than as a simple search for the worst pollution spike?

Identify the single carrying example or analogy that should do the work: it should make visible why a geological committee and a historian might disagree about the same events, and it should explain why I should not let the atomic bomb tests serve as the default mental image for the boundary.

Map the misconceptions I should expect—for instance, that the Anthropocene is already “official” in the same sense as the Pleistocene, or that picking a start date is just measuring damage—and specify the exact moment in the sequence where each should be interrupted.

Propose one check for understanding at the close that reveals whether students grasp that the “who decides” question is institutional and contested, not merely technical, and state openly what I should refuse to explain at this level and say so.

Do not provide a bibliography or cite specific committee reports; I need the architecture of the explanation, not sources.
