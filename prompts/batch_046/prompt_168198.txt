I have just finished a long and somewhat circular argument with a close friend about whether Dewey’s experimentalist conception of democracy can survive its own internal tensions. My friend kept collapsing Dewey into a generic epistemic democrat—someone who thinks voting is just a way to pool knowledge—while I kept trying to insist that Dewey’s view is stranger: democracy is supposed to be continuous, provisional social inquiry in which policies are treated like scientific hypotheses, yet democratic authority simultaneously requires binding commitment in a way that laboratory hypotheses do not. We went in circles because we had no shared map of where the positions actually diverge. I want you to build that map for me.

Please construct a branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with yes/no or either/or nodes—that sorts positions on whether democracy should be understood primarily as an institutionalized mode of collective experimental inquiry, in the specific Deweyan sense that democratic life extends the logic of problem-solving intelligence into social organization. The root commitment is that one accepts Dewey’s claim that democracy is more than a form of government and is essentially tied to inquiry; from there, split the space with four diagnostic branches. First, does democratic legitimacy depend on collective decisions tracking warranted assertibility, or is fair procedure sufficient regardless of epistemic quality? Second, does the experimental analogy apply mainly to policy outcomes—treated as revisable hypotheses tested by social consequences—or mainly to the institutional arrangements themselves, which are continuously reconstructed? Third, there is what I will call the asymmetry problem: democratic decisions bind citizens even when they fail, whereas failed scientific hypotheses are simply abandoned; is this asymmetry fatal to the analogy, manageable through mechanisms of revision, or actually a feature that distinguishes democratic inquiry from scientific inquiry? Fourth, must the inquiry be genuinely distributed across citizens, or can it be delegated to representative bodies without losing its democratic character?

For each leaf, provide the name of the corresponding position if one exists—such as Dewey’s own experimental democracy, deliberative epistemic democracy, procedural liberalism, epistocracy, or an unnamed hybrid—and one sentence stating its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. Where a logical combination of answers produces a view nobody appears to defend, label it unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence (for instance, requiring both absolute binding irreversibility and pure hypothesis-testing), unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Please also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, especially whether Dewey’s own position collapses into a generic deliberative view on these particular branches. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want an instrument that classifies people by their commitments, not by their familiarity with the literature. Aim for roughly six to eight leaves, and phrase every node as a concrete claim about democratic practice or legitimacy that a person could agree or disagree with without already knowing which philosophical label it carries.
