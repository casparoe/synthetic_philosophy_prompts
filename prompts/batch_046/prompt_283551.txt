I am currently enrolled in an undergraduate seminar on comparative political theory, and I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in both course readings and contemporary debate that I cannot name precisely. Speakers invoke "Western values" as though they formed a coherent, stable bundle—typically including liberal democracy, gender equality, secular governance, and freedom of expression. Yet when a challenger points out that one of these commitments is weakly institutionalized or actively contradicted within particular Western societies—say, by state-sponsored religious education in parts of Europe, or by hate-speech restrictions that curtail expression—the speaker rarely abandons the bundle. Instead, they treat the challenged commitment as peripheral to the "true" core and substitute another value from the cluster—perhaps the rule of law or individual autonomy—while continuing to appeal to "Western values" as the unproblematic justification for the original position. This is not quite the motte-and-bailey fallacy, since there is no clearly defensible inner keep and an indefensible outer wall; all members seem equally contestable, and the speaker shifts among them defensively rather than retreating to one fixed core. Nor is it simple equivocation, because the values genuinely co-occur historically and are not merely homonyms. Is there an established concept in political rhetoric or conceptual analysis for this specific maneuver—defensive cluster-shifting within a contested normative category—and if not, how should it be distinguished from family-resemblance arguments and from strategic ambiguity? More broadly, does this pattern suggest that "Western values" functions less as a descriptive category and more as a movable rhetorical anchor, and what would that imply for whether the category is coherent at all?
