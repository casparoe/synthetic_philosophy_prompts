Construct a rigorous philosophical argument defending the following synthetic claim about G. E. Moore's proof of an external world: the proof is dialectically effective only if reconstructed as a performative demonstration that relies on a direct-reference account of demonstrative identification—an account conceptually unavailable to Moore himself—while on its own explicit logical structure it commits a circularity that cannot be dissolved by appealing to comparative reliability judgments between perceptual error and global skeptical scenarios. Specifically, argue that (1) treating the presentation of one's own hand as a primitive epistemic ground requires a semantic framework in which demonstratives directly refer to external objects without descriptive mediation, thereby presupposing rather than establishing the existence of an external world; (2) Moore's attempt to frame the argument as an inference from premises more certain than skeptical hypotheses fails because the assignment of prior probabilities to skeptical scenarios already depends on the very perceptual reliability that is in question; and (3) if we instead interpret the proof as a non-inferential, performative act that forces the skeptic into a pragmatic contradiction, we gain circularity-avoidance at the cost of dialectical impotence, since the skeptic can consistently deny that perceptual experience carries primitive epistemic weight without self-contradictory behavior. Address the common-sense defense—namely, that Moore is merely reminding us that we know more about our hands than about skeptical hypotheses—and explain why this defense collapses into either an unargued intuitionism or a hidden reliance on the direct-reference semantics mentioned in (1). Conclude by assessing whether any reconstruction can preserve both the non-circularity and the anti-skeptical force of Moore's move, or whether the proof is best understood as a rhetorical boundary-marker between common-sense epistemology and philosophical skepticism rather than as a genuine argument.
