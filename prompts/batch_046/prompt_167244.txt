I am drafting a one-year research fellowship proposal in moral and political philosophy, focused on the tension between collective remembrance and deliberate forgetting that runs through Margalit's work on the ethics of memory and Rieff's defense of strategic forgetting. My half-formed but guiding idea is that both thinkers treat remembrance and forgetting as opposing moral postures—solidarity with victims versus release from the past—without confronting what I want to call mnemonic distributivism: the problem that commemorative attention is a scarce social resource, and that obligations to remember one wrong may compete not only with the goods of forgetting but with equally valid obligations to remember other wrongs across generations and groups. I need the proposal to state the central question with precision: can there be a principled framework for allocating mnemonic labor—through monuments, curricula, ritual, and public narrative—such that remembering some injustices does not unjustly crowd out others, and such that deliberate forgetting can be justified not as denial or forgiveness but as a legitimate redistribution of collective attention? Do not include citations, bibliographies, or literature reviews by reference; I will supply those myself.
