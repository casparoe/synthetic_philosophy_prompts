I've recently been asked to help design a three-year interdisciplinary research program that sits at the intersection of earth-system science, environmental history, and climate governance, and a framing dispute keeps surfacing: should we organize the initiative around the Anthropocene as a formal geological epoch—subject to the authority of stratigraphic bodies and defined by a globally correlatable boundary—or as a historical period whose start is negotiated through socio-ecological transformation rather than discovered in rock layers? The distinction is not merely semantic for our partners: legal scholars in the program want to know whether formal stratigraphic recognition could carry weight in climate-liability timelines, while historians are debating whether 1945, 1610, or some other marker should function as a normative turning point rather than just a descriptive one. Because my role is to advise on where limited conceptual and empirical resources should go first, I need a defended prioritization, not a literature survey.

Which open questions in this space deserve the most attention and resources, and in what dependency order? Specifically, I want to understand whether resolving the institutional-authority question—who gets to decide whether the Anthropocene is formally recognized and where its boundary is placed—is genuinely upstream of the normative and legal work on intergenerational justice and historical periodization, or whether those lines can proceed independently. How should one weigh tractability (the stratigraphic boundary debate may be closer to institutional closure, while the normative implications resist expert settlement) against downstream impact for policy and historiography? And which inquiries are overrated—perhaps the search for a single global radiometric marker, or fine-grained debates about precise dating—because settling them would not substantially change governance frameworks or historical method? If you were allocating a small team's effort across conceptual clarification of epoch versus period status, the design of decision-making authority, and the normative implications for climate liability and environmental history, how would you rank them, by what explicit criteria, and what evidence over the next couple of years would cause you to reorder the agenda?
