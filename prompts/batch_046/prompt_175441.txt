After a lengthy and rather circular argument with a close friend about whether a life of perfect simulated pleasure could be as good as one of genuine accomplishment, I want to press the point more systematically. Please rank the following four modifications of the classic experience-machine scenario—each defined precisely below—according to how damaging each is to a purely hedonistic theory of well-being: (A) the baseline case of permanent, irreversible entry with complete pre-entry amnesia and no causal connection to the external world; (B) a variant that permits a brief, scheduled 'reality audit' every year, during which the subject sees their actual physical state but cannot exit; (C) a variant that delivers only those pleasures that correspond to achievements the subject genuinely desired before entering, though those achievements remain causally inert outside the simulation; and (D) a variant allowing voluntary exit at any time, but with the penalty that every exited year permanently deletes an equivalent year of simulated achievement-memory. Then, separately, rank three broad theories of well-being—hedonism, the informed-desire account, and an objective-list account—by their overall robustness across all four variants, assigning each a tier (high, moderate, low resilience) and explaining in one or two sentences why it lands there. I am not asking for citations or a literature review; I want the reasoning to turn entirely on the internal structure of the machines and the conceptual commitments of each theory.
