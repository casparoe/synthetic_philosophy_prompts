I have been thinking about weakness of will — the way people do things they genuinely do not want to do, such as breaking a diet for a small immediate pleasure despite knowing the long-term cost. Some say the problem is a failure of reasoning; others say it is hyperbolic discounting, where we treat the present as disproportionately valuable. I am trying to work out which explanation is closer to the truth, particularly in cases where the immediate gain is trivial, the future loss is large, and the "reward" we sacrifice is not money or pleasure but the sense of being someone who follows through.

Please give me your credence — a specific number between 0 and 1 — that hyperbolic discounting alone (with no failure of practical reasoning) is the primary cause of ordinary weakness of will in adults who correctly understand their own preferences. Decompose the reasoning into at least these parts: (a) the likelihood that human decision-making actually employs hyperbolic rather than exponential discounting for identity-related goals; (b) the likelihood that a future self-concept can function as a discountable reward in the same way as a tangible good; (c) the likelihood that reasoning remains fully intact in these cases rather than being distorted by hidden rationalisation; and (d) the strength of the empirical evidence for separating these mechanisms in everyday behaviour. Give each part a rough numerical value with one line of justification, show how they combine into your overall figure, and identify the part you are least confident about, together with what kind of evidence or argument would most shift your view.
