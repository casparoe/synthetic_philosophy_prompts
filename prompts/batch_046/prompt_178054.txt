I'm drafting a philosophical novel in which a lecturer, Dr. Elara Voss, delivers a reductio against republican liberty by comparing two hypothetical societies—Anarchos, where perfect negative and positive liberty coincide with perfect material equality and no institutional hierarchy, and Demarchia, a constitutional democracy with robust checks and democratic accountability. She lays out the following chain: (1) Republican liberty is freedom from arbitrary domination, defined as being subject to another agent's will in a manner that is not answerable to oneself. (2) In Anarchos, there are no laws, authorities, or institutional hierarchies, yet every individual retains a physical and social capacity to obstruct any other person's projects without appeal or review; this vulnerability is structural and symmetric, so each inhabitant is effectively subject to the arbitrary will of all peers. (3) Therefore republican liberty is absent in Anarchos, despite the complete absence of actual interference and the presence of full resource equality. (4) In Demarchia, the state holds coercive power, but this power is constrained by democratic contestation, separation of powers, and judicial review, rendering it non-arbitrary by republican standards. (5) So republican theory must classify Demarchia as free and Anarchos as unfree. (6) But this implies that introducing hierarchical state institutions increases freedom relative to a society of pure equality and non-interference, which seems to prove too much—if republican liberty requires submission to authority merely to avoid peer-to-peer vulnerability, it collapses into a paradoxical endorsement of domination. (7) Hence either republican liberty is reducible to negative or positive liberty, or its definition of "arbitrary" must be restricted so narrowly that it can no longer apply to distributed, symmetric vulnerability.

I find this suspicious precisely because step (2) feels like a genuine conceptual discovery—this idea of distributed, symmetric domination—yet I worry it smuggles in an asymmetry that republican theory explicitly rejects. I would prefer not to abandon step (2) if I can help it, though I am open to learning that the flaw lies elsewhere.
