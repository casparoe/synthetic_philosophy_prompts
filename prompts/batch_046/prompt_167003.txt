I was discussing with a friend who works in metrology about the 2019 redefinition of the kilogram, and it struck me as an oddly philosophical institutional change. For over a century, the unit of mass was anchored to a single physical artifact—Le Grand K, the platinum-iridium cylinder kept in Sèvres—whose mass was stipulated to be exactly one kilogram by international agreement. In 2019, the General Conference on Weights and Measures replaced that definition with one derived from Planck's constant, fixing its value numerically and rendering the old artifact obsolete as a standard. I want to analyze this episode through the lens of the problem of universals.

Specifically: before the redefinition, was the kilogram a universal—some shared nature or essence that Le Grand K and other calibrated masses all instantiated—or was it a nominal kind sustained purely by collective agreement about that particular cylinder? After the shift to a constant-based definition, does the kilogram now refer to a real feature of nature (the quantum mechanical relation embodied in Planck's constant), or does it remain a conventional unit whose connection to nature is mediated by human choice of the constant's fixed value? Map the episode carefully onto the realist and nominalist positions: identify who or what plays the role of each side, what the evidence is (the observed drift of Le Grand K relative to its copies, the precision of quantum electromechanical balance measurements), and what the redefinition actually resolves versus what it leaves untouched. Then say where the universals debate falls silent here—does it help us decide whether measurement units are discovered or invented?—and what the case in turn shows about the debate: does it support a moderate realism about scientific kinds, or does it reveal that the problem of universals was never designed to adjudicate institutional revisions like this?
