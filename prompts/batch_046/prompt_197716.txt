Me and my roommate were up late arguing about whether some python script we wrote actually works for every input, not just the ones we tested. We both agree that finding a bug at input size 10 should definatly kill our confidence in the general claim, but then we started wondering about a hypothetical bug that only shows up at input size 10^12 -- like, you'd need to run the thing for years to see it. Should that possibility lower our belief right now, even though we can't check it? My roommate says yes, it's just like not knowing if there's a black swan out there. I feel like for logical or deterministic stuff it's different -- the bug being "far away" in computation-space makes it feel less threatening to the claim, not just less checked. I tried explaining this to my prof in a logic course last semester and she mentioned people study reasoning about math you haven't proven yet, but she didn't have a term for this specific thing about distance or complexity of counterexamples.

So: is there an established name for the idea that in deductive or algorithmic claims, a potential counterexample's weight in your belief depends on how complex or resource-intensive it is to exhibit? I don't mean just being lazy about computation -- I mean a principled difference in how near-failures and far-failures should update your credence. How is that different from ordinary statistical induction where every data point is supposed to count the same regardless of scale? And if there isn't a specific term, can we coin one with clear edges: does it apply only to deterministic processes, or also to probabilistic ones? Where does it sit relative to being computationally bounded versus actually treating counterexample "reachability" as part of the belief rule itself? I'm not asking for citations or a reading list, just whether the carving already exists and what distinguishes it from simpler explanations.
