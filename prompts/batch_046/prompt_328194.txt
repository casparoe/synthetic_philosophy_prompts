In a recent graduate seminar I convened on the moral psychology of democratic societies, my students and I spent considerable time contrasting the American Dream—understood not merely as economic aspiration but as a normative framework that celebrates visible, exceptional achievement—with two countervailing cultural phenomena: the Scandinavian Jantelagen, which informally penalizes those who distinguish themselves from the collective, and the Anglophone "tall poppy syndrome," in which prominent success frequently provokes resentment rather than admiration. Our discussion turned particularly acute when we considered whether these dynamics represent pathological deviations from meritocratic ideals or, conversely, necessary correctives to hierarchical arrogance.

Building upon that conversation, I want to pose a conceptual exercise that extends this tension into a domain Alexis de Tocqueville could not have anticipated but whose structural logic he might have recognized with remarkable clarity. Suppose Tocqueville were to observe the architecture of contemporary algorithmically mediated public spheres—specifically, a phenomenon we might term "algorithmic Jantelagen," wherein recommendation systems, optimizing for engagement equilibrium rather than meritocratic visibility, systematically suppress the reach of exceptionally prominent voices and elevate homogenized, median-affinity content. How would Tocqueville likely interpret this technologically administered leveling? Would he view it as a democratic safeguard against the resurgence of aristocratic distinction, or rather as a novel form of the mediocrity he warned could accompany democratic equality? In constructing your analysis, please avoid direct quotation from his writings and do not invoke specific legislative frameworks unknown in his era; instead, focus on the conceptual interplay between democratic ethos, visibility, and the social fate of exceptional merit.
