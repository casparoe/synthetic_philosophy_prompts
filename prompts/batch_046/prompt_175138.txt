As a doctoral student in philosophy of medicine and research ethics, I am cataloguing the consistency of a senior colleague's judgments about when deception vitiates informed consent. Her track record on four cases is as follows.

Case 1 (Therapeutic Concealment): In a psychiatric drug trial, participants are told they are comparing two approved antidepressants; in reality, one arm receives a placebo and the other an unapproved experimental compound. Physical risks and withdrawal rights are fully disclosed. She judges consent INVALID, holding that deception about the therapeutic nature of the intervention undermines autonomous authorization regardless of risk transparency.

Case 2 (Hypothesis Concealment): In a behavioral economics study, participants are told they are playing a resource-allocation game, and the mechanics are exactly as described; only the scientific hypothesis—that the study measures implicit bias patterns—is withheld. Risks are minimal and procedures transparent. She judges consent VALID, maintaining that withholding the research hypothesis does not impair the competence required to authorize participation.

Case 3 (Procedural Deception): In a social-psychology experiment, participants are correctly told that the study examines group decision-making under stress, but they are led to believe they interact with live peers via video when the peers are actually pre-recorded confederates. Risk is minimal and debriefing is immediate. She judges consent INVALID, arguing that deception about the procedural reality prevents participants from understanding what they are actually undergoing.

Case 4 (Layered Disclosure with Meta-Consent): In a neuroethics study, participants sign an acknowledgment stating that some task details will be concealed until debriefing, that the study involves cognitive evaluation under ambiguous conditions, and that they consent despite partial ignorance. The concealed element is subliminal priming with emotionally valenced stimuli. She judges consent VALID but CONDITIONAL, accepting that explicit meta-awareness of partial ignorance can preserve procedural legitimacy.

These four judgments appear jointly consistent with at least two governing rules. One rule—the Competence-Preservation Rule—holds that deception invalidates consent only when it directly undermines the specific cognitive or volitional competence required for authorization, such as understanding one's exposure to risk, one's social exposure, or the therapeutic status of an intervention; withholding a scientific hypothesis or concealing details within an acknowledged ambiguity does not impair that competence. Another rule—the Domain-Sensitive Materiality Rule—holds that permissibility depends on normative domain conventions: in clinical domains, therapeutic purpose is material and must be disclosed; in behavioral domains, hypotheses are immaterial but procedural realities are material; and in studies that take consent or decision-making as their direct object, any concealment affecting the consent environment is automatically material.

Now consider a new proposal. The study recruits competent adults from the general population for research described as testing a digital consent-interface for medical studies. Participants are told they will evaluate an AI-assisted language-simplification tool and that the study measures comprehension speed of synthetic risk disclosures. In reality, the study's purpose is to test whether embedding subtle normative framing within the AI-simplified language increases hypothetical enrollment rates for a high-risk trial; the risk disclosures they evaluate are synthetic and experimentally exaggerated; and the AI parser is calibrated to nudge agreement, though participants face no actual clinical risk. The deception therefore concerns both the study's true purpose and the procedural reality that the interface is an experimental manipulator rather than a neutral tool. Immediate debriefing is planned, the population is non-vulnerable, and direct risks are minimal, though professional reputation could be affected if the synthetic scenarios are later revealed.

If my colleague wishes to remain consistent with her track record, what should she decide about the validity of consent here? Which of the four precedents is the closest analog, and what principle would she be adopting with each possible choice? Furthermore, what single further case—one that does not involve AI mediation—would best distinguish whether she is running the Competence-Preservation Rule or the Domain-Sensitive Materiality Rule? I am less interested in a simple verdict than in identifying the candidate rules, stating what each predicts about this case, offering the best-guess decision with reasons for preferring it, and naming the distinguishing case that would adjudicate between them.
