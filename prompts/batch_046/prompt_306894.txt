I am preparing a seminar presentation for a philosophy of religion reading group comprising three analytic philosophers of religion sympathetic to divine command theory, two scholars of nineteenth-century Protestant theology who defend the standard knight-of-faith reading associated with the pseudonym Johannes de Silentio in Fear and Trembling, and one clinical ethicist familiar with moral injury but not with Kierkegaardian scholarship. The thesis I intend to defend is that the teleological suspension of the ethical should be interpreted not as a religious exemption from universal moral law, but as a structural precursor to contemporary clinical accounts of moral injury: Abraham is compelled by a higher-order duty to violate a core ethical commitment—his obligation to Isaac and to the ethical community—that the universal framework itself cannot recognise or justify. I further argue that Abraham's silence, and his inability to render his justification intelligible to Sarah, Isaac, or the ethical community, mirrors the isolating, incommunicable phenomenology of moral injury, and that what Kierkegaard terms the absurd is therefore not logical contradiction but the rupture between personal vocation and intersubjective ethical language.

Please prepare me for the objections I am likely to face, ordered by how damaging they are to the proposal's survival. For each objection, specify: (a) the precise form in which that subgroup will phrase it, using their characteristic vocabulary; (b) what I should concede upfront; (c) a concise reply suitable for delivery in roughly two minutes; and (d) an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Please also include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—namely, the charge that importing a post-Vietnam clinical concept into a mid-nineteenth-century Danish theological text is an indefensible anachronism. Write your answer in British English.
