I am preparing a graduate seminar on political philosophy and have been working through the following position on the phrase "Western values," which I hope to defend in a short essay. Rather than treating it as an ethnographic label for European or North American societies, I understand it as a normative cluster—what I call a procedural-individualist package—comprising three interlocking commitments: the moral priority of individual autonomy over ascriptive group identity; the institutional separation of coercive authority from comprehensive religious or metaphysical doctrines; and a reflexive universalism that presents these norms as globally applicable while simultaneously acknowledging historical failures to live up to them. I take this cluster to be coherent enough to function as a minimal framework for pluralistic governance, despite the well-documented internal diversity of societies conventionally called Western.

That said, I worry that the framing suffers from what I term normative ventriloquism—attributing contested commitments to an imagined collective voice—and that it may insulate itself through strategic ambiguity. I want to know precisely what would count against it. I am not asking for accusations of hypocrisy or lists of counterexamples; I want the conceptual distinctions, historical patterns, or structural arguments that should genuinely move a defender of this cluster. For instance, does the coherence depend on suppressing internal contradictions between universalist rhetoric and particularist policies in immigration or trade? Would evidence that highly egalitarian societies within this cluster exhibit more gendered career preferences—the gender-equality paradox—undermine the claim that these are culturally neutral procedural norms, or would defenders correctly reply that such patterns reflect freely formed preferences within a just framework? Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability by treating every failure as a deviation from the ideal rather than as a feature of the package, and if so, is that insulation a philosophical defect or a necessary feature of normative theory? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, indicate what defenders should themselves concede as counting against the view, and assess whether the cluster risks collapsing into a rival account—for example, a purely institutional-functional description with no normative content.
