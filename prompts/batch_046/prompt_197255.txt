I have been working through the literature on pain asymbolia—cases in which patients report intact nociceptive phenomenology, including sharpness, heat, and precise bodily localization, yet sincerely deny that anything is wrong, unpleasant, or worth avoiding, often due to parietal or insular disruption—and I keep running into an argument my study group reconstructed that seems to prove far more than it should. Here it is, with each step marked: (1) In pain asymbolia, subjects genuinely feel pain in the sensory sense but lack any aversive quality or withdrawal motivation, and their general cognitive and evaluative capacities remain intact except for the specific disconnect between nociceptive representation and affective response. (2) If the badness of pain were intrinsic to its phenomenal character, then anyone who undergoes that exact phenomenal character in a cognitively intact state should experience it as bad. (3) Therefore, the badness of pain is not part of its intrinsic phenomenal content; it is contributed by a separable evaluative mechanism that can be present or absent without altering the underlying sensation. (4) Any mechanism that is separable in this way can, in principle, be disabled, redirected, or cognitively overridden without changing the core sensory state. (5) By parity of reasoning, grief, nausea, dread, and other negative states also consist of a core phenomenal state plus an optional evaluative overlay. (6) Hence there are no intrinsically bad experiences at all; suffering is always contingent on an evaluation that could, in principle, be suspended. (7) If suffering is always suspendable in this manner, then moral obligations to prevent suffering are primarily obligations to restructure people's evaluations rather than to change their external circumstances, and victims of extreme conditions suffer partly because they fail to suspend the relevant evaluative layer. (8) That conclusion is morally unacceptable.

I want to hold on to (1) through (4) because they seem empirically supported and philosophically attractive: pain's badness really does look like an added affective layer rather than an inseparable feature of the sensation itself. At the same time, I want to preserve the idea that suffering carries genuine moral urgency that does not reduce to a failure of cognitive management. So I am torn between rejecting (4) or (5)—where there seems to be an unjustified modal leap from "separable in pathological cases" to "always suspendable"—and rejecting part of the move from (6) to (7), which might involve an equivocation about what "optional" means for normal human psychology versus abnormal disconnection. Which step should I actually give up? For each serious candidate—whether (3), (4), (5), or the inference to (7)—explain why that step looked plausible on its face, what exactly goes wrong if I reject it, and what theoretical commitments I take on regarding whether affective evaluation can be cleanly separated from phenomenal content, whether intrinsic badness requires inseparability or merely non-contingency in actual human functioning, and how much moral weight can rest on the distinction between altering an evaluation and altering a circumstance. I am not looking for a survey of the pain literature; I want a defended diagnosis of where the argument slips, priced in terms of what I must pay to keep the parts I care about.
