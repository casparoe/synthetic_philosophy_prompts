I'm putting together materials for a graduate reading group on macro-historical ontology, and our discussions have kept circling back to the same problem: people invoke cyclical or linear history as slogans without being able to say exactly which predictions or normative commitments distinguish them. To fix that, I want a conceptual sorting instrument—either a decision tree or a scored taxonomy—that forces a respondent to move from concrete commitments to a named position, rather than the other way around.

Please construct a tree that begins with an initial normative-empirical claim of your choosing—perhaps that a successor society can inherit and stabilize the institutional knowledge of a collapsed predecessor—and then branches through at least five diagnostic nodes. Each node should pose a specific counterfactual or scenario: for example, whether the decay of founding group solidarity necessarily dissolves administrative continuity, whether cultural exhaustion is biologically inevitable or only contingently triggered, or whether technological accumulation can proceed independently of the political cycle that produced it, yielding a hybrid layered chronology that I think is under-explored in the standard literature. The leaves must include the classic positions—Polybian anacyclosis, Khaldunian dynastic cycling, Spenglerian organic senescence, Toynbeean open-ended challenge-and-response with growth or breakdown, and at least one linear-progressive or linear-declinist view—plus any hybrid combinations that are logically coherent but rarely defended. For every branch combination that yields an unoccupied cell in the logical space, briefly diagnose whether the emptiness seems due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Crucially, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate on predictive grounds: for instance, Spengler's deterministic seasonal model and a pessimistic Toynbeean failure to respond creatively may both forecast Roman-style stagnation, and I want that overlap explicitly noted rather than papered over. Keep the audience at an advanced undergraduate or graduate level, phrase every question so it can be answered without knowing philosopher-labels in advance, and do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists; the goal is conceptual architecture, not scholarly verification.
