At a philosophy reading group, Maya and Leo argued about Bernard Williams’s integrity objection—specifically whether the “one thought too many” problem applies only to utilitarian reasoning or to any externally motivated revision of one’s ground projects.

Maya said the objection is broader: imagine a surgeon whose ground project is healing the innocent, who operates on a wounded enemy combatant because she recognizes a universal duty of care. Maya claimed this surgeon is not displaying integrity but substituting an external moral calculus for her own commitments, and that Williams’s framework lacks a principled line between legitimate project expansion and illegitimate external override, since any revision can be redescribed as “part of my project now.”

Leo replied that Williams allows ground projects to evolve, but the constraint is motivational, not narrative. The surgeon can come to see her project as healing suffering beings without losing integrity, provided the shift is internal—not adopted because a utilitarian calculation demands it. He noted that some interpreters read Williams as requiring diachronic narrative continuity, but he thinks that reading is unnecessary: what matters is that the revision is not instrumentally adopted to satisfy an external theory.

Maya countered that Leo’s “internal versus external motivation” distinction collapses in practice, since people rarely have transparent access to why they revise a project, and that without a structural criterion—like narrative coherence—integrity becomes unfalsifiable. She also observed that if Leo is right, a utilitarian could always claim her revision was internal, making the objection toothless.

Leo answered that unfalsifiability is not emptiness: the test is counterfactual—would she still hold the revised project if the external calculus disappeared?—and that this keeps the objection robust without requiring a formal theory of narrative identity.
