I'm writing a novel that moves between archaeological mystery and historical revisionism, and in my research I have developed strong gut reactions to various claims about the past — reactions I cannot quite justify to my own satisfaction. I would like you to help me articulate the principle behind them, and to tell me honestly which of my instincts must be abandoned for the sake of coherence. Here are my verdicts, grouped by domain.

In chronology and large-scale revision, I regard the modern occult version of Atlantis — a literal continent in the Atlantic destroyed in a single cataclysm near 9600 BCE, with its capital now resting near the Azores — as pseudohistory, despite Plato's geographical specificity. By contrast, I treat the interpretation of Plato's Atlantis as a philosophical allegory about imperial hubris, with no intended historical referent, as entirely legitimate scholarship. I place the ancient-astronaut explanation for the precision of the Great Pyramid or the Nazca lines — the idea that extraterrestrial engineers guided construction — firmly in pseudohistory. So too is Anatoly Fomenko's New Chronology: the claim that recorded history before roughly the eleventh century is largely a duplicated fabrication, that the Crusades and the fall of Constantinople are mirror-images of the same medieval events, and that Roman imperial chronology should be collapsed into a far narrower span.

Yet I do not dismiss all chronological or technological revision. I think Göbekli Tepe's monumental carved pillars, erected by pre-agricultural societies, legitimately challenge older evolutionary models that assumed complex social organization required farming first. I also accept that the Antikythera mechanism demonstrates a Hellenistic gear technology not recovered for over a millennium, and that this implies a lost tradition of practical engineering rather than a fantasy. Similarly, I am inclined to think the Trojan War reflects a real late-Bronze-Age conflict over a strategic Anatolian location, despite Homer's heavy mythologization — an extraction of historical kernel from narrative that I treat as sound method.

But these judgments sit uneasily with one another. Both Plato's Atlantis and Homer's Iliad are literary texts that embed specific geographic and political details; both are used by popular writers to argue for a hidden historical event behind the myth. I reject the extraction in one case and accept it in the other, and I want to know whether there is a defensible principle that justifies the split, or whether I am simply being inconsistent. The same tension appears with lost knowledge. I dismiss the idea that pyramid geometry encodes mathematical constants — approximations of pi or the golden ratio — that Egyptians could not have documented, seeing this as pseudohistorical numerology. At the same time, I regard the hypothesis that the Egyptians employed an internal-ramp construction technique now lost to us as a legitimate open question in engineering archaeology. Both invoke knowledge or techniques not attested in the surviving record; both fill a gap with an unknown. I keep returning to this pair and cannot settle whether my distinction is principled or merely aesthetic.
