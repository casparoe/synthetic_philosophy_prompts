After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about Bayesian confirmation theory, I find myself stuck on the old-evidence problem. My friend maintains that once a theory has been adjusted to accommodate a long-known fact, that fact cannot genuinely confirm the theory, since the prior probability already reflects it. I suggested that Bayesians can recover confirmation by asking what the prior would have been had the fact not yet been known, but my friend pushed further: suppose we formalize this through a principle of retroactive evidential weighting, where the confirmatory value of old evidence is scaled by the predictive divergence between our current model and the historical prior that preceded knowledge of that fact—so that old evidence confirms only to the degree that our present beliefs still "surprise" us relative to that earlier state. Write an essay assessing whether this retroactive weighting rule genuinely solves the old-evidence problem, merely disguises it, or introduces worse difficulties. Consider objections such as double-counting, the arbitrariness of selecting the baseline prior, and conflict with the likelihood principle. Do not end non-committally: defend a clear verdict on whether Bayesians should accept this rule.
