There's this weird institutional split in heritage work that I want you to dig into. In a lot of museums and land boards, if a community's collective memory conflicts with the archival record, the memory gets legal deference and shapes public display — but the record still drives excavation permits and academic funding. So the same site carries two authorized narratives, one for the community and one for scientists. What philosophical commitments about knowledge, authoroty, and group identity does that split actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory — maybe there's a hidden account of mnemonic sovereignty that makes it coherent? — and say whether the practice strictly needs it or is compatible with rival views. Then defend a verdict: is the split philosophically defensible, or does it collapse under pressure? If we took the collective-memory side seriously all the way, what would have to change in archeology or public history? Don't just list positions; argue it through and reach a conclusion. No citations needed, just work with the idea.
