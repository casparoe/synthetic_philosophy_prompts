As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I have been thinking about the various resolutions of Maxwell's demon that invoke information theory. Most discussions assume a discrete protocol: the demon measures individual molecules, stores results in binary form, and eventually erases those records, with Landauer's principle supplying the compensating entropy increase. But I keep encountering a structurally different case: a continuous feedback mechanism—say, a movable partition coupled to local kinetic energy through a smooth potential—that separates fast and slow molecules without ever digitizing data. There are no discrete bits acquired, stored, or erased; any "knowledge" is distributed continuously across the partition's position and momentum. Some treat this as merely the standard demon with analog memory, arguing that continuous degrees of freedom still carry information that must be accounted for. Others maintain that Landauer's principle concerns logical irreversibility in discrete computation, so it does not apply here, leaving the second law apparently violated.

Does this continuous, non-digitizing configuration have an established name—perhaps an "analog demon" or "continuous-feedback demon"—and is it genuinely a separate class, or just the discrete case redescribed? If the literature does not distinguish it, take a definite position: either show that it collapses into the standard information-theoretic resolution (specifying exactly how continuous variables are mapped to discrete bits for erasure accounting), or coin a precise term, define its boundary conditions (when does a mechanism count as continuous rather than discrete?), and explain why the Landauer-Bennett argument fails or succeeds for that class. Do not merely list possibilities; commit to one account and defend it with reasons.
