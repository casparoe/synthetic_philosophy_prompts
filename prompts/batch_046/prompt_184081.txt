If phenomenal consciousness is genuinely epiphenomenal—exerting no downward causal influence on neural dynamics, working memory, or articulatory behavior—then every first-person judgment about experience must be produced entirely by physical mechanisms. Now suppose we formalize the generation of such judgments through a phenomenal echo module: a representational subsystem that constructs reports such as "I am experiencing redness" solely by tracking structural invariants in the neural correlates of consciousness, with no causal interaction with the phenomenal properties themselves. Because this module yields stable, coherent, and predictively useful first-person reports without requiring consciousness to cause anything, it appears to supply exactly the missing mechanism that the paradox of phenomenal judgment demands: an account of how we can judge our experiences reliably even if those experiences are causally inert. So why is the paradox still treated as a distinctive problem for epiphenomenalism specifically, rather than as a solved engineering problem for any representational architecture that interposes a correlational proxy between physical substrate and self-report?
