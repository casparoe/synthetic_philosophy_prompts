Write an essay on whether cyclical theories of history can survive the structural discontinuities of modern global civilization. Focus on the cyclical frameworks associated with Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, and Arnold Toynbee. The central controversy is whether these models must be read as literal repetition of institutional life-cycles, or whether they can be reconstructed as "fractal recursion"—patterns that replicate across scales but accumulate transformative noise rather than returning to an identical state.

Weigh the arguments carefully. On one side, consider whether Ibn Khaldun's concept of asabiyyah can be mapped onto dispersed, digital-age communities that lack territorial coherence, and whether Toynbee's challenge-and-response model requires bounded civilizations that no longer exist in an era of global risk. On the other side, consider whether modern phenomena—climate migration, algorithmic governance, nuclear deterrence—are genuinely unprecedented breaks, or whether they are accelerated expressions of older dynamics (imperial overreach, technological assimilation, military equilibrium).

Reach a definite conclusion and justify it: either that cyclical history is now only a metaphor with no explanatory power, or that it survives in a modified, non-fatalistic form capable of accommodating structural noise. Do not conclude with a non-committal balance.

Organize your response in the following sections:
1. Introduction (about 150 words): frame the controversy and state your thesis.
2. The cyclical case reconstructed (about 300 words): explain how Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, and Toynbee understand recurrence, and how the fractal-recursion reinterpretation works.
3. The structural-break objection (about 300 words): present the strongest arguments that modern conditions destroy the conditions for cyclical repetition.
4. Evaluation and verdict (about 350 words): argue for your conclusion, addressing the asabiyyah and challenge-and-response problems specifically.
5. Conclusion (about 100 words): restate the verdict and its implications for historical explanation.
