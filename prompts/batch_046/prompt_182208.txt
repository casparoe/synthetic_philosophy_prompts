There's a peculiar legacy feature: an automated suppression system implemented in 2012 to stop a specific spam-bot pattern, which now occasionally catches legitimate technical discussions in obscure subfields. The engineering lead wants to eliminate it entirely and replace it with a modern ML-based classifier. From a direct cost-benefit view, the old mechanism is opaque, over-inclusive, and obsolete in its original purpose; removing it seems like unambiguous progress. But I keep returning to that old maxim about not tearing down a fence until you understand why it was erected—except that here I do understand the original reason, and it no longer applies.

Still, I'm wondering whether there's a deeper asymmetry: evolved institutional mechanisms might carry hidden functions—tacit coordination equilibria, deterrence structures, or epistemic filters—that aren't visible in the code or documentation, even when their explicit rationale is dead. The working group frames this as straightforward optimization: weigh false positives against compute overhead and delete if net-negative. I'm trying to articulate whether preserving the legacy system could avoid catastrophic unknown-unknown failure modes that aren't captured in our current model, without simply rationalizing status-quo bias. How should I evaluate whether my hesitation reflects a legitimate philosophical concern about the epistemic limits of institutional redesign, or just risk aversion in fancy dress? What's the precise logical structure of that concern, where does it genuinely apply, and at what point does it collapse into an unfalsifiable defense of whatever already exists?
