Following a rather long and heated argument with a close friend about whether semantic externalism genuinely undermines first-person authority, I am defending my position in a philosophy of language seminar next Tuesday. My thesis is that Putnam’s Twin Earth argument fails to establish broad semantic content because it illegitimately treats terms like “water” as pure natural-kind expressions divorced from their historical and contextual anchoring conditions. I propose a view I call indexical anchoring: narrow content fixes communicative availability, while wide content is activated only when a speaker’s causal-historical chain is epistemically transparent to them, which permits what I term local reference instability across contexts without collapsing into internalism.

The audience comprises four analytic philosophers—two sympathetic to two-dimensional semantics, one committed internalist about phenomenal concepts, and one agnostic who works on Burge-style social externalism—plus six graduate students well-versed in the standard Twin Earth and Burge literature but deeply sceptical of anything that sounds like disguised internalism. They care intensely about self-knowledge and will reject bare intuition pumps; they will press on whether my view makes reference too fragile, whether “local instability” is merely an ad hoc rescue, and whether I have smuggled in a hidden descriptivist criterion for transparency.
