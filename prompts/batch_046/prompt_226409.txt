I'm developing an essay that revisits Hofstadter's "Ant Fugue" dialogue—specifically the case of Aunt Hillary, the ant colony that behaves as if it were a single intelligent agent—to ask whether the colony can literally have a mind, not merely act like one. Rather than rehearsing the familiar emergence-versus-reduction debate, I want to press a distinction between systemic integration and phenomenal unity, and to argue that the colony achieves the former without the latter.

Here are the notes and central claims I plan to develop:

1. The standard intuition pump treats the colony's coordinated behavior—defense, foraging, repair—as evidence that a higher-order subject has emerged from lower-order non-subjects. I accept that the colony exhibits functional coherence: it maintains homeostasis, responds adaptively to perturbation, and integrates information across its constituent ants.

2. However, I want to distinguish this functional coherence from the kind of unified phenomenal field that seems necessary for subjective experience or genuine intentionality. Drawing on collective-action theory (the aggregation problem identified by List and Pettit), I propose that Aunt Hillary achieves only "aggregative coordination" rather than "joint intentionality": the colony computes outcomes that no individual ant represents, but there is no single decision procedure or representational format shared across the system.

3. To sharpen this, I introduce the idea of "transduction failure" in distributed cognitive architectures: at each node (ant), sensory input is transformed into local, indexical triggers—primarily pheromone gradients—rather than being preserved in a propositional or globally accessible format. Because the representational content is not broadcast to subsystems in a shared medium, there is no "global workspace" in which information becomes available for system-wide consumption. The colony processes, but nothing within it is in a position to know what is being processed.

4. Consequently, I argue that Aunt Hillary may qualify as a mind under functionalist or predictive-processing frameworks (it anticipates change and minimizes surprise), but not under frameworks that require unified access, phenomenal binding, or joint commitment. The burden, then, falls on the emergentist to show that integration reaches a threshold where fragmentation is overcome—not merely that the parts are well-coordinated.

5. A possible objection is that denying phenomenal unity to the colony begs the question against panpsychist or strongly emergentist positions. My reply is that the argument is not about consciousness per se, but about the conditions for ascribing a single subject of experience: without a mechanism that resolves transduction failure into shared representational access, we have many coupled processors, not one mind.

I'd welcome your comments on these notes—particularly on whether the distinction between systemic integration and phenomenal unity holds up, whether "transduction failure" is a coherent application to biological distributed systems or a category error imported from engineering, and whether I have missed a major line of objection (for instance, from enactivist or extended-mind perspectives). Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and do not present a contested view—such as the claim that functional coherence is insufficient for mindhood, or that global workspace conditions apply to non-human collectives—as if it were the consensus.
