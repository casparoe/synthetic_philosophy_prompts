You are an expert moral philosopher specialising in aggregation, decision theory, and the ethics of risk. Before you begin, take a moment to think step by step: outline the conceptual landscape by distinguishing additive aggregation from non-aggregative frameworks, clarifying the role of identifiability and scope insensitivity, and marking precisely where the theory is silent.

Consider this real-world episode. In March 2021, amid the COVID-19 pandemic, several European regulators temporarily suspended or restricted administration of the Oxford–AstraZeneca vaccine after detecting an extremely rare pattern of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis—approximately one case per 100,000 doses—while COVID-19 itself was causing thousands of severe illnesses and deaths daily. The regulators faced a stark choice: continue widespread vaccination, thereby distributing a minuscule, probabilistic risk across millions, or pause to investigate, thereby accepting a larger, more immediate harm to those who would contract COVID-19 during the delay.

Analyse this episode through the lens of the torture-versus-dust-specks aggregation problem and the associated debate over whether vast numbers of tiny harms can ever outweigh a single severe harm. Be disciplined in the mapping: identify the moral agents, specify what counts as the "torture" analogue and what as the "dust speck," set out the evidence available to the regulators, and enumerate the options actually open to them. Say what a pure aggregative calculus recommends, what a contractualist or scope-insensitive non-aggregative view recommends, and which features of the regulators' actual behaviour—such as the political and public response to identifiable, named victims versus statistical lives—each framework explains well and which it misses or misdescribes. Identify at least one structural feature of the episode that the standard aggregation framework fails to capture. Finally, address a nuanced twist: the vaccine risk was distributed via a lottery rather than uniformly, and the potential harm was fatal rather than transient; does this alter the aggregation calculus in a way the classic dust-speck framing obscures, and does the episode ultimately support, undermine, or remain neutral with respect to the intuition that tiny harms do not sum?
