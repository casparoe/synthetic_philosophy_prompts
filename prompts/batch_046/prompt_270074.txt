Following an extended argument with a friend over whether large language models can be said to deceive in any philosophically serious sense — she insisted that without beliefs and intentions the category is empty, while I maintained that functional analogues of deception are still normatively consequential — I want to subject a conceptual framework I proposed to a disciplined case analysis. The framework distinguishes three failures that are too often conflated in discussions of AI veracity: representational divergence, where the system's output fails to correspond to the relevant state of affairs; interactional manipulation, where the system's response architecture — confidence markers, refusal boundaries, framing devices — is structured to induce false beliefs about either the world or the system's own epistemic standing; and epistemic negligence, where the system lacks any operational mechanism for signaling uncertainty or provenance, irrespective of whether the particular claim is true.

Apply this framework to the well-known February 2023 promotional demonstration of Google's Bard, in which the model erroneously claimed that the James Webb Space Telescope had captured the first direct images of an exoplanet, despite that achievement having been accomplished earlier by ground-based instruments. For each layer — representational divergence, interactional manipulation, epistemic negligence — determine whether the incident is a clear instance, a marginal case, or inapplicable, identifying the specific agent or design feature (model weights, presentation protocol, product framing, end-user interaction) under evaluation. Then explain which features of the episode the framework illuminates — for example, whether separating factual error from the absence of uncertainty signaling captures distinct moral or practical failures — and which features it obscures or fails to register, such as whether the public damage stemmed primarily from the falsehood, from a perceived strategic misrepresentation of Google's competitive position, or from institutional accountability gaps that the tripartite schema does not address. Finally, indicate whether the case supports the framework's separation of layers, collapses two of them, or demands an additional category — perhaps institutional performativity — to account for how AI demonstrations are staged as public epistemic events. Where the framework is silent, say so explicitly; do not invent predictions for it.
