I've been running my grad seminar in philosphy of mind this semester and we hit a wall with pain asymbolia — those cases where patients report feeling pain but don't seem bothered, don't withdraw, don't ask for meds or recieve treatment. I ended up sketching a position I'm now half-committed to: the badness of pain isn't in the raw sensory quality or in the representational content (tissue damage, danger), but in what I'm calling an "aversive framing" — a second-order binding that weaves the sensation into a self-preservative narrative like "this is happening to me and needs to stop now." Asymbolics, on this view, aren't missing affect per se; they're missing the frame that turns a signal into a personal emergency. This lets me say pain isn't brutely bad in some pure phenomenal sense, and it explains why they don't seek relief without denying they "feel" it. But I'm worried I'm just relabeling the standard sensory/affective split with extra jargon, and I want to pressure-test it properly. So tell me what would count against this framing view — not just the stock reply that they still rate it high on a scale, but which other dissociative conditions, which neuroscience findings about ACC versus insular processing, and which formal results about representational content versus motivational force, would be fatal versus merely awkward? Would a defender have to concede that chronic pain patients with intact framing but blunted affect undermine the whole thing? And does this view genuinely risk something that plain desire-based accounts don't, or is it too well-insulated — and if it's well-insulated, is that a virtue or a vice?
