A colleague pressed this argument on me and I cannot locate the exit. (1) The moral distinction between killing and letting die rests on the difference between violating a negative duty (not to harm) and failing a positive duty (to aid). (2) But whether an omission counts as initiating harm depends on whether one occupies a role that makes one the relevant causal node—if I starve a prisoner I am guarding, my omission initiates death just as surely as poisoning would. (3) So the line between initiating and not-initiating harm is drawn not by physical movement but by prior assignment of responsibility. (4) Once responsibility is assigned, both acting and omitting are evaluated within the same causal framework: I am either fulfilling or violating my assigned role. (5) Therefore, within any assigned responsibility—doctor to patient, parent to child—the negative/positive duty distinction collapses; killing and letting die become structurally identical violations. (6) This implies that withdrawing life support is morally equivalent to active euthanasia, since the physician’s role makes both omissions and actions equally initiated. (7) Yet standard medical ethics treats withdrawal as permissible letting-die and active euthanasia as impermissible killing, and I would like to preserve that distinction. (8) So either step (3) is wrong—omission cannot be “initiated” harm in the relevant moral sense—or step (5) is wrong—assigned responsibility does not collapse the duty structure. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
