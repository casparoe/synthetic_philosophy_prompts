My study group tried to formalize the scale argument against insect sentience and ended up with something that feels like it collapses into skepticism about human consciousness too. Here's the chain, I think it's air-tight but it can't be right:

(1) For any class of entities, if each member has an independant sentience-probability p > 0, and there are N members, the expected number of sentient individuals is Np.
(2) Insects outnumber humans by about 10^9 to 1 — roughly 10^18 vs 10^9 living at any time — so even p = 10^-6 for insects yields expected sentient insects around 10^12, dwarfing humans.
(3) But this applies at every scale downward. A human brain holds ~86 billion neurons; if a small cluster has p = 10^-8 of minimal sentience, the expected sentient clusters inside one brain already outnumber humans globally.
(4) So if we take the scale argument seriously as a reason to discount insect sentience (huge N, unknown p), consistency demands we discount human sentience too, since inside each human there are vastly more sub-units with non-zero p.
(5) The only escape is to claim p drops discontinuously to exactly zero below some complexity threshold — say, below the insect brain, or below mammalian cortex.
(6) But evolutionary continuity and the absence of any known "consciousness switch" make that discontinuity look arbitrary.
(7) So either we accept that macro-sentience is morally negligible compared to micro-sentience (absurd), or we reject the scale argument — but then we lose our best reason not to be morally overwhelmed by insects.

I feel like (4) is where something sneaky happens, maybe treating nested parts as independant candidates? Or is the argument actually valid and the problem is just that (5) is the least costly premise to bite? I'd really like to keep (2) and (6) if I can. Which step do I reject, and what does it cost?
