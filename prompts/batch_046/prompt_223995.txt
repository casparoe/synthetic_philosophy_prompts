I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether the so-called Needham framing—why Britain rather than China?—is even a well-formed historical query. She maintains that Britain and Qing China were never substitutable explanatory targets: they occupied radically different positions in global trade networks, possessed incompatible state architectures, and the very notion of industrial modernisation applies anachronistically to one but not the other. I think she overstates the case, but I want to see whether students can navigate the conceptual crux precisely rather than reciting the usual Great Divergence narrative.

Please draft one 90-minute essay question for a final-year undergraduate seminar in philosophy of history and comparative explanation. The question must not be answerable by summarising familiar literature; instead, it should require students to evaluate whether a contrastive historical explanation survives what I shall call the substitutability condition—that for a well-formed why P rather than Q? the counterfactual case Q must share sufficient background causal structure with P for a difference-maker to be isolable without cascade effects altering the comparison class itself.

The question should direct students to:
- distinguish contrastive from merely comparative explanation in history;
- apply the substitutability condition specifically to the Britain/China pairing, with reference to energy regimes, state fiscal-military capacity, and market integration;
- assess whether abandoning the contrastive form necessarily collapses into historical particularism, or whether a non-contrastive comparative framework can preserve explanatory ambition;
- defend a reasoned judgement supported by conceptual analysis rather than assertion.

Please provide the exam question itself, with clear instructions and a suggested word length of approximately 1,800–2,200 words; a concise model answer outlining the essential moves; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, each with its allocated weight and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail to secure it. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; the answer must be constructible from conceptual reasoning alone. The difficulty should lie in selecting the crux—whether the contrastive form is salvageable—not in recalling labels. Write in British English.
