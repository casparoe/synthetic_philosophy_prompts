I'm preparing a position statement for a workshop on direct realism and perceptual consciousness, and I need to know whether my commitments on illusion and experience are jointly tenable before I present them. Here is where I stand, stated as concretely as I can manage. First, I accept disjunctivism in a strong form: veridical seeing and hallucinating are not two species of a common mental kind, but radically different psychological states whose superficial similarity is an artifact of the language we use to report both. Second, I think the argument from illusion fails not because hallucinations are impossible, but because it illicitly treats appearance-properties—looking red, seeming triangular—as intrinsic features of experience rather than as relational achievements that depend on the subject's direct engagement with an actual object. Third, I want to maintain that phenomenal consciousness is essentially world-involving, in the sense that there is no "pure" phenomenal residue left over once you subtract the world; yet I also find myself compelled to say that in cases of what I call structural mirage—brief, peripheral, or degraded presentations—the subject can possess qualitative character that is genuinely phenomenal but lacks the full relational structure of veridical perception, without this being a full-blown hallucination. Fourth, I hold that our first-person inability to discriminate veridical perception from a perfect hallucination is an epistemic limitation rather than an ontological identity, though I worry that if structural mirage lacks the relational structure that makes disjunctivism work, then it collapses into the very common-factor state I want to reject. Fifth, I believe that philosophers describing hallucination should abandon relational language entirely and speak only of sub-personal mechanisms and representational failure; nevertheless, in my own descriptions I keep slipping back into saying that in hallucination the subject "seems to see" an object, which sounds like the relational vocabulary I just denied applies there.

Do these commitments hang together? I am especially concerned about whether my acceptance of structural mirage undermines the strong disjunctivism I endorse, and whether my world-involving account of consciousness can survive the admission that qualitative character might exist without full relational engagement. I also want to know if my methodological prescription in the fifth point is compatible with my epistemic claim in the fourth: if hallucination is ontologically nothing like seeing, why does our discrimination failure matter at all, and does that render the epistemic-ontological distinction unstable? Please audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension rather than only apparently so, rank them by severity, specify the cheapest revision that restores consistency for each tension and what that revision costs, and tell me whether there is a single commitment I should drop that would clean up the most conflicts.
