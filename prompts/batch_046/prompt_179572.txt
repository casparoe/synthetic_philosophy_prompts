I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of critical historiography and information studies, and in a recent seminar on archives and power a fellow student offered what she presented as the standard synthesis of Foucauldian and Derridean archival theory. She put it this way: "Archives do not merely preserve pre-existing traces of the past; rather, they actively constitute the past by establishing the very conditions of recordability. Consequently, the gaps in an archive are not neutral omissions but structural presences that reveal precisely which lives an institution or state regarded as unworthy of documentation. This argument draws on Foucault's understanding of the archive as a system governing the appearance of statements, but it also appeals to Derrida's notion of archive fever — the idea that the compulsion to preserve is inseparable from a destructive impulse that fixes the living present as static memory, a dynamic sometimes characterized as a kind of death drive. From these premises, it follows that any attempt to fill archival gaps, such as digitizing oral histories from marginalized communities, necessarily extends the original violence, since it forces those lives into the same bureaucratic logic of visibility that initially excluded them. The problem has only intensified with algorithmic archives: machine-learning systems now practice what I call predictive absence, generating synthetic proxies for missing populations so that the silence itself is masked by data that never actually came from the people in question, making the archive appear complete when it is structurally hollow."

How fair is this summary, and does the reasoning hold? I would like an audit that moves through the claims sequentially: which parts a specialist in Foucault or Derrida would accept without reservation, which parts conflate two distinct theoretical registers — for instance, treating the archive as a discursive formation and as a psychoanalytic structure of preservation and destruction as if they were a single unified thesis — and whether the move from archival theory to the ethics of digitization is a valid inference or an overreach. Specifically, I want to know whether the conclusion about digitization actually follows, or whether a defender of the critical archival view might reply that gap-filling projects can be designed to resist rather than replicate the original logic of visibility. Finally, please assess whether predictive absence, as she defines it, is a legitimate theoretical extension of archive fever or rather a separate problem about algorithmic representation that borrows the vocabulary of archival theory without being governed by its actual premises.
