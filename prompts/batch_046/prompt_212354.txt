You serve on the governing board of the Meridian Climate Intervention Consortium, a federation of six autonomous research institutes with shared funding but independent execution authority. One member—the Pacific Atmospheric Dynamics Lab—has formally notified the board that it intends to launch a unilateral stratospheric aerosol injection trial over the North Pacific within sixty days unless the Consortium either collectively authorizes the trial or issues a binding prohibition. The Lab’s leadership argues that newly refined modeling of regional monsoon feedback loops shows catastrophic, near-irreversible risk from inaction that exceeds any deployment risk; two other institutes reject both the model architecture and the risk calculus, and a third warns that unilateral injection will trigger its own countervailing geoengineering program, compounding atmospheric uncertainty in ways no single model can capture. The Consortium charter contains no explicit prohibition on unilateral action, but it requires members to “coordinate on initiatives with transboundary effects.”

Your board must issue a formal decision memo recommending one of three concrete courses: (1) grant collective authorization subject to specified conditions and monitoring; (2) invoke the charter’s coordination clause to prohibit the unilateral trial and bind all members to a principle of conformity—no institute may undertake an initiative that others sincerely judge harmful to the common good—until a unified risk assessment is completed; or (3) decline to prohibit, allowing the Lab to proceed while the Consortium formally documents dissent and activates contingency protocols.

Frame your recommendation through the logic of the unilateralist’s curse: multiple agents, each capable of acting alone, each judging the common good by divergent but sincerely held standards, with the structural result that unilateral action can leave every party worse off even when every agent is purely altruistic. Address specifically whether the Lab’s appeal to “epistemic urgency”—the claim that delay equals irrecoverable harm—dissolves the symmetry conditions that generate the curse, or whether it is precisely the kind of good-faith divergence that makes enforced conformity necessary. Give an honest, substantive account of the strongest argument for allowing the Lab to proceed (likely centered on institutional autonomy and the epistemic danger of suppressing a minority view that may actually be correct), and show why your recommended course either accommodates or overcomes it. The memo should be immediately actionable: specify a decision timeline, any conditions or sunset clauses, the procedural vote or notification required, and how dissenting institutes are protected or constrained under your recommendation.
