You sit on the Metropolitan Emergency Allocation Board, which must revise its mass-casualty resource rules (the "Meridian Protocol"). The draft retains a "proximity privilege": a clinician on duty may direct one scarce intervention to an immediate family member, on the condition that they do not invoke comparative survival calculus. In a recent case, Dr. Elena Vasquez used this clause to assign the last mobile surgical unit to her sister, who had moderate trauma and a strong prognosis, rather than to an unknown adolescent with severe internal bleeding who died. Vasquez offered no utilitarian justification; she said only that the patient was her sister, and that this was sufficient. Critics contend the clause undermines impartial triage and licenses nepotism. Defenders reply that forcing Vasquez to ground her choice in an impersonal permission — that the rules allow her to save her sister — exemplifies Bernard Williams's integrity objection: it demands one thought too many, substituting moral bookkeeping for the direct loyalty that defines character. The board must circulate a written opinion before Friday's vote recommending whether to retain, amend, or eliminate the proximity privilege, with a specific implementation timeline. Your opinion should treat Williams's concern as a serious design constraint, not merely as emotional rhetoric, and should engage the strongest counter-argument to your recommendation without appealing to external authorities.
