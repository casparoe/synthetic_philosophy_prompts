Two people in my study group — Jordan and Riley — got into it after our philosophy of mind seminar. I just listened and took notes.

Jordan started by saying Wittgensteins idea of language games is a family-resemblence concept, so theres no one essential feature that makes something a language game. He applied it to LLMs: check for rule-following, social context, intentionality, correction practices, and an LLM hits some and misses others. Jordan said thats exactly what family resemblance predicts — fuzzy edges, no sharp boundary. So asking do LLMs actually play language games is kind of malformed; youre hunting for an essence that Wittgenstein told us isnt there.

Riley pushed back. She said family resemblance isnt just anything goes. For game you have board games, sports, card games — criss-crossing similarities, sure. But she argued language games have a center of gravity around social practice and being corrected by others in a shared form of life. LLMs miss that center entirely; they dont get corrected in any meaningful social way, they just update weights. So its not that LLMs sit on the fuzzy edge of the family; theyre outside altogether, and you dont need an essentialist definiton to see that.

Jordan came back saying Riley was smuggling essentialism in thru center of gravity. If you claim social correction is the heart of the concept, youve named an essential feature and abandoned family resemblance. Whats the criterion for central vs peripheral? If its just what we usually do, thats circular. And if theres no criterion, you cant exclude LLMs without being arbitrary.

Riley said centrality isnt essentialism; its about practical involvement. Humans correct each other within forms of life — thats not one feature among many, its the web holding the others together. LLMs generate text without being embedded in that web, so the similarities they share (pattern matching, syntax) are superficial, like a chess computer that moves pieces but doesnt play chess in the full sense. Family resemblance lets you have marginal cases, but not cases where the binding relations are missing.

Jordan then said Riley was privileging one thread — social normativity — over others, like statistical learning. He pointed out humans also predict next tokens, in a way, and that form of life is itself a family-resemblence idea with no clear boundary. So if Riley gets to say this thread is the glue, shes just picking her favorite feature and calling it the family name.

They went back and forth more but neither moved. Can you go thru their exchange and say whos right about each specific point? Does Jordan actually avoid essentialism, or is he hiding behind vagueness? Is Rileys center of gravity compatible with Wittgenstein, or is it a betrayal? And what are they both missing — maybe something about whether family resemblance applies to functional kinds differently than to social practices?
