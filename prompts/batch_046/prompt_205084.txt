Consider the asymmetry in cross-cultural etiquette and legal tourism: visitors to a society are routinely expected to observe local queueing norms and tipping customs as if they carried genuine moral weight—failure to queue is treated as rudeness or free-riding, and failure to tip is often judged as exploiting service workers—while the same visitors are commonly told that jaywalking is merely a local legal convention with no moral force, one they may ignore so long as they avoid causing direct harm. What implicit philosophical framework distinguishes these three practices? Reconstruct the theory of convention and obligation that licenses treating queueing and tipping as binding on outsiders while treating jaywalking as morally optional. In particular, examine whether the distinction rests on a view of coordination problems versus distributive economic structures—tipping functioning as a concealed wage-transfer mechanism, queueing as a pure coordination device, and jaywalking as a regulatory risk-allocation rule—or whether it instead relies on an unstated theory of social membership and temporary cooperative duties, under which one acquires obligations by entering a practice but not by merely traversing a jurisdiction. Assess whether this implicit framework is internally coherent, given that queueing norms vary radically in form across cultures and that tipping practices often reproduce structural inequities that visitors are not causally responsible for. Finally, specify what changes to the framework would be required if one replaced its implicit conventionalism with a universalist moral theory, or with a strict role-based account that denies outsiders any convention-derived obligations whatsoever.
