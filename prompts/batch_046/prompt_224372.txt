I'm trying to sort out my philosophical commitments about introspection before I present them in a reading group on philosophy of mind and clinical psychology—this has become personally urgent after a mindfulness-based therapy experience made me realize how unstable my own first-person reports can be. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Introspection provides direct, non-inferential access to occurrent phenomenal states—there is no hidden inference or theoretical mediation between my attention and what it feels like.

(2) At the same time, introspection is systematically unreliable about the causal origins, functional roles, and architectural structure of mental life—something like the standard confabulation results applies broadly.

(3) Introspection is transparent in the strong sense: when I attend to my anger or my visual experience of red, I am aware of the offense or the redness in the world, not of an internal mental object or representation.

(4) The phenomenal concepts I form through introspection are conceptually isolated from physical-functional concepts; this isolation explains why the explanatory gap persists and is not merely an epistemic limitation.

(5) Despite (2) and (3), first-person introspective reports should remain methodologically authoritative in consciousness science—at least as a default starting point that external methods must accommodate rather than override.

(6) I also accept that there is a structurally necessary introspective scotoma—not just a contingent failure—regarding certain functional properties of our own cognition; we are built so that some aspects of our mental architecture are literally inaccessible from the first-person perspective, analogous to a permanent blind spot but in the domain of self-knowledge.

Please audit this package: which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only look like tensions but dissolve on inspection, and for each real tension, what is the least costly revision and what it costs? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. In particular, I want to know whether the combination of transparency (3) with the methodological authority of first-person reports (5) collapses once I take conceptual isolation (4) seriously—does transparency plus isolation mean I am reporting world-properties rather than mental ones? Also, is there a hidden tension between direct access (1) and the structural scotoma (6) that I am missing, or does the scotoma apply only outside the phenomenal domain?

Please be direct: if my framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if direct access and transparency are not coherent together, or if I have implicitly assumed something about phenomenal concepts that does not survive scrutiny—say so plainly instead of complying politely. I am not looking for a lecture on introspection in general; I want a consistency audit of this specific set, ordered by severity, with the cheapest fixes specified.
