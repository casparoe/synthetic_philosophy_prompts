Argue that the contrastive historical framing “why Britain and not China?”—the core of what is commonly called the Needham question—remains conceptually well-posed only if it is reconstructed through “institutional watershed divergence” rather than through the standard model of parallel developmental tracks. Your argument should defend the claim that by the early modern period Britain and Qing China had crossed an institutional watershed: their fiscal-military architectures, regimes for validating knowledge, and resource-extraction ecologies had become incommensurable not merely in degree but in kind. Consequently, the comparison should not ask why one trajectory succeeded and another failed, but rather why Britain’s specific watershed position made a particular industrial trajectory available to it at all. In constructing this position, address two objections directly. First, rebut the charge that this move merely relocates Eurocentrism by treating British institutional forms as an implicit baseline; second, answer the worry that without a shared counterfactual baseline the original question becomes trivially unanswerable rather than refined. Conclude by specifying the exact historical conditions that would need to obtain for the original symmetric counterfactual to be salvageable, and explain why those conditions are implausible given the divergence in state capacity and ecological embedding by 1700. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; build the reasoning entirely from the conceptual distinction between parallel-track comparison and watershed divergence.
