I've been sketching the philosophical backbone for a novel in which a biotech consortium succeeds in implementing the hedonistic imperative—germline editing that eliminates nociceptive substrates and recalibrates affective baselines—only to discover that the abolition of suffering destabilizes the very institutions that made the project intelligible. Before I lock this architecture into the narrative, I need to know whether my own commitments about the project hang together, or whether I'm constructing a protagonist who holds contradictory premises without realizing it.

Here is where I stand. First, I hold that suffering is intrinsically and non-instrumentally bad, and that biotechnology—specifically heritable neural recalibration—offers not merely a permissible but a morally obligatory path toward its abolition. Second, I maintain that future generations possess an autonomy right over their own affective architecture, which would seem to forbid imposing such modifications without the consent of those who must live inside them. Third, I reject the conventional wireheading objection: engineered well-being need not diminish cognitive richness, since motivational complexity can, in principle, be decoupled from negative valence and preserved as a pure attractor dynamics. Fourth, I believe that certain negative affects—grief, the frustration of unfulfilled desire, the ache of moral failure—have been historically necessary conditions for both artistic achievement and the maturation of empathy. Fifth, and here I am genuinely unsure, I think that profound joy may depend on a contrast condition that suffering provides, though I also suspect this claim collapses if we reconceive joy not as relief from pain but as an independent positive state that biotechnology could amplify directly.

These commitments already feel strained: the abolitionist mandate pulls against the autonomy right, and the preservation of negative affect for creativity pulls against the decoupling thesis in the wireheading rebuttal. But I want more than a vague sense of friction. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict and dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision for each real tension? Please rank the conflicts by severity, indicate which single commitment I should surrender if coherence is the overriding goal, and then tell me how confident you are in this audit—singling out, in particular, the point about which you are least sure, whether that concerns the viability of decoupled motivation, the coherence of an autonomy-based objection to germline editing, or the contrast-condition argument about joy.
