In criminal and civil proceedings involving art forgery, judges routinely admit expert testimony concerning material provenance, pigment analysis, and stylistic comparison with an artist’s catalogued oeuvre, yet they systematically exclude testimony from experienced collectors, curators, or viewers about whether the disputed work produces the distinctive perceptual-affective response—the particular quality of sustained attention, emotional resonance, or phenomenological coherence—that the artist’s authenticated pieces reliably elicit in those familiar with them. This evidentiary asymmetry is treated not as an oversight but as a rule of admissibility: such responsive testimony is classified as subjective impression rather than probative knowledge. What theory of aesthetic judgment and aesthetic testimony does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does it hold that aesthetic knowledge is essentially third-person and art-historical, that first-person affective responses lack intersubjective validity, or that authenticity is a property of material origin rather than experiential effect? Assess whether the practice is internally coherent—for instance, whether it can consistently exclude responsive testimony while still permitting experts to testify about the “feel” of an artist’s hand or the “presence” of a genuine work—and describe what an authentication protocol that treated first-person aesthetic response as epistemically legitimate would have to change in its rules of evidence, expert qualification, and burden of proof.
