Two mates from my philosophy reading group, Priya and Liam, got into it over pints about advance directives and whether the earlier self really has authority over a demented later one. (1) Priya said that if you sign a living will refusing treatment once severe dementia sets in, doctors should always honour it, because the earlier self had full autonomy and the demented person is essentially a different psychological subject — so the directive is just the real you protecting your body from being hijacked by a stranger with no claim on your life story. (2) Liam said that's treating a human body like a rented flat managed by a dead landlord; the only person actually suffering or feeling relief right now is the demented patient, and if he seems content and not in pain, enforcing the old paper is just harming someone who never asked for it. He said without psychological continuity the earlier self's authority is like a will over a stranger's life, and you can't just assume narrative identity survives when all mental content is gone. (3) Priya came back saying Liam was smuggling in a dodgy theory of personal identity — he assumes broken psychological continuity wipes out all authority, but she thinks we don't need full mental continuity; we just need the same biological life continuing, and the directive expresses a critical interest in how one's narrative ends, not just momentary comfort. She said he's reducing autonomy to whatever feels nice in the moment and ignoring that people care about the shape of their lives, not just their present moods. (4) Liam then said Priya was turning a document into a sacred contract even when the party it's meant to protect doesn't exist in any meaningful sense. He asked: if I sign a refusal of blood transfusions at 25 for religious reasons, but at 60 I've loosing all belief and want to live, should my 25-year-old self still win? He said if critical interests can trump present wellbeing with no experiential link, then we could force treatments on people who clearly don't want them now, just because they ticked a box years ago, and that's a dangerous precedent.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Who is right about which specific point, and what are both missing? I'd like a point-by-point verdict, not just a general impression.
