As a physicist who spent three decades working with nonlinear dynamical systems and only recently turned to philosophy of science, I have been struck by the longevity of a particular stalemate regarding chaos, predictability, and determinism. One camp maintains that deterministic chaos—systems exhibiting positive Lyapunov exponents, strange attractors, and sensitive dependence on initial conditions—demonstrates that metaphysical determinism is fully compatible with a radical, in-principle unpredictability that effectively undermines Laplace’s demon. The opposing camp insists that this merely confuses ontology with epistemology: the governing differential equations remain entirely deterministic, and the failure to predict specific trajectories is nothing more than a practical limitation arising from finite measurement precision and exponential divergence. Both sides routinely invoke the same formal results—topological mixing, the non-integrability of the three-body problem, the existence of dense periodic orbits within chaotic sets—yet after decades of exchange neither position has yielded ground. My own suspicion is that the disagreement is partly verbal, arising from ambiguous uses of “predictability,” and partly structural: one side treats the question as ontological (whether every future state is fixed by present conditions), while the other treats it as methodological (whether scientific forecasting can ever be reliable for particular trajectories). But I am unsure whether there is also a hidden empirical issue—perhaps concerning the physical measurability of initial conditions at quantum scales, or the legitimacy of statistical ensemble predictions in ostensibly deterministic frameworks—that both sides use without examining. I would like a diagnosis: is this a substantive metaphysical dispute, a verbal disagreement about standards of explanation, a case of two different questions pursued under a single heading, or an impasse rooted in bedrock intuitions about what a scientific explanation must provide? Furthermore, is there a genuinely neglected intermediate position—say, one that distinguishes structural instability from mere sensitive dependence and asks whether determinism should be evaluated at the level of individual trajectories rather than statistical distributions—that both camps have overlooked, or does such a distinction merely relocate the same deadlock? Given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress: a sharper conceptual distinction, a formal result about computability or measurability within chaotic dynamics, a change in the framing question, or an honest admission that no resolution is to be expected and why?
