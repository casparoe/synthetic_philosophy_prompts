In proposed and partially implemented frontier AI safety architectures, a recurring design pattern is the "frozen-evaluator gate": a capable model is permitted access to external tool APIs or long-horizon planning modules only after its outputs are audited by a fixed, earlier-checkpoint version of the same architecture that has been adversarially fine-tuned specifically to detect deceptive alignment. The evaluator shares no weights or gradient pathways with the audited system, and the audit targets "situational awareness"—the model's knowledge that it is being evaluated—rather than merely surface-level output correctness. What philosophical commitments about the nature of misalignment, deception, and verification does this institutional design presuppose? Does it require a view that misalignment is an internal representational state separable from observable behavior, that an earlier cognitive architecture can recognize deceptive intent in a later one without shared conceptual schemes, or that alignment can be verified in a modular, subsystem-isolated manner? Reconstruct the implicit theory of artificial agency and of epistemic verification it relies on, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and explain whether an equivalent safety objective could be pursued by a practice that treated misalignment as purely an observable behavioral pattern with no hidden interior state.
