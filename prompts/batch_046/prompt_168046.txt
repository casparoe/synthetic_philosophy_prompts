I'm putting together a one-page pitch for our dept's small research seed grant—three months, no travel money, mostly just me and a grad student. The idea came up when I was arguing with a colleague about Peter's denial: everyone treats Molinist counterfactuals of freedom as if they're intrinsic properties of an isolated agent, but I'm wondering whether they actually depend on the relational structure the agent is embedded in—like, whether Peter denies Jesus depends not just on Peter's will but on the social dynamics of that courtyard, the presence of specific bystanders, the temperature, whatever. So here's the precise question I want the proposal to frame: if counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are extrinsic in this relational way, does middle knowledge still do the work Molinists need it to do—sepcifically, can God have pre-volitional knowledge of free actions without that knowledge being hostage to contingencies outside the creature's control? I think the answer is either that Molinism collapses into a weird occasionalism about social context, or we need a new category—maybe call it "structural middle knowledge"—but I honestly don't know which, and the proposal needs to say so. The approach should be analyzing three canonical puzzle cases (Peter's denial, Esau and the birthright, maybe the crucifixion) through a relational lens rather than the usual possible-worlds machinery; I want to spell out what a successful result looks like (a revised Molinist ontology that preserves divine control) versus failure (the relational dependencies break the control condition and middle knowledge can't be salvaged), and I need an honest assessment of whether this is too ambitious for three months. Don't bother with a literature review or citations—I'll fill those in myself; I just need the conceptual framing tight enough that the grant committee sees why it's not just rehashing the standard free-will defense.
