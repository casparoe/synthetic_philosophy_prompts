I'm a high school student working on an independent philosophy project about whether consciousness can genuinely divide, and I'm trying to figure out what principle—if any—captures my own judgments across split-brain cases and some related hypotheticals. Here are my verdicts, which mostly feel consistent but contain at least one tension I can't resolve.

Case 1: In the standard full split-brain setup, where the corpus callosum is completely severed and language is confined to the left hemisphere while the left hand is controlled by the right, a patient shown different images in each visual field—say, a chicken claw on the left and a snowy landscape on the right—will point with the left hand to a shovel and say "chicken" with the mouth, unable to explain the hand's choice. I take this as clear evidence of two conscious subjects: one experiencing snow and reaching for the shovel, another experiencing the claw and producing the word, with neither having access to the other's reasoning.

Case 2: Take the same patient after decades of adaptation, who has learned to read subtle emotional cues, posture shifts, and breathing patterns from the other hemisphere, so that the speaking side can often infer what the silent side is experiencing and integrate it into a coherent autobiography. I still think there are two subjects, but now they relate like close roommates who share an apartment and negotiate conflicts, not like one person suffering an internal glitch.

Case 3: Now consider a partial split, where the corpus callosum is cut but the anterior commissure remains intact. The patient can match emotional tone across hemispheres—feeling suddenly anxious without knowing why—but cannot share logical propositions or visual details. They report feeling "mostly one person, except my moods sometimes arrive without explanation." Here I think there is genuinely one consciousness with an internal blind spot, more analogous to partial amnesia than to two people.

Case 4: Imagine a hypothetical regenerative case in which a patient's corpus callosum is gradually rebuilt via a synthetic neural bridge over eight months. At month three they report "two voices arguing inside my head, each with its own preferences"; at month six they say "the voices have blended, but I can still recall being two"; at month eight they report being "fully one person again," though they retain autobiographical memories of the divided period. My verdict is that month three is two consciousnesses and month eight is one, but month six is the case I keep going back and forth on—sometimes it feels like a transitional fusion, other times like two people who have learned to speak in perfect unison.

Case 5: Finally, consider a hypothetical artificial coupling: two separate individuals, each with an intact brain, linked by a direct brain-computer interface that allows perfect bidirectional transfer of sensory data and emotional states, but without any shared neural substrate. They report feeling "like one self spread across two bodies." I think this is still two consciousnesses that have achieved perfect empathy and coordination, not one consciousness divided across bodies—similar in structure to Case 2 but mediated by technology rather than bodily proximity.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on narrative authority, integrated information, or access relations—that captures Cases 1 through 3 and 5, and that delivers a definite verdict on Case 4 at month six? Or do my judgments require accepting that consciousness can be genuinely ambiguous or graded? If a simpler principle forces me to drop one verdict, should I abandon my insistence that Case 3 is unified despite emotional-only integration, or my insistence that Case 5 remains plural despite perfect information flow? And what would the best principle predict for a case I haven't listed—say, a patient with a fully intact brain who undergoes temporary hemispheric suppression via targeted anesthesia, awakening with no explicit memory of the suppressed period but with continuous emotional tone throughout?
