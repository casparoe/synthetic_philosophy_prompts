In Western bioethics we usually treat an advance directive — say a DNR signed by a healthy adult — as binding on that same person decades later even if they have severe dementia. The implicit idea is that the demented patient is still the signer, so past autonomy governs. But there's this alternative framework drawn from anatta and Abhidharma momentariness thats been floated for paliative protocols in some Southeast Asian contexts: instead of substituting the prior self's judgement, you treat each present momentary agregate of consciousness and suffering as the sole moral patient, and you dont honor old directives as authoritative over it. So what philosophical commitments about personal identity does the standard legal-medical practice actually presuppose? Can it run on animalism, or does it need psychological continuity, or narrative identity? And does the Buddhist-informed protocol genuinely avoid all identity metaphysics, or does it just swap in a momentary-self ontology thats equally loaded? Im also curious whether the Western practice is philosophically consistent with itself — we hold people responsible for past actions but also honor past medical wishes, yet both seem to need diachronic identity. Is there a hidden asymmetry in what kind of identity each part requires?
