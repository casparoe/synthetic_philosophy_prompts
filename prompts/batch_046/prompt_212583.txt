As the recent executor of my late aunt’s estate, I spent months sorting her personal papers against county and court records and noticed a pattern I do not have a name for. During the decade she was estranged from her family and lost her small business, her private archive is nearly empty—no letters, no journals, no photographs—while the municipal files from those same years are dense with tax liens, property disputes, and legal correspondence about her. In the calmer decades before and after, the situation reverses: abundant intimate correspondence and family images, but almost no bureaucratic footprint. The gap does not appear to be deliberate destruction; rather, it seems that during intense crisis people stop generating the self-authored, affective documents that personal archives preserve, even as institutions produce more records about them. Is there an established concept for this inversion—where lived intensity suppresses personal documentary production while amplifying bureaucratic traces—or does it fall under broader notions like archival silence or negative evidence? If it is distinct, how should its edges be drawn: must the suppression be emotional rather than strategic, and must the bureaucratic residue be causally tied to the crisis rather than merely coincident with it?
