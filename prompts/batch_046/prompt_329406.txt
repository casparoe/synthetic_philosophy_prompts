I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been trying to systematize my own moral intuitions about food choices—specifically about animal suffering rather than carbon or land use. I want to know whether there's a coherent principle behind my gut reactions, or whether I'm just holding contradictory heuristics. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, comparing conventional beef and conventional chicken: I think eating beef is worse. Even though more chickens die per pound of meat, one cow's prolonged confinement and suffering feels like it outweighs the shorter lives of several chickens. Second, comparing a standard vegan diet—heavy on grains, legumes, and vegetable oils—to grass-fed beef raised on existing pasture that wouldn't otherwise be wild habitat: I actually lean toward the beef being less harmful, because mechanized harvesting kills field mice, shrews, and ground-nesting birds, and I suspect the total suffering in crop agriculture exceeds the direct suffering of one well-raised cow. That seems hard to square with my first verdict if I try to apply a simple "minimize deaths" rule. Third, eating farmed bivalves—oysters or mussels—feels essentially neutral or even positive; despite being animals, I don't think there's a moral cost because they lack the kind of integrated nervous system that could support suffering. Fourth, consuming invasive feral pigs that are already being culled for ecological protection feels not just permissible but possibly obligatory: it replaces wasteful killing with use and reduces pressure on native ground-nesters. Fifth, comparing lab-grown beef produced with fully synthetic, plant-derived growth media to conventional pasture beef: I feel the cultured option is clearly better, but only if the energy and supply chain don't cause comparable indirect wild harm; if they do, I'm genuinely unsure.

Is there one principle—perhaps weighting suffering by cognitive capacity, accounting for indirect ecosystem deaths, and excluding entities without integrated sentience—that delivers all five verdicts? Or does accommodating both the beef-versus-chicken and beef-versus-crop judgments force me into an implausibly complex or ad hoc rule? Which of my intuitions should I overturn, and what does your best principle predict for a case I haven't listed: eating farmed crickets, assuming they have some minimal capacity for suffering but far less than a cow? Please separate what is relatively settled in the ethics of animal suffering from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and biologists who study this, and do not present a disputed view—such as the moral status of invertebrates or the net suffering of wild animals—as if it were consensus.
