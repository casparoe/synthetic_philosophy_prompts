You serve as the methodological review chair for a consortium that funds interdisciplinary modeling projects—work spanning epidemiology, macroeconomics, climate adaptation, and urban science—where proposals routinely defend highly unrealistic assumptions (continuous time, homogeneous agents, absence of strategic behavior, smooth damage curves) by appealing to predictive success. Your task is to turn Milton Friedman’s defense of unrealistic assumptions—specifically the move often called the F-twist, in which a model’s empirical adequacy is treated as neutralizing objections to its false premises—into a reusable evaluation procedure your panel can run in about thirty minutes per proposal, with documentation clear enough for a non-specialist program officer to audit.

Design the procedure as a sequence of ordered questions the reviewer answers in writing. For each question, specify: (a) what evidence or argument counts as a sufficient answer; (b) whether a negative answer produces a clear procedural verdict (accept with caveats, flag for mandatory revision, or reject) or instead hands the decision to human judgment because the issue turns on disciplinary norms or values rather than on methodology; and (c) how to record the reasoning so the handoff point is visible.

The procedure must preserve three distinctions that Friedman’s original formulation tends to collapse, and you should build them into separate steps: (1) assumptions that are false but causally inert with respect to the prediction target, versus assumptions that are false and causally active but happen to cancel out in the specific domain; (2) predictions tested against data generated by the same modeling framework or calibration regime, versus predictions tested against an independent observational regime that does not share the assumption; and (3) contexts in which predictive failure can be traced back to the unrealistic assumption, versus contexts in which predictive failure is ambiguous because the model is underdetermined with respect to that assumption.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:
(i) an agent-based model of urban migration that assumes perfect information and rational expectations, used to predict neighborhood segregation patterns over a ten-year horizon;
(ii) a compartmental epidemic model with homogeneous mixing used to forecast hospital-capacity needs during an outbreak, where the unrealistic mixing assumption is defended by accurate predictions in two prior waves;
(iii) a macroeconomic climate-damage function that assumes a smooth, continuous temperature-response curve with no tipping points, used to estimate long-run GDP losses under a 3°C warming scenario.

For one of these cases, the procedure should yield a verdict that the proposing team is likely to reject—explain exactly why the checklist forces that result and identify the precise step at which the team will want to override it with disciplinary intuition. Finally, state explicitly what classes of proposals the procedure cannot adjudicate, including cases where the unrealistic assumption is not instrumental but normative or constitutive of the phenomenon being modeled, and mark the exact boundary where the reviewer must invoke judgment rather than continue through the checklist.
