I was recently taking part in an informal study group—mostly software engineers, none of us with formal philosophy training—where we had been working through some classic texts in philosophy of science for personal interest. The conversation turned to Quine’s rejection of the analytic–synthetic distinction, and two members of the group, both experienced developers but with very different intuitions about formal systems, got into a sustained disagreement that I could not resolve. I am hoping you can adjudicate it move by move, not merely declare an overall winner, and say whether there is any defensible notion of framework-relative analyticity that survives Quine’s critique without restoring the absolute divide he attacked.

The first, whom I will call A, argued that Quine’s critique succeeds for natural language but fails for formal domains such as programming languages or logic. He pointed out that in a typed programming language, the statement this variable is an integer is true purely in virtue of the explicit type definition within the system; it requires no empirical investigation of the world, only an inspection of the formal rules. He claimed that Quine’s attack on analyticity—particularly the argument that attempts to define analytic in terms of synonymy or semantic rules either circle or collapse into empirical claims about linguistic behaviour—depends on the vagueness of natural-language meaning. In formal systems, by contrast, the rules are stipulated precisely, so there remains a genuine distinction between truths that hold by convention within the framework and truths that depend on how the hardware or the physical world behaves.

The second, B, replied that this distinction is illusory even in code. She noted that what counts as an integer is not a pure formal essence but depends on empirical commitments about memory representation, arithmetic overflow behaviour, and compiler implementation—commitments that form part of a broader web of belief about the system. Drawing on Quine’s confirmational holism, she argued that we could, in principle, preserve any particular belief about a variable’s type by adjusting other parts of the system—revising the compiler, changing the hardware model, or even altering the logical rules—rather than conceding that the type assignment was false. She concluded that there is no sharp boundary between revising a definition and revising an empirical belief; both are pragmatic choices guided by simplicity and predictive success, exactly as Quine maintained. A countered that B was conflating the practical difficulty of isolating a belief with the absence of any functional difference: some revisions require changing our explicit conventions, others require changing our model of the world, and this difference in kind survives even if both involve choice. B responded that explicit convention is itself an empirical belief about our linguistic and programming practices, not a separate realm of pure stipulation, so the difference is at best one of degree—how central or how explicitly labelled a commitment is—rather than the categorical analytic–synthetic divide that philosophers had hoped for.

Could you please go through their exchange step by step—rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly correct with brief justification—and then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both are missing? I am particularly interested in whether the software-engineering analogy genuinely illuminates the philosophical issue or merely obscures it with technical detail, and whether a position like framework-relative analyticity can be made coherent without collapsing back into the very distinction Quine sought to dissolve.
