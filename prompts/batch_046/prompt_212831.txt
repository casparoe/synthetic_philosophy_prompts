I've been thinking on my own about the argument that unrealistic assumptions in scientific models are acceptable as long as the predictions turn out right — I understand this is sometimes called Friedman's F-twist. But I keep returning to a scenario he never seems to discuss: what if a model is accurate only because two false assumptions happen to cancel each other out? One error might overestimate an effect while another underestimates it, leaving the final result correct purely by accident. I don't know whether this has a standard name, but it strikes me as a serious hidden flaw. Could you lay out the most important implications of this "canceling-error" possibility for whether we should still accept Friedman's defense of unrealistic modeling? I am especially interested in fields where direct testing is impractical, such as long-range climate forecasting or macroeconomics, and in whether accidental accuracy undermines the very idea that predictive success can justify false premises.
