As a retired microbiologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of history, I find myself preoccupied by a tendency in contemporary historiography to treat non-human entities—not only pathogens and climate regimes, but also volcanic events and microbial ecosystems—as something more than background conditions: as historical actors possessing a form of causal agency. I am equally concerned, however, with the perils of environmental determinism, in which attributing explanatory power to germs, climate, or volcanoes risks reducing human societies to passive substrates.

I ask you to evaluate the conceptual coherence and historiographical value of this attribution across five specific cases. Rank them from 1 (the most defensible attribution of explanatory autonomy, least vulnerable to determinism) to 5 (the least defensible, most prone to determinism), and assign each a score between 0 and 10 indicating the strength with which it deserves the status of a genuine historical actor.

The cases are:

1. The bacterium Yersinia pestis during the fourteenth-century Black Death.
2. The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora and the subsequent global climatic disruption of 1816.
3. The multi-decadal climate regime commonly called the Little Ice Age (c. 1300–1850) in its influence on European agrarian structures.
4. The 1918 H1N1 influenza virus and its pandemic trajectory.
5. The collective shift in human gut microbiota that accompanied the Neolithic transition to agriculture, considered not as a passive correlate but as a biological agent altering metabolism, immunity, and perhaps social organization across generations.

For each case, please address: (a) whether the entity operates with a genuine independence from human intentions and institutions; (b) whether treating it as an actor reveals explanatory patterns that purely human-centered narratives overlook; and (c) whether such treatment collapses into biological or environmental determinism that obscures human response and institutional adaptation. I want an honest assessment of which attributions are illuminating and which are category errors.
