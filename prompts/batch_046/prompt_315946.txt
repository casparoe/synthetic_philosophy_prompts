As a novelist drafting a chapter about a sculptor's metaphysical anxieties, I have been tracking my own intuitions about when an artwork survives alterations to its matter, and I want to know whether they cohere. A carved marble statue whose nose is replaced with plaster remains the same work; melted and recast as a sphere, it is destroyed, though every atom is retained; replaced atom by atom over decades, it survives. Yet apply that gradual replacement to an unfired clay model, then split it into two halves and shape each into an identical copy, and I am inclined to call both originals, whereas with marble I would deny this. Crushing the statue to dust and scattering it feels like destruction, but melting it into an undifferentiated lump feels worse — as if the absence of form matters more than the dispersal of matter. My sculptor proposes that persistence depends not on matter or form alone, but on the continued exclusion of space the work originally defined: crushing preserves it if the volume remains empty, while melting fills that volume with a new form. I reject this, yet I cannot reconcile the marble-clay asymmetry without something akin to it. Is there a coherent principle of material constitution that accommodates these verdicts, especially the divided clay? If not, which intuition should be abandoned, and does that reduce my character's theory to mere poetic error? Please also predict what it says about an unlisted case: the sculptor frames the empty volume where the crushed statue once stood, leaving the scattered dust untouched outside.
