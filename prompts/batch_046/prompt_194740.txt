I’m in this philosophy of science seminar and we just hit Jeffrey conditionalization, which I understand is Richard Jeffrey’s rule for when your evidence is uncertain — like you don’t fully observe whether the coin is heads, but you update your belief that it’s heads from, say, 0.5 to 0.7. The prof said the key idea is that you keep all your old conditional probabilities fixed within each partiton cell and just reweight the cells themselves. But then in discussion my friend said this means Jeffrey updating is actually totally global: if I change my credence in "it’s raining" because the sky looks grey, the formula forces me to also change my credence in "Paris is the capital of France" or even "2+2=4", because you have to run every belief through the weighted average. I told him that sounds crazy — why would blurry weather affect my math beliefs? — but he said that’s literally what the kinematics require, and if you want to avoid it you have to restrict the update to a "local" subset, which Jeffrey never actually justified.

So now I’m confused about whether Jeffrey conditionalization is even about evidence. If it’s just a global consistency rule that redistributes credence everywhere whenever you tweak one partiton, then it seems like it’s not responding to the world, it’s just enforcing coherence. But people present it as the right way to handle uncertain observation. Where is the mistake in this? Is there a hidden assumption that stops the update from leaking into unrelated beliefs, or is my friend right that Jeffrey’s rule is basically just a constraint on prior distributions and not an evidential rule at all?
