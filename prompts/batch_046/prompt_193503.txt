I am defending a thesis next Thursday at our philosophy of mind and cognitive science colloquium — an audience of about fifteen, mostly philosophers of action and free will (mostly compatibilist, a couple of libertarians), plus three AI interpretability researchers and one cognitive ethologist who studies insect behavior. My position is that large language models display what I am calling "stochastic sphexishness": they generate context-sensitive, varied outputs that appear flexible, yet their underlying inferential pathways are rigidly locked to training-derived attractors, so that variation is noise around a fixed trajectory rather than genuine adaptive restructuring. I argue this undermines the common analogy between LLM reasoning and human cognitive flexibility, but it also blocks the easy reduction of LLMs to simple sphex-like automatons, since the rigidity is masked by surface-level stochasticity. This became personally urgent for me last semester when I realized my own research on decision architecture was implicitly treating flexibility and randomness as interchangeable.
