I'm preparing materials for an advanced political philosophy seminar on liberalism and its internal limits, and I want to move students past the familiar Popper quotation to actually locate themselves in the logical space of the paradox of tolerance. Please construct a taxonomy of positions on whether, and under what conditions, a tolerant society should respond to groups that reject tolerance itself, structured as a decision tree in which every internal node is a concrete claim, institutional scenario, or normative distinction on which the views genuinely come apart, phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing the label of the position they are selecting.

Begin with the following root scenario rather than an abstract definition: a democratic university has invited a speaker who explicitly advocates for the abolition of democratic deliberation and the organized suppression of dissenting speech, and who has a documented record of directing campaigns to silence opponents. From this case, the first branch should split on whether the university's obligation to tolerate extends to providing institutional resources—platform, funding, security—to such a speaker, or applies only to refraining from state-style prohibition and punishment. The next split should ask whether the danger identified by the paradox is best understood as an internal logical contradiction (tolerance necessarily undermines its own conditions of existence) or as an empirical risk of escalation that could, in principle, be managed through non-suppressive means. A third branch should distinguish symmetric responses, which apply the same restriction to all anti-democratic groups regardless of their present power, from asymmetric responses, which target only those with a credible pathway to institutional power, and ask whether that asymmetry introduces a second-order paradox of its own. The final split should ask whether tolerance is primarily an instrumental value—justified by its tendency to produce knowledge, stability, or moral progress—or a deontological commitment that holds regardless of outcomes, since this determines whether encountering intolerant groups renders the paradox fatal or merely costly.

At each leaf, provide the standard name of the position if one exists—such as Popperian militant democracy, Rawlsian lexical priority of liberty, Millian marketplace liberalism with no suppression threshold, activist no-platforming institutionalism, or procedural neutrality institutionalism—and, for any combination of answers that no named view occupies, mark it "unnamed" and supply exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, for instance whether it requires tolerating intolerance up to the point of collective self-destruction, or suppressing dissent in the very name of openness. The tree should contain at least six leaves, with at least two unoccupied combinations clearly flagged and briefly diagnosed: specify whether each gap arises from incoherence (for example, holding that tolerance is an unconditional deontological duty yet also that it must be abandoned the moment it becomes instrumentally costly), from unattractiveness, or from mere theoretical neglect. Conclude with a brief note identifying any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate—such as whether Popperian state suppression and activist no-platforming collapse into the same practical recommendation despite differing justifications—and indicate what further distinction, perhaps regarding the agent of suppression or the evidentiary standard for "credible threat," would be required to pull them apart.
