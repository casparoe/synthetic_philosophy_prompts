I've been working through an essay for a seminar on classical reception and moral philosophy, and I'd appreciate your critical comments on both its historical accuracy and its conceptual coherence. The essay argues that Plutarch's Parallel Lives should be read not as hagiography—despite its frequent use in later moral instruction—but as a technology of "moral triangulation." Rather than offering a single, emulable exemplar, Plutarch pairs a Greek and a Roman figure who share a broad virtue or vice but manifest it differently: one through strategic patience, another through audacious action. The reader, placed between these two poles, is meant to calibrate judgment not by copying either life, but by extracting a mediating virtue from the tension between them—what I call "interstitial virtue," located in the comparative gap rather than in either subject. I contrast this with hagiographic biography, whether medieval saints' lives or modern "great man" scientific biography, which tends to present a singular axis of admiration and suppress the comparative friction that makes moral learning active rather than passive. I further suggest that modern biography has largely abandoned this triangulative structure, collapsing into either heroic narrative or debunking critique, and that recovering Plutarch's paired method might restore a more nuanced form of moral education through lives.

Could you comment specifically on three things? First, is it historically defensible to claim that Plutarch's pairs are structured to produce this kind of interstitial judgment, or does he typically present clear moral victors within each comparison, undermining the triangulation thesis? Second, does the notion of "interstitial virtue" hold philosophical water, or does it risk being a decorative metaphor for the familiar idea that comparison reveals complexity? Third, in assessing whether modern biography has lost this mode, am I overlooking forms of comparative or "dual" biography that genuinely revive the Plutarchan structure? Please treat this as a conceptual audit rather than a request for sources; I am not looking for bibliographies or citations, but for arguments about whether the distinction between triangulative and hagiographic biography actually does the work I claim it does.
