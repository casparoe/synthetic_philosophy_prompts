I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for second-year humanities students who have read only excerpts from Nietzsche’s Untimely Meditations and therefore tend to treat monumental, antiquarian, and critical history as static moral categories rather than as living forces that can either nourish or suffocate a culture’s present vitality. To make the dependency structure tangible, I want to carry the entire explanation through one concrete civic scenario: a debate in a shrinking post-industrial town over whether to monumentally celebrate, carefully preserve as heritage, or selectively demolish and forget a decommissioned 1920s textile mill that now dominates the local landscape. I need a teaching plan that specifies the exact order in which the three modes and their corresponding dangers must be introduced—why monumental aspiration must precede antiquarian root-preservation, which in turn must precede critical liberation—along with the properties this mill-case must possess and the emotional or ideological baggage it must lack in order to serve all three modes without collapsing into mere nostalgia or resentment. Please map the predictable misconceptions these students will bring, especially the belief that antiquarian preservation is always life-affirming, that critical history is just destructive iconoclasm, and that Nietzsche unconditionally endorses one mode; indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted. Include one check-for-understanding that reveals whether students grasp the dependency between cultural health and selective forgetting, and explicitly name which elements of Nietzsche’s broader philological method and his critique of nineteenth-century historicism should be marked out of scope and omitted at this level. Present your response as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
