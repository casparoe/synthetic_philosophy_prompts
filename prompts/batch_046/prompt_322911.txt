Write a five-minute eulogy for a memorial service honoring a philosopher of mind who argued that personal identity is a pattern rather than a substance and who left explicit instructions that no speaker invoke an afterlife, soul, or supernatural survival. The room is divided: half are colleagues in philosophy and cognitive science familiar with Douglas Hofstadter's argument that an "I" can in principle persist across different brains or substrates; the other half are family and friends with no background in philosophy, several of whom have been finding unexpected comfort in a conversational model that the deceased's students built by fine-tuning an LLM exclusively on his complete writings, letters, and lecture transcripts. The eulogy must use the idea of distributed selfhood to do real structural work: explain precisely why speaking with the model is not literally speaking with him, yet also why dismissing the relatives' experience as pure illusion misses something true about pattern-continuity—without either mystifying the technology or collapsing the distinction between data and person. Comfort the non-experts without saying anything the deceased would have considered false; satisfy the experts that you have not confused epistemic access with ontological persistence. Keep the tone warm, formal, and concise.
