If one accepts Fritz Fischer's archival demonstration that the German leadership deliberately engineered the July crisis with the aim of securing continental hegemony—thereby treating the September Program not as improvised wartime contingency but as the disclosure of a pre-existing strategic blueprint—then one has seemingly established that the Reich possessed a unitary, premeditated will-to-war. From this historical premise, it appears to follow that the correct philosophical model for assigning responsibility is not distributive blame across individual citizens or officials, but rather a form of collective corporate culpability: the state functioned as a single intentional agent whose decision-structure produced aggression, much as a corporation can be held criminally liable for the coordinated intentions of its board. If this is right, then objections based on the innocence of ordinary Germans miss the point, since one does not excuse a corporate entity by noting that its shareholders were unaware of management's crimes. Given this, why should we resist treating the First World War as a case of literal, not metaphorical, national guilt—provided we understand the nation as a collective agent rather than an aggregate of persons? And if we do resist it, is the error in Fischer's historical inference, or in the philosophical bridge from collective intentionality to collective punishment?
