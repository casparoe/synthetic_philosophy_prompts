I have been working through the boundary problem in democratic theory—specifically, who rightfully belongs to the demos when standard principles conflict. Rather than rehearsing the familiar tension between the all-affected-interests principle and the all-subjected-to-coercion principle, I want you to defend a more reflexive criterion. Please argue that membership should be determined by what we might call reflexive co-authorship: an entity belongs to the demos if and only if it possesses the counterfactual capacity to alter the collective framework that produces the membership rules, were it granted standing within that framework. On this account, being affected or being coerced is neither necessary nor sufficient; what matters is sustained, reciprocal predictive involvement in the revision of the boundary conditions themselves.

Apply this concretely: defend the inclusion of future generations and of non-human animals embedded in long-term ecological interdependence—such as social species with shared resource cycles—as members with full standing, on the grounds that their presence structurally conditions the outcomes of collective decision-making in ways that could be reciprocally revised. At the same time, argue that transient visitors and purely hypothetical artificial agents should be excluded, even if they might be affected or subjected, because they lack the sustained interdependence required for genuine reflexive co-authorship. Then address the circularity worry directly: explain why using collective deliberation to define the demos is not viciously circular but constitutive, in the same way that a constitutional convention can establish its own authority without prior democratic authorization.

Present this as a sustained philosophical argument, not as a bullet list. Also, please be explicit about the vulnerability of your position: identify the specific counterexample or theoretical consideration that would cause you to abandon reflexive co-authorship and revert to either an affected-interests or a pure-subjection account, and point to the step in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
