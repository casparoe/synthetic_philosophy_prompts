After a rather long argument with a friend last night about whether mechanistic interpretability can ever claim objectivity, I sketched some notes for a short essay. I should be grateful for your comments — particularly on whether the central distinction holds water, whether I have missed an obvious reply, and whether the conclusion outruns the premises.

The essay is provisionally titled "What counts as the same mechanism?" Its core claim is that mechanistic anomaly detection in neural networks relies on an unstated ambiguity in the identity conditions for mechanisms. I distinguish three criteria: compositional identity (the same parameters and architectural relations), functional equivalence (the same input–output mapping across a defined distribution), and counterfactual robustness (the same pattern of dependencies under intervention). The argument is that these three routinely come apart — adversarial perturbations may preserve functional equivalence while destroying counterfactual robustness, and structured pruning may preserve the latter while violating the former. To call a circuit "anomalous" is therefore already to presuppose which criterion governs sameness, yet practitioners slide between them without explicit justification. I then consider the reply that genuine mechanistic explanation demands all three jointly, and respond that this renders anomaly detection impossibly brittle given the inherent stochasticity and redundancy of large networks. The essay closes by proposing that we need an explicit "mechanism-identity protocol" before any anomaly claim can be properly evaluated.

My questions are these. First, is the trichotomy exhaustive, or have I conflated dimensions that ought to be kept separate? Second, does the argument unfairly assume that practitioners intend a single unified notion of sameness, rather than a family-resemblance concept adequate to engineering practice? Third, is the inference from ambiguity to the need for an explicit protocol justified, or does it overstate the practical difficulty? Any suggestions on tightening the reasoning — or on whether the premise about adversarial perturbations is conceptually sound rather than merely empirically speculative — would be especially welcome.
