I run a philosophy-of-history seminar with ten students for ninety minutes. Design an in-class activity that makes participants feel the "for want of a nail" problem—how a tiny contingency can cascade into massive historical change—without letting them resolve it into either determinism or chaos. To keep it out of standard textbook territory, have groups build a five-link causal chain from a real minor event, then assign each link a "fragility weight" on a two-axis matrix they invent rules for: structural robustness versus informational sensitivity; arrange the rules so that groups discover, only after committing to weights, that sliding the cutoff on either axis flips the chain from overdetermined to wildly contingent, making them see that contingency is partly an artifact of framing. Include group roles, minimal materials, the staged collapse point where the framing fails, a debrief mapping each move onto determinism or chaos, what will predictably go wrong the first run, and an honest admission of which feature—say, quantitative chaos dynamics or actual temporal distance—the exercise cannot reproduce. Deliver it as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
