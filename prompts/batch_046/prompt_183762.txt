Write a short story that dramatizes Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, but move the problem well beyond the familiar field-linguist and "gavagai" scenario. Set the story in a near-future reconstruction effort: your protagonist is a linguist-archivist working with fragmentary texts recovered from an autonomous planning system that once managed a region's infrastructure and social coordination. She has isolated a recurring term in the system's procedural language for which she can construct two translation manuals that are equally adequate to all available behavioral and textual evidence. One manual renders the term as referring to a normative principle of distributive fairness in resource allocation; the other renders it as referring to a purely predictive criterion of supply-network stability. Both manuals account for every observed usage, yet adopting one over the other would lead to radically different institutional designs if the texts are used as blueprints for rebuilding the region.

The story should center on the moment when the protagonist recognizes that no further empirical test—no additional text, no reconstructed behavioral response—can break the tie between the manuals. Through her reasoning, her dialogue with a local stakeholder who will live under the reconstructed institutions, and the concrete material details of the planning archives, show how the choice of translation becomes a practical decision about what kind of society to build rather than a discovery of what the original system "really" meant. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific scholarly works; let the philosophical tension emerge entirely through character, setting, and the logic of the translation problem itself.
