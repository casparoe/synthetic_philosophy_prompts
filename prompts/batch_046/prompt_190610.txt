I am writing to ask for help clarifying a moral conflict that has come up in my work with a small residential support program. We have a limited number of specialized heating devices—there are twelve units, but twenty residents who could use them. Our current draft policy says that we should assign devices to the twelve residents with the greatest medical need, leaving the remaining eight with standard blankets. A colleague has proposed an alternative she considers more just: if we cannot supply everyone, we should disable four of the devices and distribute the remaining eight equally, so that every resident receives the same minimal level of support, rather than giving full support to some and none to others. She argues that creating a clear division between the served and the unserved violates a basic requirement of fairness, even if the total amount of warmth provided is lower. I find this deeply counterintuitive—it seems to me that deliberately withholding functional equipment from people who need it, solely to avoid a disparity, makes everyone worse off for no compensating gain. At the same time, I am troubled by a different worry: if our system consistently directs resources to whoever is worst off, we might be building a structure that implicitly values suffering itself, or that encourages residents to present themselves as more vulnerable than they are in order to qualify. I would like to understand whether there is a principled way to reject my colleague's equal-reduction proposal without falling into the view that suffering should be maximized or rewarded. Specifically, is the mistake in her argument about the intrinsic value of equality, about the correct way to compare groups, or about whether equality can ever justify lowering everyone's condition?
