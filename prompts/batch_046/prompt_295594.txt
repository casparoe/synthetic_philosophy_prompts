So bioethics usually rejects the idea that the "earlier self" owns or has property rights over the body of the "later self" — that's the authority problem, right? And if you take personal identity seriously, the severely demented patient isn't strictly identical to the person who signed the directive, since psychological continuity is broken or at least severely degraded. So it looks like in the exact cases where we need the directive, both usual justifications collapse: there's no metaphysical sameness, and we've already rejected the ownership story.

But then ethicists still say the directive expresses "precedent autonomy" and should be binding. I don't see what normative work that phrase is doing if we've given up on both identity and ownership. Is it just smuggling back in a diachronic consent model — like a contract between temporal parts — except we know contracts don't bind parties that don't exist anymore? Or take a four-dimensionalist view where the signatory and the patient are just different temporal slices; then honoring the directive looks like one slice imposing preferences on an unrelated slice, which sounds like straight-up paternalism. So since the conditions that would make an advance directive genuinely authoritative — either strict personal identity or a valid cross-time contract — fail precisely in severe dementia, why do people defend them as binding rather than treating them as non-binding suggestions? What's the actual ground?
