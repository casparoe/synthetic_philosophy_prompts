I've been arguing with my roommate about what emotions actually are — not just the usual feelings-vs-judgments debate but this specific take I've been workin up: emotions are essentially non-compositional evaluative framings, where bodily perturbation and situational appraisal are locked in a single gestalt so you can't pull them seperate into a "feeling part" and a "judgment part." Think of seeing danger: you don't first feel your heart race and then independently judge the situation; the fear just is the framing of the situation as dangerous, anchored in your body but not reducible to it. I'm calling it the framing-anchoring view, and it tries to sit between embodied-appraisal theories and judgment theories without collapsing into either.

But I want to know what would actually kill it, not just the standard objections like recalcitrant emotions or animals lacking conceptual resources. Specifically, what empirical or conceptual result would show the framing and anchoring can come apart in a way that makes the non-compositional claim false? For instance, if there were clear cases of what we might call "affective anosognosia" — someone with the full bodily and behavioral profile of grief but genuinely lacking any framing of loss, or vice-versa — would that be fatal, or could the view absorb it by saying the framing was present but inaccessible? And what about cross-cultural pairings where the same bodily state gets mapped onto totally different situational meanings: does that undermine the unified-gestalt idea, or just show the anchoring is flexible?

I also want an honest ranking of the defeaters. Which ones would defenders of this view have to concede actually hurt it — maybe developemental results showing infants have raw affect long before framing capacity, or neuropsychological dissociations where appraisal circuits and interoceptive circuits are selectively disrupted? Which are just awkward but manageable? And has this kind of middle position been qualified into unfalsifiability — like, if every dissociation gets explained away as "implicit framing" — and if so, is that a real problem for a theory of this kind, or just business as usual in philosophy of mind?
