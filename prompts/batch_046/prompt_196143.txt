I'm working through an argument about historical causation that seems to dissolve the Great Man thesis into nothingness, and I can't locate the leak. (1) To claim an individual is historically necessary is to claim that in the nearest possible worlds lacking that individual, the outcome they produced does not occur. (2) In evaluating counterfactuals about Nazi Germany, we hold constant the structural conditions widely cited by structuralist historians: the Treaty of Versailles, Article 48 of the Weimar Constitution, the Great Depression, and the DNVP's authoritarian conservatism. (3) Given those fixed conditions, the political vacuum of 1930-33 makes it probable that some other radical movement or authoritarian figure—perhaps a Hindenburg-backed chancellor or a different völkisch leader—would have exploited the crisis. (4) Therefore, in the nearest worlds without Hitler, an authoritarian regime structurally analogous to Nazi Germany still emerges, though with different ideological coloring and perhaps without the specific racial-ideological fixation on the Final Solution. (5) So Hitler was not necessary for an authoritarian takeover of Germany. (6) Generalize: for any agent A and structural outcome S, hold the structural conditions constant, replace A with the nearest available functional equivalent, and S (or something structurally analogous) still occurs. (7) Hence no individual is ever historically necessary. (8) But if no individual is necessary, then Great Man explanations are never valid—not just for Hitler, but for anyone. (9) Yet we have strong intuitions that some agents produce genuinely irreplaceable outcomes; for instance, the specific form and intensity of the Holocaust seems tied to Hitler's particular psychological and ideological profile in a way that no structural equivalent could replicate. (10) So either (3) is false because structural conditions do not deterministically generate functional equivalents for every agent, or (6) overgeneralizes by treating "structurally analogous regime" as identical to "the same historical outcome," or (2) is illegitimate because holding structural conditions constant independently of the agent assumes a false separation between agency and structure—Hitler's own actions prior to 1933 helped shape the very polarization and institutional breakdown we treat as background conditions. I would prefer to keep (1) and (9), since they preserve both the logic of counterfactual necessity and our intuition that some historical differences are genuinely personal. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? Specifically, if I save Hitler's uniqueness by rejecting (6), do I restore Great Man theory only for extreme outliers, or do I open the door to agential explanations everywhere?
