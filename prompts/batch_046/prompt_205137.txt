Deliver the opening remarks for a workshop titled "Boundaries in Language and Computation," attended by analytic philosophers of language, NLP researchers, and cognitive scientists. Roughly half the audience knows the technical debate over vagueness; the other half works daily with ambiguous annotations and classification thresholds but has no background in philosophy of language.

Write a five-minute welcome in which the distinction between epistemicism and supervaluationism does real structural work: you must acknowledge that it is genuinely unsettled whether certain attendees—for example, a machine-learning engineer who studies tokenization cutoffs but not semantics, or a philosopher of science concerned with measurement error—fall within the workshop's scope, and you must avoid both falsely excluding them and asserting a sharp boundary that does not exist. Use the epistemicist idea that there might be a precise boundary we simply do not know, and the supervaluationist idea that there may be no single correct demarcation, to shape what you can legitimately promise about the workshop's aims, audience, and interdisciplinary method. Do not treat the distinction as decorative ornament.

As a conceptual twist, frame the workshop's first session on "borderline competence" in large language models—cases where a model is neither clearly correct nor clearly wrong—as structurally parallel to vagueness, and suggest without resolving that treating these cases as hidden sharp boundaries (epistemicism) versus as genuinely indeterminate (supervaluationism) will yield different design principles for evaluation benchmarks. Keep the remarks accessible to non-philosophers, do not request citations or reading lists, and do not quote any specific text verbatim.
