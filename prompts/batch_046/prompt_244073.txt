I am currently drafting a multivolume narrative set in a confederation of autonomous districts where citizenship is not ascribed by birth but acquired through a seasonal bidding process, and in discussing the manuscript with a small circle of fellow writers I have become preoccupied with whether the moral obligations that bind residents to one another are genuinely associative or merely instrumental arrangements dressed in patriotic language. This has led me away from standard survey treatments of nationalism versus cosmopolitanism and toward a more granular concern: if one accepts that compatriot claims carry some normative weight, which unresolved conceptual issues in that debate actually matter for mapping partial loyalty, institutional design, and the limits of redistribution?

Rather than requesting a bibliography or reading list—which I could not properly evaluate without direct access to archives—I want a defended prioritization of the open lines of work that remain tractable for someone reasoning without specialized empirical datasets. Specifically, I am weighing three interrelated problems: first, whether associative obligations can survive the deliberate erosion of shared linguistic or cultural practice without collapsing into mere contract; second, whether the moral asymmetry between compatriots and strangers is best explained by relational properties, by institutional roles, or by a form of particularist reasoning that does not depend on shared identity at all; and third, how these answers should constrain cosmopolitan duties when exit from a national community is structurally possible but economically punishing. For each line, please explain its upstream or downstream relation to the others—whether resolving one renders the others derivative, parallel, or moot—along with the explicit criteria governing your ranking: conceptual tractability, the breadth of practical implications that hang on the answer, and the risk of neglecting one only to discover it undermines the rest. I want the reasoning that justifies the ordering, not merely a list.

Finally, I am particularly interested in whether there is an underexplored dependency involving what might be called recursive loyalty: the possibility that obligations to compatriots are themselves mediated by obligations to sub-national districts or guilds, so that cosmopolitan redistribution might be blocked not at the national boundary but at nested intermediate ones. Does this possibility change which of the three questions above deserves primary attention, and what kind of conceptual result—perhaps a formal model of layered obligation or a coherent counterexample drawn from fictional but rigorously structured institutional arrangements—would cause you to reorder your priorities? I am writing this for my own long-form planning, so I need to know which two or three issues would genuinely reshape the normative architecture of the world I am building if I took them seriously.
