I've been working through Leibniz's Theodicy for a seminar on modal metaphysics, and I keep running into an argument that seems to collapse his distinction between necessary and contingent truths into Spinozistic necessitarianism. I want to know which premise is the weakest link, because I'd prefer to preserve (1), (2), and (6) if I can, but I'm not sure the cost is bearable.

Here is the chain as I have it reconstructed. (1) If God is essentially omniscient, omnipotent, and perfectly good, and if there exists a unique best among all possible worlds, then God necessarily actualizes that world. (2) Our world is that best world. (3) Therefore God necessarily actualized this world. (4) Now consider any particular event e that occurs here — say, a specific act of free choice. If e had not occurred, then either (a) the collection of actualized substances and their relations would differ, in which case the result is not this world but some other possible world, or (b) the collection remains identical except for the absence of e, in which case that collection is not maximally compossible (since the internal relations of the substances involved are disrupted) and thus does not constitute a possible world at all. (5) So if e had not occurred, either we are in a different world or we are in an impossible world. (6) But Leibniz holds that contingent truths are true in this world yet false in other genuinely possible worlds. (7) Therefore, for any e, there is no genuinely possible world in which e fails to occur; hence every event in this world is necessary in the strongest modal sense. (8) This erases Leibniz's distinction between necessary truths (grounded in the principle of contradiction) and contingent truths (grounded in the principle of sufficient reason), reducing his system to Spinozism, which he explicitly denies.

The move from (4) to (5) looks suspicious — it seems to trade on an ambiguity about what makes a world the world it is — but I can't tell whether the real damage is done earlier, perhaps in (1) with the uniqueness assumption, or in the hidden premise that world-identity is tied so tightly to every particular event. If I reject (4) or (5), do I have to give up Leibniz's claim that every substance contains the complete reason for everything that happens to it? If I reject (1), do I lose the rationalist ground for the best-world thesis altogether? Which step should I abandon, and what does each rejection cost for the coherence of Leibniz's broader metaphysics?
