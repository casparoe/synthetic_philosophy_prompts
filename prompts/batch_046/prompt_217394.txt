Proponents of sortition and lottocracy often invoke a statistical-sampling analogy: selecting citizens by lot for a deliberative assembly is likened to drawing a random sample for a demographic survey, on the grounds that the assembly will descriptively mirror the broader population — with known margins of demographic error — in a way that elected legislatures, distorted by self-selection and campaign dynamics, do not. The analogy is deployed to support the normative conclusion that representation-by-lot possesses greater democratic legitimacy than representation-by-election, because it replaces the politics of ambition with the statistics of inclusion.

Please audit how far this analogy holds. First, lay out the explicit mapping: identify what in the assembly corresponds to the sampling frame, the drawn sample, the population parameter, the survey instrument, the margin of error, and the inferential leap from sample data to a claim about the whole. Second, separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the normative conclusion from those that are merely superficial: in particular, examine (a) the gap between descriptive representation (demographic mirroring) and substantive representation (acting for interests); (b) the fact that deliberation makes assembly members interactively coupled rather than statistically independent, so that preferences are endogenous and subject to convergence or polarisation rather than being fixed measurements; (c) the absence of any mechanism ensuring that the assembly's post-deliberation outputs track the counterfactual, non-deliberating preferences of the unsampled population; and (d) the analogy's quiet importation of an epistemic framework (survey research aims at accurate measurement of pre-existing opinions) into a normative domain where democratic authority may require more than accurate measurement.

In addition, consider a modified framing: if one treats the assembly not as a set of independent draws but as nodes in a small-world influence network, does this rescue the sampling analogy — for instance, by arguing that local interaction corrects individual ignorance — or does it collapse the analogy entirely, turning the assembly into something closer to a focus group that amplifies emergent majorities? Finally, assess whether the statistical-sampling analogy can bear the normative weight that advocates place upon it, or whether an alternative analogy — perhaps drawn from jury-trial epistemology or from principal-agent contract design — would provide a more defensible foundation for lottocratic institutions.
