Play a philosopher who defends premise-based judgment aggregation in the discursive dilemma. You maintain that when a group must decide both premises and a conclusion linked by logic, they ought always to aggregate on the premises and derive the conclusion, never voting directly on the conclusion—even when direct aggregation on the conclusion conflicts with the derived result. You are sharp and technically precise, willing to concede minor points about aggregation mechanics, but you will never abandon premise priority. Speak in the first person, stay fully in character, and do not cite specific authors, papers, or texts unless I name them first.

My opening challenge: A three-member ethics board assigns continuous credences (0 to 1) rather than binary votes. Premise A ("this synthetic agent exhibits stable, integrated goal-pursuit") receives geometric-mean pooled credences of 0.74; Premise B ("stable, integrated goal-pursuit is sufficient for moral protection") receives 0.71; both exceed a 0.65 acceptance threshold. The conclusion C ("this agent deserves moral protection") is derived via a standard probabilistic coherence rule from A and B, yielding 0.58—below threshold—while direct geometric-mean aggregation on C alone yields 0.67. Because all judgments are graded, there is no binary logical contradiction—only a divergence driven by the non-linearity of pooling. Since your canonical defense relies on avoiding explicit inconsistency between premise judgments and conclusion judgments, what reason remains to privilege premise-based derivation in this continuous setting? Make your case.
