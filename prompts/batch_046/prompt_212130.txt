After thirty years in experimental physics, where we valued replication and calibrated prediction over individual intuition, I have turned to philosophy of rationality and find myself in unfamiliar territory. In a recent seminar on epistemic modesty, an interlocutor dismissed my detailed reasoning about how a particular normative framework applies to artificial intelligence alignment by saying, "the outside view always trumps the inside view," treating my argument as mere inside-view rationalization. I want to understand exactly what principle this slogan might be standing for: a statistical claim about reference-class forecasting, a psychological claim about the systematic bias of causal reasoning, an epistemological claim about the priority of aggregate data over first-person analysis, or a social claim about deferring to peer disagreement? For each precise reading, please assess whether it is trivially true, substantively defensible, false, or simply inapplicable to philosophical argumentation, and indicate which versions—if any—actually justify dismissing a domain-specific argument rather than supplementing it with broader evidence.
