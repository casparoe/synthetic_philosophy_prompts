I've been thinking through Sartre's idea of radical freedom and bad faith entirely on my own, without formal training, and I want to organize the logical space of responses to a concrete situation rather than memorize school names. The situation is this: you discover that a predictive algorithm has correctly anticipated your major life choices—career moves, relationships, significant purchases—for the past year with near-perfect accuracy, and you feel both relieved and deeply unsettled. Please build a decision tree that sorts positions by the commitments they make about this case, with each branch phrased as a question a non-specialist could answer without knowing philosophical labels. The first split should be whether this kind of predictability undermines freedom at all. From there, branch on whether the right response is to treat the algorithm's predictions as revealing your "true" preferences, and then on whether bad faith here consists in refusing to admit you were always going to make these choices, or in refusing to admit you could still have done otherwise despite the predictions. At the leaves, place named positions where they genuinely belong—Sartre's own radical-freedom view, a compatibilist account, a hard determinist rejection, and any other named view that fits—plus an "unnamed" leaf for any coherent combination that seems unoccupied, with one sentence on why nobody might defend it. Every leaf should carry one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, especially for the views that claim the algorithm changes nothing important about freedom. Flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate, and say exactly which further distinction—between predictability and authorship, or between facticity and transcendence—would be needed to split them. Keep the tree to about six to eight leaves, and frame it as a tool for someone without academic background to discover which view they actually hold.
