I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of history seminar on multi-causal explanation, and I want to test whether the standard framework of causal overdetermination actually fits a well-known concrete case. Could you analyze the collapse of the East German regime in autumn 1989 through that lens? Please distinguish carefully between redundant overdetermination—where multiple causes are each sufficient on their own—and synergistic interdetermination—where causes are individually insufficient but jointly sufficient, interacting in non-linear ways. Apply this distinction to the usual explanatory cluster: Gorbachev's refusal to back the regime with Soviet force, long-term economic stagnation in the GDR, the rising mass protest movement, and the opening of the Hungarian border. Does the 1989 case look like redundant overdetermination, synergistic interdetermination, or neither? Then reverse the analysis: what does the episode reveal about the limits of the framework itself? In particular, does the accidental nature of Schabowski's press-conference announcement—where a misread note instantly changed travel rules—function as a cause the framework cannot classify, or does it force historians to add a separate category for contingent "trigger events" that sit outside the overdetermined causal structure?
