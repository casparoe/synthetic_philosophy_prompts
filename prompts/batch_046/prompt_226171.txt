I am currently studying environmental ethics at sixth-form level, and I have become perplexed by an apparent tension in how conservation institutions actually operate. In nearly every national park authority and biodiversity trust I have examined, habitat restoration is evaluated exclusively through ecological metrics—species richness, trophic completeness, and landscape connectivity—while the subjective welfare of the individual wild animals within those habitats is treated as either immeasurable or morally irrelevant. What strikes me as particularly puzzling is that these same bodies will vigorously oppose the clearing of a dense, parasite-ridden forest on the grounds that it destroys ecosystem integrity, even though the destruction might reduce the total number of sentient beings experiencing suffering, and they will celebrate the reintroduction of apex predators despite knowing that this increases predation-related distress among prey populations. This suggests that there is an implicit theory of value embedded in the practice.

What philosophical commitments about welfare, intrinsic value, and moral standing does this institutional pattern actually presuppose? Must it be read as endorsing a form of ecocentrism in which ecosystems or species possess value independent of the experiences of their members, or can it be reconstructed as a pragmatic stance that simply treats wild-animal suffering as intractable rather than non-existent? Furthermore, if one were to take seriously the possibility that habitat destruction sometimes reduces net suffering—by lowering population densities of r-selected species, for instance—what elements of current conservation practice would have to be abandoned or redesigned, and is the existing framework philosophically consistent with itself, or does it quietly presuppose incompatible views about the moral status of individuals versus collective biological structures?
