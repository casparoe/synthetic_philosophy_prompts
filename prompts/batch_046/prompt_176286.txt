As a high school student preparing an independent philosophy presentation, I have been studying a difficult passage from John Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), specifically the part in which he examines personal identity. In this section, Locke argues that being the same person over time depends not on the continuity of any bodily or immaterial substance, but on the continuity of consciousness, with the result that if the consciousness of one individual were transferred into another substance, that substance would become the same person. I am struggling to understand exactly why Locke considers substance irrelevant to identity, and whether his reasoning permits one continuous consciousness to exist simultaneously across two distinct brains—shared or duplicated—rather than being relocated from one brain to another. In connection with this, I have been considering Douglas Hofstadter's proposal that the self is best understood as an I-pattern, a kind of informational loop that could in principle persist within other minds. Given Locke's argument in the passage, could you help me determine whether his substance-independent criterion allows for a single I-pattern to be genuinely distributed across two neural substrates at once, or whether it forces the conclusion that each substrate contains a separate person? I would value an explanation that stays close to Locke's own logic while addressing this distributed-self possibility.
