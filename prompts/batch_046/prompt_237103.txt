For my junior-year history coursework on colonial Latin American thought, I have reconstructed five implied positions of Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz on women’s education, drawn from her defense of study and her responses to ecclesiastical restriction, all paraphrased rather than quoted. First, she approves a noblewoman studying theology and natural philosophy in private. Second, she condemns a convent school that restricts girls to prayer and needlework, arguing it wastes God-given reason. Third, she defends a woman who publishes an essay defending female learning under her own name, even after an episcopal reprimand. Fourth, she declines an invitation for a learned woman to lecture publicly on scripture to male audiences, distinguishing study from ordained teaching authority. Fifth, she approves a girl learning mathematics through correspondence with a male tutor but warns that secrecy risks scandal.

Now consider this new case: a criolla scholar in seventeenth-century New Spain is formally commissioned by a bishop to prepare a printed critical edition and annotations of a theological commentary, working alone in her convent cell, publishing under her own name, with no preaching or sacramental role. Predict what Sor Juana’s verdict would be if we apply her track record consistently. Identify the closest analog among the five cases above, state the underlying principle—whether unrestricted private study, rejection of arbitrary gender barriers, deference to institutional authority structures, or practical caution about visibility—that best explains her pattern, and say whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. If they do, specify what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice.
