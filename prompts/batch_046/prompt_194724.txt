Here is one exam question and the student's response. The prompt asked: Explain Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism. The student wrote: Kim argues that physical causation is closed, meaning every physical effect has a fully sufficient physical cause. He then introduces the exclusion principle, which is essentially the same idea restated—if one cause is already sufficient, no other cause can also be sufficient. Given that mental properties are distinct types from physical properties, while mental events are token-identical to physical events, we face a competition problem. If physical event P realizes mental property M and P causes effect E, then M also appears to cause E, violating closure. Non-reductive physicalists appeal to supervenience—mental properties depend on physical ones, so identical physical bases yield identical mental states. To escape exclusion, they invoke multiple realizability: since M can be realized by P1, P2, or P3, M is not tied to any particular physical cause and therefore does not compete with its realizers. The student cites Davidson's anomalous monism as a model for this strategy, claiming Davidson denies that mental properties supervene on physical properties, thereby freeing mental causation from physical competition. The student concludes that functional properties inherit causal efficacy from their heterogeneous realizers without redundancy, thereby resolving causal inheritance failure and preserving mental causation.
