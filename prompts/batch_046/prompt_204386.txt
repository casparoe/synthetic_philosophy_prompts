(a) I treat collapse as an evaluative rather than purely descriptive concept: a society collapses when its defining institutional purposes are systematically frustrated, not whenever population, complexity, or territory declines.

(b) The Late Bronze Age breakdown of palace economies (c. 1177 BCE) was a genuine collapse under this standard: the redistributive administrative core disappeared and was not replaced by any functional equivalent at comparable scale.

(c) The Classic Maya transformation (c. 800–900 CE) was not collapse but adaptive decentralization; the persistence of writing, calendrics, and ritual practice shows that the civilization's core cultural and religious purposes were not frustrated, only relocated to smaller polities.

(d) Rapa Nui (Easter Island) is different: the moai-building socio-ritual economy collapsed due to ecological depletion, yet the population, language, and subsistence systems survived—so I classify it as a subsystem collapse, not a societal one.

(e) I reject the framing that societies "merely change" in a value-neutral way; every major transformation involves the frustration of some prior institutional ends, and collapse is the extreme case in which those ends were central.

(f) Consequently, I hold that if large-scale political integration and monumental construction cease while smaller-scale cultural practices persist—exactly the Maya pattern—that is sufficient to deny collapse.

(g) Yet for Rapa Nui I apply a stricter continuity standard, counting the loss of the moai system as collapse despite comparable survival of population and culture, which suggests I am not applying (a) consistently across cases.

(h) I also think there is a conceptual no-free-lunch problem here: any criterion flexible enough to save the Maya from collapse should analogously save other subsystems I want to call collapses, unless I smuggle in ad hoc adjustments.
