I sit on my university's General Education Reform Committee. We must decide the structural framework for a revised required course, currently titled "Intellectual Foundations of the West." The existing syllabus moves chronologically through three units—"The Middle Ages," "The Renaissance," and "The Scientific Revolution"—each treated as a distinct epoch with its own epistemic norms. A working group proposes abolishing these period divisions in favor of four cross-temporal thematic modules: "Cosmological Order," "Textual Authority," "Experimental Practice," and "Institutional Knowledge." They contend that the tripartite scheme is a retrospective historiographical construction, developed largely in the nineteenth century, that misleads students into seeing sharp ruptures where there was actually gradual transformation in practices and institutions. A minority report, signed by two faculty historians, argues that abandoning the categories entirely erases genuinely different ways knowledge was authorized and transmitted, and warns that thematic modules risk imposing anachronistic questions onto past thinkers. The committee must recommend whether to adopt the thematic structure, retain the chronological-period model, or implement a hybrid, with specific guidance on labeling, sequencing, and how instructors should discuss continuity and change. Draft the committee's decision memo: recommend a concrete course architecture, explain the philosophical and pedagogical reasoning behind it, and address the strongest objection from the opposing side with intellectual honesty rather than dismissal.
