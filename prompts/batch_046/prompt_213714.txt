As a journalist covering participatory governance, I have been tracking a pattern across municipal climate-adaptation councils that I lack vocabulary for. A deliberative body designs a policy; implementation yields poorer outcomes than projected; the response is never to question whether collective inquiry suits the problem, but to adjust the procedural architecture—reweighting expert input, changing sampling frames, tightening evidentiary thresholds. Each adjustment is locally justified, yet cumulatively they produce a framework in which no outcome can genuinely challenge the democratic method itself, since every failure is redescribed as a calibration of the inquiry conditions. Does Deweyan experimentalism, or the broader philosophy of democratic inquiry, recognize a specific concept for this drift—this progressive immunization of the procedural apparatus against its own negative results? I want to understand exactly where legitimate instrumental revision ends and this form of epistemic closure begins, and whether the phenomenon carries an established name or requires a new one with clear boundary conditions.
