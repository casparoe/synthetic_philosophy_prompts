I'm trying to write something about why originals matter in art, and I'm using Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray as a kind of philospohical test case, but I'm not sure I'm not just forcing the book to fit my idea. The idea — which I basically made up, it's not pulled from one specific philosopher — is that an original has what I'd call "narrative vulnerability": it's genuinely exposed to the risks and accidents of its own history, like a canvas that can crack or be overpainted, while a perfect forgery is "narratively insulated" because its history is manufactured to avoid those risks. In the novel, Dorian's portrait seems to absorb all that vulnerability — it ages, rots, records every sin — while Dorian himself becomes this weird insulated copy who stays young regardless of what he does. So I'm wondering: does the novel actually support the claim that being an original means being vulnerable to your own choices? Or does it do the opposite — show that the "copy" (Dorian's body) is the one living a real life, while the portrait is just a magical accounting device?
