My study group has been working through Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle and built an argument that seems to turn it into an engine for epistemic paralysis. I’m suspicious because the conclusion feels wrong, but I can’t locate the slip. Here is the chain:

(1) Reflection says that if I am now rationally certain my future self will assign credence r to P, then my present credence in P ought to be r—unless I have specific reason to think that future assignment is corrupted.

(2) For any proposition P and any target value t, I can design a transparent “epistemic treatment”—a specific sequence of framing shifts, selective evidence exposure, and reasoning protocols—that I know in advance will move my credence in P to t tomorrow, regardless of P’s actual merits.

(3) Because the mechanism is transparent to me now, I can be certain in advance that after undergoing treatment T(t) my credence will be exactly t.

(4) By (1), applying Reflection to each treatment, my present credence in P ought to equal t for every t I could choose.

(5) So Reflection seems to require me to hold every credence in P simultaneously, or to abandon the idea that I can currently evaluate which treatments are corrupting.

(6) But if I abandon the distinction between corrupting and sound treatments, I lose any basis for preferring rational belief-change over manipulation, which undermines the motivation for Reflection itself.

(7) Therefore Reflection must be false, or at least inapplicable to any agent who can plan her own updating procedures.

I want to keep (1) and (6) if possible—the idea that we should defer to our rational future selves, but not to arbitrary manipulation. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there an equivocation hidden in (3) about what “certain” means across different planned futures, or is the problem a scope shift in applying (1) to counterfactual treatments rather than the actual path?
