I'm a doctoral student in ancient philosophy preparing a 75-minute graduate seminar session for about fifteen students on Aristotle's account of exchange, money, and unnatural acquisition. I want the session to move beyond the familiar distinction between oikonomia and chrematistike so that participants actually experience how a medium invented for commensuration can become an autonomous end that displaces use-value. To that end, I am injecting a structural twist: the simulation should include a self-referential rule-amending mechanism in which accumulated currency can be used to vote on altering the exchange rules themselves, so that the logic of accumulation gradually captures governance over the system's telos. This is meant to mirror Aristotle's worry that when chrematistike dominates, the ordering principle of the polis is replaced by unlimited acquisition, though I recognize this framing may be anachronistic.

Please design a runnable classroom simulation or structured game that achieves the following. First, specify the materials, roles, and rules, including a payoff or scoring scheme that requires no real money. Second, arrange the incentives and roles so that participants encounter the displacement of need-satisfaction by accumulation before I name it in the debrief. The game should have at least two phases: an initial phase of direct exchange with goods that have intrinsic use-limits (perishable or single-use items), followed by the introduction of a durable token currency that initially serves only as a measure of value but can be made to generate additional tokens through an interest-like mechanism. In the second phase, participants should also hold vote-tokens proportional to their accumulated currency, allowing them to propose amendments—such as lowering the cost of borrowing tokens or removing use-limits on goods. Third, provide a debrief plan (about fifteen minutes) that maps specific moments in the activity onto Aristotle's claims: the natural limit of household management, the shoe that can be worn or exchanged but not both, the barrenness of currency by nature, and the unnaturalness of tokos that makes money appear fertile. Fourth, be explicit about what feature of Aristotle's ethical view—such as the orientation of the soul or the biological analogies for natural growth—this activity necessarily cannot reproduce and that I must supply verbally. Fifth, predict what will go wrong in the first run—likely coordination failures, confusion about the voting rules, or participants treating the simulation as a competitive win-condition rather than an economic model—and tell me how to handle it. Finally, please write in American English, and if this request rests on a confused premise—if, for example, the self-referential voting mechanism distorts Aristotle's argument beyond recognition, or if the distinction between natural and unnatural acquisition cannot be staged without anachronism—say so plainly rather than polishing the design.
