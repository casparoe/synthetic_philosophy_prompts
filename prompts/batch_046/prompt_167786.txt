My aesthetics professor has given consistent verdicts on whether testimony can yield aesthetic knowledge. She said yes when a student read a poem and accepted a critic's claim that its meter is anapestic tetrameter; yes when a viewer, seeing a large abstract painting, accepted the curator's claim that the artist intended its palette to evoke post-industrial decay, even without feeling that decay herself; yes when a visitor observed a building's facade and accepted an architect's claim that its proportions obey the golden ratio; but she said no when someone who had never heard a particular late-Romantic symphony accepted a program note describing it as expressing resigned acceptance of mortality; and no when a diner who had tasted a dish but found it flat accepted a chef's claim that its flavors stood in subtle tension. Next week she will consider a student who has never seen, photographed, or otherwise encountered a specific land-art earthwork—she knows only a verbal description—accepting an art historian's claim that its ground plan exhibits perfect radial symmetry. Predict her verdict. Then lay out the two most plausible implicit rules that both fit her five earlier verdicts, explain precisely why they diverge on this earthwork case, give your best guess which rule she actually runs with reasons for preferring it, and specify the one further decoupled case—where direct acquaintance is present but the property is of the kind her verdicts treat differently—that would settle which rule is correct.
