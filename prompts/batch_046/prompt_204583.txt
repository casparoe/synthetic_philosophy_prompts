I'm currently taking an advanced seminar on normative ethics, and during our recent unit on demandingness objections to consequentialism, two of my classmates—Maya and Julian—worked through a disagreement that I found genuinely difficult to resolve, so I'm reporting it here for adjudication rather than asking for advice about my own view.

Maya began by arguing that the standard demandingness objection misidentifies the problem. She proposed what she calls a proportional contribution framework: each agent owes only a calculable share of global welfare improvement, indexed to capacity and opportunity cost, with everything beyond that share being genuinely supererogatory rather than merely excused. She claimed this dissolves demandingness because it supplies a non-arbitrary quantitative cap—say, a fixed percentage of disposable resources and time—without requiring full optimization. Julian replied that any such cap is either arbitrary or consequentialist in disguise. If the figure is fifteen percent, he asked, why not fourteen or sixteen? If the answer appeals to intuition, then consequentialism has been abandoned for deontological intuition; if the answer appeals to long-run compliance or system-level welfare, then the cap is merely a strategic approximation of full demandingness, and the objection returns at the design level.

Maya then introduced a concept she terms normative residue: when an agent abandons a long-standing personal project—she offered the case of a musician leaving behind a decade-long interpretive tradition—the loss is not merely psychological grief or foregone utility, but a specific moral remainder. The agent has violated a standing commitment that carried a normative weight different in kind from a general welfare-promoting reason. Julian countered that this is essentially Bernard Williams's integrity objection relabeled. He argued that normative residue is phenomenologically real but morally weightless, comparable to a surgeon's unavoidable grief: present, but not a competing reason. He added that personal projects are fungible over a life; the musician could eventually produce comparable well-being through other artistic or non-artistic activities, so the standing commitment reduces to a high-value long-term plan that consequentialism can already weight appropriately.

In her final round, Maya insisted that Julian conflates outcome-equivalence with reason-structure. She distinguished between the possibility that the musician might eventually write comparable music elsewhere—outcome-equivalence—and the fact that the original commitment was not adopted as an instrument for well-being, but as a source of meaning generating its own reasons. Therefore, treating it as replaceable misses the logical form of the commitment itself. Julian closed by observing that if reasons can possess logical forms that block trade-offs entirely, then Maya is not refining consequentialism to meet demandingness, but replacing it with a pluralist deontology; she should therefore concede that she has abandoned the view she claims to defend.

Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether the proportional contribution framework avoids arbitrariness; whether normative residue is genuinely distinct from psychological grief or reducible to long-term utility weighting; whether reason-structure can block trade-offs without collapsing consequentialism into deontology; and what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether demandingness is best understood as a quantitative problem about caps on sacrifice or as a qualitative problem about which kinds of reasons morality permits. If one party is more right overall, say so and explain why; if neither fully captures the issue, say where the real disagreement lies.
