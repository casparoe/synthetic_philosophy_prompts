I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy in earnest, and I am preparing a short position paper for an interdisciplinary seminar. Before I present, I want a rigorous consistency audit of the following commitments, which I have arrived at independently but which now feel somewhat crowded together. Here they are, stated as plainly as I can:

(1) I take the dogmatism paradox—the structural pressure by which a rational agent, once justified in believing p and in believing that her justification for p is adequate, seems permitted to treat subsequent counterevidence as misleading rather than defeating—to reveal a genuine feature of our ordinary concept of rational belief, not a mere verbal confusion or logical slip.

(2) I accept closure for knowledge under competent deduction, but only within a stable conceptual framework: if S knows p and competently derives q from p without altering her framing assumptions, then S is in a position to know q.

(3) I deny that recognizing one's own justification is an automatic, cost-free cognitive achievement. In my scientific career, calibrating an instrument was always as demanding as using it; analogously, justifying the belief that one is justified in p requires independent epistemic labor and is not delivered for free by the first-order justification itself.

(4) I am committed to what I call "model openness" in belief revision: not only parameter estimates but framing assumptions themselves must remain revisable in principle. A single anomalous observation should prompt replication and scrutiny, but a persistent, cross-method pattern of anomalies must eventually force revision of the framework itself.

(5) Once a theoretical framework has survived extensive robustness analysis—its predictions tested across independent model specifications, varied datasets, and different instrumental configurations—it acquires a high prior that justifies setting aside isolated anomalies as likely instrumental error or statistical fluke, without immediate abandonment of the framework.

(6) In practice, when a long-established physical constant—embedded in a dense web of interdependent measurements and theoretical predictions—conflicts with a single new high-precision reading, I judge that we should maintain belief in the constant and treat the reading as erroneous, even before replication is completed.

(7) I hold that scientific and ordinary empirical belief are governed by the same epistemic norms; science differs only in precision, institutional checks, and the explicitness of its robustness procedures, not in possessing a special kind of rationality.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension indicate its severity and the least costly revision that would restore consistency. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally damaging, and be specific about which commitment is doing the work in each case. I am not signaling in advance which view I would most readily surrender.
