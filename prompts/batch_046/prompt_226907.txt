In my graduate seminar on comparative political economy, we have spent the better part of a semester circling the same problem: why some nations are wealthy and others remain poor, and whether the primary answer lies in institutions, in geographic endowments, or in long-run cultural frameworks. The institutionalists emphasize the divergence in colonial settlement patterns and the persistence of extractive versus inclusive political arrangements; the geographic determinists point to malaria ecology, agricultural suitability, and proximity to navigable waterways; and a smaller group stresses variables such as social trust, norms of cooperation, and religious heritage. Every participant accepts that all three clusters correlate strongly with contemporary income levels, and everyone can produce convincing historical narratives linking one cluster to regional outcomes. Yet after months of discussion, no one has revised their assessment of which factor is primary, and the disagreement feels less like a clash over missing evidence than like ships passing in fog. I have begun to suspect that the stall is not empirical but structural: the sides may be operating with incompatible criteria for what counts as a causal explanation in macrohistory—some demanding mechanism-level portability across contexts, others requiring only system-level coherence, and still others treating deep historical constraints as sufficient regardless of intermediate pathways—while sharing enough vocabulary to hide the divergence.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, turning on different uses of "cause" or "institution"? Is it substantive but masked—perhaps a hidden dispute over whether counterfactual stability across centuries is required for a macrohistorical claim, or over whether natural experiments in colonial history isolate mechanisms rather than bundles of correlated conditions? Are the sides answering different questions under a single heading—one asking which policy levers are available today, another asking which constraints shaped the historical possibility space of development, and a third asking which narrative best organizes the record? Or is the question itself ill-posed, because "why are some nations rich" implicitly demands a unified answer where only a plural, context-dependent explanation is possible? Support your diagnosis with reference to what each side actually emphasizes in its arguments and case selections, and be explicit about whether progress is achievable. If so, specify what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about identification in historical data, an empirical strategy, or a reframing of the question. If not, explain why the impasse is permanent rather than merely difficult.
