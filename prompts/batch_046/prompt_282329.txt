I am a doctoral candidate in ancient philosophy with a secondary concentration in the conceptual architecture of economic norms, and I am assisting in the design of the final examination for a graduate seminar titled "Aristotle and the Structure of Exchange." The course treats the relevant passages in the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics not as historical curiosities but as live attempts to draw a normative boundary between exchange practices that serve the self-sufficiency of the household and those that treat acquisition as an unlimited end in itself. I want one essay question that is unanswerable by anyone who has merely memorized the standard distinction between oikonomia and chrematistike, or who can recite that usury is "unnatural" because money is barren. The question must instead require students to apply Aristotle's criteria—especially the teleological limit of acquisition, the distinction between use-value and exchange-value, and the relation of exchange to the good life—to a conceptual scenario that is not treated in the standard literature, so that they have to reason through the framework rather than retrieve it.

Please draft an exam question appropriate for a 90-minute written response, together with a model answer of approximately 650 words and a detailed marking scheme. The question should present students with the following hypothetical institutional form: a decentralized, non-currency mutual-credit network in which participants accumulate exchange-credits without interest, yet in which there is no intrinsic ceiling on accumulation, the credits remain fully fungible across all domains of goods (including those necessary for bare subsistence), and the system is sustained not by household need but by the continuous expansion of credit relations. Students should be asked to determine, using Aristotelian categories alone, whether this network counts as natural exchange, as unnatural chrematistike, or as a hybrid that exposes an ambiguity in Aristotle's own framework. The model answer should demonstrate how an excellent response navigates this ambiguity—particularly by showing that Aristotle's objection to money is not reducible to the presence of interest but concerns the decoupling of exchange from the bounded needs of the household—rather than resolving the case by simple classification. The marking scheme should allocate specific point values to distinct conceptual moves (for example, correct identification of the teleological criterion, analysis of fungibility versus intrinsic limit, recognition of the normative rather than merely descriptive status of the distinction, and honest assessment of where the hypothetical case strains the framework), and should specify the errors that separate an A-level answer from a B-level one, such as conflating the absence of interest with naturalness, treating fungibility as irrelevant, or failing to see that the decoupling of exchange from use-value is the core issue. The entire deliverable should avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or specialized modern scholarship, and should be constructed so that a well-trained model can produce it from conceptual reasoning about Aristotelian economic thought without access to external sources. Please present the question first, then the model answer, then the marking scheme with its point allocations and brief notes on typical failures.
