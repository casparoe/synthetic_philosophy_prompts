In a recent discussion about Plato's Meno, a colleague and I kept circling the same point: if knowledge and true belief produce identical outcomes in every practical and predictive respect, does knowledge still carry a surplus value, or does the problem dissolve into a category mistake? To avoid the usual replies, I want to restrict attention to an unusually stripped-down scenario—call it a "frozen" epistemic state. Consider an agent who holds a belief that p, which is true, formed by a perfectly reliable but entirely opaque process, and this belief will never be revised, never guide action, and never be communicated to anyone. In the corresponding knowledge-state, the agent also possesses a correct grasp of why p is true—an appropriate justification or understanding—yet this grasp makes no causal, practical, or modal difference whatsoever: it does not stabilize the belief, since revision is impossible by stipulation, and it yields no instrumental benefit. Given this setup, please give me your actual credence, expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1, that knowledge is still genuinely more valuable than true belief in a constitutive, non-instrumental sense.

Please decompose your reasoning into four explicit terms, each with a rough probability and a one-line justification. First, the probability that epistemic value pluralism allows any non-instrumental distinction between knowing and merely believing truly at all. Second, conditional on there being such a distinction, the probability that it is best explained by an achievement-based or virtue-theoretic account—knowing as the successful exercise of cognitive agency—rather than by a modal-reliability or stability account. Third, the probability that this distinction survives the frozen-state restriction I have imposed, where guidance, revision, transmission, and all instrumental differences have been engineered away. Fourth, the probability that our lingering intuition of surplus value is actually tracking a social or distributive good—for instance, that knowledge is intersubjectively verifiable or shareable—rather than an individual constitutive one. Then combine these terms—multiplicatively, or however you think appropriate—to arrive at your final number, and identify which single term your overall credence is most sensitive to.

Finally, tell me which of the four terms you are least confident in, and specify what kind of conceptual move or thought experiment—not a bibliography, citation, or named authority—would most substantially shift that term and therefore your final credence. I am not asking whether the standard stability-based reply to the Meno succeeds in ordinary contexts; I am asking whether, in this case where all practical and modal differences have been eliminated, there remains a coherent reason to prefer knowledge, and exactly where your confidence frays.
