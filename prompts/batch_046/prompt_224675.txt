In covering the consciousness literature for years, I have watched the standoff between global workspace theorists and defenders of phenomenal consciousness harden into what feels like a ritual rather than an investigation. The workspace camp—drawing on the tradition of Baars and later Dehaene—holds that once information is globally broadcast across cognitive architectures, access consciousness is fully achieved, and that this functional profile is all that a scientific explanation needs to capture; phenomenal consciousness, if it exceeds this broadcast and availability, is either a philosophical artifact or an illusion generated by introspective confusion. The opposing camp—following Ned Block’s well-known distinction—insists that global broadcasting explains only access consciousness, leaving phenomenal experience (the subjective “what-it-is-likeness”) entirely untouched, and that any theory equating the two thereby collapses the very phenomenon it claims to account for. Both sides cite the same masking paradigms, attentional-blink results, and reportability studies; both largely agree on the neural correlates of broadcast; yet after decades, neither side has conceded a single substantive premise. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about neuroscience at all, but about what an explanation is obligated to do—specifically, whether explaining the functional architecture of report, reasoning, and control automatically dissolves the question of subjective presence, or whether the latter is an independent datum that any adequate theory must accommodate separately rather than explain away. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps about whether phenomenal consciousness can occur without access, or access without phenomenal consciousness—that is being masked by methodological choices about which dissociation cases count as legitimate data? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says: are they answering different questions under one heading (one about cognitive architecture, one about the metaphysics of experience), is the dispute verbal around terms like “consciousness” and “explanation,” or is it a substantive clash about the ontology of mental states? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly whether anything—a formally rigorous dissociation proof, a new experimental design that forces a shared interpretation, a conceptual distinction that splits the debate into separable problems—could actually move it, or whether the stall is permanent because the sides begin with bedrock intuitions about what needs explaining and no shared standard for what would count as having explained it.
