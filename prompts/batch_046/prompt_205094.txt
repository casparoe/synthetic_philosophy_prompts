I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy serving on a regional heritage commission that is deadlocked over the design of a public monument to a failed insurrection that occurred in the late nineteenth century. The rebellion was crushed within days, was militarily hopeless from the outset, and ended in mass executions; nevertheless, for more than a century it has been invoked by successive political movements—some democratic and federalist, others authoritarian and exclusionary—as a foundational episode of collective identity.

The commission is divided between two design philosophies. One group proposes a monument that treats the decision to fight despite certain defeat as a form of moral purity, framing the leaders’ refusal to surrender as an enduring ethical credential for the community. They want to emphasize that the rebellion’s value lies precisely in its futility: because victory was impossible, the act can only be read as an uncompromising statement of principle. The other group warns that commemorating strategic failure as virtue risks licensing future reckless sacrifices, erases the documented fact that the rebellion’s leader had earlier negotiated a settlement that would have averted the bloodshed, and supplies symbolic resources to present-day movements that invoke the defeat to justify exclusionary politics.

The specific point of contention is a documented but ambiguous final address by the rebellion’s leader, in which he rejected the negotiated settlement in order to preserve what he called the “integrity” of the cause. Some commissioners wish to inscribe this as evidence of admirable steadfastness; others interpret it as a culpable preference for symbolic martyrdom over the welfare of the people he claimed to represent.

My request is for conceptual guidance rather than historical detail. Is there a philosophically defensible line between honoring resistance to injustice and romanticizing defeat into a perpetual license for grievance? Does the moral assessment of such an event depend on whether the suffering it caused was instrumentally necessary for the emergence of a later, more legitimate political order—or is that reasoning available to any failed rebellion and therefore a post-hoc rationalization? And how should we evaluate the leader’s rejection of compromise: as a refusal to dilute moral principle, or as a failure of practical responsibility that privileged his own symbolic legacy over the lives of others?

I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations or citations. I want to understand whether theories of collective memory, the ethics of resistance, or the sociology of commemorative practice can illuminate this deadlock without simply reproducing the narrative that the monument is meant to interrogate. What framework should I bring to the commission?
