As a novelist presently engaged with questions of collective memory and institutional power, I have been sketching the architecture of a near-future city-state—tentatively named Aethelburg—whose Ministry of Remembrance governs the past through a tripartite taxonomy that echoes, without replicating, the distinctions Nietzsche drew in his meditation upon the uses and disadvantages of history for life. In my notes, I have divided the Ministry’s holdings into three registers: the Monumental, which selects and elevates exemplary lives for civic emulation; the Antiquarian, which preserves every document, however trivial, in climate-controlled vaults without evaluative hierarchy; and the Critical, which holds the legal authority to excise records deemed to imprison citizens within obsolete identities. I was discussing these registers with a friend over dinner last week, and she observed that such a system would not merely organise the past but actively manufacture the present; I should like to explore that tension in fiction.

Would you write me a short story—perhaps of two to three thousand words—centred upon Elin Voss, a junior archivist who discovers that her own childhood has been distributed, erroneously or maliciously, across all three registers? The narrative ought to render the philosophical pressure through concrete bureaucratic ritual: colour-coded filing protocols, ceremonial incinerations of obsolete records, public recitations of exemplary lives, and the clinical language of deletion orders. Please do not cite philosophical sources or provide bibliographic notes; rather, allow the story’s world to embody the problem. I am especially keen to see whether the three modes of history can coexist within a single institution, or whether they must inevitably devour one another, leaving the archivist—like the citizen—without a stable ground from which to understand herself.
