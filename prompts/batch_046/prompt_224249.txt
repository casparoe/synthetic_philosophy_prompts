I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on philosophy of history and grand narratives, and I am drafting a 90-minute take-home exam worth 40 percent of the course grade. I want one essay question that cannot be answered by summarizing Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens or David Graeber and David Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything. The question should require students to work with both frameworks simultaneously through a specific conceptual lens I am introducing: "scale-lock," defined as the tacit restriction of explanatory causation to a single temporal scale—cognitive-evolutionary in Harari, local-political in Graeber and Wengrow—which renders hybrid social formations conceptually invisible. The prompt should present a hypothetical archaeological case: a 9,000-year-old settlement with large-scale ritual architecture, absence of storage surplus or administrative artifacts, and skeletal evidence of roughly equal nutritional status across social roles. Students must (1) show how Harari's "shared fiction" thesis and Graeber and Wengrow's "seasonal flexibility" thesis each handle the case; (2) identify the precise point of scale-lock in each framework; (3) argue whether the case undermines one, both, or neither; and (4) propose the minimal conceptual adjustment needed to avoid scale-lock without abandoning the core insight of either view. The answer should be about 700 words. Please provide the exam question, a model answer, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A-grade response—one that diagnoses scale-lock structurally in both authors, uses the hypothetical to demonstrate underdetermination rather than mere exception, and offers a non-ad hoc conceptual fix—from a B-grade response, which describes both authors accurately but treats the hybrid case as an outlier and fails to identify the scale restriction as the root problem. Do not ask for bibliographies or external readings; the assessment should depend only on conceptual analysis of the two works and the invented case.
