I sit on the advisory board of the Municipal Historical Archive, and I am writing this shortly after receiving declassified family records confirming that my maternal grandfather was assigned to the police reserve unit that Christopher Browning studied in his well-known analysis of ordinary perpetrators. The Archive is preparing its first permanent exhibition on the moral psychology of bureaucratic violence, and the board must approve a decision memo by the end of this month.

Our curatorial staff is divided among three framing options. The first follows a situational explanation, emphasizing institutional pressures—hierarchy, conformity, career incentives, and the diffusion of responsibility within bureaucratic structures—along the lines Browning documented. The second follows a cultural-ideological explanation, emphasizing deep-seated eliminationist antisemitism and the voluntary commitment of perpetrators, along lines associated with Daniel Goldhagen's argument that ordinary Germans were willing executioners. The third, proposed by our education director, presents both models in an unresolved dialectical structure, refusing to privilege either.

The philosophical stakes extend beyond historiography. Adopting the situational frame as primary risks implying that moral culpability is diluted whenever agents act within organizational hierarchies—a position that bears directly on debates about collective responsibility and whether explanation by institutional role undermines individual blame. Adopting the ideological frame as primary risks portraying perpetrators as aberrant monsters, which may allow visitors to comfort themselves with the belief that only those holding extreme beliefs could participate in such acts, thereby defeating the exhibition's ethical purpose of universal warning. The dialectical option avoids commitment but may leave visitors with moral paralysis rather than reflective judgment.

Please draft the board's decision memo recommending one of these three options—or a specific hybrid—with a concrete bottom line that the board can vote on. The recommendation should specify curatorial consequences: for instance, the wording of the central gallery text, the sequencing of the audio guide (whether the two explanations should be presented sequentially or as competing accounts), and whether an interactive component should invite visitors to examine their own susceptibility to institutional pressure. The reasoning should reconstruct the implicit philosophy of moral responsibility in each framing, address whether situational explanation is genuinely compatible with holding individual agents blameworthy, and treat the strongest objection from the rejected option with genuine seriousness rather than dismissal. Write the memo as a formal institutional document with an actionable recommendation and a brief implementation timeline. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the board needs reasoning it can assess on its own merits.
