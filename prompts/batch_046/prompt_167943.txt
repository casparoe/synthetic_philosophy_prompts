Recently I have become preoccupied with a practical problem of institutional taxonomy: two entities that share every functionally relevant property are assigned to different legal categories solely because they carry different administrative subtype or color-coded designations, and I am trying to articulate precisely why this feels like a category mistake rather than a harmless administrative convenience. A colleague mentioned the Mingjia slogan usually rendered as a white horse is not a horse and suggested it might illuminate my case, but I realize I do not actually know what precise claim that line expresses. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single explanation, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be standing for—including but not limited to: a logical claim about class inclusion and the role of differentia; a metaphysical claim that color is an essential property displacing kind-membership; a pragmatic or felicity-conditional claim about applying bare kind-terms versus modified ones; a claim that identity conditions are sortal-relative in a way that makes cross-sortal identity fail; and a dialectical claim involving some form of performative contradiction in self-categorization. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or incoherent, and say specifically which ones—if any—would actually license using the slogan to criticize a taxonomy that splits on administratively assigned subtype labels when the split tracks no functionally relevant difference. Please treat the slogan as an ambiguous piece of language whose philosophical work depends entirely on which precisification is intended, and avoid reducing it to a single doctrine of the School of Names.
