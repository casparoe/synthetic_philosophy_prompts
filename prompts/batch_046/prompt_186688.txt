For a 90-minute philosophy-of-law workshop with 14 advanced students, design a structured role-play that makes participants feel the procedural instability of what I will call the recursive delegation trap in constitutional self-amendment. The core idea is a fictional constitutional clause, Article Omega, which authorizes a specific body—the Amending Convention—to revise Article Omega itself; however, the membership criteria, quorum threshold, and voting rules for that Convention are all defined inside Article Omega. The activity should not present this as an abstract logical puzzle but should force the group to attempt an actual amendment in real time, using only the rules as they stand, so that they discover that invoking the authority to change the rule depends on a version of the rule that is itself contested.

Divide the participants into three roles with explicit interaction rules: the Amenders (who propose revisions to Article Omega), the Guardians of Continuity (who must certify that any proposed revision respects the canonical version of Article Omega currently in force), and the Recorders of the Canonical Text (who physically maintain the official document and determine, according to Article Omega’s membership rules, who is currently entitled to sit as an Amender or Guardian). Give each role a hidden or partially hidden incentive: Amenders gain 3 symbolic points for each successfully ratified amendment but lose 2 for every attempt ruled procedurally invalid; Guardians gain 2 points for blocking an invalid amendment but lose 3 if they block a valid one; Recorders gain points only for maintaining an unbroken chain of canonical versions, but they are penalized if they record a version that other roles reject. Use only paper, pens, and a single physical token representing the Canonical Document that must be signed by a quorum before any vote counts. The rules should require that any amendment to Article Omega be ratified by the Convention as constituted under the version of Article Omega that existed immediately prior to the amendment—creating the loop.

Structure the 90 minutes in three phases: 15 minutes for silent reading of the rules and role assignment; 45 minutes of open negotiation and at least two formal attempts to amend Article Omega; and 30 minutes for debrief. In the debrief, map the deadlock onto the formal distinction between a semantic contradiction (a statement that asserts its own falsehood) and a pragmatic or procedural regress (an authority that can only be invoked through the very conditions it seeks to change). Identify the precise moment—likely when the Recorders and Amenders disagree about which membership roster applies—where coordination collapses not because anyone violates a rule but because there is no non-circular procedure to settle which rule governs the settlement procedure.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run: participants will likely attempt to break the deadlock by appealing to an external authority—perhaps the facilitator, or an unwritten “spirit” of the constitution—that the rules explicitly forbid, and they will be frustrated when you enforce the rules strictly. Give me a script for handling this without breaking character. Finally, be frank about which feature of legal self-reference—probably the distinction between a rule’s semantic content and its pragmatic authority to bind future interpreters—the activity cannot reproduce through role-play alone, and that you, as facilitator, must supply in the closing explanation. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; design the instructions so that a facilitator with no prior experience can run the session from your description alone.
