I'm trying to think through where the most productive philosophical work on psychiatric classification and Hacking's looping effects should go over the next few years, especially for someone with a background in philosophy of science and about two to three years of focused research time, not clinical training. The classic first-order looping story — that classifying people as having a particular disorder can change their behavior and self-conception in ways that feed back into the classification itself — is well-trodden, but I'm convinced that the newer dimensional and trait-based frameworks, particularly the dimensional models of personality disorder that have migrated from research contexts into official nosological texts, open up a distinct second-order problem. Specifically, if clinicians and researchers know that continuous scales are especially susceptible to reactive effects — because granular ratings invite more refined self-monitoring and social comparison than categorical labels do — they may begin calibrating thresholds or selecting instruments precisely to anticipate or suppress those loops. This creates what we might call anticipatory looping: the classification system is modified not in response to an already-occurred feedback effect, but in expectation of one. I want to know which open questions and lines of work arising from this situation actually deserve priority.

If you had to advise such a researcher on how to divide limited effort, which three conceptual problems should come first? I'm particularly interested in whether the core task is to formalize the structural difference between reactive and anticipatory looping, to examine whether dimensional architectures genuinely amplify loop susceptibility relative to categorical ones, or to assess the normative implications for patient autonomy when classification becomes self-consciously preemptive. But I don't want a flat list: defend an ordering. For each, explain why it is tractable for a philosopher without an empirical lab, how many other questions depend on settling it first, and what kind of conceptual or empirical finding over the next couple of years would cause you to reorder them. Also, which of the standard implications people draw from looping — for the metaphysical reality of mental disorders, for anti-psychiatry arguments, or for the design of future diagnostic manuals — are genuinely worth pursuing in light of this anticipatory dimension, and which are overrated or fail to follow? Give me a defended agenda rather than a survey, and please do not include reading lists or citations: I want reasoning about what deserves the years, not pointers to where the literature sits.
