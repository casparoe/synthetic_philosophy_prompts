I'm moderating a podcast Q&A with a philosopher defending an epistemic reading of Spinoza's democratic theory: democratic sovereignty, on her view, derives not from majority vote but from the collective formation of adequate ideas—common notions—among citizens who are sui juris, legally and cognitively independent. She uses this to explain both Spinoza's claim that democracy is the most natural government and his exclusion of women, servants, and foreigners from citizenship. I want six to eight questions that go beyond exposition. Specifically: (1) a question pressing the correlation she asserts between legal dependence and cognitive heteronomy, anticipating her defense that Spinoza's sociology makes the link necessary, with a follow-up asking whether the correlation collapses once we reject his seventeenth-century premises about gender and servitude; (2) a question on modern polarization—whether citizens trapped in passion-driven echo chambers are, on Spinoza's terms, failing to exercise democratic authority at all rather than exercising it poorly, with a follow-up keyed to her likely distinction between democratic malfunction and democratic non-participation; (3) a question on the compatibility between this convergence-based democracy and Spinoza's defense of free philosophical inquiry in the Theological-Political Treatise, anticipating her reply that free expression is the causal engine of common notions, followed by the challenge that free expression in practice often entrenches disagreement rather than resolving it; and (4) one question I'm hoping she doesn't have to answer about what empirical or conceptual evidence could dislodge the epistemic reading altogether. No biographical questions; keep the focus on the text, the interpretation, and its friction with contemporary concerns about arguing in good faith across deep disagreement.
