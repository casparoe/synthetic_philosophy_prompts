I am a retired physicist who spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter work before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science and epistemology. Next month I will present a paper at an interdisciplinary philosophy of science seminar defending a position I have developed over the past two years, which I call reference-class calibration monism within accuracy-first epistemology. My core thesis is that epistemic utility should be measured solely by long-run predictive calibration within explicitly defined reference classes, using a domain-specific null model derived from the empirical distribution of past predictions in that class rather than uniform ignorance, and that traditional epistemic goods such as explanatory coherence, mechanistic understanding, and theoretical simplicity are not independent normative values but rather cognitive heuristics that maintain calibration when sample sizes are small. I argue further that once belief evaluation is structured this way, the classic problem of justified false belief dissolves: justification is not a binary evidential status but a sustained statistical outperformance of the class-relative null, and a belief that is well-calibrated within its reference class retains its epistemic standing even when it turns out false in a particular instance, provided the calibration curve remains intact. The audience will be composed mainly of philosophers of science who are committed scientific realists and who take the values-in-science literature seriously, meaning they will defend explanatory power and theoretical virtue as genuinely non-instrumental constraints on theory choice; there will also be one philosopher of statistics familiar with proper scoring rules and calibration curves, and two general epistemologists who think belief formation is governed by evidential norms rather than predictive performance metrics.
