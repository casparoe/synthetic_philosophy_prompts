I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy of religion and ethics, and I sit on my university's Interdisciplinary Committee for Institutional Wellness and Contemplative Funding. We are reviewing a proposal—submitted jointly by the Department of Psychology and the Center for Contemplative Studies—to restructure the university's mental health and well-being programming around what its authors call institutional tranquilism. Rather than supplementing existing services with additional positive-psychology modules, the proposal argues that the university should eliminate all flourishing-oriented programming—resilience training framed as achievement-capacity, gratitude practices aimed at positive affect, and career-counseling metrics that presuppose desire-fulfillment—and replace them with a unified craving-attenuation framework. The core philosophical claim is that only the absence of craving (taṇhā) and suffering (dukkha) carries institutional value; any program that treats happiness, accomplishment, or positive emotional states as goods to be promoted is not merely superfluous but structurally counterproductive, because it generates what the proposal terms second-order institutional craving: students learn to desire the very states the institution promises to deliver. The proposal further recommends that we adopt a Craving-Absence Index in place of standard well-being surveys, and it asks the committee to approve a pilot restructuring of first-year orientation and counseling services according to this negative-eudaimonic model.

The opposition—led by colleagues in student affairs and moral philosophy—does not deny that suffering reduction matters, but argues that the university has a fiduciary and educational duty to promote positive human flourishing, not merely to minimize negative states. They contend that the proposal misreads the ethical mandate of a public research university by treating all desire-formation as pathological, and they warn that removing achievement-oriented support structures will harm students from disadvantaged backgrounds who depend on institutional encouragement to pursue competitive opportunities. Meanwhile, a minority on the committee, including one Buddhist-studies scholar and a clinical psychologist, supports the proposal precisely because they think current wellness culture is already saturated with what they call metric craving—the hidden curriculum that teaches students to monitor, rank, and optimize their own emotional states. Our meeting is in ten days, and the chair has asked me to draft the committee's written opinion: it should recommend either approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions, and it must include reasoning that the researchers and opponents could actually engage with rather than dismiss. The memo should treat the tranquilist framework seriously—engaging its claim that positive-flourishing programs are not neutral but actively generate the suffering they claim to cure—while also addressing the strongest concern from the opposition about institutional duty and distributive impact on vulnerable students.

Please draft a formal decision memo for circulation before our meeting. It should open with a clear recommendation, set out the reasoning in sections that distinguish conceptual issues (what well-being consists in) from practical issues (what the university can reliably deliver), and include an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. Because the proposal introduces a specific conceptual apparatus—the distinction between first-order craving reduction and second-order structural detachment, and the notion of metric craving generated by assessment itself—I want the memo to engage these ideas on their own terms rather than translating them back into standard positive-psychology language. The recommendation should be concrete: if conditional approval, specify what conditions (e.g., pilot scope, evaluation criteria, protections for students who opt out); if rejection, specify what alternative action the committee takes; if full approval, explain why the opposition's concerns about distributive harm are not decisive in this institutional context.
