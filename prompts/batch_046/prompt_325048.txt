I'm taking a module on digital ethics and got into a weird argument with my flatmate about whether virtual harms are actually "real" harms. I tried to formalise our disagreement and now I've walked myself into a corner. Here goes, with the steps numbered so you can see exactly where I trip:

(1) If destroying something in a virtual world counts as genuinely harmful, the harm must either be felt by a real person or be intrinsic to the virtual object itself.

(2) But virtual objects are ontologically dependent on servers and code; they have no exstant properties outside that substrate, so they can't carry intrinsic harm-value in the way a real tree or animal might.

(3) Therefore any virtual harm must reduce to harm experienced by a real person — psychological distress, economic loss, social injury, and so on.

(4) Now consider a persistent virtual world running on an isolated server that nobody logs into for years. A glitch deletes a unique virtual sculpture that existed only there, and no human ever notices.

(5) By (3), no harm occured, because no real person was affected.

(6) But if that's right, then shutting the server down intentionally — knowing it contains thousands of such unique virtual objects — also causes no harm, even if the world was designed to eventually be revisted.

(7) That feels wrong: it seems closer to destroying an unknown archive or an unvisited museum, not to harmless tidying.

(8) So (3) must fail; virtual objects can be harmed even without real-world observers or sufferers.

(9) But if virtual harm doesn't need real observers, then every time I close a browser tab with a procedurally generated landscape, I'm destroying virtual entities that might have had value, and formatting a drive becomes a kind of mass destruction.

(10) So either I'm committed to absurd moral weight for every pixel, or I have to accept (3) and deny that destroying the isolated server is harmful at all.

Where does this go wrong? I want to keep (2) — that virtual things are substrate-dependent — and I'd also like to keep the intuition in (7). But I can't see how to hold both without falling straight into (9). Is there a hidden equivocation between "harm to an object" and "moral wrongness"? Or does the argument collapse somewhere between (4) and (8)? Please answer in British English.
