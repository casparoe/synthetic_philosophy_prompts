Design a structured 75-minute classroom activity for a graduate seminar of 14 students, arranged in small groups, that makes participants experience the explanatory tension between situational and cultural-character accounts of perpetrator behavior—specifically the debate between explanations emphasizing immediate social dynamics and explanations emphasizing an internalized, anticipatory disposition to participate in exclusionary violence that operates without direct orders. The deliverable is a runnable simulation with assigned roles, decision rules, a timeline, and a debrief. Participants should come away seeing why a purely situational framework struggles to account for self-policing in the absence of visible peer pressure or authority, and why critics regard culturally internalized explanations as methodologically risky.

Structure the simulation around a cascading bureaucratic task—allocating access to a shared, scarce resource—where no authority figure issues explicit harmful directives. Assign three roles per group: an allocator (makes binary inclusion or exclusion decisions in isolation), a norm-observer (records decision patterns and reports back without any punitive power), and an archivist (tracks cumulative outcomes and presents them at round’s end). Give each allocator only ambiguous efficiency guidelines; make direct coercion structurally impossible by separating roles physically and prohibiting verbal instruction. However, design the payoff and reporting structure so that allocators anticipate future social evaluation by the norm-observer and therefore converge on harsh exclusionary outcomes even though the observer never commands them. The rules, not the facilitator, should generate the coordination failure.

Provide: (1) a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75-minute session, including role rotation; (2) the precise rules, incentives, and information flows that produce anticipatory conformity rather than explicit peer pressure; (3) a debrief script that maps specific in-game moments onto situational mechanisms—such as role fragmentation and careerist calculation—and onto cultural-character mechanisms—specifically anticipatory shame and an internalized predictive model of group norms—while distinguishing the two; (4) an honest forecast of predictable failure modes, such as participants inventing hidden authority figures or the observer role collapsing into an implicit commander; and (5) a frank acknowledgment of which feature of the cultural-character thesis—namely the historically specific, content-laden ideological commitment rather than generic harsh conformity—the simulation cannot reproduce without direct facilitator explanation, and how to supply that gap verbally.

The prompt should not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should treat the conceptual dispute as something to be staged through rules and roles rather than summarized.
