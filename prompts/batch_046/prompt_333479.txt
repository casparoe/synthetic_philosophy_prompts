I'm a novelist drafting a multiverse narrative in which a particular designed artifact—the so-called Orpheus Clock, first fabricated in iron in one narrative branch and rebuilt in bronze in another—recurs with altered material histories, and I have been auditing an advanced metaphysics seminar to keep the internal logic from collapsing into incoherence. Last week's exercise asked students to compare David Lewis's counterpart theory with Alvin Plantinga's transworld-identity view regarding whether that very clock could be literally the same individual across worlds while differing in material composition, and whether either framework necessarily treats that composition as essential. Here is the answer I am grading; I want no general impression but a precise, point-by-point evaluation. Please present your assessment as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first item should state the grade I should record, and the remaining four should isolate the specific conceptual errors—including any misattributed view or hidden inferential gap—while noting where the reasoning is sound. The student wrote: "Lewis's counterpart theory denies that any object exists in more than one world, so the iron Orpheus Clock and the bronze Orpheus Clock are not identical; they are merely counterparts linked by qualitative resemblance and shared narrative origin. This allows the same clock to be bronze in one branch and iron in another, because counterpart theory treats cross-world similarity as a relation between distinct entities that can represent one another. Plantinga's view allows transworld identity, meaning one and the same clock can exist in both branches; if its material differs, then being made of iron is not part of its individual essence. Both philosophers agree that essential properties are those possessed in every world where the object exists, but Plantinga adds that an essence is a set of world-indexed properties, so the clock's material can vary by world without contradiction. Lewis, by contrast, rejects world-indexed properties altogether and instead defines essence relationally through the counterpart relation itself, which means he cannot say the clock lacks an essential material—he can only say its iron-counterpart and bronze-counterpart share enough properties to count as the same clock. Therefore, only the transworld-identity view truly allows for modal change in an artifact's material constitution."
