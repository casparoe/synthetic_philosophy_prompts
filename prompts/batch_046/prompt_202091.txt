I'm preparing a methodological position statement for a workshop on historiographical presentism, and I want a consistency check before I defend it. Here is the package of commitments I have arrived at:

(a) Normative presentism is a methodological vice: historians should not condemn past societies for failing to meet ethical standards those societies could not have articulated.

(b) Analytical presentism is unavoidable and epistemically productive: every archival selection, research question, and causal narrative is framed by present concepts, and attempting to "bracket" our own conceptual scheme yields not neutrality but silence.

(c) Explanation and evaluation must be kept in strict separation: explaining an action requires reconstructing the agent's internal "reasons space" using categories native to their worldview; evaluating it requires applying our norms; and neither task should contaminate the other.

(d) Moral judgment of past conduct is legitimate only as "immanent critique" — demonstrating that agents violated norms they themselves recognized — never as the imposition of our own moral ontology.

(e) The historian owes past subjects representational justice: practices and beliefs should be described with conceptual fidelity, which sometimes requires using categories that are ontologically alien or morally repellent to us (for instance, describing a ritual practice not as "superstition" but as "cosmological maintenance").

(f) In actual scholarly practice, the boundary in (c) is porous: judgments about which events are significant, which sources are authoritative, and which causes are salient are always guided by present concerns, so explanation is never value-neutral.

(g) When a historian achieves deep conceptual immersion in a past worldview, they should, for the duration of that engagement, suspend not only moral evaluation but also the ontological commitments that render that worldview false, because retaining them implicitly frames the past as mere error.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision for each real conflict? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal. In particular: can (c) survive (f)? Is (e) compatible with (b)'s claim that present framing is inescapable? Can (d) survive (g)'s demand for total suspension of our commitments during immersion? And is there an apparent conflict between (a) and (d) that dissolves on inspection? Do not signal which commitment I would least want to surrender; just trace what each tension commits me to giving up.
