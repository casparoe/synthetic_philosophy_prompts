I am preparing a seminar plan for a group of second-year philosophy students who have read some political philosophy but have not encountered Keynes's ethical essays, and I want to explain Keynes's view of the good life—not as a macroeconomic forecast, but as a normative argument about what abundance should mean for human activity. The central idea I need them to grasp is Keynes's distinction between the temporary "economic problem" (securing absolute needs) and the permanent problem of living wisely once scarcity is overcome, together with his claim that solving the first reveals the second only if people can overcome what he describes as a kind of psychological fixation on accumulation and purposiveness. To keep the discussion from collapsing into the familiar cliché that Keynes "predicted a fifteen-hour work week and was wrong," I want to inject a less-discussed framing: that Keynes treats the "love of money" not merely as greed but as a specific confusion between means and ends that becomes pathological once absolute needs are satisfied, and that he connects this to a broader critique of constant "purposiveness"—the habit of treating every present moment as instrumental to a future goal—as an obstacle to the kind of leisure he values, which is not idleness but what he calls the art of living well for its own sake. 

Please design the teaching plan: the exact order in which these components must be introduced so that the normative claim depends properly on the conceptual distinction, the one concrete comparative scenario that carries the idea (it should show two post-scarcity communities, one that channels freed labor into ever-more-refined status competition through socially constructed relative needs, and one that genuinely shortens working hours to pursue activities valued intrinsically, with clear features that make the difference visible), the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or form—such as treating Keynes's extrapolation as an empirical prediction about labor markets, equating leisure with doing nothing, assuming he ignored human status competition, or reading his critique of accumulation as a simple moralistic condemnation of capitalism rather than a psychological diagnosis of a means-end confusion—and the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off. Include a check for understanding that would reveal whether students have actually separated Keynes's ethical framework from his macroeconomics, specify what macroeconomic mechanics and biographical context (for instance, details about his Bloomsbury associations that do not directly illuminate the argument) should be explicitly left out at this level, and confirm that nothing in the plan requires students to consult external sources or memorize specific dates or titles.
