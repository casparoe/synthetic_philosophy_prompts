In a philosophy-of-medicine seminar, a colleague dismissed my argument for retaining race-based coefficients in clinical risk scores by declaring, "Race is a social construct" — as if that sentence alone resolved both the ontology and the policy question. I am convinced the slogan is doing ambiguous work, and I want its precise readings separated.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single thesis, the distinct claims that "race is a social construct" could be expressing. Order them from the most platitudinous to the most controversial, and for each specify:

1. Its precise ontological commitment: for instance, that racial categories are invented rather than discovered; that they lack biological essences; that they are institutional kinds sustained by legal and social practices; that they are kinds for which being classified causally affects the classified; that they have no statistical correlation with genetic ancestry or health outcomes; or that observed racial health disparities are entirely caused by racism with no confounding biological contribution.

2. Whether it is defensible, false, or conceptually confused.

3. Whether that specific reading actually supports removing race from clinical algorithms, leaves the policy question open, or even cuts the other way (for example, if race tracks socially produced exposure to harm, it might be a necessary predictive variable).

In particular, separate the metaphysical claim that race lacks essential biological properties from the causal claim that racial disparities are purely socially produced, and from the normative claim that using race in medicine reinforces the construct. Also distinguish any reading that treats race as purely subjective from any reading that treats it as a real but socially sustained institutional category.

Finally, identify which of these readings is the one typically invoked when the slogan is used to shut down a debate, and assess whether that particular reading actually delivers the conclusion it is being asked to carry.
