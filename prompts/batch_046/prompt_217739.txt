I am presenting at a moral-philosophy workshop the view that hypocrisy undermines standing to blame not through unfairness or tu quoque, but through what I call normative authority dissolution: a hypocrite lacks the requisite moral address to hold another accountable because their blame implicitly claims an exemption they do not possess. I distinguish comparative hypocrisy (blaming for an act one also commits) from structural hypocrisy (blaming others for conditions one benefits from and could alter, yet treats as unavoidable for them). I defend that only structural hypocrisy genuinely dissolves standing, since it exposes a failure in the authority relation itself rather than mere character inconsistency. The audience is a mixed seminar of Kantian ethicists, virtue theorists, and legal philosophers of standing; they treat blame as a relational practice, are sceptical of psychologised explanations, and will press for precise distinctions between moral, legal, and political standing. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging they are and roughly in the sequence they will arise, each phrased as a Kantian, virtue ethicist, or legal philosopher would put it. For each objection, provide a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, note the softest point in that reply, and state what I should concede up front. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises — namely, that my distinction between comparative and structural hypocrisy collapses once we recognise that all blame occurs within unequal social structures — and sketch how I should defend the boundary without overstating it. Write the response in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
