You have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at the dedication of an international memorial archive documenting state-sponsored displacement. The audience includes survivors' descendants, professional historians, and diplomatic representatives of the successor state; roughly half regard collective guilt as a coherent burden transmitted across generations, while the other half reject it as a category error that illegitimately fuses individual and institutional responsibility. Construct a ten-minute address in which Karl Jaspers' fourfold distinction — criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt — does real structural work rather than serving as decoration: use it to determine precisely which claims about present-day responsibility are permissible and which are not. In particular, address the niche objection that if political guilt is tied to citizenship in a continuing legal entity, then the formal dissolution and constitutional reconstitution of the original state should extinguish that guilt rather than inherit it, and show whether your speech can avoid both blanket exculpation and inherited culpability without losing the non-philosophers in the room. The address should be conceptually rigorous enough that the historians and diplomats recognize your exact position, yet fully intelligible to family members and survivors present.
