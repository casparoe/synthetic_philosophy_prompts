I'm leading a single ninety-minute session for a cross-disciplinary graduate reading group — mostly law and cognitive science students with no prior philosophy of religion — on the conceptual clash between James's essay on the will to believe, with its taxonomy of live, dead, forced, and unforced options, and Clifford's essay on the ethics of belief, with its evidentialist prohibition on belief without sufficient evidence. Do not explain the texts to me; plan how I should teach the idea. What must come first: should I open with Clifford's moralized evidentialism or with James's taxonomy of options, and what conceptual dependency makes that order necessary rather than arbitrary? Choose one carrying example that is not religious faith and not the ship-at-sea case; specify exactly what properties it must possess — genuine epistemic risk, practical urgency, absence of decisive prior evidence — and evaluate whether adopting a contested interpretive framework in legal hermeneutics before all textual evidence is resolved succeeds or fails as that example. Then map the misconception sequence I should expect: first, that James's appeal to our passional nature is merely cognitive bias; second, that the forced/unforced distinction collapses into ordinary risk-reward reasoning; third, that Clifford is only urging general caution rather than issuing a deontological prohibition on belief-formation itself. For each, state precisely where in the sequence to intercept it and the one-sentence corrective move. Give me a closing diagnostic question that tests whether students understand James as restructuring the conditions under which belief is permissible rather than licensing wishful thinking, and describe what a wrong answer sounds like. Finally, state openly what I should refuse to explain at this level — whether James's original theological audience, the finer points of Clifford's utilitarian calculus, or the later pragmatist reception — and say explicitly that I will acknowledge it as omitted.
