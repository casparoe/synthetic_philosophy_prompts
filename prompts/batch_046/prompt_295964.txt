I have been trying to work out whether Dewey's experimentalist account of democracy can be reconciled with the adversarial disagreement norms that rationalist forums often try to cultivate. The idea I want to test is that Dewey's claim that democratic inquiry transforms the problem situation rather than resolving it into a pre-given answer maps surprisingly well onto practices like isolating the precise crux of disagreement and testing it experimentally—treating dispute not as a zero-sum belief update but as a restructuring of the shared problem space. But I worry I am importing a false framework. Dewey explicitly denies that democratic deliberation is an epistemic aggregation mechanism comparable to a weighted prediction market or a calibrated belief pool; for him, warranted assertibility is achieved through shared experience and reconstructed habits, not through individual probabilities converging to an objective value. Does this mean his anti-foundationalism is fundamentally at odds with the calibrationist expectation that beliefs should track objective frequencies? Or can his experimentalism be read as saying that convergence itself is the wrong success metric—that the proper output of democratic inquiry is not agreement but a more intelligent, experimentally tested problem formulation? I would value comments specifically on whether I am misrepresenting Dewey's critique of spectator accounts of knowledge, and whether the parallel to adversarial, high-stakes disagreement resolution holds without anachronistically turning Dewey into a crypto-rationalist. I am not looking for citations or further reading; I want to know if the conceptual architecture holds together.
