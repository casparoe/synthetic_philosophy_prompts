In a seminar discussion last week, a classmate offered the following summary of a classic metaphysical dispute. He said that Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles claims that any two genuinely distinct objects must differ in at least one property, but that Max Black's well-known sphere scenario was designed to show the opposite of what philosophers usually think: Black imagined two identical iron spheres suspended in an otherwise empty universe, separated by exactly two miles, with the same mass, temperature, and chemical composition. According to my classmate, because the spheres stand in different spatial relations—one lies two miles to the left of the other—they are already discernible by a relational property, which means Black's example actually saves Leibniz's principle rather than refuting it, so long as we treat spatial position as genuinely relational and not intrinsic. He went further, arguing that this reading is confirmed by quantum mechanics: elementary particles of the same kind are genuinely indistinguishable only because they lack definite spatial trajectories altogether, which shows Leibniz was right about localized macroscopic objects but wrong about quantum entities. I suspect this reverses the standard dialectic and imports physics in a questionable way, yet I cannot identify precisely where the misrepresentation occurs. Is this summary fair? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of metaphysics and physics, and avoid presenting any contested interpretation as the consensus. Specifically, indicate which parts accurately capture Black's argumentative purpose, Leibniz's relational theory of space, and the relationship to quantum indistinguishability; which parts subtly conflate relational discernibility with intrinsic difference or invert the direction of Black's challenge; and whether the conclusion that Black vindicates the principle follows from the corrected premises.
