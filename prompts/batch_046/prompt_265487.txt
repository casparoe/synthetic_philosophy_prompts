I'm in an honors philosophy seminar during my junior year, and after our recent unit on speech acts and conceptual vagueness I've become stuck on a problem that keeps surfacing in my own discussions. I'm trying to determine whether there's a coherent principle behind my intuitive verdicts about when a question counts as genuinely open versus rhetorically loaded, but only within the microdomain of peer-to-peer philosophical exchange—say, in seminar debate or in text messages with classmates about course material. Here are the cases, judged by gut reaction alone.

If a classmate asks, "What do you think is the strongest objection to utilitarianism?" right after presenting her own view, that strikes me as genuinely open, even though she clearly holds an answer. If she asks, "How can anyone still defend utilitarianism after the transplant surgeon case?" that feels rhetorically loaded and not genuinely open, despite having the same surface form. If I text a friend, "Do you actually believe morality is relative, or are you just saying that for the assignment?" that feels like a real, if confrontational, question. But if I write in an essay, "Who in their right mind could deny that consciousness is mysterious?" I feel I am being rhetorical, even though I am genuinely puzzled by consciousness. There is also the case I keep revising: asking my study partner, "Are you sure you've read the chapter?" when he just made an error that suggests he hasn't. Sometimes that feels like a sincere check; other times it feels like a disguised accusation, and I cannot tell which feature decides.

My initial guess was that the distinction tracks speaker vulnerability—whether I risk being shown wrong—but that fails, since in the text-message case I am vulnerable and the question is still real, while in the essay case I am protected and it feels rhetorical. Another guess is that it depends on whether the speaker has already revealed her position, but then the first seminar case should feel rhetorical and it does not. So I am wondering whether there is a principle, perhaps involving what I want to call a "copycat" emotional valence that mimics the posture of inquiry without carrying its commitment to revision, that captures these verdicts. If there is such a principle, what does it say about the study-partner case? Or must I abandon one of these judgments—perhaps the essay judgment or the seminar judgment—to preserve simplicity? I would like either a single precise principle with its motivating clauses, or a small set of competing candidates, each tied to the case that supports it, together with an honest statement of which of my verdicts each one cannot accommodate.
