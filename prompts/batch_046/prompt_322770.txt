I'm taking an advanced seminar on the historiography of philosophy, and I've adopted a strong anti-Whig thesis: not merely that progress narratives distort the past, but that the very selection of figures, problems, and texts as philosophically significant is structurally determined by present concerns in a way that makes genuinely non-teleological conceptual history impossible. Before I defend this in my term paper, I want a rigorous assessment of its vulnerability. What would have to be true for this inevitability claim to be wrong? Specifically, address (i) concrete historiographical practices or patterns of archival recovery that could show selection criteria operating independently of present values; (ii) formal properties of narrative—perhaps in reception history or conceptual genealogy—that might allow reconstruction without an implicit endpoint; and (iii) whether alternative models, such as contextualist or genealogical approaches, actually escape teleology or merely disguise it. Rank these potential defeaters by decisiveness: which would be fatal to the thesis, which merely awkward, and which defenders of the view would concede as damaging but not conclusive? Finally, assess whether the thesis risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent counter-example can be redescribed as hidden presentism—and whether that insulation counts as a defect for a historiographical claim rather than for a metaphysical one.
