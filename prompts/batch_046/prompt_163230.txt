Take the disappearance of public executions in Britain and France — not the abolition of capital punishment itself, which is a different story, but the move from open-air hangings watched by huge crowds to private executions behind prison walls. Run it through the Appiah / Williams moral-revolution lens: did it end because arguments convinced people it was wrong, because state interests or costs changed, or because being seen at a hanging became dishonorable for respectable folk? Be honest about which mechanisms acutally drive the British shift around 1868 and the French one in 1939, and where the frame breaks down. Did honor dynamics hit the crowd, the executioners, or the bureaucrats? And does this case back the idea that revolutions run on honor rather than reason, or does it just show state rationalization doing all the work? I want the maping to be tight — who is the actor, what counts as the moral norm here, and what the theory predicts versus what actually happened.
