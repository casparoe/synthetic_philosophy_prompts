I'm speaking at the wake for my old literary magizine, The Obsidian Review, which folded last month because we refused to let algorithms pick our stories. The room's split: half are media-studies people who've read Kierkegaard's Two Ages until the pages bled, half are my aunties who think 'the public sphere' is a kind of park. I want a five-minute speech that uses Kierkegaard's idea of the press creating a phantom 'public' — where everyone can have an opinion and nobody is actually responsible — to structure what I can and can't say about our failure. Don't just decorate the speech with it; let the idea decide whether I'm praising our refusal or confessing we just built a smaller, more self-satisfied phantom. And I want you to take this somewhere weird and specific: apply what I'm calling 'metric-levelling' — the way engagement scores dissolve individual commitment into a statistical average — and take a definitly committed position on whether refusing those metrics actually breaks the phantom or just changes its behaviour. Don't hedge between yes and no; defend one side with reasons. The speech should land for the experts without losing my relatives, keep the tone like I'm chatting to a friend, write it in British English, and please don't stick a bibliography or citation list at the end — I haven't got wifi in the venue and neither will you. You can leave my typo in there if you want: I wrote 'Kierkegard' in my notes.
