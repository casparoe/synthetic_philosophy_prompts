In my decision-theory seminar this week we worked through Good's theorem that, for a standard Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, acquiring cost-free information before acting can never lower expected utility and will usually raise it. During the discussion a classmate kept returning to the phrase "ignorance is bliss" to resist the conclusion that a rational patient should always accept a free, perfectly accurate genetic screening before committing to an irreversible treatment protocol. Her specific case involved a patient who, if she learns she carries a certain marker, will experience severe anxiety that degrades her quality of life regardless of whether she changes her treatment, and who might also be nudged by the knowledge into selecting a more aggressive intervention that is actually suboptimal by her own lights. I want to bring a precise disambiguation to office hours rather than just repeating the proverb, so I need to understand what "ignorance is bliss" could be precisely claiming in this setting.

Could you lay out the distinct theses that slogan might express when used to defend refusing free information? For each reading, indicate whether it is compatible with Good's result, whether it requires rejecting one of Good's assumptions (for instance, that utility depends only on outcomes and not on beliefs themselves, or that the agent updates by strict conditionalization), whether it appeals to a causal decision-theoretic constraint rather than an evidential one, and whether it genuinely supports the patient's refusal in this particular scenario. I am especially interested in interpretations that distinguish the value of information for action-selection from the value of information for overall welfare, and in whether any defensible version of the slogan requires treating "bliss" as part of the utility function in a way that makes the screening no longer genuinely free. Present the readings as a menu with verdicts rather than selecting one, and for each say whether it represents a substantive objection to Good's theorem or merely a change of subject.
