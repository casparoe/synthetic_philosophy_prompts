I am preparing for an advanced seminar on mechanist theories of mind, and a colleague recently sketched an interpretation of the Lucas–Penrose argument that I find intriguing but not fully convincing. Rather than treating Gödel’s result as proof that human mathematicians can “see” truths that formal systems cannot prove, she reframes the issue around the dynamics of self-referential belief revision. If a mechanised agent is modelled as a consistent formal system S, then S can construct a Gödel sentence G(S) that is true but unprovable within S; yet if the agent then incorporates the recognition that G(S) holds into an expanded system S′, the very same construction applies to S′, generating an infinite regress of epistemic expansion that no fixed algorithm can complete. She takes this to support what she calls “open-ended anti-mechanism” — the claim that human cognition is not necessarily non-computational in principle, but that any adequate computational model must be indefinitely extendible, lacking the closure property required for a complete mechanism.

Please argue for this open-ended anti-mechanist position, but do so by addressing three specific objections that are usually raised against the classical Lucas–Penrose argument. First, explain why the standard reply that human mathematicians are themselves inconsistent does not undermine the open-ended version, given that the argument concerns structural extendibility rather than actual consistency. Second, show how this reinterpretation avoids Penrose’s stronger claim that human insight involves non-computational physics, by restricting the conclusion to epistemic architecture rather than ontology. Third, address the worry that an algorithm could simply be designed to iterate through successive expansions S, S′, S′′, … and explain why such an iterative algorithm still fails to capture the single-agent epistemic unity that the argument presupposes. In constructing your reasoning, please use British English and avoid relying on bibliographic references or citations; develop the argument from first principles, and feel free to introduce a concrete illustrative scenario — for instance, a dialogue between two researchers attempting to formalise mathematical intuition — if it helps to clarify the structure of the regress.
