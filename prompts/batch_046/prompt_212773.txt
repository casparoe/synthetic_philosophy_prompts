After a long and somewhat heated argument last night with a friend about whether Frege’s puzzle about identity statements can be coherently extended beyond proper names to institutional texts—rather than remaining confined to canonical examples like “Hesperus” and “Phosphorus”—I am now grading exam answers for an upper-division philosophy of language course and want a rigorous second opinion. You are an expert philosopher of language and logic with extensive experience grading graduate-level work; please think carefully and systematically. Before you evaluate the answer below, take a moment to brainstorm the core commitments of Frege’s original distinction between sense and reference, the standard astronomical case, and the most common subtle missteps students make when they try to export that framework into bureaucratic or legal contexts.

Here is the exam question I assigned:

First, explain why the identity statement “Hesperus is Hesperus” differs in cognitive value from “Hesperus is Phosphorus,” despite both names referring to Venus, using Frege’s distinction between sense and reference. Second, apply this framework to the following institutional case: A federal agency issues two internal compliance directives, Directive Alpha and Directive Beta. Although both were drafted by the same senior bureaucrat and share identical regulatory intent, they are filed under different departmental codes and carry different procedural descriptions—one mandates quarterly audits, the other mandates annual reviews. Staff members treat them as imposing distinct obligations. When an internal review reveals that both directives were authored by the same individual, the staff does not revise their interpretation of the obligations; they continue to treat the directives as functionally separate. Does Frege’s sense/reference distinction illuminate why the discovery of shared authorship fails to collapse the two obligations into one? If not, what does the case reveal about the limits of extending the puzzle to institutional language?

Below is one student’s answer. I need you to grade it precisely and take a fully committed position: assign a specific letter grade, justify it point by point, and do not present a menu of possible grades or leave the final call to me. Identify exactly where the answer is correct, where it commits subtle errors that a hasty grader might let slide—particularly any conflation of Frege’s objective notion of sense with Gricean conversational implicature, any mischaracterization of sense as purely subjective or psychological rather than objective, and any misapplication of “reference” to the bureaucrat-author rather than to the regulatory content or obligation itself. Assess whether these errors are merely imprecise or whether they fatally undermine the second half of the answer. Also state clearly whether the answer demonstrates sufficient mastery to pass at this level, and identify the single correction that would most improve it. Please conclude with a brief but definite statement on whether Frege’s framework can be repaired for institutional texts or whether the student’s misapplication shows it should be left confined to its canonical domain.

Student answer:

Frege argued that identity statements can be informative because a name has both a reference—the object it picks out—and a sense, which is the mode of presentation. So “Hesperus” and “Phosphorus” both refer to Venus, but they present Venus differently: one as the evening star, one as the morning star. That explains why learning they are identical is genuinely informative.

In the agency case, the directives both refer to the same bureaucrat, just as the names refer to Venus. But the staff treats them differently because the departmental codes and audit schedules present the obligations differently. So the “sense” of each directive is its procedural description, and the “reference” is the author. When they learn the author is the same, it doesn’t change anything, because the sense hasn’t changed—just like learning Hesperus is Phosphorus doesn’t change the sense of either name. Therefore Frege’s framework applies well: the shared reference doesn’t determine shared cognitive significance, and the staff is right to keep treating them separately.

However, I think there’s a Gricean element too: the staff might be implicating different things by filing them in different departments, and that’s why they don’t merge them. But even without that, Frege’s point holds.

One problem is that Frege thought sense was subjective, in the mind of the speaker, so different staff members might have different senses for the same directive. That makes the framework messy for institutional texts, where we want objective obligations. So the case shows that extending Frege to institutions requires making sense intersubjective, which Frege didn’t really do.
