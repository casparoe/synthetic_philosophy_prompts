I've been working through the assigned readings for a graduate seminar in philosophy of science—specifically, the section where Quine dismantles the analytic-synthetic distinction in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism"—and I'm trying to figure out how far the damage extends. If I understand the argument correctly, Quine shows that any attempt to isolate analytic truths (those true in virtue of meaning alone) either collapses into an unexplained notion of synonymy or presupposes the very logical reducibility it's meant to justify. This strikes me as more destructive than it first appears, because the standard philosophical explanation for why arithmetic and logical truths are necessary has always been that they are analytic: they hold regardless of empirical fact precisely because they depend only on the meanings of their terms. Once Quine removes that boundary, it seems there is no longer any principled basis for treating mathematical truths as necessary rather than as extremely well-confirmed but ultimately revisable empirical generalizations—no different in kind from theoretical physics. So my question is this: for a philosopher of mathematics who wants to preserve the necessity of arithmetic, is the only honest post-Quinean option to reconstruct modality entirely outside the analytic-synthetic framework (for instance, through metaphysical necessity), or can Quine's own apparatus—perhaps his account of ontological commitment and the web of belief—provide unexpected resources for defending a non-analytic but still non-contingent status for mathematical truths? I'm writing a short essay on whether Quine's holism inadvertently undermines his own skepticism about modality, and I want to make sure I'm not misrepresenting the scope of his original argument.
