As a retired experimental physicist who spent three decades studying sequential decay chains in radioactive isotopes, I have recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of rationality and decision theory, and I find the formal architecture of the centipede game—where rational agents should, by backward induction, terminate interaction at the first decision node—strikingly analogous to a cascade that can halt or propagate. Yet in any physical system, perfect epistemic closure is an idealization: there is always thermal noise, detector error, or probabilistic tunneling. I wish to translate this intuition into a rigorous assessment for an upper-level master’s seminar on rationality, but I need a question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard paradox and its textbook resolution.

Please design a take-home essay examination, carrying thirty-five marks and requiring approximately eight hundred words, in which students analyze a modified six-node, two-player centipede game with the usual alternating payoff structure. The essential twist—which they must treat as an epistemic restructuring rather than a superficial parameter change—is that each player assigns a small independent prior ε (where 0 < ε < 1/2) to the possibility that the opponent is not fully rational, specifically that the opponent might continue one step further through bounded computation, curiosity, or noise. Consequently, common knowledge of perfect rationality holds only with probability (1−ε)² at each stage rather than with certainty. The exam prompt should ask students whether backward induction survives exactly, survives approximately with a modified termination point, or collapses entirely; it should require them to trace the belief-revision dynamics across nodes and to distinguish this probabilistic restructuring from ordinary strategic risk aversion.

Together with the exam prompt itself, provide a model answer of about seven hundred words and a detailed marking scheme that separates an A-grade response—which must explicitly address the degradation of common knowledge, describe or derive the approximate induction path under ε-perturbation, and avoid conflating probabilistic belief revision with risk preferences—from a B-grade response, which correctly describes the unperturbed paradox but treats ε merely as an ad hoc addition. Specify a seventy-two-hour writing window, explicitly forbid bibliographies or citations, and frame the task so that success depends on understanding the interaction between epistemic conditions and rational choice rather than on memorized labels. Present everything in a formal, polished register suitable for direct inclusion in a course syllabus.
