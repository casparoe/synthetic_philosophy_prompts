In a recent philosophy of religion seminar, a discussant dismissed objections to widespread suffering by invoking what she called the Leibnizian maxim—this is the best of all possible worlds—therefore any apparent evil must be compensated by some greater good, and to complain is to misunderstand modal optimality. I want to understand whether the slogan can actually carry that dismissive weight, but I first need to know what precise claims it could be expressing. Could you disambiguate “this world is the best of all possible worlds” into a menu of distinct, precisely formulated theses, ranging from tautological or definitional readings to substantive metaphysical, axiological, and normative ones? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivially true, empirically empty, theologically loaded, or genuinely substantive; whether it is defensible on commitments standardly attributed to Leibniz—such as the principle of sufficient reason, the principle of plenitude, or a specific metric that balances variety against simplicity; and, crucially, whether it licenses the inference that moral complaint about suffering is irrational. In particular, distinguish global optimality (no other possible world has greater overall value) from local optimality (each particular feature is necessary for the global maximum) from a normative-political reading (the actual status quo is maximally good and should not be altered). Then tell me which of these readings people typically mean when they deploy the phrase to shut down ethical objection, whether that reading is consistent with Leibniz’s own view that divine selection operates through a calculus of compossibility rather than through a simple linear ranking of worlds, and what a defender of that specific reading would have to show to make the dismissive move valid rather than a non sequitur.
