I'm putting together notes for an essay on the limits of Friedman's defense of unrealistic assumptions—often summarized as the F-twist—when it is applied outside predictive physics and into policy-facing social science. The standard line is that a model's premises need not be descriptively accurate so long as its outputs match observation; I want to press the idea that this separation between instrument and target collapses in domains where the model itself becomes part of the causal machinery it describes. Specifically, I am looking at climate-economic integrated assessment models that treat national economies as single representative agents with perfect intertemporal optimization. In conventional physics, an unrealistic assumption like a frictionless plane is methodologically insulated: running the experiment does not change the coefficient of friction in the world. But in climate governance, the representative-agent framework does not merely approximate behavior; it is written into cost-benefit protocols and carbon-pricing mechanisms, so that institutional reality is gradually reshaped to match the simplifying fiction. The unrealistic assumption therefore becomes performatively self-fulfilling—not in the benign sense that predictions come true, but in the restrictive sense that the world is narrowed to fit the model's ontology, undermining the very external validity that Friedman's defense requires.

I sketch a possible distinction to save part of the argument: unrealistic assumptions might be permissible in closed measurement contexts, where the model is tested against an independently existing target, but become dangerous in open governance contexts, where the model is embedded in the feedback loop it studies. I am unsure whether this distinction actually holds. One worry is that even laboratory physics involves some performative framing—choosing what counts as a measurement already shapes the phenomenon—so that the boundary between closed and open is more of a gradient than a firewall. Another worry is that this objection sounds like a variant of the Lucas critique, but I think it is structurally different: the Lucas problem is about parameter instability under policy change, whereas my concern is about ontological entanglement, where the model's categories become causally efficacious in constructing the reality they purport to represent.

I'd like your comments on the coherence and accuracy of this line. First, does the performative-feedback objection genuinely break the logic of the F-twist, or could Friedman accommodate it by saying that predictive success in the policy domain is all that matters, even if the success is partly self-generated? Second, is my closed versus open distinction a useful refinement, or does it collapse under scrutiny? Third, I am trying to avoid framing this as yet another complaint about economics lacking realism; my aim is to show that unrealistic assumptions are not uniformly harmless or harmful, but that their methodological status depends on the institutional architecture surrounding the model. Does the essay idea actually achieve that specificity, or does it drift back into a generic anti-formalism? Any suggestions for sharpening the middle position, or for ways it might be mistaken for familiar criticisms, would be especially helpful.
