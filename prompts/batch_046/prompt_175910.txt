I chair the interdisciplinary funding review board of the Meridian Institute for Evolutionary and Social Research. We must vote on whether to approve a five-year exclusive grant for a program titled “Spandrel-First Institutional Epistemology.” The applicants—evolutionary anthropologists and organizational theorists—argue that several behaviors currently treated as adaptations in peer-review and committee governance, especially the gradual drift of evaluation criteria away from original mandates (which they call “epistemic spandrels”), are structural byproducts of hierarchical information-filtering architectures rather than functional responses to selection pressures for quality control. They invoke Gould and Lewontin’s architectural analogy but extend it to bureaucratic cognition: just as architectural spandrels are necessary geometric consequences of arches rather than designed decorative features, they claim that criteria drift is a necessary geometric consequence of layered approval structures, carrying no adaptive function and therefore requiring no evolutionary explanation. They ask us to fund only research that treats such organizational phenomena as spandrels and to reject all competing adaptationist proposals in social behavior for the grant period.

The board is split. One faction holds that this corrects an overuse of adaptationist narratives and opens legitimate methodological pluralism; the other warns that treating every institutional feature as a non-adaptive byproduct disables predictive causal modeling and risks reducing science to descriptive taxonomy. I need to circulate a formal written opinion before our vote recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions. The memo should state a concrete decision with a defined scope or timeline, engage seriously with the strongest argument from the losing side, and apply the spandrel-adaptationism distinction precisely—as an analytical framework rather than a generic metaphor—without requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or citations that a respondent without web access could not verify. Please conclude with a brief self-critical note stating what empirical finding or conceptual argument would change your recommendation, and identifying the specific point in your reasoning where failure is most likely.
