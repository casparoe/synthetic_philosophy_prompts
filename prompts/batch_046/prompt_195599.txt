Consider the institutional architecture that hospital clinical-ethics committees and nursing professional guidelines apply to what they term moral distress. The practice defines that distress as psychological harm arising when a clinician knows the single correct ethical action but is prevented from taking it by hierarchical pressure, resource scarcity, or institutional rules. Ethics consultation reports are structured around identifying that one correct course and then recommending the removal of barriers; their standard documentation templates include fields for the decisive recommendation and for advocacy steps, but contain no formal category for an irreducible conflict between two duties that remain equally binding even after full deliberation. Parallel to this, conscientious-objection policies permit staff to refuse participation in a procedure, yet only on the condition that the objector can cite one clear, overriding moral principle that renders the procedure wrong—not on the grounds that the situation itself presents a genuinely dilemmatic choice in which every available option violates a serious obligation.

That pairing is philosophically puzzling: the institution systematically frames moral suffering as the product of an external obstacle to a pre-existing moral fact, never as a rational response to an objective conflict of duties. What implicit theory of practical reason, moral knowledge, and the ontology of moral conflict does this architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the view that makes both the moral-distress framework and the conscientious-objection criteria hang together. Be precise about what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely accommodates: does it deny that genuine moral dilemmas exist, or only deny that they call for a distinctive institutional remedy? Evaluate whether the reconstructed position is internally coherent, especially in light of cases—such as triage under absolute scarcity, or conflicts between patient autonomy and non-maleficence—where two moral demands appear to collide without either being overridden. Finally, take a definite position: if the institution replaced its implicit anti-dilemma theory with a framework that accepts some moral conflicts as genuinely unresolvable, what concrete revisions would be required in ethics-committee reporting structures, in the criteria for conscientious refusal, and in staff-support and debriefing protocols? Defend each proposed change with reasons, and state clearly where you think the revised practice would be more defensible, and where less so, than the current one.
