As a novelist composing a work of historical fiction set in the long eighteenth century, I have been returning to Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography — specifically to Part Two, where he describes his scheme for moral perfection through the sequential mastery of thirteen virtues, one per week in rotation. Franklin admits, however, that Order perpetually eluded him, and he compares himself to an axe that remains speckled however often it is ground against the stone. I am struggling to understand the philosophical tension concealed within this admission: on the one hand, he frames virtue as a rational, modular project susceptible to methodical self-correction; on the other, his failure suggests that human character possesses an irreducible density that resists such piecemeal engineering. Would you help me unpack what this passage reveals about the relationship between systematic discipline and the holistic nature of moral personality? In particular, I should like you to consider whether Franklin's rotational method implicitly treats virtues as isolable skills, and whether that assumption stands in tension with the view — which I understand to be a significant, if contested, modern position — that moral virtues are not discrete competencies but integrated dispositions that can develop only through sustained, contextual practice rather than through sequential drill. Please provide your explanation in British English, without supplying a bibliography, reading list, or named citations to secondary scholarship.
