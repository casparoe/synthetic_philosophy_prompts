I'm planning a 90-minute session for first-year philosophy graduate students who have just completed a course in modal logic—possible worlds, accessibility relations, Leibniz's law—but have never studied philosophy of language. The aim is to teach the Kripkean distinction between rigid and non-rigid designators and its consequence for necessary a posteriori truths, using water's identity with H2O as the continuous thread. I need the explanatory plan, not the lecture: the exact dependency order in which ideas must be introduced so that each is intelligible only once the prior one is secure; the single carrying example that should run through the session and the specific properties it must possess (it should be an empirical discovery with one side that functions like a proper name and one that functions like a definite description, and it should behave differently in a nearby counterfactual world where the descriptive condition is satisfied by something else) and the properties it must lack (it should not be resolvable by pure logic or by direct perceptual comparison alone); the misconceptions these students will predictably bring or form, in the order they will arise—beginning with the conflation of epistemic and modal necessity, then the assumption that proper names are synonymous with definite descriptions, then an overgeneralization about natural-kind terms behaving rigidly in identical ways—and the precise point in the arc where each should be headed off; the check for understanding that demonstrates they have grasped the structure rather than repeated the slogan, which should ask them to apply the rigidity distinction to a case not discussed in the session; and a clear statement of what should be left out at this level and why. Give a committed answer: choose one definite arc, one example, and rank the misconceptions by pedagogical danger, with your reasons; do not present options or ask me to choose.
