I've come round to a pretty specific take on the absent-minded driver problem: I think it's not actually a knock-down argument against standard expected utility theory, but just a self-locating belief puzzle that gets misread as a decision-theoretic paradox. Once you model the driver's uncertainty about which exit he's reached as a centered-world problem — basically Sleeping Beauty with actions bolted on — the optimal strategy falls out of ordinary Bayesian updating over centered propositions, with no special need for "plan-based" rationality or pre-commitment devices. The driver exits when his credance in being at the right location crosses the threshold, exactly as act-based theory predicts.

What would count against this? I'm not asking for the standard intuition that he "should have decided in advance"; I want the serious defeaters. Are there formal results showing that no framework for self-locating belief can reproduce the planning solution without smuggling in commitment mechanisms? Have philosophers of action argued that centered belief updates can't guide behaviour because actions aren't propositional in the right sense? Or are there empirical studies — maybe in psychology with forgotten-state tasks — showing people don't behave like the model predicts? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether defenders of the planning view would concede any of them or just dismiss them as changing the subject. Also, be honest: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability, for instance by treating any counterexample as just "using the wrong prior," and is that actually a problem?

Please write in British English, and say what specific argument or finding would change your mind, or where your reasoning here is probably weakest — I think my treatment of the Sleeping Beauty analogy might be shakier than I'm admitting.
