I’ve been helping my cousin run a small grant fund for local environmental projects, and lately we’ve been arguing about how to tell whether a research group we’re funding is actually advancing knowledge or just moving the goalposts after every failure. A friend mentioned Imre Lakatos’s idea of scientific research programmes—the notion that a theory has a central hard core you don’t abandon easily, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary claims that can change, and that the key distinction is between a progressive program, which changes its belt in ways that generate surprising new predictions that then pan out, and a degenerating one, which only tweaks its belt to absorb failures after the fact. I don’t have a philosophy background, but the framework feels like it could solve our practical problem: we review proposals on three-year cycles, we have about ninety minutes per decision, nobody on our panel has studied philosophy of science, and both I and a community stakeholder have to sign off. The stakes are moderate—mostly local taxpayer support—but the decision repeats every quarter, so I need a reusable procedure, not a one-time opinion.

Please turn Lakatos’s framework into an actual decision procedure my review team can run on any proposal. Lay it out as ordered questions: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure outputs a clear do-not-renew, where it outputs renew, and exactly where it must stop and hand judgment to a person because the issue is values rather than facts. Translate the technical terms into language someone without the theory can use, keep only the distinctions that actually change the funding decision, drop anything that doesn’t survive that simplification, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire or give an answer I won’t like. Then apply it to three seeded cases. First, a fungal-network soil-remediation group that has started predicting specific toxin-breakdown rates under local conditions and then confirmed them in the field. Second, a long-running chemical-remediation project that keeps revising its dosage and delivery models after each failed trial but has not produced any novel, risky prediction that succeeded in five years. Third, a participatory water-monitoring program that blends traditional ecological knowledge with sensor data, where the central commitment is not a specific biological claim but a principle of community authority over interpretation, and where adjustments to methods keep happening but do not clearly yield testable predictions that could fail. For each case, show precisely where the procedure lands, including the one where my personal inclination—to support community-based work regardless—conflicts with the procedure’s output. Finally, tell me plainly what this procedure cannot do, especially for research that is not conventional laboratory science, and write the answer in American English.
