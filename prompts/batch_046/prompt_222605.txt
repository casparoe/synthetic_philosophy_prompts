A municipal heritage board I advise is deciding whether to grant protected status to an annual community ritual that was first assembled in 1983. A local archivist reconstructed it from three unrelated nineteenth-century agricultural practices, added ceremonial clothing borrowed from a 1920s regional pageant, and composed a collective song that had never previously existed. For four decades, residents have treated the ritual as the core expression of their shared identity: it is taught in schools, used to welcome newcomers, and experienced by participants as a direct link to ancestral life. The board's charter requires proof of unbroken transmission since before 1900 for protected status, but a faction proposes amending the criterion to continuous collective belief in continuity, arguing that once a practice is sincerely lived as ancient, its documentary origin becomes irrelevant. Opponents counter that this collapses heritage protection into mere popularity, erasing the distinction between recovered history and invented tradition. I need a recommendation that does not simply split the difference. Specifically: is there a philosophically coherent way to distinguish a community whose identity is genuinely constituted by this practice from one that merely performs it, without relying on the false premise that all traditions are either purely discovered or purely fabricated? Apply the relevant conceptual framework—without treating invented tradition or imagined community as labels to invoke, but as analytical structures—to determine whether the amended criterion captures something real about collective identity, or whether it dissolves the very idea of heritage into present-day sentiment.
