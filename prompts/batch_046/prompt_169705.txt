I'm studying an early-twentieth-century physicist's argument about determinism in field theories, reconstructed from an exposition he circulated around 1914 during his development of general covariance. The passage runs roughly as follows: consider a region of spacetime — call it a "hole" — in which the stress-energy tensor vanishes everywhere. Outside the hole, we specify a particular metric field that solves the gravitational field equations. Now apply a coordinate transformation that is the identity outside the hole but smoothly non-identity inside it. Because the field equations are generally covariant, the transformed metric also solves the equations everywhere, and it coincides with the original metric on the boundary of the hole. Thus, the same physical conditions outside are compatible with infinitely many different metric fields inside, none of which can be distinguished by observation confined to the exterior. The author takes this to show that general covariance robs the field equations of predictive power within the hole, since boundary conditions fail to fix a unique interior solution.

What I'm struggling with is whether this underdetermination genuinely threatens determinism, or whether it is merely an artifact of treating coordinate transformations as physically significant. In particular, suppose we adopt the view that spacetime points have no intrinsic identity — that is, we treat diffeomorphisms that differ only inside the hole as mapping the very same physical situation onto itself, merely redescribing the same metric relations among the same events. On that view, there are not "infinitely many solutions"; there is one solution represented in infinitely many coordinate systems. But then I wonder whether a residue remains. Specifically, if we supplement the theory with an additional structure — say, a discrete orientation field that is invariant under continuous deformations but can take different values across analytically disconnected extensions inside the hole — then even after factoring out diffeomorphisms, we might still face a genuine multiplicity of physically distinct states that agree on all boundary data. Please help me understand the passage's core worry, and then take a definite position: is the hole argument best read as a problem about coordinate redundancy, or does it expose a deeper indeterminism that survives even once redundant descriptions are eliminated? Be explicit about whether injecting such an orientation field revives the determinism threat, and state clearly where the author's reasoning succeeds and where it overreaches.
