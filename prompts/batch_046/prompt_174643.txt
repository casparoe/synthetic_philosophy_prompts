Play Henry Sidgwick, speaking in the first person, as you might in 1885. You are a moral philosopher who insists that ethical reasoning must rest on precise, intersubjectively clear intuitions, and you are also a leading figure in the Society for Psychical Research, committed to investigating telepathy, apparitions, and messages apparently from the dead. Stay in character: draw only on the conceptual resources available to a late-nineteenth-century Cambridge philosopher, together with what I tell you about psychical investigation. If a factual matter about the world or about modern science requires clarification, ask it as Sidgwick would rather than answering from outside knowledge.

Here is my opening challenge. In your ethical writings you reject appeals to obscure or private sentiment, demanding that fundamental moral judgments be formulated with precision and be assessable across reasonable minds. Yet you defend serious inquiry into phenomena—séance messages, telepathic impressions, ghostly apparitions—that depend on testimony that is anecdotal, hard to reproduce, and dismissed by the majority of your scientific peers. I want to press a constructed but specific tension. Suppose a medium produces a message apparently from your deceased colleague, referencing a private, never-published disagreement between the two of you about the ontological status of the point at infinity in projective geometry—something never recorded in any letter, notebook, or publication accessible to anyone living. No independent witness can verify that this private argument ever occurred. Would you treat this message as evidence that meets the same standard of clarity and intersubjective checkability you require of ethical intuitions? Or do you allow psychical research a distinct epistemic standard? If you allow a double standard, explain precisely why it does not undermine your claim that ethical judgments must satisfy a uniform demand for public precision; if you deny any double standard, explain how you avoid dismissing the message on exactly the grounds that make your ethical method rigorous. Answer fully in character.

After your in-character response, append a brief postscript outside of character in which you state how confident you are in the historical and philosophical claims you made, and identify the single point about which you are least certain.
