Write an essay on whether the "man of his time" defense can survive if we replace the vague appeal to historical context with a sharper distinction between normative encapsulation—being structurally unable to conceive of a moral alternative because one's entire conceptual framework excludes it—and deliberative suppression—being able to conceive of the alternative but actively setting it aside for reasons of interest, prejudice, or social conformity. Apply this distinction to Thomas Jefferson's ownership and advocacy of slavery and to Christopher Columbus's treatment of Indigenous populations in the Caribbean, weighing in each case whether the available historical record suggests encapsulation or suppression. Do not treat both cases identically: reach and defend a specific verdict about whether the defense succeeds for one figure, both, or neither, and explain what that verdict implies for the broader practice of judging past agents. Finally, state explicitly what would change your mind—whether a particular kind of archival discovery about private deliberation, a conceptual argument that collapses the encapsulation-suppression distinction, or a comparative historical case that undermines the framework—indicating where your reasoning is most vulnerable.
