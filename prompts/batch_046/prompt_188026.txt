As a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up philosophical logic, I serve on our university's Committee for Emerging Research Architectures. We have received a proposal to deploy a Reflexive Confidence Architecture—designated RCA—in an AI-assisted peer-review system for preprint screening. The architecture implements a modified Löbian inference rule: whenever the system proves, within an internal proof module, that trusting its own reasoning mechanism is rational, it is authorized to increase the epistemic weight of every conclusion reached through that mechanism in its belief-update module. The investigators argue that this produces calibrated self-trust without circularity, because the proof of rationality is executed in an object-language module formally distinct from the module that applies the confidence boost. Several committee members, myself included, are concerned that this design precisely instantiates the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents: an agent that applies Löb's conditions to its own doxastic rules cannot treat the resulting self-endorsement as genuinely grounded, since the proof of reliability already presupposes the very reasoning standards it seeks to validate, regardless of modular partitioning. The proposal therefore requires us to determine whether a multi-module architecture genuinely blocks the circularity or merely relocates it across boundaries, and to reach an institutional decision with actionable conditions if any.

Please draft the committee's formal decision memo—recommending approval, rejection, or conditional approval—with reasoning to which the investigators could directly respond. Your memo should: (1) state the bottom-line recommendation unambiguously; (2) explain how the Löbian obstacle applies, or fails to apply, to a system partitioned into proof and belief-update modules; (3) assess the investigators' modular-distinction defense, treating it either as a successful dissolution or as a relocation of the circularity; (4) present the strongest consideration favoring the opposite outcome and explain precisely why it is outweighed, or, if your recommendation accommodates it, how the conditions address it; and (5) if you recommend anything other than outright rejection, specify concrete, enforceable conditions—such as domain restrictions, mandatory external audit nodes, or hard epistemic caps—under which deployment would be responsible. Write in the formal, precise register appropriate to an institutional decision document, avoiding appeals to authority and avoiding requests for bibliographic material.
