I'm a student in an upper-division metaphysics course focusing on material constitution, and I've been going back and forth with two classmates, Maya and Leo, about the classic statue-and-clay case, though our disagreement has taken a somewhat specific form. Maya defends a coincidence view: the statue and the lump of bronze (or clay) are numerically distinct objects that occupy the same region. She emphasizes modal differences—if the statue is melted down and recast as a vase, the statue is destroyed, yet the bronze persists—plus property differences, such as being admired, being the subject of an art-historical narrative, or falling under a museum's restoration charter. Leo rejects coincidence. He argues that there is only one concrete object, the material arranged statue-wise, and that apparent differences in persistence conditions are just kind-relative descriptions of that single thing's career. He appeals to what he calls functional continuity: an artifact's identity is fixed not by its exact parts but by its role in an institutional practice of restoration and display, governed by a curatorial charter specifying which replacements preserve the original work. So if the statue loses an arm and it is replaced according to the charter, there is no second object that survives; rather, the one object continues under the sortal statue while its material parts change, just as a ship can survive plank replacement.

Maya replies that Leo's move collapses the distinction between the concrete object and an abstract type or plan. She proposes a material-trace constraint: if every original particle can be replaced and the result is still called the original statue, then the statue is not constituted by that particular matter at all, but is something like a functional kind instantiated in successive materials. She thinks this is a reductio of Leo's one-object view, because it makes the statue's concrete location and causal powers mysterious—why should we say the statue weighs 200 kilograms now if none of the original 200 kilograms remain? Leo responds that weight and location are also kind-relative: we weigh the object-under-the-sort-statue, which includes whatever parts currently satisfy the functional role, not a fixed lump. He adds that Maya's coincidence view struggles with causal overdetermination—if both the statue and the bronze cause the display case to creak, we have redundant causation—whereas his view avoids this by recognizing only one cause.

They asked me to referee, and I genuinely cannot tell who is winning. Please adjudicate the disagreement point by point: on modal differences and kind-relative persistence, on whether functional continuity avoids coincidence or collapses into type-instantiation, on the material-trace constraint and its reductio force, and on the causal-overdetermination worry. Say who is right about each specific point, who is more right overall, and what both are missing—perhaps about how institutional practices actually constrain ontology, or about whether the debate assumes mereological essentialism for artifacts that few metaphysicians now accept. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
