As a journalist who covers ideas and social norms, I recently listened to a debate between two philosophers—call them Mira and Jonas—about whether everyday conventions like tipping, queuing, and obeying pedestrian signals carry genuine moral obligations for visitors, or whether treating them as binding is a category error. They were not arguing about particular laws, but about the conceptual status of local convention itself. I am asking you to adjudicate their exchange point by point, because I need to understand where the argument actually turns, not merely who sounds more reasonable.

Mira opened with a framework she calls "normative hospitality": when a visitor enters a social space governed by a coordination convention, they incur a localized deontic duty not because the convention tracks universal morality, but because others have arranged their lives and welfare around it. She argued that queuing is not merely prudent but morally required in contexts where people depend on orderly lines to access scarce goods; that in wage structures where gratuities constitute expected income, withholding a tip exploits a cooperative scheme that specific workers rely upon; and that this obligation is context-sensitive—where tipping is not embedded in economic architecture, insisting on it can violate local norms, so the moral weight travels with the coordination dependency rather than with the act itself.

Jonas replied that Mira is practicing "moral inflation." He said conventions generate prudential and social reasons—avoid annoyance, avoid penalties, show courtesy—but labeling them moral obligations dilutes the category and produces what he termed "reverse normative imperialism": forcing visitors to treat arbitrary local rules as if they were universal moral laws. He granted that cutting a line harms others, but insisted the harm is contingent on social expectation, not on intrinsic wrongness. On tipping, he argued that if a wage system is unjust, the moral demand is to reform wages, not to comply with the local convention; participating in an unjust scheme is not rendered obligatory simply because locals depend on it. He added that if queuing only matters because people care, then its force is sociological, not deontic.

Mira answered that Jonas mistakes the source of moral force for its absence. Promises are also contingent on social practices, she said, yet we treat them as binding; the past investment in a convention can supply present reasons without being a sunk-cost fallacy applied to ethics. She conceded that not every local quirk binds—she distinguished conventions that solve genuine coordination problems from mere etiquette—but maintained that once a convention is embedded in welfare-relevant expectations, violating it wrongs specific people in predictable ways.

Jonas countered that promises are explicitly undertaken, whereas conventions are inherited by arrival; the comparison fails because consent is missing. He also pressed a disanalogy: if queuing is genuinely moral, then jaywalking in societies with strict pedestrian-signal norms should be equally wrong, yet most people treat it as a minor legal or social infraction rather than a moral failing. This suggests, he said, that our actual judgments track law and custom, not morality, and that Mira is relabeling social pressure as ethical duty.
