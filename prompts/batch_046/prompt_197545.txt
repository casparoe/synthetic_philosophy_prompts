I hold the view that the standard anti-speciesist argument from marginal cases fails not because species membership carries intrinsic moral weight, but because moral considerably is not a unidimensional scalar—something like aggregate capacity for suffering—when in fact it is topologically structured: capacities such as sentience, autonomous agency, social reciprocity, and narrative selfhood generate distinct, non-commensurable moral claims that cluster around evolutionary lineages in ways that are neither arbitrary nor reducible to biological taxonomy alone. On this framework, differential treatment of a pig and a human infant need not be speciesism if the separation tracks genuine topological distance in moral-status-space rather than membership in Homo sapiens; yet treating a cognitively rich mammal and a human with genuinely equivalent capacities differently solely on taxonomic grounds remains wrong. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical discoveries about animal cognition, which formal results about the aggregation of incommensurable moral claims, which thought experiments involving chimeric or hybrid entities, and which arguments from disability ethics would genuinely threaten the framework rather than merely create awkwardness? In particular, does this topological pluralism risk collapsing either into standard sentientism or into disguised speciesism, and if so, is that collapse fatal? Please distinguish the defeaters that a committed defender of this view should themselves concede as damaging from those they can legitimately dismiss, rank them from decisive to merely uncomfortable, and assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and if it has, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a framework of this kind.
