I'm preparing a ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy of mind students who have encountered the classic exchange between Locke and William Molyneux in their survey course but have not yet studied modern cross-modal perception or sensory substitution. Rather than treating Molyneux's problem as a settled historical episode about innate ideas, I want to frame it as a live conceptual question about whether spatial shape recognition can transfer across qualitatively different sensory channels without an intermediate learning period. To do this, I have constructed a carrying case: imagine a congenitally blind subject, call her K, who has spent a decade navigating her environment through a high-resolution tactile-visual substitution system that renders camera input as dynamic patterns of vibration across her back. Over time she comes to identify cubes and spheres reliably through this mediated interface, initially experiencing the patterns as proximal sensations on her skin but eventually describing the objects as located in distal space. Then, through a hypothetical intervention, she acquires direct retinal function. The seminar's central question is whether K can immediately match the tactile cube she knows via substitution with a visually presented cube, or whether she must learn the correlation from scratch — and, more importantly, what this reveals about the conceptual distinction between mediated spatial mapping and direct qualitative recognition.

Plan the teaching sequence for this session: specify the order in which the conceptual components must be introduced — for instance, the difference between egocentric tactile mapping and allocentric visual framing, the separation of knowing spatial relations from recognizing a qualitative shape, and the precise sense in which immediate recognition is being tested — and justify the dependency structure, showing what must be secured before each subsequent layer can be added. Identify the one concrete scenario or modified thought experiment that should carry the entire discussion, detailing exactly which features it must possess and which it must deliberately lack so that the case does not collapse into an empirical question about neuroplasticity or revert to Locke's original formulation about innate ideas. Then map the predictable misconceptions: I expect students will conflate cross-modal transfer with innate knowledge, will treat sensory substitution as direct proof that Locke was wrong, and will confuse the phenomenological sense of distance with actual spatial cognition; for each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and defused. Include a final check — a specific task or question — that reveals whether the students have grasped the conceptual dependency rather than merely memorizing a historical answer, and explicitly list what empirical details about neural remapping or device mechanics should be openly refused at this philosophical level, with a brief rationale for each refusal.
