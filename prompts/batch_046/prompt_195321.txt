A friend and I spent the evening arguing about whether culture can genuinely cause historical change, specifically in connection with Weber's thesis linking Protestant values to the development of modern capitalism. Rather than continuing to talk past each other, I want us to formalize our disagreement as the document an adversarial collaboration should have produced.

Draft the joint statement that a Weberian cultural-causationist and a structural-materialist opponent could both sign. Name the camps precisely: Camp A holds that cultural values can function as autonomous causal factors in explaining large-scale economic development, not reducible to underlying material conditions; Camp B holds that cultural values are selected and sustained by structural conditions and operate only as mediating mechanisms within those structures, never as independent causes.

Format the document in two parts: first, a numbered section of agreed claims; second, a disagreements table.

In the agreed-claims section, include propositions both sides accept about what Weber actually argued, about the logical distinction between explaining an outcome with reference to actors' values versus explaining it with reference to property relations or resource distributions, and about what a causal claim in social explanation requires—specifically, whether counterfactual stability under value-replacement is a necessary condition.

In the disagreements table, present the precise formulations of disagreement, each phrased so that the respective camp would accept its own wording. Identify at least three cruxes: (1) whether value-causation requires counterfactual value-replacement stability; (2) whether societies with shared religious values but divergent land-tenure and inheritance structures in the seventeenth century demonstrate values operating independently of structure; and (3) whether invoking values in an explanation commits the explainer to a non-reductive ontology of culture.

For each crux, specify the concrete case, conceptual test, or comparative argument both sides agree is relevant—for crux (2), the comparison of regions with shared Calvinist commitments but different property regimes; for the others, specify the appropriate counterfactual or ontological test—and record in advance what each camp predicts about the result.

Add a section distinguishing merely verbal disagreements from substantive ones, specifically analyzing whether the statement "culture is a cause" is ambiguous among at least three readings: culture as efficient cause, culture as selecting cause, and culture as part of the causal mechanism.

The document should contain no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should rely solely on conceptual analysis and the shared historical and counterfactual cases described above. Write in the formal, polished register of a philosophical working paper.
