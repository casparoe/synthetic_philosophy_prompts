For a graduate seminar on democratic theory, specify the complete list of desiderata that an adequate theory of democratic boundary-drawing—determining who belongs to the demos—must satisfy. Address the following dimensions in your specification: (a) the normative ground of inclusion, distinguishing affected-interests, subjection-to-coercive-law, and co-authorship of collective decisions; (b) temporal scope, specifically how the theory treats membership claims by future generations who are determinately identifiable through present reproductive or institutional commitments but not yet born; (c) status differentiation, requiring the theory to distinguish citizens, long-term residents, and transient stakeholders without reducing them to a single metric of affectedness; and (d) consistency with self-determination and non-domination, including whether the theory permits nested demoi in which local, regional, and global bodies each claim authority over the same individual without a lexical priority rule. For each desideratum, provide the motivating conceptual tension or counterexample that makes it necessary, and mark whether it is non-negotiable (failure disqualifies the theory) or a negotiable preference of a particular camp, such as cosmopolitan universalism versus democratic closure. Finally, identify whether any subset of your non-negotiable desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—paying special attention to whether the all-affected principle generates an infinitely expansive demos, whether the subjection principle collapses into pure statism, and whether nested authority violates non-domination—and state which single desideratum does the most exclusionary work against mainstream candidates.
