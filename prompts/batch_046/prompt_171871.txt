I sit on the faculty senate of a small university governed by a charter ratified in 1972 and amended only twice since. Buried in Article VII, Section 4 is a provision authorizing the dean to suspend any academic program that fails to serve the university's essential purpose, with no required notice, hearing, or appeal. In fifty years it was never invoked. Last month the dean used it to suspend an interdisciplinary program that had publicly criticized the administration's budget priorities. The suspended faculty contend that the provision is so indeterminate and so structurally open to arbitrary application that it fails to be a valid institutional rule at all—it is not bad law, but no law, a blank check dressed in institutional language. The dean responds that it was adopted through the charter's formal amendment process, remains in the official text, and therefore binds the community regardless of its moral quality or practical wisdom. He also points to a 2019 appendix—adopted by a faculty vote but never formally woven into the charter's articles—that establishes a mediated restorative-review protocol requiring collective deliberation before any punitive institutional action. He treats this appendix as advisory and subordinate to the explicit article.

The senate must advise whether to uphold the suspension. The dispute seems to turn on whether institutional rules can be invalidated by defects of form and fairness rather than merely criticized for them, and on how an unincorporated but democratically adopted protocol relates to an older, formally entrenched clause. Can you lay out the philosophical structure here—what corresponds to the claim that pedigree alone determines validity, what corresponds to the claim that minimal conditions of clarity, generality, and congruence with practice are prerequisites for being a rule at all, how the 2019 appendix should figure in the analysis, and whether the conclusion that Article VII, Section 4 is void rather than merely unjust survives once we distinguish defects that undermine institutional authority from those that only make an authoritative rule morally objectionable? Please also indicate what would change your mind: for example, if it turned out the clause had been routinely used for minor adjustments in the 1980s, or if the restorative protocol had been ratified through the same formal mechanism that governs charter amendments. Where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
