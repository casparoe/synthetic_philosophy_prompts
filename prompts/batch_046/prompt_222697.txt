Next month I defend my thesis at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-religion seminar: that Buddhist anatta should not be read as the total dissolution of personal continuity, but as the denial of a substantial owner (svabhava) of the five skandhas, leaving conceptual space for a "thin diachronic agent" held together not by Lockean psychological continuity but by what I call skandhic threading—the functional coherence of volitional formations (samskara) across temporal slices, sufficient to ground karmic accountability without a metaphysical substrate. The room is split: on one side, analytic metaphysicians (mostly physicalists, with one or two substance dualists) who regard Buddhist no-self as either crypto-eliminativism or an unstable middle position; on the other, Theravada scholars and comparative philosophers who will accuse me of smuggling Western personal-identity machinery into a framework that rejects it root and branch for soteriological reasons. Prepare my Q&A defense: list the objections I will actually face, ordered by likely appearance and damage, phrased in the vocabulary each side would use. For each, indicate what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and provide a concise reply (roughly two minutes spoken) with a note on where that reply is soft. Specifically defend—do not ignore—my weakest point: the claim that karmic accountability can survive without any substrate if continuity is only threading. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks: that my skandhic threading is merely Derek Parfit's reductionist view with Sanskrit terms. Please conclude by stating how confident you are in the structure of this prep and single out the point about which you are least certain.
