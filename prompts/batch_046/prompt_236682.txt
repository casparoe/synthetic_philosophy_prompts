I teach a graduate seminar in moral psychology and metaethics—eight doctoral students, ninety minutes. Design a structured in-class deliberation that makes the wrong-kind-reasons problem visceral, specifically the gap between having an instrumental reason to adopt an attitude and having a reason that can genuinely justify that attitude. To keep the scenario from collapsing into familiar moral-exhortation examples, frame it around museum deaccession: the group must reach a binding recommendation on whether a university museum should remove a canonical but controversial work from its permanent collection, but the rules of deliberation restrict each participant to reasons drawn from exactly one of four assigned categories—fiduciary liability, pedagogical access, provenance risk, and institutional reputation—while direct appeals to aesthetic merit, historical significance, or moral content of the work are formally excluded. Assign roles (trustee, curator, faculty senator, student representative, alumni donor) with asymmetric information about budget and legal exposure; require coalition-building across roles before any vote; and structure payoffs in non-monetary stakes (e.g., each role has a hidden “integrity score” that is revealed only after the vote, showing whether the coalition’s winning argument actually supplies a justifying reason for that role’s attitude). The problem should emerge from the rules: participants should construct a successful instrumental coalition, then feel the hollowness when they recognize that the arguments that won them over give them no reason to actually believe the work ought to go. Include a precise timeline, the coalition-building rules, a debrief that maps specific moments in the deliberation onto the distinction between motivating and justifying reasons, an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time (likely smuggling of aesthetic reasons despite the ban, and one dominant personality collapsing the coalition dynamics), and a frank admission of which feature of the literature—probably the second-order question of whether one should care about caring for the right reasons—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied in the closing discussion. No lecture up front, and do not name the problem until the debrief.
