I've been having long conversations with a colleague who studies mathematical logic—I'm not a mathematician myself, just someone who reads philosophy of science—about what we should actually do if the basic axioms of mathematics turned out to be inconsistent. Over coffee she's given me her verdicts on a series of hypothetical scenarios, and I'm trying to figure out what principle she's actually following.

Here is her track record:

- If Russell's paradox showed naive set theory was broken, she said we shouldn't throw out logic itself; we should keep the logical rules and just restrict the comprehension axiom, the way Zermelo-Fraenkel does. So: fix the axiom, keep the framework.
- If Peano Arithmetic turned out to have a contradiction, but only in proofs about numbers larger than any physically meaningful quantity, she said we'd keep using PA for all real mathematics and just acknowledge it's technically unsound at the extreme edges.
- If ZFC were inconsistent specifically because of the Axiom of Choice, she said drop Choice and keep everything else intact.
- If second-order logic were inconsistent while first-order logic remained sound, she said abandon second-order logic completely and do all mathematics in first-order terms.
- If a categorical foundation for mathematics—she mentioned something called ETCS—had a hidden contradiction, but ZFC stayed sound, she said we'd stick with ZFC and drop the categorical approach.
- If even basic rules like modus ponens turned out to fail in some edge case, she said that wouldn't be a local problem; we'd have to rebuild reasoning from the ground up.

Now here's the case I'm stuck on. Imagine that a contradiction is discovered inside Peano Arithmetic's induction principle, but only when induction is applied to properties that quantify over the entire collection of subsets of natural numbers—what she called "reflective" or circular properties. For ordinary calculations with specific numbers, and even for most standard proofs, everything works fine. There's an alternative system—let's call it graded arithmetic—that restricts induction to properties that don't refer back to the totality of subsets in this circular way. Graded arithmetic avoids the contradiction entirely, but it can't prove some familiar results, like that every non-empty subset of natural numbers has a least element, or certain theorems about infinite combinatorics that mathematicians currently take for granted.

What would she say? Would she recommend repairing Peano Arithmetic by restricting its induction schema to the safe, non-circular cases (keeping the framework but excising the dangerous rule), or would she say we should abandon Peano Arithmetic altogether and adopt graded arithmetic as our new foundation (retreating to a weaker but sound system)? Or would she say we should keep full Peano Arithmetic exactly as it is, but agree never to apply induction to those circular properties in practice?

Tell me which of these three—surgical repair, hierarchical retreat, or pragmatic compartmentalization—best fits her earlier verdicts, identify the closest analog in her track record, and say what further hypothetical case would clearly separate the leading rule from its runner-up.
