I've been trying to work out my own spin on Benatar's asymmetry, but I'm getting tied in knots with edge cases and I wonder if there's a cleaner principle hiding behind them. Here are my verdicts — they feel right but I can't square them all.

Case 1: A couple has a child who lives 80 years of mild, steady contentment — no real suffering, but also no deep joy or meaning. I think it's okay, maybe even good.

Case 2: Same kind of life, but they suffer three months of excruciating chronic pain in their twenties. Now I think it's definatly wrong to have created them, even though the rest is bliss.

Case 3: A child lives only in neutral states — no pleasure, no pain, just consciousness without affect. I think it's not wrong, but it's not good either; there's no harm done, but no benefit either.

Case 4: A child has one single hour of unbearable torture at age five, then 79 years of ecstatic happiness. I still lean wrong, though less strongly than case 2.

Case 5: A child is never conceived at all. Obviosly no wrong done.

My tentative principle is something I call counterfactual debt: creating someone is wrong iff they experience harm that wouldn't have occured in the nearest world where they don't exist, weighted by how modally stable that absence is — but pleasure doesn't generate credit because absence of pleasure isn't bad. But then case 4 messes this up: the harm is tiny in duration but huge in intensity, and the nearest world without them is very close. Meanwhile case 2 has more total suffering spread out. And case 1 seems fine even though they miss out on deep joy that they could have received in a different form? I don't know.

Is there a coherent account that saves all five verdicts? Or do I have to drop one? And what does the principle say about a life of constant mild boredom with occasional brief euphoria — does the boredom count as harm? I'd like the principle stated precisely, with which clause handles which case, and an honest note on which of my verdicts it can't keep.
