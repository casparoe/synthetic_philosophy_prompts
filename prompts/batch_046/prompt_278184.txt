I've been reading popular books on evolutionary biology and human nature, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I can't quite map onto the concepts I know. A writer explains some human trait—say, our tendency to seek out music, our particular jaw structure, or our need for long sleep—by proposing a specific survival advantage it conferred on our ancestors. Then a critic, or sometimes the writer themselves, raises the possibility that the trait isn't purpose-built at all but rather an unavoidable structural consequence of selecting for something else entirely—like a byproduct that comes along for the ride, comparable to the architectural spandrels that appear because of how arches are constructed rather than because anyone designed them for decoration. What interests me is the response that almost always follows. Rather than treating the "byproduct" explanation as a rival, the adaptive story is expanded: the trait may have begun as a leftover, but once present it was "recruited" or "repurposed," and its current utility explains why it persisted. I have seen this with explanations for religious belief, for the human chin, and even for fatigue. In each case, the objection that something is "just a side effect" seems to be absorbed rather than answered, so that direct adaptation, co-opted leftover, and pure accident all end up described with the same language of evolutionary purpose. I have encountered Gould and Lewontin's argument about spandrels in passing, but only in a general way, and I do not have the philosophical vocabulary to name what happens next.

Is there an established concept for this particular rhetorical or conceptual move—the collapse of the distinction between a trait built for a function and a trait that merely found a function after arising? Or is there a broader term for the pattern by which adaptive explanations become insulated from the "mere byproduct" counterexample? I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of authors; I want to understand what the phenomenon actually consists of, how it differs from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis in science, and whether specialists in philosophy of biology regard it as a genuine failure mode or simply a feature of how evolutionary reasoning is supposed to work. If there is no precise name, I would also value a careful carving of the edges: what cases genuinely belong to this pattern, what cases only look similar, and where the boundary lies between a legitimate appeal to co-option and the kind of immunizing maneuver I keep noticing.
