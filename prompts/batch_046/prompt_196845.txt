I write a column on science and ideas, and I have noticed a recurring structural pattern that I cannot name precisely. A scientific theory is constructed to describe entities or spaces of genuinely enormous—perhaps infinite—scale. When that theory is formalised in a precise logical language, there often exists an interpretation that is, from the outside, surprisingly small or bounded; for instance, a cosmological model intended to describe an infinite spatial expanse might be formalised in a language that permits a finite, circular interpretation in which every theorem about infinite extension is satisfied because distance is measured along closed loops. Within that small interpretation, all of the theory's internal theorems about enormity remain true, because the interpretation defines 'large' relative to its own internal structure. Practitioners do not reject the formalisation; instead, they distinguish between an internal and an external view of scale, treating the minimal interpretation as technically valid but conceptually misleading. I first encountered this in mathematical discussions of uncountable collections, yet the pattern seems to recur whenever a formal system lacks the expressive resources to exclude unintended minimal models. Is there an established term for this specific configuration—where formal correctness is preserved precisely while the motivating intuition about scale is undermined in the smallest valid model? Or is it merely one manifestation of a broader phenomenon? Please take a committed position: either identify the precise existing concept, state exactly why the broader pattern either falls within its boundaries or lies outside them, and explain where the edges are; or, if nothing fits, propose a new term with clear conditions, distinguish it from ordinary model-theoretic multiplicity and from underdetermination, and defend that choice. Please reply in British English.
