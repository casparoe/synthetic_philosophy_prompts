I'm writing my dissertation at the intersection of metaethics and comparative moral psychology, and I'm trying to get clear on a conceptual problem that sits between WEIRD psychology findings and standard arguments about moral objectivity. The empirical literature documents robust cross-cultural variation in moral cognition—particularly differences in how heavily individualizing foundations (harm, fairness) are weighted against binding foundations (loyalty, authority, purity), with WEIRD populations typically showing stronger individualizing patterns. Philosophers often interpret this in one of two directions: either as evidence that moral cognition tracks culturally contingent norms, supporting some form of anti-realism or strong pluralism, or as superficial variation that masks a shared underlying normative architecture, supporting objectivity.

What I need to understand is whether there's a principled conceptual distinction—let's call it the calibration versus normative divergence distinction—that can do the philosophical work required. By calibration, I mean cases where different groups apply the same normative rules but with different empirical inputs, different contextual triggers, or different weightings of values they both recognize. By normative divergence, I mean cases where groups genuinely disagree about which considerations count as moral reasons at all, or about the authority structure of morality itself—disagreements not resolvable by correcting beliefs.

Can you explain the conceptual structure of this distinction and its precise limits? Specifically: (1) How can we determine whether a given cross-cultural difference in moral judgment—say, divergent assessments of loyalty-based obligations versus harm-based obligations—represents a calibration difference within a shared framework rather than a deep normative divergence, without circularly assuming the very framework we're testing for objectivity? (2) If we grant that some divergence is genuinely normative rather than calibrational, does that necessarily undermine moral objectivity conceived as stance-independent authority, or does it only undermine a convergence thesis—the expectation that rational agents in ideal conditions must arrive at a single framework? (3) Are there conceptual reasons, independent of further empirical resolution, why certain patterns in the WEIRD/non-WEIRD data push more naturally toward the calibration interpretation, while others resist it?

I'm not asking for a bibliography or citation list; I want to clarify the conceptual machinery so I can apply it rigorously to the empirical material in my own work.
