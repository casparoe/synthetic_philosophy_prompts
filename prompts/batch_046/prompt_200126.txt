I'm working through Hegel's Philosophy of Right for a graduate seminar on political philosophy, and I've built an argument that seems to push his defense of war into either absurdity or moral monstrosity. Here are the steps as I have them.

(1) For Hegel, war is not an absolute evil but performs an irreplaceable ethical function: it disrupts the atomism of civil society, where individuals treat the state as a mere instrument for securing private property and welfare, and it forces recognition that the universal—the state—takes precedence over particular life and interest.

(2) If an institution performs an essential ethical function, and if that function can be realized through alternative arrangements that preserve the function while eliminating the institution's destructive byproducts, then rational reform demands substituting the alternative.

(3) Modern democratic societies already possess mechanisms—universal civic conscription for infrastructure or disaster response, genuinely progressive taxation that risks personal welfare for collective ends, or mandatory national service involving life-threatening duties—that require citizens to sacrifice private interest for the universal without organized interstate violence.

(4) Therefore, on Hegel's own premises, these domestic mechanisms should fully satisfy the ethical need he assigns to war, and he should welcome perpetual peace supplemented by civic sacrifice.

(5) Yet Hegel explicitly rejects Kant's project of perpetual peace, arguing that peace without the real possibility of death and destruction produces a "stagnation" and "corruption" of ethical life, because only the actual risk of life makes the sacrifice meaningful.

(6) So Hegel must hold either that domestic civic sacrifice is ethically inadequate despite performing the same structural role, which seems arbitrary, or that the violence and destruction of war are not separable side-effects but constitutive of the ethical value he claims for it.

(7) But if destruction is constitutive, then his view does not merely permit war as a regrettable necessity; it requires periodic actual violence and death as conditions for the state's health, which is the reductio.

The chain feels valid, but its conclusion—that Hegel is committed to the moral necessity of slaughter—strikes me as stronger than he intends. I would prefer to keep (1) and (5), since they capture his position faithfully, and I am reluctant to abandon (2) because it expresses a standard principle of institutional rationality. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Is there an equivocation between "sacrifice" in the civic sense and "sacrifice" in the martial sense—perhaps involving the specific recognition of sovereignty between states that domestic service cannot replicate? Or does Hegel have a hidden premise about the "moment of negativity" that makes interstate conflict structurally unique? If I reject (2) I preserve Hegel but pay a price in general rationality; if I reject (6) I seem to soften his view into something he explicitly denies. I want to know exactly which link is weakest and what giving it up costs.
