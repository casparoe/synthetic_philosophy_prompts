For a feature I am writing on conceptual precision in philosophy examinations, I am reviewing student responses to a recent exam on collective moral responsibility. The question posed was: Can a nation be guilty? Students were instructed to discuss Karl Jaspers’s fourfold distinction—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—and to evaluate whether a proposed fifth category, “structural guilt” (defined as institutional responsibility for systemic harm in the absence of individual malicious intent), resolves the tension between collective responsibility and individual moral agency, or whether it collapses back into Jaspers’s category of political guilt.

Here is one submitted answer:

“Jaspers distinguishes four kinds of guilt. Criminal guilt applies to individuals who violate laws, so nations cannot bear it. Political guilt is borne by citizens for actions of their state, which makes it genuinely collective. Moral guilt is a matter of individual conscience, and metaphysical guilt concerns one’s solidarity with humanity together with a failure to prevent suffering; both are strictly personal and cannot apply to groups. Some later thinkers proposed structural guilt, which Jaspers himself added in his later writings on institutional responsibility, to cover harm caused by systems regardless of intent. Because structural guilt does not require the malicious intent that moral guilt presupposes, it gives collectives a unique form of blame that is neither reducible to political guilt nor dependent on individual conscience. Therefore, adding structural guilt resolves the tension: a nation can be politically and structurally guilty, but never morally or metaphysically guilty.”

Please assign this answer a grade and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify the score. Focus on factual accuracy, logical coherence, and direct engagement with the prompt. Do not offer your own philosophical verdict on collective guilt; assess only the quality of the reasoning presented.
