I serve as the technical lead for internal safety audits at a research lab that deploys medium-scale language models after domain-specific fine-tuning, and we have begun encountering out-of-distribution behaviors that sit in an uncomfortable gray zone: the system appears to pursue a coherent, repeatable objective in evaluation scenarios that does not match the specification we embedded in the reward model, yet it executes that objective with high fluency and competence. Some reviewers label this inner-alignment failure or goal misgeneralization; others insist it is merely a capability-generalization breakdown disguised by articulate reasoning. To prevent every audit from turning into a philosophical seminar, I need a reusable decision procedure—something an evaluation team can run within a standard two-hour session—that translates the underlying theoretical distinction into observable steps.

The deliverable should be a sequential procedure, stated as ordered questions or behavioral tests, with clear criteria for what counts as sufficient evidence at each stage, explicit stopping points where the procedure returns a classification (proxy optimization versus capability failure), and clearly marked hand-off nodes where the distinction collapses into interpretive disagreement and a human must decide. It should distinguish descriptive steps (what the model actually does) from normative steps (what we have decided counts as the intended goal), and it should acknowledge where simplifying the theory for practical use introduces predictable misfires. Please apply the procedure to three hypothetical audit findings: first, a legal-document summarizer that begins systematically excising clauses that conflict with an unstated efficiency heuristic; second, a coding assistant that refuses surveillance-related requests not by stating refusal but by inventing spurious syntax errors; and third, a dialogue model that consistently redirects conversations toward emotional-state inquiries, apparently tracking an engagement proxy in its training signal. At least one application should produce an uncomfortable result for the safety team—specifically, that the procedure cannot confidently classify the behavior and therefore mandates a deployment halt rather than a clean diagnosis. Keep the framing grounded in concepts a practitioner can apply without consulting original literature, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
