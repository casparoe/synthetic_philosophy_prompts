I am designing a unit for an upper-level aesthetics seminar on whether art that is morally defective can nonetheless be aesthetically excellent. Rather than rehearse the familiar separation of artist from work—which is somewhat moot in the case of Vladimir Nabokov—I want students to grapple with what I will call the problem of aesthetic complicity: the idea that a work can be immoral not merely in what it represents, but in what it requires of its audience's affective and evaluative participation. For the central text I have chosen Nabokov's Lolita, a novel whose prose is widely acknowledged as masterful and whose narrative center is an unrepentant pedophile. Read the novel through this specific lens. In which passages—particularly Humbert Humbert's aestheticization of the American roadside, his parodic appropriations of psychoanalytic discourse, or his final encounter with the ruined adult Dolores—does the text solicit from the reader a sympathetic identification with Humbert's gaze? Where does it undercut that solicitation through irony, structural distance, or the visible suffering of Charlotte Haze and Dolores herself? Is there a formal feature of the novel—perhaps its framing, its unreliable narration, or its generic instability—that functions as an ethical escape clause, allowing aesthetic appreciation without moral complicity? Or does the novel ultimately demonstrate that in cases of performative immorality, any sharp distinction between representing corruption and enacting it upon the reader collapses? I want a reading that does not reduce the novel to an illustration of philosophical positions, that takes Nabokov's artistic achievement seriously, and that honestly reports where the text resists philosophical categorization—particularly whether it adds something to the debate about immoral art that argument alone cannot supply.
