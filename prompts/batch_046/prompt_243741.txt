Consider the institutional practice, now common in several liberal democracies, of extending civil marriage to same-sex couples on the explicit ground that the state should not privilege reproductive potential, while at the same time maintaining that marriage is structurally limited to two persons—denying legal recognition to committed triads or polyamorous groups that seek the identical bundle of rights and obligations, and justifying that exclusion not by appeal to procreation but by appeal to the binary form of the marital contract itself. What conception of marriage's purpose does this combination of policies strictly presuppose? Does it require a conjugal view centered on sexual exclusivity and intimate union, a revisionist view centered on mutual care and legal coordination, or a hybrid that is internally unstable? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice genuinely entails from what merely accompanies it, identify the point at which the equality rationale for same-sex inclusion conflicts with the dyadic restriction, and say what changes in family law would be required if the underlying conception were replaced with a consistently non-reproductive, contract-based account.
