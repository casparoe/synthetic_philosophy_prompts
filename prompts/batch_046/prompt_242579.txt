I'm delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on consciousness and sleep, with roughly half the room being philosophers of mind and half being sleep researchers who don't spend their days with Malcolm or Dennett. I'd like the speech—about six minutes—to be structured around the disagreement over whether dreams are experiences at all: Malcolm's argument that dreaming fails the criteria for experience because it lacks concurrent reportability and behavioral access, versus Dennett's view that dreams are experiences but that their narrative coherence is largely reconstructed upon waking. Rather than treating this as a settled literature review, I want to inject a framing that isn't part of the standard classroom version: the phenomenon of lucid dreamers executing pre-arranged eye-movement signals to outside observers during REM sleep. This seems to hand Malcolm exactly the real-time behavioral criterion he demands for attributing an experience within the dream, yet the lucid dreamer's own post-waking narrative still exhibits the kind of confabulation and reconstruction that Dennett emphasizes. The remarks should navigate this tension without collapsing it—welcoming both camps, avoiding any claim that presupposes one side is obviously wrong, and doing real structural work so that the speech's welcome is genuinely different depending on which view one holds. It should be precise enough that a philosopher recognizes the architecture, but accessible enough that a neuroscientist doesn't feel lectured. Please write the speech.
