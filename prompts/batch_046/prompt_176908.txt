I have been serving for the past year on the governing board of a community archive established in the late 1970s, and we have reached an impasse that feels less like a bureaucratic dispute and more like a conceptual knot. Our charter—adopted in 1978 and never fully rewritten—contains Article IX, which states that any provision of the charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the nine trustees, but adds that Article IX itself may be revised only by unanimous consent. At our most recent meeting, seven trustees voted to strike the unanimous-consent clause and apply the two-thirds standard to Article IX as well. The chair maintains that the amendment is effective, arguing that Article IX's general wording ("any provision") necessarily includes itself, so the seven-vote majority satisfied the very rule being changed. Three dissenting trustees reject this, insisting that because the old version of Article IX required unanimity for its own alteration, the seven-vote change failed to meet its own conditions of validity and is therefore null.

We are now unable to agree on whether subsequent amendments—such as a proposed revision to our acquisition policy—should be conducted under the old unanimous-consent framework or the new two-thirds rule, and the dissenters have begun treating all post-meeting actions as illegitimate. Complicating matters further, the handwritten minutes from the 1978 founding meeting contain a marginal note, never formally incorporated into the charter text, in which the original drafters wrote that Article IX was intended to be "permanently fixed" to prevent future majorities from eroding the archive's core mission. I am not asking for legal counsel or a reference to specific constitutional doctrines; rather, I want to understand whether there is a philosophically coherent resolution that does not simply collapse into whoever holds the majority. Specifically, can a rule that specifies its own conditions of change consistently authorize its replacement by a laxer standard, or does that generate a circularity in the conditions of validity that forces us to treat the original entrenchment as binding? And if there is a principled distinction between applying an amendment procedure to itself and altering the standard that governs that very application, how should it guide our board? I would also appreciate your view on whether an informal, unincorporated expression of intent—like that 1978 marginal note—should carry any weight in resolving the logical structure of the rule, or whether it becomes irrelevant once we are dealing with the formal mechanics of self-amendment.
