I'm a high school junior delivering the opening address at our school's annual "Future of Ideas" symposium next Friday. The audience is split: half are students and teachers who have studied Malthus's geometric-ratio argument in our economics unit, and half are parents and local guests with no background in it. I want the speech structured around whether a long-run prediction can be "right but early" without becoming unfalsifiable, and I want to introduce the idea of "falsification debt"—the thought that each time a failing forecast is rescued by an unforeseen variable (yield substitution, energy transition, demographic shift), the rescue does not pay off the original claim's epistemic liability but accumulates it. This concept should do real structural work: it should constrain what I can honestly say about forecasting ethics for our generation, not decorate the talk. The address needs to respect the informed listeners without losing everyone else, and it should treat the tension as a live problem rather than asking the audience to adjudicate technical disputes. Please write a polished five-minute speech that uses this framework to argue that predicting the future is a moral as well as an intellectual problem, without citing specific texts, offering bibliographies, or asking anyone to take sides on technical rescue hypotheses.
