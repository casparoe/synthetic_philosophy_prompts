I was recently appointed to my firm’s algorithmic accountability board, and I am puzzled by our model-evaluation protocol. For every predictive system we deploy—hiring, lending, or risk scoring—the board mandates that protected groups simultaneously satisfy demographic parity, equal false-positive and false-negative rates, and within-group calibration. We apply this trio as a rigid, non-negotiable checklist and never relax one condition to accommodate another, even when the underlying base rates differ sharply across groups. Since these three criteria are jointly unsatisfiable except in trivial cases, what philosophical commitments about fairness, equality, and the moral relevance of statistical base rates does our practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes this checklist seem appropriate; distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what it merely fits well with; and indicate whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories might license a different weighting or the abandonment of one criterion. Please separate what is mathematically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study algorithmic fairness, and do not present any contested normative view—such as whether calibration should always yield to parity, or whether base-rate differences are morally arbitrary—as the consensus position.
