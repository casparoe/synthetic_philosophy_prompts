Write an essay assessing whether the contemporary revival of Stoicism philosophically distorts the ancient framework by reducing a three-tier taxonomy of control to a binary filter. In widespread Stoic-inspired self-help and in some cognitive-therapeutic adaptations, practitioners are taught to divide all circumstances into two exhaustive categories: those fully internal to one's agency—such as judgments, intentions, and acts of assent—and those fully external, encompassing bodily health, professional outcomes, the behavior of others, and natural events. Yet Stoic moral psychology, as reconstructed from its practical exercises, appears to recognize an intermediate tier: domains in which one's efforts are necessary but not sufficient, including the cultivation of physical health, the maintenance of relationships, and the pursuit of career objectives that depend partly on fortune. Modern revivalists typically resolve this middle tier in one of two ways—either by treating it as fully internal through cognitive reframing, or by advising complete emotional detachment from it as if it were fully external. Your essay should evaluate whether this binary collapse is a defensible simplification that makes Stoic ethics practicable in modern life, or whether it eliminates the very domain in which Stoic virtue—understood as excellence in navigating conditions of partial control—is meant to operate. Consider in particular whether erasing the intermediate tier undermines Stoic accounts of moral responsibility, appropriate emotional engagement, and the distinction between virtue and mere indifference. Weigh the practical argument that a binary filter is easier to apply against the philosophical argument that Stoicism without the middle tier becomes either an implausible form of mentalism or a quietist withdrawal from active life. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it: defend either the legitimacy of the modern binary as an adaptation, the claim that it betrays core Stoic commitments, or a carefully qualified intermediate position—provided that position is argued rather than merely asserted.
