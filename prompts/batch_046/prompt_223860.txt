As a retired physicist now studying philosophy of science at the graduate level, I am designing a final exam essay for an upper-level epistemology course. I want a single 45-minute essay prompt—approximately 600 to 800 words—that weaves together the formal no-free-lunch theorems in supervised learning with Hume's problem of induction, but in a way that resists rote recitation. Rather than asking students simply to state that no algorithm dominates all others over every possible target function, the prompt should require them to examine whether these theorems genuinely deepen the skeptical challenge or instead expose a hidden metaphysical premise: that selecting an inductive bias is not merely a methodological convenience but amounts to committing in advance to a symmetry principle over the space of possible worlds, analogous to a physicist's assumption of conservation laws prior to empirical testing. The question should ask students to evaluate whether this framing shifts Hume's burden—away from justifying induction from experience and toward justifying the prior distribution over hypotheses—while distinguishing epistemic uniformity from metaphysical symmetry commitments. Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, with explicit instructions that answers should not cite texts or provide bibliographies; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words that addresses the circularity worry, explains why the theorems apply only relative to a uniform prior over target functions, and assesses whether the problem of induction is dissolved, relocated, or intensified; and (3) a marking scheme that separates an A response—one that treats the inductive bias as a substantive philosophical commitment and explains how the formal result relocates rather than resolves the skeptical challenge—from a B response that merely summarizes algorithmic equivalence under uniform averaging or repeats the claim that induction is unjustified without engaging the symmetry framing.
