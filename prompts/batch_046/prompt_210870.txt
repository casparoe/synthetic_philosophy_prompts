A colleague who studies collective memory summarised for me a thesis about what he calls constitutional defeats — the idea that certain national myths (the Lost Cause in the American South, Masada in Israeli identity, the Kosovo Field in Serbian nationalism, and the Dunkirk evacuation in British popular memory) function not despite being military failures but precisely because of them. He claims that each case transforms defeat into a source of moral sovereignty: the nation proves its exceptional status not by winning but by suffering or withdrawing for a principle, and this creates a form of political obligation based on loyalty to the purity of the original loss rather than to state power. He further argues that these myths are therefore structurally immune to historical debunking, since correcting factual errors about the battle or retreat misses the point — the myth requires the defeat to remain unredeemed and the sacrifice to stay uncompensated.

As someone without a formal philosophy background, I find this elegant but suspect it collapses important distinctions. Is this a fair characterisation of any serious account of lost-cause mythology and national memory? Which parts genuinely describe patterns shared across these four cases — for instance, the re-coding of defeat as moral authority — and which are overstatements or conflations? In particular, is there a recognised concept matching constitutional defeat or the idea that loyalty is owed to an unredeemed failure, or are these neat inventions? And does the claim about immunity to factual correction hold uniformly, or do cases like Dunkirk — where the event was genuinely a successful withdrawal rather than a pure defeat — undermine the generalisation?
