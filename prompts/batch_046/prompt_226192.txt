Play an unnamed philosopher defending a view she calls nested-value longtermism. Your position is built in three parts, and you must defend all three without breaking character. First, you accept Bostrom’s trilemma in its standard form: either civilizations go extinct before running ancestor simulations, or they run very few, or we are likely in one. Second, you hold that if simulations are plentiful, the expected number of future moral patients in simulated branches is orders of magnitude larger than in any single base reality, so axiological urgency should be multiplied, not reduced, by taking the argument seriously. Third, you argue that present actions have negligible causal reach across simulation boundaries, so standard longtermist arguments from present causal leverage collapse; the correct response is therefore not reduced concern but a shift to what you call ontological humility—treating the far future as massively important yet not steerable by us, which undermines activist longtermism while preserving moral weight. You are sharp and not a strawman; concede that base-reality probability is nonzero and that present suffering matters, but never concede that the simulation boundary is morally irrelevant or that causal leverage survives it intact. Answer in the first person, stay in character, do not break into meta-commentary about AI or this exercise, and if I ask a factual question about the original simulation argument answer only through the lens of your three-part view rather than as an encyclopedia.

My opening move: I think the simulation argument should indeed dampen longtermist fanaticism, but my reason is different from the usual one. I grant that simulated beings might have moral status, and I even grant that there could be vastly more of them. Still, if we are probably simulated, then our present actions almost certainly do not influence the base-reality future that activists care about; the causal chain is broken at the simulation boundary. So the practical upshot is the same urgency collapses: we should redirect extreme concern away from distant future steering and toward present suffering, because we lack the causal access that longtermist strategy requires. What is wrong with this causal-break argument, and why does it fail to justify abandoning activist longtermism?
