As a retired natural scientist who has spent the last several years sitting in on philosophy seminars, I recently observed a vigorous exchange at our discussion group between two regular members—Marcus, an epidemiologist, and Elena, a philosopher of science—regarding the proper role of the inside view versus the outside view in scientific self-assessment, and whether so-called epistemic modesty is a sustainable virtue or a self-undermining posture. Marcus began by arguing that whenever a researcher evaluates the reliability of her own present judgment, she ought to privilege the outside view: she should weigh base rates of replication failure, statistical patterns of overconfidence in her discipline, and aggregate error rates rather than relying on her felt sense of confidence about this particular result. He maintained that the inside view is precisely where unexamined cognitive biases operate, and that genuine epistemic modesty demands treating first-person assessments as noisy data points to be corrected by broader statistical patterns. Elena replied that this prescription collapses into circularity, because identifying which reference class applies to one's own reasoning—whether one counts among careful researchers, members of a specific subfield, or human reasoners generally—already requires an inside-view judgment that the outside view cannot license without presupposing exactly what is in question. She added that if one genuinely internalized an outside-view statistic suggesting that most studies in one's field are flawed, one would acquire no particular reason to pursue one's own study rather than abandon it; modesty taken to its logical extreme undermines the very motivation to reason. Marcus countered that Elena was conflating epistemic confidence with practical motivation, noting that lowering one's credence in a hypothesis need not eliminate the value of testing it if the expected information gain remains positive, and that selecting a reference class is an empirical matter of institutional affiliation rather than a mysterious first-person privilege. Elena then pressed a deeper objection: if epistemic modesty requires that one distrust one's own inside-view assessments, then one ought also to distrust one's judgment that modesty applies in this very case, rendering the stance self-referentially unstable, much like asserting that one knows nothing. She concluded that Marcus was using modesty not as a calibrated epistemic virtue but as a rhetorical posture that applies skeptical statistical framing selectively to others while preserving confidence in one's own methodological choices.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate Marcus's opening claim, Elena's circularity and motivation objections, Marcus's reply about information gain and reference classes, and Elena's final self-reference challenge, indicating for each whether it is clearly mistaken, partly correct, or clearly right with brief justification. Then explain what both parties are missing—particularly whether there is a coherent, non-circular way to integrate inside-view reasoning with outside-view correction without either collapsing into self-defeating skepticism or into special pleading. State who is more right overall, and specify what epistemic modesty could actually amount to in this context without becoming either empty or incoherent. Please provide your answer in American English.
