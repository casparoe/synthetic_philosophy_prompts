Compare the concept of coherent extrapolated volition—understood as the set of preferences an agent or collective would endorse after indefinite reflection, full information, and internal coherence—with a hypothetical framework we might call recursive dissent equilibrium. In the latter, coherence is not defined by convergence toward a unified will but by the stable preservation of structured disagreement: each preference is extrapolated not to eliminate conflict with others, but to determine the conditions under which it should rationally resist assimilation. Where the first framework treats unresolved pluralism as a failure of extrapolation, the second treats it as a success condition. Examine whether these are genuinely competing normative architectures or whether the second can be reconstructed as a special case of the first applied at the meta-level. Specifically, address: (1) whether an extrapolated volition that retains minority preferences violates the coherence requirement of the first framework; (2) whether the second framework collapses into a hidden aggregation rule; and (3) what each implies about the moral authority of preferences that only survive in non-dominant form.
