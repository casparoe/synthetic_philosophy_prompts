hey, i'm a science and ideas journalist working on a feature about what "healthy" actually means, and i'm trying to sort out my own gut checks before i write the piece. here's where i land on some concrete cases, and i want to know if there's a principle that holds them together or if i'm just being inconsistent.

1. lactose intolerance in adults. globally it's the statistical default, but in dairy-heavy societies people call it an intolerance. i say: not a disease, just an enviromental mismatch.

2. a genetic tweak that gives you sickle-cell trait and makes you resistant to malaria. not a disease, clearly a tradeoff.

3. severe clinical depression that stops you from working and maintaining relationships. disease, no question.

4. being born with no sense of physical pain. sounds like a superpower, but you keep breaking bones and burning yourself without noticing. i call this a disease, even though it's "better" in some abstract sense.

5. a brain wiring that lets you hyperfocus for hours but makes small talk exhuasting. if it causes real suffering and isolation, i lean toward disorder; if it's just a quirk that makes you a weird programmer, i say not a disease. so my verdict seems to depend on suffering and social friction, not just stats.

6. the ability to hear ultrasonic frequencies. totally useless in modern life, sometimes gives you headaches near machinery. still, i say not a disease, just an odd trait.

7. being able to thrive on four hours of sleep with zero harm. statistically abnormal, but if there's no damage and the person functions well, i don't think it's a pathology.

now here's the tension: case 4 (no pain) and case 6 (ultrasonic hearing) are both abnormal functional variations. one i call diseased because it causes hidden harm, the other i don't even though it causes occasional headaches. and case 5 makes me think suffering is key, but case 3 is suffering plus dysfunction, while case 7 has neither. is there a coherent account?

i've been playing with this idea of "enviromental locking" -- a condition only counts as a real disease if it would be harmful in pretty much any human environment, not just ours. but that might make case 4 not a disease if we imagine a world with perfect safety gear, which feels wrong.

so write an essay laying out the pattern behind these verdicts. defend one principle, or maybe a small family of them, and tell me honestly which of my gut checks it can't save. don't end on a fence-sitting note -- take a side. also, apply it to the case i keep going back and forth on: a person whose brain is wired so they never feel anxiety, not even in genuinely dangerous situations. they seem "healthy" in modern life but keep walking into traffic. is that a disease, a gift, or just a mismatch? and does your principle force me to drop any of the seven above?
