I do not have any formal training in philosophy, but I have been working through Derek Parfit's hitchhiker example on my own: a driver agrees to take you to a distant town if you promise to pay upon arrival, yet once you have arrived, the payment benefits only him and costs you. Parfit presents the choice to pay anyway—despite it being against your immediate interest—as a case of rational irrationality, and he treats it as a deep problem about the structure of practical reason. I keep feeling that this framing depends on an artificial assumption: namely, that there is no repetition, no social memory, and no technology that could link your present choice to your future opportunities. If we introduce even a minimal reputation token, a simple visible record that future drivers could check before offering you a ride, then honoring your promise becomes a straightforward strategic investment in credibility rather than a philosophical paradox. Given that, why do ethicists still discuss rational irrationality as if it exposes a fundamental tension within rationality itself, instead of treating it as an obvious consequence of missing institutional safeguards? Put differently, am I wrong to think that Parfit has essentially described what any rational agent should do in a zero-accountability environment—break the promise—and that the only real lesson is that we need enforcement mechanisms? Please respond in American English, and I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want to locate the error in my own reasoning.
