I'm a senior software engineer managing a distributed engineering team, and I've become preoccupied with a recurring credibility problem. Some colleagues establish authority through labor-intensive but invisible activities—maintaining legacy systems, writing exhaustive internal documentation, or performing deep, unrewarded debugging—while others make bold architectural claims in meetings but leave minimal durable artifacts. A teammate recently described this dynamic using evolutionary biology terminology: "costly signaling" and the "handicap principle," the idea that a signal is credible precisely because it imposes a burden that a lower-quality agent could not easily bear. I have no formal training in philosophy or biology, but I need to translate this concept into an operational framework I can actually use.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure—a checklist or ordered sequence of tests—that I can apply when evaluating whether a technical demonstration or claim from a teammate constitutes a credible costly signal rather than cheap talk or performance. The procedure must be tailored to software engineering contexts where "cost" takes non-obvious forms: irreversible reputation exposure through public commit histories, opportunity cost from diverting sprint capacity to non-feature work, sustained cognitive load across months of maintenance, and social risk within the team. It needs to handle modern distortions specifically, including cases where visible labor is delegated to an AI assistant, where cost structures are asymmetric across seniority levels (a senior engineer can absorb a risky week-long refactor that would endanger a junior engineer's standing), and where the handicap is deliberately obscured behind team abstractions. Apply the procedure to three concrete cases: a developer who delivers a meticulously documented but functionally broken prototype; a contractor who declines to share intermediate git history despite producing a final deliverable; and a junior engineer who voluntarily spends weekends resolving obscure legacy bugs with no management mandate. For each case, provide the procedure's verdict, but explicitly flag the judgment calls that cannot be automated—particularly whether the observed cost was genuinely borne by the signaler or was subsidized, and whether the handicap is proportionate to the specific claim being made. Finally, state clearly what this framework is incapable of resolving, especially the risk that high-cost behavior functions not as proof of competence but as camouflage for deficiencies in core deliverables.
