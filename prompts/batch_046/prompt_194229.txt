The topic is Otto Neurath’s defense of picture languages—specifically his ISOTYPE pictograms—as a vehicle for intersubjective, theory-neutral scientific communication. Rather than revisiting the familiar question of whether images are theory-laden or culturally relative, I want to investigate a different problem: whether the internal “grammar” of ISOTYPE—proportional scaling, icon repetition, and spatial ordering—operates as a hidden set of translation conventions analogous to the calibration protocols I once used with laboratory instruments. Do picture languages actually bypass linguistic ambiguity, or do they merely relocate conventional rules into graphic design? Write the proposal with a precise statement of that question; an explanation of why existing work on pictorial representation and protocol sentences leaves this calibration analogy untouched; a philosophical (not empirical) method that compares Neurath’s design rules with measurement-standard conventions and explains why that angle is preferable to the obvious alternatives; a clear account of what a useful deliverable would look like for debates about scientific objectivity; an honest assessment of what would count as failure or as the project being too large for six months; and a realistic scope. Keep the response in American English, no longer than 800 words, and do not include a bibliography, citation requests, or reading lists—I will supply references myself.
