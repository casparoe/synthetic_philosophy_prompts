I've been trying to work out where I stand on consciousness without relying on anything I've read in philosophy journals — mostly just thinking through the computer-analogy and what I actually notice when I pay attention to my own experience. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I'd like to know if it's actually consistent or if I'm smuggling something in:

(1) Consciousness is a kind of "user illusion" — not a false belief I hold about my mind, but the way my brain renders its own processing into a simplified, spatial, narrative format, like a desktop interface that shows files and folders rather than binary data.

(2) There is no separate "inner watcher" or Cartesian theater; the feeling of being a subject is just part of the interface layout, like an avatar that appears on screen but doesn't sit behind the monitor.

(3) Introspection is pretty reliable about the structure of what's displayed — I can genuinely discover that I experience time as linear, or that fear feels physically hot — but it's systematically wrong about what's behind the display, because there's no "behind" that looks like the display suggests.

(4) Two people could have brains that work identically at every functional level but present completely different interface metaphors — one might experience thoughts as spoken words, another as visual shapes — and they could behave identically in every test, yet report genuinely different phenomenal structures without either being mistaken about their own display.

(5) This doesn't make consciousness "fake" or unimportant, because the interface does real work: it shapes what I remember, how I weigh risks, and how I coordinate with others, in ways that wouldn't happen if the brain just processed information without rendering it.

(6) So the "hard problem" isn't about explaining how matter produces subjective feeling; it's an evolutionary design question about why natural selection built this particular rendering engine rather than a different one.

Do these hang together? I'm especially unsure about the relationship between (3) and (4): if introspection reveals real structure but that structure can vary without changing function, then the causal work in (5) seems to belong to the brain, not to the interface. And there's something that feels off between (2) — denying any inner observer — and (3) — claiming I can introspect the display — but maybe that's just an apparent tension that dissolves once you see introspection as the interface checking itself? I want an audit, not a lecture: rank the tensions by severity, say which commitment has to give or be modified for each, and point out any conflicts I'm missing. What's the cheapest way to restore consistency, and what does it cost me?
