The debate over whether societies bear a collective duty to remember mass violence — Margalit's claim that shared memory is a moral obligation owed to victims, set against Rieff's argument that institutional remembrance often hardens into a destructive politics of grievance and that strategic forgetting can be ethically necessary — appears to have stalled without either side conceding ground. Both accept that archives can be distorted, that trauma shapes political identity, and that commemorative practices have social effects; yet defenders of remembrance and advocates of forgetting remain unmoved. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict or a survey. My working hypothesis is that the sides are answering different questions under one heading: one about mnemonic trusteeship — the obligation to preserve an accurate moral record for future strangers — and one about the political psychology of ritual repetition. But I want you to test this. Is the disagreement verbal, turning on whether "remembering" means archival fidelity or affective performance? Is it substantive, about whether obligations can be owed to the dead, or about whether reconciliation requires the active moral achievement of forgetting rather than mere absence? Or is there a hidden empirical question — perhaps about the actual social effects of memorialisation — doing the normative work in disguise?
