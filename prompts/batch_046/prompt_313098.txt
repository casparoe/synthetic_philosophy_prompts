I've been trying to understand the debates around prostitution law—the Nordic approach that criminalizes buyers, the legalized and regulated systems I've read about in Germany and the Netherlands, and the decriminalized framework in New Zealand. In nearly every discussion, someone says "sex work is work," yet they invoke it for opposite ends: demanding ordinary labor protections, rejecting special registration or licensing, or arguing that framing all sellers as victims is conceptually wrong.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could express? I see a spectrum: the trivial observation that people exchange sexual services for money; the normative claim that such exchange lacks special moral status compared to other intimate or physical labor; the legal claim that criminal law should be replaced by labor and contract law; and perhaps even an ontological claim that "prostitution" mischaracterizes a standard service transaction. Which of these are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or misleading?

Then, for each policy context—legalization with regulation, decriminalization without special rules, and opposition to the Nordic model—tell me which precise reading is actually being invoked, and whether that reading genuinely supports the conclusion drawn. I want a menu of interpretations, not a single correct one, with each tied to the argument it is made to serve.

One further comparison keeps bothering me: defenders sometimes treat the slogan as licensing full contractual freedom, since "it's just work." But many societies prohibit or heavily restrict markets in surrogacy or organ donation, even though those also involve paid labor. Is there a hidden premise that sexual labor is permissible-to-market but not uniquely restricted, and does that premise hold up, or does it reveal that the slogan smuggles in an unstated moral boundary?
