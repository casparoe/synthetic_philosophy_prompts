In my philosophy reading group—I am a retired physicist only recently drawn to these debates—one member has been assessing whether particular subsistence transitions count as mistakes in the sense discussed by anthropologists of the Neolithic. Her verdicts are these. The Levantine shift to wheat and barley cultivation was a mistake: it produced hereditary social ranks, reduced leisure, and locked populations into an irreversible system. The adoption of reindeer pastoralism among some Siberian groups was not a mistake, because mobility and minimal status differences were preserved. The development of irrigated rice agriculture in early state societies was a mistake, for the same combination of stratification, labor intensification, and irreversibility. The adoption of small-scale taro gardening in highland New Guinea was not a mistake, since it supplemented foraging without generating durable hierarchy or eliminating mobility.

These four judgments are jointly consistent with at least two distinct rules. Under one rule, a transition is a mistake exactly when it introduces durable stratification together with a net loss of leisure, whatever the technology. Under another, a transition is a mistake exactly when it makes the organizational form irreversible, regardless of equality or leisure.

Consider now a different case: a community abandons nomadism for intensive cultivation of a long-lived tree crop in permanent groves. The shift is technically irreversible—the trees require generations to mature, and wild foraging grounds are lost—yet the society maintains rotating leadership, strictly equal harvest distribution, and leisure levels comparable to those of foragers, with no hereditary ranks.

Predict her verdict—mistake or not—state which rule best explains her earlier track record, and tell me which further case, involving high stratification but full organizational reversibility, would settle the matter between the two rules.
