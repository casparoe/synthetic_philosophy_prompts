At a workshop on practical reasoning, two colleagues—Mara and Jonas—debated whether extreme expected-value reasoning should dominate moral choice. (1) Mara defended what she called expected-value fanaticism: if an option carries even a small but non-negligible chance of an astronomically good outcome—say, preserving a vast future civilization—then rationality demands prioritizing it over near-certain but modest benefits, however large their aggregate value. (2) Jonas objected that this amplifies epistemic noise: in realistic decision contexts, probabilities assigned to far-future, high-dimensional outcomes are diffuse intervals rather than precise point estimates, and multiplying diffuse probabilities by enormous values tracks error rather than genuine opportunity. (3) Mara replied that imprecise-probability frameworks—interval-valued or belief-based approaches—still tend to rank the extreme-outcome option above modest alternatives, so Jonas's worry is essentially risk aversion in formal clothing. (4) Jonas then raised what he termed sacrificial asymmetry: committing to the extreme-utility path typically requires dismantling a portfolio of intermediate, robust goods, so failure is not merely a lost lottery ticket but a structural collapse of fallback capacities. Mara countered that this misrepresents long-horizon agency: many such projects require irreversible dedication, and complaining about lost fallback goods falsely assumes that intermediate values are storable independently of the path taken. They asked a bystander to referee.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. In particular, address whether Jonas's sacrificial asymmetry genuinely differs from standard risk aversion, whether Mara's appeal to imprecise probabilities actually resolves the noise-amplification problem, and whether either party is missing a distinction—say, between epistemic and practical rationality, or between reversible and path-dependent commitments—that would break the deadlock. Also say who is more right overall, and identify anything important both sides overlook.
