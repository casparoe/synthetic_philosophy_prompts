I'm trying to map my actual intuitions about historical explanation, and they don't line up cleanly with the standard covering-law versus narrative distinction. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

1. Explaining the fall of the Western Roman Empire by citing currency debasement, recruitment failure, and demographic pressure feels genuinely explanatory. The conditions are modular: drop any one, and the rest still do explanatory work.

2. Explaining the same collapse by narrating the deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476—Odoacer's personal motives, the winter weather, the lack of resistance—feels like chronicle, not explanation, even though every mentioned factor is causally relevant.

3. A macro-model that predicts imperial collapse whenever tax extraction falls below defense expenditure feels explanatory. But if I learn it applies to twenty empires and only Rome receives a full narrative treatment, I feel the narrative is supplying something the model lacks. I can't tell whether that surplus is epistemic value or merely aesthetic closure.

4. For British industrialization, listing coal geography, patent institutions, and capital accumulation feels like explanation. Adding James Watt's specific conversation with Matthew Boulton about engine efficiency feels like narrative ornament—yet if I remove it, the account feels thinner rather than cleaner.

5. Explaining a revolution's success by verifying that structural strain, elite defection, and organizational capacity were all present feels strongly explanatory. Arranging those same three conditions into a story with rising tension and a climactic popular uprising feels, paradoxically, less explanatory to me, despite identical factual content.

6. Now the tension: in explaining a treaty signing, I think including the diplomats' personal fatigue and the rainstorm on the final day is causally redundant—those details don't alter the payoff structure—yet the explanation feels hollow without them. Meanwhile, I think adding an extra redundant structural condition to a covering-law explanation makes it strictly worse.

So I seem to penalize redundancy in law-style explanations but require it in narrative ones, and I treat narrative structure as harmful in case 5 but essential in case 6. Is there one principle that captures this asymmetry, or does it collapse the distinction? Which of my verdicts—especially 5 and 6—should I overturn to restore coherence? And what does the resulting account predict for a stripped-down narrative that retains only its bare causal bones: does it become a covering-law explanation, or does it lose explanatory power altogether?
