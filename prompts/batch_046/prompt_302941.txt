I've been reading Hofstadter's work on strange loops and I'm pretty bought into the idea that the self is just a self-referential symbol — basically a pattern in neural dynamics that points back at itself and creates the illusion of an "I" sitting behind the eyes. There's no soul or Cartesian ego, just this recusrive loop running in the brain and misrepresenting itself as a unified owner of experience. I want to treat this as my working position and see where it actually cracks, not just where people feel uncomfortable with it.

So what would count against it, seriously? Not the standard "but it feels like more" objection, which Hofstadter already handles by saying the loop is just deceiving itself. I'm talking about harder defeaters. How do split-brain cases work — do two separate loops mean two selves, or does that prove the loop was never truly unitary? What about embodied and predicitive processing results that tie self-modeling to interoceptive bodily loops that aren't purely symbolic — can the strange-loop view absorb that, or does it force the loop to be more embodied than Hofstadter wants? And there's the substrate-independence problem: if the loop is just a formal recusrive structure, shouldn't it emerge in large language models or other architectures with enough recursion, and does their apparent lack of an "I" hurt the view, or is that just a category mistake?

Rank these from fatal — like finding a case where self-reference is intact but there's clearly no self, or showing the loop collapses into a rival theory — down to merely awkward adjustments. I also want to know what a committed strange-loop defender would concede versus dismiss. And be honest if the view is actually well-insulated: maybe the loop is flexible enough that nothing short of a complete neuroscience revolution would dislodge it, and I should know whether that's a strength or a weakness.
