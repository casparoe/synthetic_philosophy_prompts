In my current novel, I am developing two artisans whose abilities stand in a peculiar inverse relation: an aging glassblower who shapes crystal with preternatural precision—he never misjudges the thermal gradient, yet he cannot articulate why he waits for a particular iridescence in the molten mass—while his apprentice has thoroughly internalized the chemical taxonomy of silicate networks and can expound upon viscosity coefficients with perfect fluency, though her own vessels remain structurally defective. This asymmetry led me back to Plato's Meno, particularly to Socrates' concession that true belief guides action no less effectively than knowledge. If both artisans ultimately produce a sound vessel—the correct practical result—then the glassblower's unexamined intuition and the apprentice's articulated science appear functionally equivalent at the decisive moment.

Yet Socrates insists that knowledge is more valuable because true belief, once secured by an account, acquires stability and can be transmitted. Here is where my confusion deepens. The account—the explanatory chain linking thermal dynamics to material outcome—strikes me not as a tether but as an additional propositional architecture, each element of which is itself susceptible to error or misapplication. The glassblower's belief, being direct and unmediated by theory, introduces no such intermediate vulnerability; it is true, and it succeeds. By requiring an account, do we not risk corrupting a correct belief with a faulty explanation, thereby making knowledge less reliable and, by Plato's own practical standard, less valuable? Or does the account function differently—not as a layered belief but as the very structure of understanding that renders the belief genuinely one's own—such that its presence transforms value in a dimension that mere practical success cannot measure?
