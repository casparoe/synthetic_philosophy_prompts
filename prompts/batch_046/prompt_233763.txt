I've been playing with this hybrid view: the self isn't just a Dennett-style center of narrative gravity, but more like a local minimum in a kind of interpretive energy landscape — competing self-narratives don't just lose because they're incoherent, but because there's a friction cost to switching between them, almost like a cognative viscosity that keeps the current story in orbit. So my position is that personal identity persists not because of psychological continuity per se, but because the brain maintains a kind of narrative viscocity that makes deviation energetically expensive, and this is what Dennett was gesturing at without the mechanichs. What would actually count against this? I'm not asking for generic anti-Dennett objections — I want to know which empirical findings about confabulation, split-brain cases, or memory reconsolidation would genuinely threaten the viscosity claim, and whether there's a clean conceptual distinction between narrative gravity as a useful fiction and this stronger claim that there's a real dynamical process holding it in place. Also, is there a risk that viscocity is just a redescription of confirmation bias or cognitive dissonance with extra steps? I'd like an honest map of where this view is exposed, including whether it risks being unfalsifiable if every counter-narrative can just be explained away as not viscous enough.
