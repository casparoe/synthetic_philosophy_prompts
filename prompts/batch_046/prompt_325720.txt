I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about workfare, benefit fraud, and the rhetoric of the undeserving poor, and I want to audit my own commitments for coherence. Here is where I stand: I think legitimate conditionality must satisfy what I call epistemic reciprocity—the state may impose obligations only through rules that are transparent enough for a claimant of ordinary literacy to understand without legal assistance. I also think that what is routinely classified as benefit fraud is mostly rule-opacity harm: ambiguous reporting requirements generate violations that are then moralized as intentional deception. I reject the category of the undeserving poor altogether; moral desert should not determine access to subsistence. At the same time, I defend strict temporary disqualification for claimants who deliberately and knowingly conceal substantial earned income, on the grounds that conditional programs depend on good-faith participation rather than on moral worth. Finally, I favor presumptive eligibility with ex post audit over continuous surveillance, even if that means tolerating a higher rate of undetected error. Do these commitments hang together? I am particularly concerned about three possible fractures: that my rejection of moral desert undermines my justification for disqualification; that my view of fraud as mainly opacity makes it hard to defend punishing the genuinely intentional cases; and that epistemic reciprocity, pushed far enough, might simplify rules so radically that concealing income becomes structurally impossible, rendering my fourth commitment either redundant or institutionally naive. Which of these are real tensions and which dissolve on inspection? For each genuine one, rank its severity, identify the cheapest revision, and say what it costs. Then tell me which single change to my set would resolve the most inconsistencies.
