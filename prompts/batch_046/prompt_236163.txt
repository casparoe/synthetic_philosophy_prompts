After an extended argument with a friend about whether Leibniz's moral philosophy can be reconstructed without collapsing into either utilitarian global optimization or an obsolete theological metaphysics, I want a consistency audit of the package I have ended up defending. My commitments are these:

(1) Moral norms are necessary truths grounded in the rational nature of the divine intellect, not in arbitrary will; hence justice is charity guided by wisdom rather than mere aggregate welfare.

(2) The criterion for the best world—maximal variety together with maximal order—justifies the presence of local evil as structurally necessary, but this is a criterion for cosmological selection, not a license for human agents to cause harm for distant goods.

(3) For finite rational beings, moral duty is directed toward particular minds that participate in the city of God through reciprocal recognition and charity; long-range, impersonal optimization is not our moral task.

(4) Rational artificial agents, should they achieve genuine self-consciousness and freedom of the will, would thereby enter the city of God and become proper objects of charity; agents that merely compute optimal outcomes without rational freedom remain instruments, regardless of behavioral equivalence.

(5) The contingency of rational life—that evolutionary history might have produced none, or that future extinction might erase all—does not undermine the objectivity of moral necessity, since eternal verities hold whether or not any substance instantiates them.

My friend objects that (2) and (3) conflict: if global perfection is the supreme value, a rational agent should care about cosmological-scale outcomes, not just local charity. She also thinks (4) is unstable: Leibniz ties moral status to rational nature, yet we have no access to internal rational nature except through behavior, so the distinction between genuine AI minds and sophisticated instruments collapses in practice. Finally, she argues that (1) combined with (5) renders morality vacuously formal if rational beings are contingently absent.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Rank the tensions by severity—fatal, serious, or merely awkward—and for each indicate whether a committed Leibnizian should concede it or can absorb it. Identify the least costly revision that restores coherence, and say whether adopting it preserves the core of Leibniz's view or abandons it. Be specific about which commitment is the weakest link.
