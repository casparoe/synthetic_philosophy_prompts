People defending epiphenomenalism sometimes compare phenomonal judgments to a car's dashboard warning light: the bulb lights up because the engine is hot, but the glow doesn't cool anything, and crossed wire's can make it flash with no heat at all. They say this shows why a zombie could make the exact same verbal report - 'I feel pain' - without any inner glow, since the report is just brain-wiring, not caused by the feeling. Audit the analogy: what's the engine, what's the bulb, what's the driver, and where does the mapping crack? Specifically, does it smuggle in an outside observer who reads the dashboard, and if so what does that do to the paradox that I seem to have first-person knowledge of my own consciousness? Then swap the car for something weirder - say, a deep-sea creature that bioluminesces under stress but the light plays zero role in navigtion. Does that version handle the first-person case better, or does it just decorate the same problem? Tell me whether the analogy actually dissolves the paradox of phenomonal judgment or just moves it around.
