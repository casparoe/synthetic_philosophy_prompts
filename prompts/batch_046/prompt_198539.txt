I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind working on the metaphysics of consciousness and its neurobiological bases. I am preparing a conceptual framework for an adversarial collaboration between two positions on phenomenal unity in split-brain patients: the "unitary-preservationist" camp, which maintains that complete corpus callosotomy leaves a single phenomenal subject intact—separating only cognitive access, reportability, and hemispheric control—versus the "dual-stream phenomenalist" camp, which maintains that the procedure can generate two simultaneous, non-overlapping streams of phenomenal consciousness, each with its own minimal subject.

Please draft the joint statement that representatives of both camps could co-sign after good-faith collaboration. The document should be organized as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover: the basic neuroanatomy of callosal and anterior/posterior commissural pathways; the standard behavioral profile (independent hemispheric motor response, failure of cross-hemispheric comparison tasks, and cross-cueing via environmental or bodily mediation); the conceptual distinctions among phenomenal unity, access unity, and narrative self-unity; and explicit agreement about where disputes are verbal—particularly if one camp uses "consciousness" to denote reportable access while the other uses it to denote phenomenal presence. Include agreement on what classic divided-field experiments actually establish and where they underdetermine the interpretation.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should present one camp's position in wording that its own adherents would accept, not as a strawman. Identify exactly three cruxes: (a) whether residual subcortical interhemispheric pathways—thalamic, brainstem, and cerebellar—can sustain a minimal binding mechanism sufficient for unitary phenomenal subjecthood despite cortical disconnection; (b) whether cross-cueing demonstrates a single integrating problem-solving system or merely two coupled but phenomenally isolated subsystems; and (c) whether the absence of explicit interhemispheric comparison behavior entails the absence of comparative phenomenal content within one subject.

For each crux, specify the empirical result or conceptual argument that both sides agree would genuinely bear on it, and record each side's predicted interpretation of that result in advance. For instance, both might agree that independent hemispheric attribution of emotional valence to an identical stimulus—without subsequent cross-correction—bears on crux (c); the preservationist should predict that this demonstrates dissociated access within one affective subject, while the dual-streamist should predict that it demonstrates genuinely separate affective subjects.

Finally, inject and precisely define one non-standard but conceptually sharp distinction—"diachronic phenomenal tethering" (the persistence of a single subject across temporal intervals despite spatial separation of processing) versus "synchronic phenomenal co-presence" (the simultaneous givenness of content to one subject at a single time)—and require both camps to state whether they regard this distinction as illuminating the debate or as obscuring it.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the deliverable should be evaluable entirely on internal coherence. Maintain a formal tone appropriate for a philosophy-of-mind working group.
