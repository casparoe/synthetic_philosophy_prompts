I want to understand what I actually think about the ethics of remembering and forgetting—specifically whether there is a coherent principle behind my judgments about mnemonic fidelity, mnemonic sovereignty, and the prohibition on fabricated comfort. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, mostly institutional and personal.

Case 1: A town that suffered a massacre maintains a museum with unaltered exhibits of victims’ personal effects. After twenty years, the survivors collectively request that the museum be converted into a community garden with only a single stone marker. I think they have the standing to demand this, and outsiders—historians, tourists, or the state—should not resist.

Case 2: The same survivors ask that all digitized testimonies they gave to a research institute be permanently deleted from servers and backups, even though scholars study trauma narratives. I think they also have this right; the historical loss is real but subordinate to their authority over how they are archived.

Case 3: A government, facing pressure to “reconcile,” proposes destroying the administrative archives of its past secret police and replacing them with a generic monument to “all victims.” I think this is wrong; there is a collective duty to preserve the specific documentary record, regardless of political convenience.

Case 4: A child of a perpetrator feels no emotional obligation to visit memorials but participates in general education about the crimes. I think this is not a moral failure—there is no personal mnemonic debt that binds descendants in this way.

Case 5: A nursing home caring for dementia patients decides to answer truthfully each time a patient asks about a dead spouse, accepting the repeated grief, rather than inventing a comforting fiction. I think this is correct, even though it causes suffering.

The case I keep returning to: In Case 1 I allow survivors to diminish the physical, embodied presence of memory, and in Case 3 I resist the state’s destruction of archives. Both involve removing material traces of harm. Is the difference only who decides—the affected party versus the perpetrator-state—or does the form of the memory itself matter (spectacle versus documentary record)?

Please also consider an unlisted case: A university learns its endowment was built on forced labor. The descendants request that the university rename the building and destroy the benefactor’s official portrait, but also maintain an uncurated, access-restricted basement archive of all related documents, available only to in-person researchers who justify their need. What should happen?

I am looking for a principle—perhaps involving “mnemonic sovereignty of the affected,” “fidelity to specific record,” and “prohibition on manufactured comfort”—that captures as many verdicts as possible, with honest acknowledgment of failures.
