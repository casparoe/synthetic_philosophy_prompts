I'm the audit chair for a university endowment's investment committee, which reviews member institutions' quarterly rebalancing requests. Over the past year we've seen patterns where an institution shifts from risk-averse to risk-seeking and back in ways that look exploitable by counterparty banks offering structured swaps. I need you to turn the diachronic rationality constraint—specifically the principle that an agent should not be vulnerable to a money pump across sequential decisions—into a reusable audit procedure my committee can run in about thirty minutes per case, without requiring anyone to solve formal decision-theoretic equations.

The deliverable should be a sequence of ordered tests in plain language: (1) classify whether the revision reflects genuinely new information or a pure preference reversal; (2) check whether the sequence of risk allocations contains a cyclical ordering that could be looped; (3) determine whether a counterparty could construct a Dutch-book-style extraction using only the observed reversal pattern and the institution's stated utility over outcomes; (4) assess whether intertemporal trade-offs violate a consistency condition that survives rational belief updating; and (5) mark the precise point where computation ends and human judgment must take over—especially when institutional mission, fiduciary duty, or ethical commitments are invoked.

For each test, specify what counts as sufficient evidence to proceed, what counts as a failure that stops the procedure with a flag or rejection, and what documentation the institution must provide. The procedure must handle the hard case where an institution claims its reversal is justified by a newly adopted ethical investment screen that was not in effect earlier, and it must be honest about where false positives are structurally unavoidable.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (A) a college that moves sixty percent of its portfolio to bonds after a market shock, then back to eighty percent equities within two quarters with no change in endowment size, liability structure, or investment policy statement; (B) a hospital foundation that gradually increases risk exposure only in quarters immediately following successful grant announcements, then reduces exposure sharply in the quarter before grant reports are filed; and (C) a religiously affiliated institution whose sequence triggers a flag under test (3), but which argues that the reversal reflects a binding moral resolution adopted between quarters—so the procedure will yield an answer my committee will resist accepting.

Conclude by stating exactly two things this audit procedure cannot determine about an institution's rationality, and identify one structural feature of sequential preference data that makes false positives inevitable regardless of how the tests are calibrated.
