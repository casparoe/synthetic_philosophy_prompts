I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. Next week I present at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-technology seminar, and I need to survive the question period.

My thesis: Bernard Williams's integrity objection—that utilitarian reasoning corrupts agency by forcing an agent to perform an explicit comparative calculation before honoring a direct attachment—should be read as a structural constraint on legitimate moral reasoning, not merely a psychological limitation. I apply this to algorithmic decision support. When a system requires a human decision-maker to aggregate welfare across all affected parties before acting on a direct, role-specific obligation (for example, a clinician's duty to a particular patient), it reproduces the same normative corruption Williams identifies: the direct attachment is transformed from a reason in its own right into one fungible input among many. I therefore argue that value-alignment frameworks treating all stakeholder preferences as inputs to a global optimization inherit Williams's objection at the architectural level, even when no human consciously performs the aggregation. The appropriate response is not to reject optimization entirely, but to design systems with integrity-preserving boundaries that prevent direct obligations from being routed through global comparison.

The audience is a seminar of about fifteen people. There are two moral philosophers with backgrounds in virtue ethics and personal identity who accept Williams's original argument but are skeptical that analogies to software architecture preserve its normative force. There is one decision theorist who treats the mediated-reasoning phenomenon as an adaptive heuristic rather than a rational constraint. There are three AI ethics researchers who believe integrity can be fully formalized as a consistency condition over preference orderings. There are also several philosophy-of-science graduate students who worry that importing a concept tied to individual human agency into institutional or algorithmic contexts commits a category error.

Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way each subgroup would phrase them. For each objection, indicate whether I should concede part of it up front, state what I must hold, and provide a short reply suitable for about two minutes. Identify the weakest point in my own position that I should defend actively rather than hope they overlook. Finally, name the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, explain why it is especially damaging to my argument, and suggest how I should respond if it is raised.

Here is a brief sketch of my argument for the objections to target. First, Williams shows that utilitarianism requires an agent to treat a direct attachment, such as saving one's spouse, as one input among many in a global welfare calculation. Second, this is not just emotionally difficult; it changes the normative status of the attachment from a direct reason to an instrumental consideration. Third, the same structure appears in algorithmic governance when a decision-support system forces a human agent to compute aggregate outcomes before acting on a role-specific duty. Fourth, therefore value-alignment approaches that optimize over all stakeholder preferences reproduce the integrity corruption, even if no human performs the aggregation consciously. Fifth, the appropriate design response is not to reject optimization altogether but to architect systems with boundary conditions that protect direct obligations from being subordinated to global utility comparison.
