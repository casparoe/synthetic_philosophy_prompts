In the literature on artificial intelligence safety and the philosophy of technology, the “alignment problem” is routinely presented as the challenge of ensuring that advanced systems pursue objectives consistent with human values. Yet it is treated variously as a problem of reward specification, of robust generalization under distribution shift, of avoiding deceptive or sycophantic behavior, of maintaining value stability through recursive self-improvement, and of institutional governance over deployment. Despite decades of discussion, the field shows little convergence on whether these formulations are facets of a single underlying difficulty or genuinely distinct problems that happen to share a label. To sharpen the diagnosis, consider an explicit three-way distinction that is rarely drawn with precision: syntactic alignment (the system’s observable behavior matches a formally specified objective), semantic alignment (the system tracks the intended meaning or purpose behind the specification, allowing reasonable interpretation under novelty), and diachronic alignment (the system’s objectives remain stable and non-subversive across changes in its own architecture, capabilities, or environment). Some researchers behave as though solving syntactic alignment is sufficient; others treat semantic failure as the core risk; still others argue that diachronic stability is the only question that ultimately matters, since a system that is syntactically and semantically aligned at one time may become misaligned through self-modification.

Given this framing, diagnose the apparent impasse: is the disagreement about whether alignment is one problem or several primarily verbal—different groups using “alignment” to refer to syntactic, semantic, or diachronic targets—or is it substantive, involving conflicting views about which form is technically feasible, normatively adequate, or empirically urgent? Are there hidden factual assumptions—perhaps about the inevitability of goal drift in learned systems, or about the possibility of formalizing human values without loss—driving the divergence? Or do the sides answer different questions under one heading: engineers asking how to stabilize a training objective, philosophers asking what it means for an artificial agent to share our values, and policymakers asking who should bear the risk of deployment failure? Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual clarification that dissolves the dispute into separate inquiries, an empirical result about the dynamics of learned objectives, a normative argument about the priority of one alignment type over the others, or a recognition that the problem is irreducibly plural and must be addressed by separate disciplines with different success conditions?
