My epistemology professor has been issuing consistent rulings on cases that combine self-verification with attempts to derive broad anti-skeptical conclusions from ordinary knowledge. Her record is as follows. First, she grants that a driver who notices her car starts smoothly knows the car works, but she denies that this belief can be leveraged to rule out that she is a brain-in-a-vat being fed car-starting experiences. Second, she allows knowledge when someone checks a wristwatch against an independently visible public clock, but she denies it when the watch is checked only against one’s own unaided memory of its prior reading, with no external access. Third, she approves knowledge when a scientist calibrates a new instrument against a reference standard certified in a different laboratory, yet she denies knowledge when that scientist later uses the new instrument’s consistent long-term readings to retroactively justify the original reference standard, with no further outside check. Fourth, she denies knowledge when a person uses the general reliability of her own visual system to defeat the hypothesis that she is systematically hallucinating, but she allows knowledge when she uses vision to inspect an object and then asks a separate, independent observer to confirm.

The new case involves what I will call epistemic reflux. A research team trains a predictive model on a large dataset labeled by human experts. They validate the model on a held-out portion drawn from that same dataset and labeling protocol, achieving high accuracy. They then claim that this success confirms not only the model’s reliability but also the accuracy of the original human labeling protocol—indeed, they argue that any labeling errors must be negligible precisely because the model, trained on those labels, predicts so well on the held-out data. There is no ground-truth verification of the labels that lies outside this loop.

Predict her verdict on whether they have knowledge of the model’s reliability, of the labeling protocol’s accuracy, of both, or of neither. Identify the competing rules her earlier rulings jointly support—such as an independence-of-source requirement, a prohibition on circular method-justification, and a block on deriving anti-skeptical conclusions too easily from ordinary beliefs—and say what each rule predicts here. Give your best-guess reconstruction of the single rule she is actually running, with reasons, and identify which earlier ruling is the closest analog. If her principles genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state precisely what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, propose a further case—perhaps one in which a second model is trained solely on the predictions of the first, then used to validate the original dataset—that would best discriminate between the competing rules.
