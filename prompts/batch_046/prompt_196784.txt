In a graduate seminar on democratic theory, two classmates—Maya and Julian—debated how literally to read John Dewey's idea that democratic life is a form of shared experimental inquiry. Maya argued that if democracy is genuinely modeled on scientific problem-solving, then legislative outcomes should be treated as provisional working hypotheses rather than authoritative settlements. She proposed that a consistent Deweyan should support what she called "institutional adversarial testing": running parallel pilot versions of contested policies in different jurisdictions, comparing outcomes, and treating repeal not as democratic failure but as successful collective adjustment. Julian objected that this collapses Dewey into managerial technocracy. Dewey, he insisted, required inquiry to be social and participatory precisely because no expert class can stand outside the affected community and design valid experiments for them; outsourcing evaluation to controlled pilots replaces democratic deliberation with administrative measurement. Maya replied that participatory design resolves the worry—citizens are co-inquirers who help frame testing conditions, so democratic authority lies not in any single vote but in the continuous, fallible process of collective revision. Julian then pressed a practical objection: macro-level structures—tax codes, treaty obligations, constitutional provisions—cannot be subjected to localized pilot testing without generating coordination chaos and eroding the very stability that makes cooperative inquiry possible. He concluded that Dewey's experimentalism was meant for local, reconstructive problem-solving, not for dissolving all institutional continuity into perpetual trial. They asked me to referee, and I genuinely could not. Please go through their exchange step by step and adjudicate: rate Maya's adversarial-testing interpretation, Julian's technocracy objection, Maya's participatory-defense reply, and Julian's scale-and-stability argument—saying who is right about what in each move. Also identify what both miss, particularly whether Dewey's concept of inquiry demands strict empirical falsifiability or rather a form of reflective social intelligence, and whether a coherent middle position can preserve both democratic stability and genuine experimental revisability.
