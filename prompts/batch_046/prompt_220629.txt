Design a 90-minute classroom simulation for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar of about 14 students that makes them feel, rather than merely hear about, how the Condorcet jury theorem collapses when its independence condition is eroded by rational epistemic deference rather than by irrational bias. Give the group a hidden binary factual task—e.g., identifying which of two displayed statistical distributions generated a sample of 20 numbers—so that “competence” is genuinely variable and not a matter of opinion. Before the session, prepare private cards assigning each student an actual accuracy rate (55%, 60%, 75%, or 90%) and a private signal (their own answer). The verdict must be reached through three rounds of sequential public voting: in each round students may either cast a sincere vote matching their private signal, cast a “defer” vote that copies the majority of the previous round, or abstain; once a vote is cast it is visible to all. Use a payoff scheme with no real money: each student earns +2 points if the collective verdict is correct, +1 point if they voted consistently with their private signal regardless of outcome, and −1 point if they deferred but the majority they followed turned out wrong. Include the exact rules for information revelation, the timeline across rounds, any physical materials needed, and a debrief that maps each round onto the formal conditions of the theorem—independence, homogeneous competence versus heterogeneous competence, and the >0.5 threshold—showing precisely where in the gameplay independence was lost and why rational agents caused the loss. Be frank about which feature of real-world jury dynamics this simulation necessarily omits—such as non-binary choices, emotional rhetoric, or persistent identity-based disagreement—and state how you would supply that missing feature in discussion afterward. Finally, predict what will go wrong the first time the activity is run—e.g., students refusing to defer out of pride, or the highest-competence student being ignored—and explain how to handle it without undermining the pedagogical point.
