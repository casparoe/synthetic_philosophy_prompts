I am preparing to defend my thesis that valid consent should be evaluated through a dependency-weighted deception framework rather than through the standard binary distinction between material and immaterial deception. In this view, deception undermines consent proportionally to how much the consenting party's decision structurally depends on the false belief—so that a patient consenting to an invasive procedure after being misled about the probability of functional recovery suffers a deeper vitiation of autonomy than one misled about the surgeon's personal habits or institutional affiliations, even if both falsehoods are technically material to the disclosure requirements. The framework also introduces a contextualized competence threshold: rather than treating decision-making capacity as a uniform binary, it requires that the consenting agent possess domain-specific reasoning abilities calibrated to the particular epistemic demands of the decision at hand, which means a person might be fully competent to consent to a low-stakes dental procedure but not to a complex clinical trial with ambiguous risk profiles.
