Please evaluate the following student answer to an exam question from my upper-division philosophy course on collective intentionality and joint action. The exam asked students to compare Michael Bratman’s analysis of shared cooperative activity with Margaret Gilbert’s account of joint commitment, applying both to this case: two strangers, without prior agreement or explicit communication, begin pushing a stalled vehicle out of snow; each initially acts with an individual intention to help, but upon noticing the other they adjust their efforts and coordinate successfully. Students were instructed to be precise about Bratman’s conditions—mutual responsiveness, commitment to the joint activity, and commitment to mutual support—and about Gilbert’s conditions for the formation of a plural subject through joint commitment. They were also asked whether either framework can accommodate the normative expectation that a party who suddenly withdraws owes some form of explanation or remedy, and whether accommodating that expectation requires the relational ontology Gilbert proposes or can be derived from interlocking individual intentions alone.

The student’s response is below. Please assign a letter grade (A through F) and provide point-by-point comments that justify the evaluation. Your assessment should identify specific conceptual confusions, misattributions, or argumentative gaps—including subtle ones that a hasty grader might overlook—without merely summarizing the answer. Do not signal your own independent philosophical view on the dispute, and do not include a bibliography, suggested readings, or citations.

Student answer:

Bratman understands shared cooperative activity as constituted by three interlocking conditions: mutual responsiveness to each other’s intentions and actions, commitment to the joint activity itself, and commitment to mutual support. In the snow case, the strangers begin with parallel individual intentions—each intends to free the car—without mutual awareness. Once they notice each other and adjust their pushing, they satisfy all three conditions and thereby form a shared cooperative activity. The commitment to mutual support operates here primarily as a practical expectation that each will continue contributing; it generates predictive reliability rather than a genuine normative entitlement to demand continued effort.

Gilbert argues that collective intentionality is grounded in joint commitment, which creates a plural subject. Two people become a plural subject only when they are jointly committed to a shared goal, an arrangement that generates reciprocal obligations and rights to rebuke non-performance. In the snow case, implicit recognition of shared effort is sufficient to form this joint commitment; explicit verbal agreement is unnecessary. Once constituted, the plural subject has an ontological status that is not reducible to the aggregate of individual mental states. Notably, the plural subject can persist even if one member withdraws, since the joint commitment binds the remaining members unless all parties release each other. This relational structure explains why one stranger could legitimately demand an explanation if the other abruptly stops pushing—an expectation that Bratman’s framework cannot fully accommodate because it lacks the relational ontology required for such normative claims.

Comparing the two, Bratman offers a reductionist analysis in which shared intention is decomposable into interlocking individual intentions with appropriate content. Gilbert rejects this reduction, treating collective intentionality as primitive and constituted by social relations rather than individual psychology. John Searle’s account of we-intention is closer to Bratman’s reductionism, since Searle also analyzes collective intentionality in terms of individual brains possessing we-mode intentional content arranged through mutual belief. Consequently, Gilbert’s framework provides the more robust explanation of spontaneous coordination in the snow case, because it captures the normative dimensions of joint action without requiring prior explicit agreement.
