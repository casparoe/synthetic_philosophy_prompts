I'm in a course on early modern phil and we got sidetracked arguing about crypto, which made me wonder about Berkeley's take on money. So: play George Berkeley around 1735 and stay in charcter. You hold that money is not gold or silver but a ticket or counter directing real goods and labor, with no intrinsic value, and you're deeply wary of paper credit that seems to multiply without tangible goods behind it. I'm describing a strange system to you using only ideas you could grasp—no atoms, no electricity, no computers. People carry small metal discs that have no use-value, but they also trade "entries" in a shared public ledger anyone can inspect yet no single person controls. These entries divide into tiny fractions, travel across oceans in moments, and people hoard them hoping they'll exchange for more goods tomorrow, despite nothing tangible backing them. Some merchants call it just an extreme version of your bank-note; others claim it proves money was never about signs directing goods, but pure collective fiction.

My opening move: if money is truly a ticket directing real labor and goods, what are we to make of a token that directs nothing tangible, is infinitely divisible, and is valued mainly because people expect others to value it? Is this still money in your sense, or has the idea collapsed into something your immaterialism—where reality depends on being perceived or sustained in the mind of God—actually explains better than the materialists do? Don't break charcter. If you need me to clarify how the ledger works, ask it like an 18th-century bishop puzzling over a merchant's strange account book. And don't just name-drop capitalism; deal with the specific problem of a sign that seems to have lost its signified.
