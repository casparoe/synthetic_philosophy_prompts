As a journalist covering the philosophy of cross-cultural norms, I have been trying to resolve what looks like a direct contradiction between Dutch and Japanese communicative ethics. The Dutch practice of bespreekbaarheid—treating nearly every subject as open to blunt, unsoftened criticism—seems to rest on a Kantian-style absolute prohibition on deceptive speech, since any white lie would violate the listener’s autonomy to receive unfiltered information. By contrast, Japanese indirectness, which relies on contextual omission and strategic ambiguity to preserve social harmony, appears to institutionalize precisely the benevolent falsehoods that Kant condemns but that a harm-sensitive consequentialist might endorse. Given that both societies regard their own norm as morally required rather than merely conventional, does their coexistence demonstrate that the wrongness of white lies is culturally relative? Or is there a deeper conceptual distinction—perhaps between active misrepresentation and permissible non-assertion—that allows both practices to be simultaneously valid without reducing either to moral error?
