Consider the standard Allais pairing: in Pair 1, option A yields $1 million with certainty, while B yields $1 million with 89% probability, $5 million with 10%, and nothing with 1%; in Pair 2, option C yields $1 million with 11% probability and nothing otherwise, while D yields $5 million with 10% probability and nothing otherwise. The independence axiom treats the 89% chance of $1 million as a common consequence that should cancel, yet many people choose A over B but D over C.

In notes for an essay titled "Counterfactual Stability and the Ontological Partition of Risk," I propose reframing the axiom not as a bare consistency rule but as a principle of counterfactual stability: an agent’s ranking of two prospects should survive substituting identical background outcomes for both. I then argue that people violate this not through generic risk aversion but through an implicit partition of decision spaces by what I call ontological category. Certainty and probability are treated as belonging to different kinds of value-bearing states, so replacing an 89% chance of $1 million with nothing is psychologically different when the remaining option is guaranteed (moving from security to risk) than when both options remain probabilistic. My tentative claim is that if both pairs were presented entirely within a homogeneous risk domain or entirely within a security domain, the reversal would diminish or disappear.

Please comment on three points. First, does framing independence as counterfactual stability accurately capture the formal requirement that preferences depend only on outcomes that differ, or does it illegitimately import psychological assumptions that the axiom deliberately brackets? Second, is the "ontological category" distinction genuinely explanatory, or is it merely a redescription of the certainty effect and Allais-style weighting functions? Third, my essay implies that homogenizing the ontological category across both pairs should eliminate the paradox; is there a conceptual or empirical reason to think this prediction fails? I am particularly interested in whether the partition account can be distinguished from standard non-expected-utility models without appealing to empirical data I do not cite here.
