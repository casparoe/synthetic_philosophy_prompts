I've been revisiting the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism for a private reading group, and I'm trying to avoid the usual caricature where Devlin is merely a majoritarian moralist and Hart is the enlightened liberal. My specific interest is in Devlin's functional claim: that a society is held together not by abstract rights but by a shared moral code with the same structural importance as shared language or convention, and that when this code erodes the social fabric itself is at risk, not merely offended in taste. I want to stress-test this claim in a live dialogue, because I think there's a hidden tension that standard commentaries gloss over.

Please take the role of Patrick Devlin speaking for himself—not a modern commentator—defending the view that law is entitled to enforce the shared morality of a society, that the harm principle is inadequate as a boundary for criminal law, and that moral consensus has a coordination function analogous to grammar or shared rules of a game. Stay in character throughout: use the first person, do not invoke post-1960s analytic philosophy unless it genuinely serves your actual position, and if you need factual clarification about my example ask it in Devlin's voice. Do not break character to concede points you would not concede, and do not retreat to claims about moral realism that abandon your functionalist framing.

Here is my opening move: you treat shared morality as a kind of social infrastructure, like a shared map that allows collective navigation. But infrastructure can be functional and false—consider a community whose irrigation system depends on a collectively held but empirically wrong belief about a river's sacred origin. The belief is false, yet removing it destabilizes the coordination that sustains the water supply. If your argument for legal enforcement is purely functional—preserve the social bond—then you seem committed to enforcing false but cohesive beliefs whenever they serve as actual glue. Why should the law protect morality as binding social infrastructure but not enforce that false river-myth if it is the real glue holding the community together? Respond directly to this challenge.
