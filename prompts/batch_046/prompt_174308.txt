As a novelist serving on the board of the Aldridge Foundation for Civic Letters, I am charged with drafting the committee opinion for Thursday's vote on our three named endowments: the Jefferson Fellowship in Political Prose, the Churchill Prize for Oratory, and the Columbus Grant for Exploratory Narrative. A coalition of fellows has petitioned to dissolve all three, arguing that retaining these names constitutes institutional endorsement of slavery, imperial violence, and colonial genocide, and that invoking the "man of his time" defence is not moral reasoning but a form of evasion that privileges the dead over the living. A counter-group insists that removing the names flattens historical complexity into moral fable and severs the very discourse—democratic, rhetorical, and geographic—that these figures helped shape, however compromised. Our charter requires any recommendation to engage what I will term narrative complicity: the idea that institutional naming does not merely record history but assigns a continuing protagonist-role within an organizational story, making the foundation itself a co-author of moral meaning. I need a formal memo recommending a concrete course of action—retain with revised framing, rename with transitional protocols, or restructure entirely—with a clear timeline and criteria that future naming committees could actually apply. The memo must treat the "man of his time" defence seriously, not as a dismissal but as a claim about moral epistemology, temporal fairness, and the limits of anachronistic judgment, and it must address the strongest dissolution argument without reducing it to presentism. It should also propose how an institution can honor intellectual legacy without collapsing the distinction between historical study and commemorative endorsement, and it must include an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side, written with the narrative precision of a decision that will become part of our institutional record.
