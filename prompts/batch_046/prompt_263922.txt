I am preparing a long-form feature on the resurgence of interest in classical epistemologies among researchers studying synthetic media, and I have been working through an older Nyāya commentary on the four pramāṇas—specifically the treatment of śabda, or verbal testimony. The text is difficult: it insists that testimony is not mere sound transmission but requires an āpta, a speaker who is both competent and sincerely directed toward the hearer’s understanding. Crucially, the commentator argues that recognizing such a speaker is not itself part of the testimonial cognition; rather, it belongs to a preliminary inferential assessment (anumāna) of character and context, while the actual uptake of the testimony—grasping the meaning intended—operates through a distinct, non-derivative mechanism comparable to upamāna, where prior familiarity with the speaker’s reliability allows a present recognition of the content’s applicability.

What I cannot resolve is the precise boundary the text draws between these two stages. If the listener’s prior confidence in the speaker’s authority is already the product of inference, and if the present grasp of meaning requires that prior confidence as a condition, in what sense does śabda remain an independent pramāṇa rather than a species of inferred belief? The commentary seems to suggest that there is a direct cognitive appropriation—something like an immediate assent to the intended meaning—that is not itself syllogistically derived, yet I am unsure whether this is meant as a psychological description or a normative claim about justified belief-formation.

This distinction matters for my piece because I want to apply it—without anachronism—to a specific contemporary problem: synthetic speech systems that replicate the acoustic and stylistic signatures of a trusted human speaker but possess no intentional state directed at any listener. In such cases, the preliminary inferential check might misfire—the hearer infers authority from the voice—while the second stage, the direct appropriation of meaning through testimony, is arguably absent, since there is no speaker genuinely directing content toward understanding. I would like help clarifying whether the Nyāya framework genuinely supports this bifurcation, or whether I am forcing a modern anxiety onto an older logical architecture. I do not need references or a reading list; I am trying to think through the conceptual mechanics on my own.
