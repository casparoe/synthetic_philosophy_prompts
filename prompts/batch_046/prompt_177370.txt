I'm taking a seminar on conceptual engineering where we've been examining copycat and fluid concepts—specifically, how a concept anchored in one causal and normative context can slip its extension when applied to replicas that preserve surface or functional properties but alter provenance. To clarify my own view, I've been testing my intuitions across a single microdomain: whether a reconstructed, relocated, or reverse-engineered craft object counts as a canonical instance of its original concept, or merely as a copycat instance.

Here are my verdicts. They feel individually defensible, but they don't line up neatly, and I want to know whether they can be unified.

1. A Japanese workshop acquires the original hand-forged dies and wooden molds from a defunct 1890s Sheffield cutlery factory, then produces knives using those exact tools, the same steel-alloy formula, and apprenticeship-trained smiths, but sells them under a new Japanese brand. Verdict: these are copycat instances, not Sheffield masterworks; physical continuity with the original means is not sufficient.

2. A chef who completed the full ten-year apprenticeship at a specific Kyoto kaiseki restaurant opens a restaurant in Copenhagen, using recipes she memorized, plating styles she practiced, and seasonal principles she was taught, but substituting Nordic root vegetables for Japanese ones because of local availability. Verdict: the dishes are canonical instances of that kaiseki tradition, not copycats; training-based continuity survives material substitution.

3. A composer writes a symphony that, unknown to her, is note-for-note identical to an obscure unpublished manuscript by a dead composer she has never studied; she composed it in isolation. Verdict: her symphony is an independent original, not a copycat of the dead composer's work, despite perfect audible identity.

4. A museum commissions a clockmaker to build a functioning astronomical clock using only the original builder's unpublished 1670 sketches and period-appropriate tools; the clock is signed with the original builder's name and installed in the reconstructed tower of the original building. Verdict: this counts as a canonical masterwork by the original builder, not a copycat, despite being constructed 350 years later by different hands.

5. A tech firm reverse-engineers a proprietary mechanical calculator, rewrites the entire design in new technical language, manufactures it with modern precision, and distributes it open-source; every input-output mapping is preserved exactly. Verdict: this is functionally the same calculator, but conceptually it is a copycat instance, not the canonical masterwork.

6. A poet translates an epic poem into a language with no equivalent meter or rhyme scheme, preserving every propositional content and emotional arc, and publishes it as the same poem in translation. Verdict: this remains the canonical poem, not a copycat, even though its surface linguistic form is entirely replaced.

The sharpest tension is between case 1 and case 4: in both there is direct physical or design continuity with the original source, yet I classify one as copycat and the other as canonical. Meanwhile, case 3 and case 5 both involve perfect structural identity with an original, but I call one an independent original and the other a copycat.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving normative authorization, intentional continuity, or causal provenance—that delivers all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should I overturn to preserve the simplest coherent account? And whatever principle survives, what does it predict for the case I keep revisiting: a fashion house buys the original 1952 pattern paper and sewing machines of a defunct couture atelier, trains new artisans to execute those patterns exactly, but labels the garments with the new house's name—canonical masterworks, copycats, or something in between?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause of it is motivated by which case, give your prediction for the fashion-house case, and be explicit about which of my six verdicts your account cannot accommodate and whether saving that verdict requires an exception.
