In my philosophy of mind reading group we've been stuck on mental causation for ages, specifically Kim-style exclusion. The setup's familiar: physical closure says every mental event has a physical cause that's sufficient, so if mental properties aren't identical to physical ones they're causally redundant. The non-reductive side answers with causal relevance, downward emergence, or somtimes they just bite the bullet on overdetermination. But we've been repeating these moves for years with nobody budging and I'm wondering if we're even fighting about the same thing.

My working theory is that the stall isn't about overdetermination but about whether causation is basically a single-level production relation or a multilevel acounting relation where higher-level patterns can have weight even when lower-level ones are sufficient. The exclusion camp treats the physical base like a closed ledger; the other side treats it like nested budgets where mental entries do real work. Is that a real metaphysical divide, or just two different jobs being called 'cause' — one for production, one for explanation? Or are they answering different questions entirely, one ontological and one about scientific practice?

What kind of disagreement is this actually? Are there hidden empirical questions about how neuroscience assigns causal roles, or is it bedrock intuition about what a 'complete' causal story has to include? And given that diagnosis, what would progress even look like — a formal framework for multilevel causation, a distinction both sides accept, or is the question ill-posed because 'mental causation' hasn't been pinned down? I especially want to know if shifting the framing from event-based to process-based descriptions of the physical base changes the argument, or if that's just moving the goalposts. Don't need a survey of every view, just the structure of the stall and what, if anything, could break it.
