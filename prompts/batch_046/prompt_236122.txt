Here is an argument that seems to prove rational agents must reject all trust in their own future reasoning, which cannot be right. (1) Rationality essentially requires the capacity to revise beliefs through future reasoning—learning must remain possible. (2) Full self-trust can be formalized as accepting the schema: if my future self soundly concludes B, then B. (3) In any formal system satisfying the Hilbert-Bernays derivability conditions, Löb’s theorem gives that if the system proves [Trust → □B], then it already proves B; so a self-trusting system can bootstrap any belief it expects to reach into a present commitment. (4) Hence full self-trust locks the agent’s current doxastic state, making genuine revision impossible. (5) But if learning is impossible, rationality is undermined. (6) Therefore rational agents ought to be entirely skeptical of their future cognition. (7) Yet total self-skepticism is paralyzing, not rational—so the argument proves too much. Where does it fail? Is the mistake applying Löb’s theorem outside formal arithmetic (step 3), conflating provability with epistemic entitlement (step 4), or treating self-trust as an all-or-nothing axiom rather than a defeasible default (step 2)? I would prefer to keep (1) and (6) if possible; tell me which rejection costs the least and what it forces us to say about rational self-trust.
