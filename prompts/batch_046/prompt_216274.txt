You have been asked to advise me in a family dispute that turns on what makes an object the very thing it was, though I do not want you to name any philosophical doctrine by its textbook label. Three years ago my grandfather’s violin—built in 1923 by a Cremona maker, with the original scroll, neck, and back still intact—was severely damaged in a basement flood. A master restorer replaced the soundboard, all four ribs, the bridge, tailpiece, and fingerboard with new materials, using the original maker’s varnish formula recovered from workshop notes and Alpine spruce sourced from the same grove that supplied the 1923 soundboard. The instrument now plays with the tone for which my grandfather was known, and a regional museum has offered to acquire it for its collection, cataloguing it under the original serial number engraved solely on the scroll. My aunt insists that what sits in the case is essentially a high-quality replica assembled around a few relics; she argues that we should separate the original scroll and back, preserve them as memorial artifacts, and never reassemble them into a playing instrument. I feel that playing the reconstructed violin honors his memory, yet I worry I may be disregarding something decisive about material continuity, provenance, or the conditions under which repair becomes replacement. Please analyze whether the reconstructed instrument is genuinely the same violin, what kinds of considerations should weigh in that judgment, and where the strongest objections to your analysis lie, then tell me how I ought to treat the object—play it, preserve it intact, or separate its parts—without treating my emotional attachment as decisive. In your answer, clearly distinguish what is broadly settled in the relevant philosophical discussion from what remains genuinely contested among people who know the topic well, and do not present any disputed view as if it were the consensus.
