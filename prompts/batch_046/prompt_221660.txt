I'm preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for an audience of philosophy undergraduates together with graduate students from cognitive science, none of whom have read Adam Smith. The goal is not to summarize Smith's ethics but to teach the internal structure of his account: sympathy as an imaginative, judgmental exchange of situations rather than emotional contagion, and the impartial spectator as an internalized, idealized point of view that arises through repeated sympathetic comparison rather than as an external moral authority. Design the teaching plan. First, lay out the dependency order: which component—sympathetic projection, the distinction between direct and indirect sympathy, or the spectator—must come first and why the others collapse if introduced out of sequence. Second, identify the one concrete scenario or structured example that should carry the session; specify what it must include (for instance, an asymmetry between observer and sufferer, a lack of direct emotional infection, and a need for proportional judgment) and what it must avoid. Third, map the misconceptions this particular audience will likely import—especially the identification of sympathy with modern empathic accuracy or mirror-neuron resonance, the interpretation of the spectator as a literal other person, and the reduction of Smith's framework to a proto-utilitarian aggregation of welfare—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and neutralized. Fourth, propose a closing check—one targeted question or brief exercise—that would reveal whether participants understand sympathy as a judgment of propriety rather than as a shared affective state. Fifth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and acknowledged as omitted. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or suggested readings.
