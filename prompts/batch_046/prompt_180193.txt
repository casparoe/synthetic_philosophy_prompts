I'm putting together my position for a presentation in my cognitive science elective on how we recognize letterforms across fonts, and after working through the Letter Spirit idea that letters have an abstract "spirit" made of relational roles on a grid, I've assembled a cluster of views that I want to check for coherence before I present them. Here is exactly where I stand.

First, I think the essence of a letter like 'a' is not any particular outline but a set of structural relations—something like "a closed region on the left with an open exit on the right and a downward stroke that does not close"—and that every actual printed 'a' is just one stylistic instantiation of that relational skeleton. From this I conclude that a recognition system ought to work top-down: it should parse the relational roles first and then check whether the metric details fall within an acceptable range, rather than building up recognition from raw pixels. At the same time, I am convinced that in real human vision we do essentially the opposite: we recognize letters bottom-up from local visual features and contextual expectation, with no conscious access to abstract roles. I also hold that "style"—serifs, weight, slant, or even extreme geometric distortion—is purely ornamental in exactly the sense that it never changes which letter is present; it only changes how the same letter looks. Yet I want to maintain that there are genuinely ambiguous borderline forms, such as a continuous-script form where the loop and tail merge so completely that the relational roles themselves become indeterminate, and in those cases classification should depend partly on the writer's intention or on the surrounding word, not solely on the shape in isolation. Finally, I believe that if we could fully formalize the relational spirit of each alphabet character, we would possess a complete generative grammar that produces all and only legitimate tokens of that letter, leaving no residual ambiguity that formal rules cannot settle.

My worry is that these commitments pull in incompatible directions. The normative claim that recognition should be top-down seems hard to square with my descriptive claim that human perception is bottom-up, unless I am smuggling in an unstated assumption about what counts as "real" recognition. The claim that style is purely superficial also sits awkwardly with my willingness to let writer intention or word context resolve ambiguity, since that looks like allowing non-formal, contextual factors to determine letter identity in a way that style was supposed never to do. And my belief in a complete formal grammar of letter-spirit seems undermined by my admission that some forms are irreducibly ambiguous. To help myself navigate this, I invented a distinction—between "structural essence" and "recognitional trigger"—where the essence is what makes an 'a' an 'a' across all possible worlds, while the trigger is just whatever feature set our brains happen to lock onto in practice. I want to preserve this distinction, but I do not know whether it actually resolves any of the conflicts above or merely renames them.

Can you audit this package? Which commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to conflict once the essence-trigger distinction is applied properly, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity and tell me which single view I should be most prepared to weaken if I want the rest of the cluster to survive intact. I am especially interested in whether my admission of irreducible ambiguity is compatible with both the formal-grammar belief and the claim that style is never identity-constituting, and whether the bottom-up/top-down pair is a real contradiction or only an apparent one.
