Consider the taxonomic revision of giraffes in the 2010s, when genetic and morphological analyses led to the recognition of four distinct species (G. camelopardalis, G. reticulata, G. tippelskirchi, and G. giraffa) rather than a single species with nine subspecies, despite observed hybridization in zones of geographic overlap. Analyze this episode through the lens of the species problem, but do not treat it as a routine choice between the Biological Species Concept and the Phylogenetic Species Concept. Instead, apply a distinction between synchronic individuation conditions—what makes an organism belong to a species at a given time slice—and diachronic persistence conditions—what allows that lineage to maintain ontological identity across evolutionary change in the presence of gene flow and intermittent hybridization. Frame the analysis in terms of two competing ontologies: a process-ontology on which species are spatiotemporally continuous historical individuals (like organisms), and a cluster-ontology on which species are natural kinds defined by shared properties, reproductive isolation, or evolutionary trajectory.

In particular, map out what each ontology says about the standing of the four giraffe lineages: who or what has the relevant ontological status (the taxonomist classifying, the conservation policymaker allocating protection, the populations themselves), what counts as decisive evidence (genetic divergence metrics, observed hybrid fertility, geographic continuity), and where each framework either explains a feature of the case well or misdescribes it. Address the fact that direct experimental access to reproductive potential across wild African ranges is largely unavailable, so that the identification of species membership relies on epistemic proxies—genetic distance, morphological clustering, phylogenetic branching—rather than on direct modal access to interbreeding capacity. Say whether this inaccessibility reveals that reproductive isolation was never doing the ontological work we assigned to it, or whether it instead shows that our ontology must distinguish between species-level mechanisms of cohesion and population-level mechanisms of gene flow.

Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the giraffe case show about the theory? Does the persistence of hybridization alongside deep genetic divergence constitute an anomaly for any concept that requires reproductive isolation, or does it confirm that species are best understood as processes that can tolerate internal exchange? Be disciplined about where the conceptual framework is silent—identify at least one feature of the actual geographic and genetic data for which neither ontology has a clear verdict—and conclude with an assessment of whether the episode forces a revision in how we understand ontological identification when the information that would settle membership is partly inaccessible, leaving taxonomy to operate as an epistemic stand-in for metaphysics.
