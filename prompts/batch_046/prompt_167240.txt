I've been lurking on rationalist forums for years and finally have a question that's been bugging me since I read some old LessWrong threads on democratic epistemics. So Arrow's impossibility theorem requires ranking three or more options, right? And in direct democracy—Swiss referendums, California ballot propositions, even the Brexit vote—you're almost always facing a binary yes/no on a specific text. There's no ranking of candidates, just a single choice. So doesn't that mean Arrow's result literally doesn't apply to direct democracy, and the whole "voting is mathematically impossible" framing is just people misapplying a legislative aggregation model to referendum systems?

But then I think about Brexit: the ballot was binary (Leave/Remain), yet the actual policy space was multidimensional—customs union, single market, immigration rules. So the government got to pick which two options were on the ballot, which feels like it just moves Arrow's paradox upstream into the framing stage. Meanwhile, I keep mixing this up with Condorcet's jury theorem, which assumes voters are independant and each slightly better than chance. In California prop campaigns everyone watches the same ads, so independence is broken—does correlation just weaken the result, or can it make the group systematically worse? I feel like I'm conflating belief aggregation with preference aggregation, but I can't pinpoint where the slip happens.

Anyway, what's the actual mistake? I'd especially like to know whether binary referendums really sidestep Arrow and, if not, where the aggregation failure hides—maybe in sequential votes or agenda control. Please also say what would change your mind, like a formal result linking binary framing back to Arrow's conditions, and be honest about where this reasoning is most likely to fail.
