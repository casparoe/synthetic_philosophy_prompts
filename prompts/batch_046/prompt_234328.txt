I've been thinking Scanlon's contractualism gets unfairly beaten up over the agregation problem. Here's my take: if you apply 'reasonably rejectable' to general policies rather than pairwise choices, a rule like 'save more lives when risks are equal' might survive - not because five outweighs one, but because nobody could reject that rule ex-ante without knowing if they'd be in the five or the one. So maybe contractualism handles aggregation, just at the policy level. But I worry this is just smuggling in consequentialist reasoning. What would actually count against this reading? Does it collapse into rule-utilitarianism? Are there disaster cases where the policy view still fails? And what would Scanlon concede - has he already said this ex-ante framing misses the point of contractualism? I want an honest assessment of how exposed this position is, including whether I've qualified it into unfalsifiability.
