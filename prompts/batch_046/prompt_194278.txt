I'm trying to understand whether the person who writes an advance directive is really the same authority figure for the person who later lives with severe dementia, or whether something essential breaks in between. In Lisa Genova's Still Alice, Alice Howland writes herself instructions for ending her life once her Alzheimer's reaches a specific stage, but by the time she reaches that stage she can no longer read or understand the letter. Read the novel and its central scenes—the hidden letter, the bottle of pills, and the family members who intervene when she nearly acts on the plan—through the problem of the earlier self's authority. Where does Alice's earlier self seem to have legitimate command over her later self, and where does the later Alice seem to have become someone new enough that the directive functions like a stranger's will? I want a reading that takes seriously both the bioethical idea of precedent autonomy and a stricter view: that authority across time requires not just psychological continuity but a kind of narrative sovereignty—the ability of the later self to remain the protagonist of the story the earlier self was telling. Does the novel suggest that Alzheimer's destroys that sovereignty, or does it show that the later Alice retains a form of present dignity that makes honoring the directive an act of violence? I'd like to know where the philosophical framing illuminates the plot, where it forces the novel into a theory it doesn't actually hold, and whether Still Alice adds something to the debate about advance directives that philosophical argument alone tends to miss.
