In thinking through Jain anekāntavāda—the doctrine that reality genuinely admits of multiple, apparently incompatible descriptions, each true from its own standpoint—I have become preoccupied by a recurring argumentative pattern that seems related but not identical to the doctrine itself. I would like to know whether this pattern has an established name, how precisely it relates to many-sided truth, and where its conceptual boundaries lie.

The pattern appears when a speaker treats a multi-perspective framework not as a reason to suspend or integrate judgments, but as a mechanism for selective defense. For instance, in a policy debate a speaker might insist that both “economic efficiency requires deregulation” and “ecological integrity requires restriction” are true descriptions of the good society, each valid from its own standpoint. Yet when she advocates deregulation, she treats the economic description as decisive; and when opponents cite ecological harm, she deflects by saying that harm is “only true from the ecological standpoint,” which she respects in principle but refuses to let constrain action. A second instance: a manager tells an employee that “from the organizational standpoint, your performance is inadequate,” but when the employee cites resource constraints, the manager replies that those constraints are “only true from the personal standpoint” and therefore cannot override the organizational judgment. In both cases the speaker genuinely accepts both perspectives, shifts between them explicitly rather than covertly, and is not merely advancing a weak defensible claim while secretly relying on a stronger one. Nevertheless, one standpoint is consistently used to deliver conclusions while the other is used only to neutralize objections, so the framework functions less like balanced judgment and more like an asymmetric shield.

This is not straightforward hypocrisy, because the speaker does not deny the validity of the opposing standpoint; it is not the motte-and-bailey, because neither version is an indefensible extension of a defensible core; and it is not plain equivocation, because the shifts are openly flagged rather than hidden. What I am after is the specific failure mode—if it is one—in which the acknowledgment of many-sidedness is deployed to immunize a preferred standpoint against criticism rather than to complicate or suspend it. Is there a recognized concept for this “asymmetric deployment” of multi-perspective reasoning, either within Jain epistemology (perhaps as a particular misuse of standpoint-analysis) or in broader argumentation theory? If there is no established term, can it be defined with enough precision to distinguish it cleanly from these neighboring phenomena, and what would be the critical boundary cases that show when multi-sidedness is being used genuinely versus defensively? I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and how its edges should be drawn.
