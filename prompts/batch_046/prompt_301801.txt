I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the conditions for a meaningful life or activity, using Schlick's broad orientation—that meaning resides not in the achievement of external ends but in a mode of self-contained absorption closer to play than to instrumental work—as a rough guide, though my intuitions consistently pull away from any simple statement of it. To see whether a coherent principle can capture them, I offer the following verdicts on concrete cases. A mathematician spends thirty years on an abstract problem with no practical application, lives without concern for recognition, and reports long stretches of pure present-focused absorption; she eventually solves it, but the result remains unused. I judge her life deeply meaningful, and specifically because of the absorption rather than the outcome. A factory worker performs precise assembly for forty years, takes quiet pride in error-free output, but describes every shift as endured solely for salary and pension; I judge this not meaningful, despite the skill. A retired engineer spends six hours daily in complex strategy games, is fully absorbed, forgets meals, and has no competitive ranking or social audience; I think this is meaningful, though perhaps thinner than the mathematician's case. A postdoctoral researcher runs experiments she despises solely to secure tenure, accidentally makes a significant discovery, and publishes it; I say her years of work were not meaningful, and the discovery does not retroactively redeem them. A professional climber pursues difficult ascents not for records or sponsorship but for the state of total present-focus she reports, and dies in an avalanche; I still judge her life meaningful, with the death not erasing that value. A young child builds block towers only to knock them down, absorbed entirely in the doing with no further aim; this strikes me as clearly meaningful. The case I keep returning to, however, involves an elderly person with advanced dementia who lives in a nursing home, smiles at music, seems content in each isolated moment, but retains no memory of family, past projects, or any future orientation. Part of me says these moments are meaningful in precisely the Schlickian sense—pure present-play—while part of me insists that without some continuity of self or narrative project, they cannot constitute a meaningful life, only pleasant fragments.

Is there a single principle that accommodates all of these verdicts, including the tension between the child and the dementia patient? If so, state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, say what it implies about situations I have not mentioned—such as a musician who performs solely in private, or a scientist funded to pursue a goal she considers useless but who loves the laboratory process itself—and be explicit about which of my verdicts it must overturn if it is to avoid ad hoc complexity. I am especially interested in whether the principle needs to distinguish between active creation and passive consumption, or whether it must introduce some minimal condition of narrative or self-continuity that Schlick's original framework seems to lack.
