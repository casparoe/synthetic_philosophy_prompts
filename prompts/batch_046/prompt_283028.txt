A colleague recently summarized the debate between Avishai Margalit and David Rieff as follows. Margalit, she claimed, defends an unconditional societal duty to preserve the memory of mass atrocity; survivors and descendants must bear witness on behalf of the dead, and this obligation remains in force even when remembrance intensifies social division. Rieff, she said, regards collective remembrance as a political tool that traps communities in cycles of grievance, so he advocates institutional forgetting—such as amnesty laws and the removal of commemorative monuments—as a prerequisite for peace. She concluded that the two views are direct antipodes, with Margalit treating remembrance as an absolute moral law and Rieff treating strategic forgetting as an imperative. She further argued that because digital archives now make the erasure of records practically impossible, Rieff's program is technologically obsolete, leaving Margalit's unconditional duty as the only viable stance.
