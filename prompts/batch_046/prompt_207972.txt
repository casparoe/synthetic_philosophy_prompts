I've spent the better part of a decade tracking the archaeology and anthropology of societal collapses, and I'm increasingly convinced that a particular interpretive debate is structurally stalled rather than empirically open. The cases are the Late Bronze Age crisis (c. 1200 BCE), the Classic Maya lowland transition (c. 800–900 CE), and the Rapa Nui ecological-demographic restructuring. By now the evidentiary baseline is unusually rich: LiDAR reveals dense, dispersed post-monument settlement across the Maya lowlands; isotopic and ceramic data show Bronze Age interregional trade networks fragmenting while local production continues; Rapa Nui's deforestation chronology is well-dated, yet demographic reconstructions and subsistence strategies remain contested only in framing, not in raw numbers. Despite this convergence of data, the interpretive camps haven't moved. One side—the discontinuity camp—treats these events as collapses: rapid, nonlinear losses of centralized political complexity, monument-building capacity, and interregional integration, often carrying an implicit normative charge (failure, tragedy, warning). The other side—the continuity camp—treats them as transformations or adaptive reorganizations: palace economies dissolve, but local polities, religious practices, and demographic presence persist; complexity doesn't vanish, it redistributes to smaller scales and different institutional forms.

What frustrates me is that new findings don't resolve anything. When LiDAR shows continued Maya occupation outside the old city-states, the discontinuity camp describes it as "post-collapse resilience" and the continuity camp describes it as "evidence that collapse never occurred." This symmetry suggests the disagreement isn't about facts but about classification conditions, success conditions, or hidden premises.

My working hypothesis is that the stall reflects a conflation of at least three distinct questions being answered under the single term "collapse": (1) Did a specific institutional form—the palace-state, the elite monument network, the Bronze Age palace economy—lose viability? (2) Did the broader cultural-demographic system experience a discontinuity in its trajectory? (3) Is the post-transition state worse by some normative or functional metric—lower energy capture, reduced problem-solving capacity, greater systemic vulnerability? The discontinuity camp tends to answer yes to all three; the continuity camp tends to answer yes to (1) but no to (2) and (3). But I'm unsure whether this is the right diagnosis. It might instead be a verbal disagreement about what "society" denotes in these contexts—the state apparatus versus the distributed cultural-demographic network. Or it could be a disagreement about counterfactual stability: whether the pre-transition systems could have persisted given climate and resource constraints, which is an empirical claim disguised as interpretation. Or perhaps it's about standards of explanation: one side demands a single dominant causal driver (climate, invasion, internal contradiction) and treats multi-causal reorganization as non-explanatory, while the other sees multi-causality and distributed adaptation as the central phenomenon.

Can you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? I want an analysis that engages what each side actually claims—not a survey of Diamond, Tainter, or recent Maya literature, but a structural diagnosis: is it verbal about "collapse" or "society"; is there a hidden empirical question about counterfactual viability or energy-capture dynamics; is it about which cases count as data (does Rapa Nui belong in the same category as the Maya, or is Easter Island's small-scale isolation a different kind of event entirely); is it about bedrock intuitions regarding whether centralization and large-scale integration are goods worth preserving; or is the question itself ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the needle? Would it take a formal distinction—say, between administrative collapse, demographic collapse, and informational discontinuity, or between thermodynamic release and institutional failure—or a reconceptualization of "society" as a multi-scale adaptive network rather than a unitary system? Or is there an honest argument that no resolution should be expected, because the two camps are tracking genuinely different phenomena under one name? Please be precise about which of my proposed distinctions (institutional, systemic, normative) holds up, whether there's a further distinction I'm missing that would unlock the debate, and what—if anything—would count as progress rather than mere restatement.
