I am a novelist revising a historical novel in which I invented a private letter attributed to a real nineteenth-century scientist—a document that never existed. In the letter, the scientist writes: "I have destroyed the only record, and now the absence is complete." Literary critics have interpreted the line in incompatible ways, as an emblem of artistic liberation or of guilty concealment, while several historians have begun citing the novel's letter as primary evidence of the scientist's actual destruction of laboratory notes. My publisher wants an author's note that fixes my intended meaning: psychological biography, pure fictional invention, or open symbol. I feel that specifying one reading would violate the ambiguity that gives the scene its force, yet refusing any clarification feels irresponsible given the historical confusion my fiction has caused. How should I understand the relation between what I meant in composing the sentence, what the sentence does within the work, and what obligation—if any—I have to correct readers who take my invention for historical fact?
