I am a novelist presently at work on a manuscript that weaves together fictional narrative and the ethics of historical preservation, and in preparing material for a small lecture I have become preoccupied with a rather recondite tension within archival philosophy. During a recent conversation with fellow writers I noticed that debates about whether archives are neutral storehouses or active instruments of domination tend to devolve into familiar slogans; I should therefore like you to write a dialogue that escapes this routine. Present two interlocutors of equal intellectual competence—one who holds that the silences in our sources are essentially epistemic lacunae that responsible scholarship ought to repair through inference and statistical reconstruction, and another who insists that every absence is itself a trace of power, readable only through hermeneutic suspicion rather than filled in. To keep the exchange genuinely dialectical, have them confront a specific conceptual device I have been contemplating: the notion of 'negative provenance', meaning the attribution to a missing record of its own custodial history, so that an archive does not simply fail to contain an item but actively maintains a documented history of that failure. The speakers should examine whether cataloguing absence in this way neutralises its political origins or, paradoxically, renders the mechanisms of exclusion more visible. They must respond to each other's actual arguments, avoid any straw-man simplification, and refrain from reaching a tidy compromise. Please compose the dialogue in formal British English, maintain rigorous reasoning without invoking obscure specialised texts or bibliographies, and treat the problem as one that must be resolved through conceptual analysis alone.
