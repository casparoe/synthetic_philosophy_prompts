I am currently enrolled in an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I am drafting a take-home final exam question that tests whether students can think through the problem of collective mentality rather than merely summarizing familiar thought experiments. I want the prompt to center on Douglas Hofstadter's dialogue involving Aunt Hillary, the sentient ant colony, and the broader Ant Fugue analogy between ant societies and brains, but I want to avoid answers that simply recount Hofstadter's narrative or invoke standard functionalist slogans. To force genuine analysis, please build the question around the following injected scenario: researchers have engineered an ant colony structurally analogous to Aunt Hillary to stabilize a novel pheromone grammar that allows the collective to solve simple arithmetic puzzles presented through chemical gradients, even though no individual ant possesses a representational capacity for numbers or syntax, and even though every local pheromone exchange remains mechanically causal with no global observer or central register. The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether this colony now possesses a mind, and it should require them to take and defend a specific philosophical position by confronting three precise cruxes. First, they must address whether the colony's grammatical problem-solving satisfies a functionalist criterion for cognition without collapsing into an uncritical application of the other-minds problem to groups. Second, they must examine whether the total absence of individual-level understanding blocks emergent mental content in the same way that Searle's Chinese Room is meant to block machine understanding, or whether collective syntax alone can carry semantic content. Third, they must assess whether Hofstadter's neuron-ant analogy survives once we acknowledge that individual neurons do not understand neural signals yet the brain as a whole apparently does, and they must say what this implies for attributing mental states to the colony rather than merely describing its behavior. Please provide a model answer of approximately six hundred words that argues for one coherent view, using the arithmetic-grammar scenario as the central test case rather than as an afterthought, and please provide a marking scheme in continuous prose that distinguishes an excellent answer from a competent one by naming the specific conceptual moves required: for example, whether the student distinguishes collective representation from mere distributed computation, whether they explain why the pheromone system is not reducible to a digestive causal chain, whether they avoid conflating consciousness with problem-solving ability, and whether they engage with the analogy's limits rather than assuming it proves the case. The marking scheme should also note the typical errors that should cost marks, such as treating the colony's success as automatically proving functionalism, or treating Searle's argument as obviously applying without analyzing the structural differences between symbol manipulation in a room and chemical coordination in a colony. Please present the exam question, the model answer, and the marking scheme entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or enumerated lists, arranged as paragraphs in the style of a formal letter or email. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not rely on obscure technical literature that a student could not be expected to recall from coursework alone.
