I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop called "The Crowd and the Editor." Half the room are media historians who live inside Kierkegaard's Present Age; the other half are working reporters who are their mainly because they care about accountability, not philosophy. I want to use Kierkegaard's idea that the press creates "the public" — this abstract, irresponsible phantom that levels every opinion into the same weightless noise — not as a cheap shot at Twitter, but as the real frame for why we're here: to talk about editorial responsibility in an age where the crowd is always watching. The talk needs to land for both groups, and the concept should shape what I can and can't say — for instance, I shouldn't pretend there's an easy institutional fix if the deeper problem is that the crowd dissolves the very self that could fix anything. To get there, I want to weave in the less-discussed link to his earlier upbuilding discourses: leveling doesn't just flatten opinions, it flattens the inwardness that makes having an opinion matter at all. So please write the remarks in American English, keep it casual, and don't turn it into a lecture for one half of the room.
