I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and lately I've been reading some older moral philosophy on my own to think through questions about decision-making under uncertainty—something that comes up constantly in the systems I design. I recently worked through a section of Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics, published in the 1870s, in which he considers the problem of judging an action by its total consequences. As I understand the argument, Sidgwick observes that any act initiates a chain of effects that extends far beyond what we can trace: not only do we lack information about distant outcomes, but the very attempt to weigh them against one another seems to outstrip our capacity for comparative judgment. He appears to conclude that a strict consequentialist calculus quickly runs into a wall once it tries to reach beyond immediate and foreseeable results.

What I am struggling to understand is the exact nature of that wall. Is Sidgwick making a purely epistemic point—that we are like a predictive model with too little training data to forecast remote states—or is he advancing a more structural claim, namely that the framework for evaluating outcomes becomes unstable as the causal chain lengthens? I have seen modern discussions of consequentialist cluelessness that distinguish between ignorance of probabilities and a deeper problem in which our actions might alter the evaluative standards themselves. Does Sidgwick's passage anticipate that second, structural kind of cluelessness, or is he confined to the first? And if he is making the structural claim, does he present it as a practical limitation that better reasoning could eventually overcome, or as an in-principle boundary on the scope of consequentialist evaluation?

Could someone help me unpack the logic of this passage? I want to know whether Sidgwick is offering an objection that could be addressed by more information—like improving the accuracy of a forecasting system—or whether he is arguing that consequentialism contains an internal tension that forces us to restrict its application to a narrower domain of actions. If the latter, I am curious whether that restriction is meant to save consequentialism by limiting its ambitions, or whether it amounts to a more serious undermining of the view altogether.
