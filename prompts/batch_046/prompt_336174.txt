I have been thinking about Libet’s experiments and the neuroscience of free will, and I have built an argument that now seems suspicious to me because its conclusion is far more extreme than anything I want to accept. Here are the steps as I see them.

(1) In Libet-style studies, a readiness potential in the brain begins several hundred milliseconds before a person becomes consciously aware of having decided to move a finger.

(2) If the neural initiation of an action precedes conscious awareness of the decision, then the conscious decision is not the true cause; it is at best a retrospective report. So free will in the ordinary sense — that I consciously cause my voluntary actions — is undermined for bodily movement.

(3) More recent extensions of this paradigm, using multivariate decoding of neural signals, claim that similar pre-conscious signatures can predict not just simple movements but also which of two reasoning strategies a person will adopt in a logical puzzle, several seconds before they report feeling that they have chosen that strategy.

(4) Therefore, if free will requires that conscious reasoning be the causal source of our mental acts, then we lack free will not only for bodily actions but also for forming beliefs, selecting arguments, and reasoning itself.

(5) But if our reasoning processes are not causally governed by conscious choice, then our belief in determinism — and indeed our acceptance of this very argument — is not rationally justified; it is merely the output of pre-conscious neural dynamics.

(6) Consequently, the argument for the absence of free will undermines the rational authority of anyone who accepts it, including myself.

(7) Yet it seems intolerable to conclude that rational belief-formation is impossible.

(8) So either we must reject one of (1)–(3) despite the scientific findings, or we must swallow this self-defeating result. I cannot see which is worse, and I do not want to deny that I can reason at all.

Where does this chain go wrong, if it does? I should say that I am most reluctant to give up (1) and (2), because the neuroscience seems clear to me, and I am even somewhat inclined to accept (3) as a plausible extension. But I do not want to arrive at (7). Is there an equivocation between the causal role of consciousness in initiating a finger movement and its role in guiding or endorsing reasoning? Or is there a scope shift between “this particular reasoning step was predicted pre-consciously” and “all reasoning is unfree”? Or does the self-defeat in (5)–(6) not actually damage the argument, meaning my discomfort rests on a confusion about what rational justification requires?
