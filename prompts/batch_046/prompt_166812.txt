My physician prescribed a medication after reading a summary in a specialist journal. That summary reported a clinical study whose authors never personally examined the raw patient records; instead, they relied on an automated data-analysis pipeline that selected which outcomes to highlight and present. I have no medical training, so I cannot evaluate those raw records myself, and I realize that my belief that the drug works rests entirely on a chain of acceptance—me trusting my doctor, my doctor trusting the article, the authors trusting the system—without any direct observational check on my part. If I later discovered that the pipeline had systematically excluded negative results, should I conclude that my original belief was irrational because I failed to verify the evidence independently? Or is there something genuinely legitimate about accepting what others tell me, even when the chain includes non-human intermediaries and I have no independent access to the underlying facts? I want to understand whether relying on this kind of layered, mediated testimony counts as a basic form of rational belief in its own right, or whether it is only justified if I could, in principle, trace it back to direct evidence that I myself could assess.
