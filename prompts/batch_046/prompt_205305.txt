So here's something that's always bugged me: French public schools ban students from wearing conspicuous religious symbols—headscarves, big crosses, kippahs—while the same republic pays for private Catholic and Jewish schools through state contracts. Meanwhile in the Anglo-American world we usually protect religious dress in public institutions and ask the state to accomodate rather than suppress visible difference. What theory of neutrality, or of the self, does the French pairing actually presuppose? Is it that the state must be invisible in its citizens, that religious identity is a private choice that should be hidden in shared spaces, or something about the school as a factory for republican citizens? And does funding private religious schools while banning symbols in public ones reveal an incoherent commitment, or can it be made consistent with a particular view of pluralism? Contrast that with the implicit philosophy behind Anglo-American accomodation—maybe a liberal view of group rights, or just a pragmatic fear of conflict—and tell me which practice is more philosphically defensible, or whether they're actually answering different questions about what integration means.
