I am a retired natural scientist now working through recent philosophy of history, and I want to understand a specific conceptual knot in treating germs, climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions as historical actors. The difficulty seems to be this: if we deny that agency requires intentionality, then mere causal efficacy makes volcanoes actors, but it also makes gravity and friction actors, erasing the distinction between background conditions and active causes. Conversely, if we say a non-human entity counts as an actor only when it can redirect a social trajectory in ways not fully determined by prior human intentions, we risk either smuggling teleology back in or falling into environmental determinism—treating human culture as merely forced by external conditions. Is there a coherent middle position—perhaps using the distinction between linear forcing and non-linear feedback in complex adaptive systems, or between enabling conditions and triggering events—to draw this boundary without anthropomorphism? Please explain the conceptual structure of such a position, and identify precisely where it collapses into one of the two extremes it tries to avoid.
