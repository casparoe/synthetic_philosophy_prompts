I've been drafting a novel about a physicist who discovers that her weather models are determinist but totally useless, and she starts wondering if determinism even means anything if you can't compute the future. Talking it over with my writing group, I realized the actual philosophy debate about chaos and determinism has been going in circles for decades without anyone budging — her editor keeps saying she's making a catagory error, but that just made me want to figure out what's really stuck.

On one side, people argue that sensitive dependence on initial conditions kills Laplace's demon for good — not just that prediction is hard, but that determinism was always tied to the idea that you could in principle calculate what's next, and chaos breaks that link. They point to the double pendulum and weather systems as proof that determinism is an empty shell. On the other side, philosophers say determinism was never about computation or prediction; it's just the metaphysical claim that one state plus the laws fixes exactly one future state. Chaos is just an epistemic wall, not an ontological crack. Both sides cite the same examples and neither concedes, which is frankly exausting.

I think part of the stall might be that they're using determinism for two different things — maybe what we should call accessibility determinism versus nomological determinism — but I'm not sure if that's a real distinction or just me trying to fix my plot. Is this actually a verbal dispute about the word, or are there hidden formal questions about computability of initial conditions doing all the work? And what would count as progress here — a sharp distinction, a result from computability theory, or should we just admit the question is ill-posed? Also, is one side just protecting a bedrock intuition about scientific law and the other protecting free will? I'd love a diagnosis that actually looks at what each camp says rather than just picking a winner.
