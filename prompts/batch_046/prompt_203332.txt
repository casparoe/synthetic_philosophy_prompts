I'm preparing a position paper for a graduate seminar on climate ethics and want an internal consistency check before I circulate it to the group. Over the past year I have settled on the following package of commitments, which each seem defensible to me in isolation, but I have not yet stress-tested them together.

First, I accept a version of the non-identity problem: if our emissions trajectories affect which particular people are conceived and born, then the individuals who live in a high-emissions future are not the same ones who would have lived in a low-emissions future, and so we do not specifically harm them by failing to mitigate.

Second, despite that, I hold that we have robust obligations of intergenerational justice to stabilize the climate, but I ground these not in harm-avoidance but in an option-preservation principle: we ought not foreclose valuable ways of life that future people might choose to pursue, regardless of which individuals they turn out to be.

Third, I think democratic authorization for costly climate policy should come through deliberative citizens' assemblies rather than through ordinary majoritarian voting, on the grounds that the technical complexity and long time horizons of climate decisions exceed what standard electoral mechanisms can legitimately process.

Fourth, I am drawn to a contractualist framework in which moral principles must be justifiable to each actual person affected, with the capacity for reciprocity serving as a boundary condition on who falls within the direct scope of justice.

Fifth, I favor carbon pricing coupled with equal per-capita revenue dividends as the primary mitigation instrument, rather than direct regulatory mandates or state-led investment programs.

Sixth, I draw a sharp line between mitigation obligations, which I classify as duties of justice, and adaptation obligations, which I classify as matters of beneficence or charity rather than strict entitlement.

My worry is that some of these commitments pull against one another in ways I have not fully faced. I want an audit, not a lecture: go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or substantially modifying one commitment—would clean up the largest share of the problems without collapsing the whole package. Please be specific about whether the problem is that one principle undermines the usual motivation for another, or that a case verdict my stated principles should deliver they actually cannot.
