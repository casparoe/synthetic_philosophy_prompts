In my philosophy course we have been examining Glaucon’s challenge in Plato’s Republic, presented through the story of the ring of Gyges: if a person could become invisible and thereby escape every social consequence, would justice still have any independent reason to be pursued? In classroom discussions and in essays I have read, however, people do not seem to treat invisibility as a simple binary condition. Instead, they respond differently to three variations that occupy a narrow space the original story does not clearly map.

First, the classic case: complete and permanent invisibility, with no risk whatsoever that the agent will be linked to the unjust act. Second, a modified case in which the agent is invisible only during the act itself, but becomes fully visible immediately afterward, still holding the fruits of the injustice, with no practical possibility of being traced back to the hidden moment—so external punishment remains impossible, yet the agent is now seen with the stolen goods. Third, a case in which the agent is never physically hidden at all, but acts within a crowd so vast that any particular contribution is statistically undetectable, even though every movement is in principle recorded.

What strikes me is that many people who say they would act unjustly in the first case report that they would refrain in the second, despite the fact that the risk of punishment is equally absent, and they treat the third case as morally closer to the first than to ordinary visible action, even though they are technically observed throughout. This suggests that the operative factor may not be visibility or risk of sanction per se, but something like the agent’s ability to maintain a continuous, singular narrative that links them specifically to the unjust act without an intervening rupture—whether that rupture is temporal invisibility or statistical diffusion.

Is there an established concept in moral psychology or in the literature on the ring of Gyges that captures this precise sensitivity to narrative continuity, or to what one might call singular traceability? Or am I merely redescribing familiar distinctions—such as that between internal and external motivation, or between shame and guilt—without recognizing them? If no precise term exists, I would like a careful definition of the phenomenon that distinguishes it both from ordinary risk-aversion and from the standard internalist reply to Glaucon, together with an account of why the second case feels different from the first despite both lacking external sanction. I am especially interested in the boundary conditions: does the phenomenon apply when the rupture is not temporal or statistical but cognitive, for instance when the agent acts in a state of extreme emotional arousal and later genuinely cannot recall whether they were the specific one who acted?
