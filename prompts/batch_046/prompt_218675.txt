I'm preparing remarks for a graduate seminar in political philosophy, and I would value critical feedback—particularly on whether the framing is accurate to Weil's texts, whether it is philosophically interesting rather than merely contrarian, and whether the central analogy survives scrutiny. Rather than a finished essay, I offer the core argument and ask you to stress-test it. Simone Weil's well-known critique of political parties holds that they are totalitarian in embryo: they manufacture collective passions that suppress independent judgment and substitute group loyalty for direct moral perception. I want to read this not merely as institutional sociology but as an extension of her ethics of attention—the idea that genuine moral knowledge requires the suspension of the ego, a disciplined openness to the particular suffering of another. On this reading, a party is dangerous not only because it enforces doctrinal orthodoxy, but because it constructs a collective ego that makes such attention structurally impossible; the partisan receives a pre-calibrated emotional script—indignation, solidarity, outrage—tailored to group identity rather than to the object itself.

I am considering extending this by linking party loyalty to Weil's broader distinction between gravity and grace, specifically to her observation that imagination tends to substitute mental images for reality, thereby relieving us of the burden of direct encounter. The worry is that this imports too much metaphysics into political theory, or that it obscures Weil's own practical commitments—she worked in unions and resistance networks, so she clearly accepted some forms of collective action. The essay would therefore close with a conceptual question: is any collective political identity compatible with Weil's standard, or is a group defined purely by shared practical commitments, without emotional identification or imaginative substitution, merely liberal individualism disguised? I want to avoid treating her as a naive anti-institutionalist, but I also do not want to dilute her radical claim.

Please respond plainly if I am misrepresenting Weil's actual view of collective bodies, if the comparison between party loyalty and the mechanism of imagination collapses, or if the task itself rests on a false dichotomy between pure attention and organized politics.
