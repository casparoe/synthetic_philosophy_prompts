I'm preparing a discussion for a philosophy-of-biology reading group and want to avoid the standard "playing God" interpretation of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein. Instead, please read the novel—particularly Victor's construction of the creature and the creature's own narrative of learning to survive in the woods—through the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. On the etiological side, Wright's selected-effects theory and Millikan's proper-function framework hold that a trait's function is fixed by its history of selection or design; on the causal-role side, Cummins's systemic-capacity analysis and Boorse's goal-contribution view hold that function is constituted by present contribution to a containing system's capacities. The creature is a limit case: its heart, brain, and sensory organs were never jointly selected, and their original evolutionary histories belong to different bodies, yet they are assembled into a single living system. Does the novel treat the creature as possessing genuine biological functions, and if so, what fixes them—Victor's design intentions, the parts' past histories, or the actual causal roles they come to play in sustaining this particular organism?

I'd like a reading that treats both Shelley and the philosophical debate with equal seriousness, rather than reducing the novel to a mere illustration. Focus especially on the fire scene, where the creature discovers causal powers—warmth, cooking, light—that were never part of his intended design, and on Victor's revulsion, which seems directed not just at appearance but at a failure of parts to cohere into something with a "natural" functional unity. Where does the text support the causal-role theorist, suggesting that living makes its own functions? Where does it resist, perhaps implying that without a unified evolutionary or design history the creature is functionally hollow—parts that perform tasks but lack proper functions? To push the analysis further, please also consider the niche distinction between "derived proper functions" inherited from the parts' original biological contexts and "systemic functions that emerge de novo" within the assembled body. Does Shelley dramatize the collapse of that distinction, and if so, does that collapse tell us something about real-world chimeras or synthetic organisms that a standard thought experiment misses? Conclude by identifying what the novel contributes to the philosophical argument—something about embodiment, horror, or the normative force of "natural" origin—that argument alone cannot supply.
