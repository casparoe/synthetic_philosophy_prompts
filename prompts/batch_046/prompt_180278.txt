Consider the institutional practice in predictive public-health planning where a risk model assigns a fixed mortality count to a specific cohort tied to a specific place—say, that three hundred members of a high-risk group are forecast to die in Damascus over the next decade. If a substantial portion of that cohort relocates to Beirut, the model does not reduce the total expected deaths; it reassigns the same three hundred fatalities to Beirut and shifts the corresponding preventive budget with them. The framework thus treats mortality as geographically invariant for the cohort, yet it also treats the assigned city as the causally relevant site where the deaths occur.

What philosophical commitments about causation, inevitability, and responsibility does this forecasting practice presuppose? Does it implicitly endorse a fatalist logic analogous to the Death in Damascus parable—where escaping the predicted location only relocates the fulfillment—or can it be reconstructed as a purely correlational allocation rule carrying no metaphysical baggage? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether a genuinely location-sensitive, non-determinist policy could retain the statistical framework or would have to abandon it entirely.
