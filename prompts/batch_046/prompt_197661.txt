Explain the conceptual structure of Bernard Williams's objection about having one thought too many, distinguishing carefully between its psychological, justificatory, and structural dimensions. Begin by clarifying whether the problem is that the agent calculates when they should not, that they justify their action through an impartial principle that misrepresents the nature of the attachment, or that the very presence of such a principle in their motivational economy corrupts the integrity of their ground projects. Then examine whether the objection depends essentially on Williams's broader account of integrity and ground projects, or whether it can stand independently as a critique of any moral theory that requires explicit deliberative mediation between agent and beloved. Finally, consider what happens if we attempt to translate the objection into the standard analytic vocabulary of agent-relative versus agent-neutral reasons: does the distinctive phenomenological and structural force of the objection survive that translation intact, get distorted into a merely quantitative weighting problem, or reveal a gap in the agent-relative/agent-neutral framework itself?
