Here are my verdicts about when archaeological patterns license an inference to a shared belief rather than merely to an institution or practical convention, and I want to know whether a single principle captures them.

Case 1: Every house in a settlement faces one mountain peak, with no functional reason for orientation, sustained for centuries across all social ranks. I take this to warrant attributing a shared cosmological belief.

Case 2: The same settlement uses standardized storage jars with identical capacities, found in both administrative and domestic contexts. I take this to warrant a centralized measuring institution, but not any particular belief about storage.

Case 3: Burial practice invariantly places a specific iron tool in the deceased’s right hand, regardless of age, sex, or status, over generations. I read this as evidence of a cosmological belief about post-mortem agency.

Case 4: Every house also contains a raised mud-brick platform in its northeast corner, invariant across rank and time. Despite the parallel uniformity, I think this is best explained as an institutional building convention or practical adaptation—not a directional belief.

Case 5: After a sudden stratigraphic break, cremation is replaced by inhumation using the same grave goods, while settlement patterns and toolkits remain continuous. I think we may infer an institutional or political imposition, but I am not entitled to conclude that the population’s beliefs about death changed.

Case 6: A single rural site yields one sophisticated astronomical instrument, with no texts, calendrical debris, or elite architecture nearby. I treat it as an imported curiosity or personal artifact, not as evidence of institutional astronomy or widespread cosmological belief.

Case 7: Wall paintings across a region share an identical abstract geometric motif, but pigment analysis shows they were applied in different centuries with chemically distinct recipes and no shared workshop traces. Despite visual uniformity, I deny that this supports either a unified ritual belief or a centralized artistic institution.

The hard tension is between Cases 1 and 3 (uniformity read as belief) and Case 4 (identical uniformity read as institution/practice), while Case 7 shows uniformity I reject altogether and Case 5 shows discontinuity I read only institutionally.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving functional redundancy, symbolic saturation, or the explanatory priority of practical alternatives—that delivers all seven verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause handles each case, predict its verdict for an unlisted scenario (for example, a standardized weight system found exclusively in temples but absent from markets), and say honestly which of my verdicts it must overturn if simplicity is preferred. If no single principle works, present the two strongest candidates, map each to the cases it saves and sacrifices, and defend which trade-off is more defensible.
