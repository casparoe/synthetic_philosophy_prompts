I am preparing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of dispositions, and I want to move beyond the usual toy examples—Martin’s electro-finked wire, the antidote counterexamples to simple conditional analyses, and the distinction between finkish prevention and masking—to a literary case that tests the framework from the inside. For this session I have assigned Robert Louis Stevenson’s Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde, a text whose plot and major scenes are common knowledge but whose metaphysical structure is rarely examined with philosophical precision.

The novella presents what I want to call a self-finking disposition: Jekyll bears a tendency to become Hyde, yet he also possesses and administers the chemical mechanism that either triggers or suppresses that very tendency. Read the work through the lens of contemporary disposition theory, treating the potion not merely as a plot device but as a metaphysical instrument. Is Jekyll’s sober self best understood as a mask that blocks Hyde’s underlying disposition, or is Hyde merely a manifested state lacking an independent dispositional base? When the drug eventually fails and Hyde emerges spontaneously, does this indicate that the mask has been destroyed, that the fink has been disabled, or that Jekyll and Hyde are multi-track dispositions of a single substance? I want the analysis faithful in both directions: do not distort Stevenson’s Gothic psychology to fit the metaphysics, and do not reduce the philosophical problem to a narrative illustration.

Please address where the novella resists the analysis—perhaps in Hyde’s apparent moral autonomy, or in the reversibility of the transformation, which seems difficult to reconcile with standard accounts of dispositional loss or identity over time. Conclude, if possible, with what the work adds to the debate that argument alone misses: perhaps a problem about dispositional identity under voluntary control, or about whether an agent can genuinely instantiate a disposition antithetical to their conscious intentions. The audience will be students who know the metaphysical literature but may not have read the novella recently, so work from the plot as commonly remembered and do not ask them to consult the text for verbatim quotations. Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations.
