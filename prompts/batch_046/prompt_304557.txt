I serve on a university committee charged with adjudicating proposals to rename, contextualize, or retain campus landmarks. Our published precedents are as follows. First, we approved removing a bronze statue of the institution's founder after concluding that its commemorative function—honoring philanthropic vision—could not be decoupled from the subject's active participation in slaveholding, and that contextual plaques do not neutralize the honorific posture of a freestanding monument. Second, we required a permanent critical exhibition inside a science building that retains the name of a researcher whose data derived from non-consensual human experiments, holding that retention with internal contextualization is permissible when the name serves a primary referential and institutional-historical function rather than an exemplary one. Third, we rejected renaming a central plaza named after a regional politician who endorsed segregationist legislation, reasoning that over decades the toponym had undergone sufficient referential drift—functioning now as pure spatial index rather than moral endorsement—to make renaming a form of presentist erasure rather than ethical correction. Fourth, we approved replacing the name of an endowed lecture hall named after an industrialist who violently suppressed labor organizing, but only after negotiating that the original family foundation fund an independent archival repository documenting that suppression; we treated the archive as a necessary condition for severing commemorative honor without destroying institutional memory. Fifth, we refused to remove a mural depicting colonial settlement that is structurally integrated into a protected architectural ensemble, mandating instead an interpretive panel and periodic public programming, on the ground that aesthetic-functional integration can constrain removal even when the depicted events warrant moral condemnation.

The new proposal concerns a pedestrian bridge—used in navigation systems and postal addresses—named after a civil engineer whose design for a different bridge collapsed due to his negligence, killing forty-three people. There is no plaque, portrait, or ceremony attached to the bridge; the name operates almost entirely as a spatial coordinate. The proposal asks not for removal of the name but for two measures: installing an adjacent "counter-bridge" memorial listing the victims, and adding a small plaque noting the engineer's responsibility for the other disaster. A minority on the committee argues that retaining the name in any form constitutes an ongoing honorific, especially since navigation algorithms still vocalize the engineer's surname; a majority counters that the name has undergone near-complete commemorative decoupling and now functions as dead toponymy, and that the counter-memorial transforms the site into a dialectical space rather than a shrine.

Assuming we want consistency with our own track record rather than an independent ethical theory, what should we decide—retain with the counter-memorial, rename entirely, or adopt the hybrid? Identify the closest analog among our five precedents, explain which implicit rule best predicts that analog and therefore our verdict, and state honestly whether our precedents genuinely underdetermine this case. If they do, specify the principle we would be adopting with each available choice—particularly regarding whether commemorative decoupling is a matter of empirical usage, institutional intent, or normative reconstruction. Finally, name the further hypothetical case—different in one specific dimension—that would best distinguish between the rule you think we are running and the closest competing rule.
