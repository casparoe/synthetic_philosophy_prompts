I want to know whether the psychology of moral foundations tells us anything normative, and I will lay out my own verdicts on specific cases to see if they hang together. First, the familiar left-right divergence—liberals weighting care and fairness more heavily, conservatives distributing weight across loyalty, authority, and sanctity—strikes me as purely descriptive; it gives no reason to think one side is closer to moral truth. Second, if an entire culture scores extremely high on loyalty and authority but very low on care, I do not take that profile to justify its practices; I read it as a collective moral blind spot rather than an alternative valid equilibrium. Third, I want to introduce a distinction I have been calling reach-weight asymmetry: people may differ not only in how intensely they activate each foundation, but in how far they extend it—care for kin only, for strangers, for non-human animals—without any change in emotional intensity. I feel that this reach dimension, not the underlying weight profile, is what carries normative significance; extending care widely looks like moral progress, not merely like being more liberal. Fourth, I am genuinely torn about two individuals who share identical reach and weight across all five foundations, but one ranks care lexically over authority while the other integrates them through what I will call ordered reciprocity, allowing authority to bind only within relationships that already satisfy care. Both seem rationally coherent, yet I am inclined to say the first view is closer to correct if authority is genuinely non-fundamental, though I worry that declaring it non-fundamental is circular. Fifth, imagine a hypothetical finding—call it contamination mapping—showing that people high in sanctity experience purity violations as literal tactile contamination, while people high in care experience neglect as auditory distress. I do not think these embodied correlations validate either foundation; they look like evolutionary packaging that should carry no justificatory weight. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that separates descriptive weighting from normative reach, treats embodied correlations as non-justificatory, and accounts for my ambivalence about ordered reciprocity—that preserves all five verdicts? If not, which one must I abandon?
