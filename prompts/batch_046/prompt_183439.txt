I'm preparing a consistency check for my comparative politics seminar paper on democratic erosion, and I want to see whether my commitments actually cohere before I present them. Here is where I stand. (1) Democratic backsliding should be defined narrowly as ex ante institutional degradation—changes to constitutional courts, media structures, or electoral rules that systematically weaken the conditions for future competitive elections—rather than ex post fraud, election subversion, or partisan rhetoric; and it should be kept analytically separate from broader "liberal democratic decay," which can erode rights and pluralism without necessarily distorting electoral fairness. (2) Applying this framework, I treat Hungary under Fidesz and Poland under Law and Justice as clear cases of backsliding, because their court-packing and capture of public media constitute ex ante restructuring that tilts future electoral playing fields, even though national elections remain technically competitive and opposition parties still win some offices. (3) By contrast, I maintain that descriptions of the contemporary United States as undergoing democratic backsliding are largely conceptually imprecise and function as partisan mobilization: US controversies involve ex post attempts to overturn specific election results, aggressive partisan gerrymandering, and judicial politicization, but I do not see these as amounting to ex ante systemic degradation of national electoral conditions on a par with Budapest or Warsaw. (4) At the same time, I deny that there is any neutral arbiter for these classifications: international measurement indices embed liberal-democratic normative assumptions that are not politically innocent, and domestic constitutional courts are themselves actors in the conflict rather than impartial referees. (5) I further hold that backsliding is best understood as an intentional strategy of executive aggrandizement, not as an unintended systemic drift, which means analysis should prioritize institutional design and agent-level intent rather than relying solely on outcome-based metrics. (6) Finally, I think the concept is gradational and non-binary, with rapid, non-linear trajectories—Turkey being the paradigmatic case—so that a regime can slide significantly without ever crossing a discrete authoritarian threshold.

My question is whether this package is jointly tenable. Specifically: does my narrow ex ante definition in (1) genuinely support the Hungary/Poland classification in (2), or does their court-capture also involve liberal-decay elements that should push me to separate the two phenomena? Does my dismissal of US backsliding in (3) collapse once I acknowledge in (4) that there is no neutral standard—if classification is inherently contested and normatively loaded, on what non-partisan ground do I reject US applications while accepting Hungarian and Polish ones? Does my emphasis on intentional aggrandizement in (5) require me to attribute specific intent in ways that my skepticism about neutral arbiters in (4) undermines? And does my gradational, non-binary view in (6) sit comfortably with the binary-looking distinction I draw in (3) between "genuine backsliding" and "partisan labeling"?

Please rank these tensions by severity, indicate which single commitment is the least costly to revise without destroying the overall framework, and tell me whether there is one adjustment—perhaps redefining the ex ante/ex post boundary, softening the US exceptionalism claim, or reconceiving the role of intent—that resolves the most tension at once. I am not asking for a literature review, bibliography, or citation list; I want an internal consistency audit that treats my position as the object.
