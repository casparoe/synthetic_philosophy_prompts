As a novelist running a workshop for fellow writers of historical fiction, I need a teaching plan — not the lecture itself — for a single ninety-minute session with authors who are drafting novels set before 1800 and who unconsciously write their characters as proto-modern thinkers marching toward present-day values. The concept is Whig history: the temptation to treat the past as a failed or incomplete draft of the present, especially the belief that historical actors were groping toward conclusions we now hold. Before I ask for the arc, specify the dependency order: which idea must be secure before the writers can recognize this error in their own narrative choices, and what hinges on what? For the carrying case, do not use Galileo, the Scientific Revolution, or the standard dark-ages-to-enlightenment trajectory; instead, construct or select one concrete historical moment in which a pre-modern culture maintained a coherent, internally complete worldview — one that modern scholarship often misreads as primitive science, failed philosophy, or an incomplete version of our own categories — and explain exactly what this case must contain and what it must lack in order to do the work that the usual examples cannot. Then map the misconceptions: I expect at least two — first, that if my medieval protagonist does not secretly long for modern individualism the character feels historically implausible, and second, that progress is an observable pattern in the record rather than a narrative frame the writer is imposing — and indicate precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and defused, with reference to the dependency structure you have laid out. Include a brief check for understanding: a writing exercise or targeted question that distinguishes whether the participants have genuinely understood the historiographical distortion from whether they have simply replaced it with a nihilistic stance that nothing ever improves. State explicitly what standard academic apparatus — named historiographical schools, specific scholarly controversies, or canonical texts — should be left out at this level and acknowledged as omitted. Conclude with a single line beginning Summary: that captures the essential pedagogical principle of the plan.
