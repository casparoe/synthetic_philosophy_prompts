In a graduate seminar on Marxist aesthetics, a colleague recently appealed to the familiar line that the economic base determines the superstructure in order to reject any autonomous logic of modernist poetic form—contending that every formal innovation in verse was ultimately explicable by shifts in publishing capital and in the class composition of the readership, so that art retained no genuinely independent developmental dynamics. I want to understand precisely what was being claimed, because the same formulation seemed to shift its meaning across moments in the exchange. Please disentangle the distinct, precise theses that this slogan could be expressing. I have in mind at least these candidates: a causal thesis about the institutional origins of cultural practices; a functional thesis about the reproductive role of ideology within a given mode of production; an explanatory-privilege thesis that assigns economic relations priority in the order of explanation without denying reciprocal influence; a strong determinist thesis that denies any causal efficacy running from culture back to the economy; and a reductionist thesis that treats aesthetic properties as ontologically nothing over and above economic relations. For each, indicate whether it amounts to a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, an empirically vulnerable thesis, or a false or conceptually incoherent overstatement. Then specify which of these readings was actually required for my colleague's dismissal of poetic autonomy to succeed, and whether that particular reading is one that analytical Marxists or Marxist humanists could consistently accept. I am not asking for a bibliography or a historical genealogy; I want a conceptual map with verdicts, and I want to know which version of the claim, if any, genuinely carries the argumentative weight in that specific context.
