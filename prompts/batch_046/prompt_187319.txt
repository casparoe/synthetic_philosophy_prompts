After my older brother underwent a partial corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy earlier this year, I have found myself unable to stop considering whether he remains a single conscious subject or whether the surgery has quietly bifurcated him into two. His neurologist explained that explicit linguistic reasoning remains largely left-lateralized, while spatial attention and emotional prosody lean rightward, yet in daily life he behaves as one social agent, sometimes reaching for an object with his left hand in ways that seem to surprise his own verbal account. I am not seeking clinical prognosis; rather, I want the philosophical framework that can determine what a theory of consciousness in split-brain cases actually needs to deliver if it is to count as adequate rather than merely suggestive.

Please set out the complete desiderata that any theory of post-callosotomy consciousness must satisfy, framing the problem around the unity of phenomenal and access consciousness rather than treating it as a purely neuroanatomical puzzle. For each requirement, provide the specific case or empirical consideration that motivates it: how the theory must account for simultaneous contradictory motor intentions, the preservation of a single autobiographical narrative over time, the phenomenon of cross-cuing between hemispheres, and what I am calling the binding-gap dilemma, in which contradictory sensory inputs are fully processed within each hemisphere yet fail to achieve the temporal synchronization necessary for a unified conscious gestalt. You should also specify the distinctions that must be preserved rather than collapsed, such as that between being a subject of experience and being an agent of action, between diachronic personal identity and synchronic mental integration, and between phenomenal presence and cognitive accessibility. Indicate clearly which desiderata are non-negotiable given established neuroscience and which are merely commitments of particular philosophical camps, for instance an insistence on a Cartesian ego versus a functionalist approach to personal identity. Most importantly, address whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: in particular, can a theory consistently maintain strict phenomenal unity at every instant while also accommodating the functional independence that cross-cuing experiments appear to allow? Present everything as continuous prose in the manner of a formal letter or email.
