I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level graduate seminar on formal epistemology and probabilistic reasoning that I have been teaching this term. We have worked through standard Bayesian conditioning, the problem of old evidence, and Richard Jeffrey's proposal for conditioning on uncertain evidence, but I am concerned that students might answer by simply reciting the canonical formula and its standard motivation without grasping the structural conditions that make the rule valid. To prevent this, I want an essay question built around a constructed scenario—the "calibrated-instrument problem"—in which a researcher attempts to update beliefs about a continuous physical parameter using an instrument whose measurement variance is itself unknown and, crucially, depends non-linearly on the parameter's true value. The student must determine whether Jeffrey conditionalization applies without circularity, identify which independence condition is violated, explain how a hierarchical Bayesian model differs, and relate the failure back to the debate about whether Jeffrey conditioning preserves prior ratios within partition cells. Please write the exam prompt (appropriate for a 90-minute sitting, with an expected response of 800 to 1,000 words), a model answer of roughly 800 words that makes the specific moves required for a top mark, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer—which must explicitly discuss the partition-independence condition and propose the hierarchical alternative—from a B answer that applies Jeffrey's rule mechanically, and from a C answer that conflates the procedure with Bayes-factor updating. I also want the scheme to flag the common mistake of treating the instrument's reliability as fixed background knowledge rather than as a parameter to be updated jointly.
