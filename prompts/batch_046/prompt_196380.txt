I'm a journalist covering the philosophy of computation, and I've noticed a framing gap in how the Church-Turing thesis is usually debated. The thesis is typically cast either as a mathematical result about Turing-equivalence or as an empirical conjecture about physical processes. But there is a third reading that treats it as a normative boundary: effective calculability is defined precisely as what a discrete, step-by-step mechanism can do, which means any physical process that computes non-computable functions—say, via continuous analog dynamics or infinite-precision quantum states—can be dismissed not for failing experimentally, but for falling outside the thesis's intended background conditions. Critics call this move empty; defenders say it preserves a pre-theoretic intuition about mechanical procedure.

Write an essay weighing these three interpretations—mathematical, empirical, and normative-definitional. Focus especially on whether analog or quantum hypercomputation proposals genuinely threaten the thesis, or whether they merely violate its background conditions (continuous values, non-computable initial conditions, unbounded measurement precision) rather than its core claim. Reach and justify a specific conclusion about the thesis's actual epistemic status, and explain what this implies for whether hypercomputation research is conceptually confused or empirically legitimate.
