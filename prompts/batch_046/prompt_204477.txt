I am trying to work out whether everyday conventions—tipping norms, queue discipline, and rules about jaywalking—generate genuine moral obligations for visitors, or whether they remain merely local customs that outsiders may observe or ignore without fault. Here are my verdicts, and I want to know whether one principle can capture them, or whether I must abandon some.

A tourist in Tokyo leaves cash on the table, not knowing that tipping is considered offensive there; I think she has done nothing morally wrong, though she ought to learn the local custom. A visitor in London who knowingly pushes past others to board a train: I think this is not merely rude but a minor moral failure. A pedestrian in Berlin who crosses against a red light at 3 a.m. on an empty street, when locals wait: I think this is permissible, but if she does it repeatedly during busy hours to save a few seconds, it becomes wrong. A foreign student who learns that tipping is expected in her host country and then refuses out of principle: I think this is wrong, though less seriously wrong than refusing to pay a bill she owes. A traveler who scrupulously follows queue norms in London but ignores them in Rome, arguing that both conventions are arbitrary: I think she owes an explanation—either she follows both or justifies an exception—yet I am unsure whether she owes this because of tacit consent, coordination needs, or simple respect. A diplomat with formal immunity who routinely jaywalks in a country where the convention is strongly observed but rarely enforced: I am genuinely divided—does status suspend the obligation, or does it apply regardless?

What principle, if any, unifies these six judgments? Must I drop the idea that these obligations require some form of knowledge or implicit acceptance, or can a middle position distinguish conventions that function as pure coordination devices from those that carry expressive or respect-based weight? Please state your best candidate precisely, show which clause of it is motivated by each case, and say which of my verdicts it cannot save. Then apply it to the case I keep returning to: a visitor who learns that in this particular culture leaving a small tip is interpreted as an insult, yet leaves one out of ingrained habit—does your principle condemn her, excuse her, or split the difference? Finally, tell me what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a tourist who observes every local convention except one she finds personally degrading, and who refuses it openly rather than secretly.
