I hold a view drawn from Hofstadter’s patternist account of the self but pushed into less familiar territory: that an “I” is not merely capable of sequential continuation in another substrate, as in copying or gradual neural replacement, but can be genuinely distributed—simultaneously co-instantiated across two or more functioning brains with real-time, integrated first-person access, such that one subject might literally have two bodies with a unified, non-duplicated experiential field rather than two separate subjects or one hidden “master” brain. What would count against this distributed-self thesis?

I want an honest exposure map, not a defense. Which empirical findings in neuroscience or clinical neurology—say, about split-brain dynamics, corpus-callosum integration failures, or the neural correlates of unified consciousness—would actually threaten the claim rather than just complicate its implementation? Which phenomenological intuitions—the apparent singularity of the “now,” the exclusivity of bodily ownership, or the seeming impossibility of two simultaneous first-person perspectives—are genuine defeaters, and which can the view absorb by redefining the phenomenology of distributed embodiment? Are there formal or logical obstacles—perhaps involving self-reference, contradictions in attributing one mental state to spatially separated processes, or failures of transitivity in identity—such that simultaneous co-instantiation collapses into either a single hidden location or into two distinct subjects?

Distinguish the fatal from the merely awkward. Which of these defeaters would a committed patternist about the self concede as counting against the distributed version, and which would they dismiss as substrate-chauvinism or as misapplying sequential-identity intuitions to a non-sequential case? Is there a live risk that defenders can retreat to an unfalsifiable core—claiming that integration is “just a matter of sufficient pattern fidelity” with no empirical boundary condition—and if so, does that insulation undermine the view’s explanatory value or is it an acceptable feature of a metaphysical claim? Rank the defeaters by decisiveness, indicate which you take to be already live concerns rather than hypothetical ones, and say whether you think the thesis is well-insulated in a way that is a virtue or a vice.
