I recently joined a working group drafting language for a national memorial recognizing atrocities committed by prior regimes. In our sessions, several members invoke the line: "Guilt is always individual; a nation may carry political responsibility, but it can never be collectively guilty." They deploy it specifically to resist framing the memorial as an act of national atonement, arguing that since no living citizen committed the crimes, attributing guilt to the present collective is conceptually confused. I want to know whether this is a serious distinction or a rhetorical stopper. Could you lay out the precise theses that slogan might express — for instance, distinctions between criminal culpability and institutional liability, between diachronic collective continuity and synchronic group agency, between inherited moral stain and present structural complicity, or between metaphysical guilt and its absence? For each reading, say whether it is a near-truism, a substantive and defensible claim, or a false category error, and indicate which readings actually support blocking an atonement framing and which leave it open. I am looking for a disambiguation menu I can bring back to the group rather than a single interpretation.
