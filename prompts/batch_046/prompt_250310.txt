I've been preparing notes for a metaethics and population ethics reading group, and I want to check whether my package of views is internally coherent before I present it. Here is where I've roughly landed: (1) I accept Benatar's asymmetry in its standard form — the absence of suffering counts as good even without a subject to benefit, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad; (2) I draw from this a categorical antinatalist conclusion: there is always decisive reason not to procreate, irrespective of how pleasant the prospective life might be; (3) I hold that potential persons have negative but not positive moral status — they can be harmed by being brought into suffering, but they are not wronged by being left in non-existence, however blissful that existence might have been; (4) I explain the asymmetry through an impersonal axiology: suffering carries intrinsic negative value that need not be borne by anyone, whereas pleasure lacks symmetric intrinsic value; (5) yet I also maintain that normative reasons must be ultimately anchored in the interests or well-being of actual beings, and that purely impersonal values generate no direct obligations; (6) I believe that even if a technology could guarantee every future child a life of unblemished bliss with a risk of suffering that is only infinitesimal, one should still refrain from procreation, because the asymmetry removes any positive reason to create while leaving intact even the faintest possibility of harm; and (7) I take it that this position is fully compatible with the Epicurean view that death is not harmful to the deceased, since there is no subject present to suffer deprivation. Do these commitments hang together, or am I smuggling in incompatible motivations? Specifically, is there a deep tension between (4) and (5), and does (6) implicitly rely on a risk-weighted expected-utility calculus that sits awkwardly with the lexical priority of suffering I seem to endorse in (2)? Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguish genuine conflicts from merely apparent ones, and identify the single least costly revision that would restore coherence.
