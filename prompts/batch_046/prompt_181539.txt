I'm trying to map my own considered judgments about the ethics of bringing people into existence, specifically in relation to the asymmetry between harm and benefit associated with Benatar. I want to see whether they hang together around a single principle, or whether I must discard one or two as outliers. Here are my verdicts, given as a real person's gut reactions rather than as a formal system.

First, if a couple knows their child will suffer severely and avoidably—for instance, a genetic disorder that produces chronic pain, and screening was available but they declined—then creating that child is clearly wrong. The harm is real, and the fact that the non-existent child is not "deprived" of pleasure does not cancel it.

Second, if a couple knows their child would live a life of continuous, intense pleasure with no suffering whatsoever, but they choose not to conceive, I do not think they have done anything wrong. The absence of that pleasure is not a harm to anyone.

Third, if a child will live a life that is exactly welfare-neutral—conscious, but with no suffering and no pleasure, just persistent neutral affect—I still think it is wrong to create them. The reason is not that they are actively harmed, but that existence introduces the structural possibility of harm without any mirror-image evaluative pull in the opposite direction.

Fourth, if a couple can either conceive a new child with a net welfare of +80, or adopt an existing child with a current net welfare of -30 and improve that child's life to +40, and they choose conception, I think they have done something wrong. Yet I do not think this wrongness comes from harming the new child—they are not harmed—but from failing to prevent an existing harm when they could have.

Fifth, if parents conceive a child knowing the child will have a net-positive life of +60 but will experience one brief, unavoidable episode of extreme suffering that serves no purpose, I judge this wrong, though less severely wrong than the avoidable suffering in the first case.

Sixth, if a society collectively decides not to reproduce for a century, knowing that future generations would have led rich, happy lives, I do not think this is wrong. There is no one who is deprived.

The pair that keeps pulling me in opposite directions: in the third case I treat the introduction of a neutral existence as procreatively wrong, but in the sixth case I treat the permanent withholding of a vast number of happy lives as not wrong. The structural logic looks parallel—absence of benefit, no one to be deprived—yet my verdicts diverge sharply depending on whether existence is actually introduced versus collectively withheld, or whether the evaluation attaches to an individual welfare-slot versus an aggregate.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a modified or restricted version of Benatar's asymmetry—that delivers all six verdicts, including that tension? Please state the principle precisely, identifying which clause is motivated by each case. Then apply it to two cases I have not listed: (a) parents who conceive a child with a net welfare of +10 but who will live in a world where climate collapse is probable and the child will suffer moderate, foreseeable harm in old age that they could have avoided by not conceiving; and (b) a decision not to conceive because the parents prefer to invest resources in creating art rather than raising a child who would have been moderately happy. Finally, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts the principle must overturn if it is to remain simple, and whether that overturning is plausible.
