Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—Dr. K., who defends the view that rational agency requires a functional "Cartesian separation" (an approximate but real boundary that allows an agent to model itself as distinct from its environment), and Dr. M., who defends embedded agency (the claim that decision-making is always causally entangled with the environment, and that drawing an agent-world boundary is only a pragmatic convenience, not an ontological necessity).

Ground the exchange in a specific, concrete case: a hypothetical distributed forecasting system that runs entirely within the cooling infrastructure it monitors, so that any computation it performs to predict future heat loads also physically alters the dissipation patterns it is trying to forecast, and any "choice" to reroute processing changes the very substrate on which its reasoning runs. Dr. K. should argue that without a stable representational boundary separating the system's intentions from these physical feedback loops, the system cannot genuinely plan for future states but can only react; she should press that recursive self-modeling still presupposes a distinguishable modeler. Dr. M. should respond that internal models can be recursively structured without requiring an external "view from nowhere," and that treating the boundary as real creates paradoxes of self-reference—particularly when the system updates its own predictive architecture in real time. Require real back-and-forth: each speaker must address the other's actual point about representational stability versus causal entanglement, not talk past it. Include at least one turn in which one speaker anticipates and directly answers the standard reply the other side typically offers. Keep both characters free of strawman positions, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts.
