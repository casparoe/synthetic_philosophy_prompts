As a journalist who reports on science and public policy, I have been tracing how economists and philosophers apply intertemporal valuation to very long-horizon problems—climate mitigation, biodiversity preservation, and the maintenance of measurement infrastructures that must remain comparable across centuries. In the course of this work I have noticed a recurring pattern that I cannot place precisely in the literature on pure time preference. The pattern is this. When analysts discount future streams of material welfare—clean air, harvest yields, avoided damages—they almost uniformly apply an exponential or hyperbolic discount rate and treat the result as a present-value cost or benefit. Yet when they consider the preservation of a conceptual or institutional framework that makes those future streams intelligible—say, the continuity of a climatic baseline dataset, a legal definition of property rights that must span generations, or a calibration standard without which future measurements become incommensurable—they frequently resist applying the same discount rate. Instead, they treat the framework's survival across time as having a kind of temporal immunity: its value at date t is not discounted relative to its value now, or is discounted by a markedly flatter curve, on the grounds that losing the framework collapses the possibility of evaluating the material streams at all. This is not merely the claim that some goods are priceless or that capital goods should be treated differently from consumption goods; it is a specific asymmetry between the temporal location of a material flow and the temporal location of the evaluative conditions that make that flow assessable. The same analyst will discount a river's recreational yield in 2120 at three percent, but treat the preservation of the hydrological taxonomy used to describe that river in 2120 as if it carried near-full present value, because without the taxonomy, the yield is not the same yield. I am trying to determine whether this asymmetry is recognized as a distinct problem in the theory of pure time preference—perhaps as a boundary case for what should be included in the utility stream being discounted, or as an implicit distinction between first-order and second-order intertemporal goods—or whether it is an unexamined category error that runs through applied practice. Does it have an established name? How does it differ from the ordinary capital-versus-consumption distinction, from the literature on framework goods or incommensurability, and from arguments that invoke the non-identity problem? If there is no established term, how should one define the phenomenon precisely, and what would be the correct way to integrate it into a formal discounting model without either collapsing it into standard capital maintenance or granting it an unjustified exemption?
