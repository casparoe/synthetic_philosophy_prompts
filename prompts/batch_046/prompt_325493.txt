I've noticed a recurring structure when reading moral biography—Plutarch's Lives, some medieval vitae, even modern exemplary narratives—where a subject's specific decision is rendered in enough granular detail that the reader can follow the reasoning, as if for instruction. At the same time, those same details emphasize a unique convergence of character, office, and crisis that makes the decision look unrepeatable in any other context. The biography offers the life as a model, yet builds its narrative power out of the very particularity that blocks direct imitation. This isn't quite the old worry about hagiographic falsehood, nor the simple claim that exemplars are superhuman; it's a pedagogical tension where moral intelligibility and non-transferability are jointly required. Does this configuration have an established name in work on moral exemplarity or the uses of biography? If there is one, how exactly does it differ from standard discussions of inimitable virtue or from ordinary context-sensitivity in moral reasoning? If not, how should its edges be drawn—does it require a religious framing, does it apply to secular exemplars, and what separates it from cases where a biography simply warns against repeating an error?
