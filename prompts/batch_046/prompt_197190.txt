I'm drafting my position for a workshop on moral responsibility and want to check whether these commitments actually cohere. I hold: (1) that standing to blame is scalar rather than binary—one can possess it to greater or lesser degrees, and the appropriate modality of blame (public, authoritative, private, merely expressive) should track that degree; (2) that hypocrisy undermines standing only, and entirely, when the blamer is guilty of the very same kind of wrong at a comparable level of seriousness, not when they are merely flawed in adjacent ways; (3) that institutional roles (judge, parent, official) confer a baseline standing that personal hypocrisy can diminish but never fully extinguish, since part of the authority to blame is constituted by the role itself; (4) that the familiar "clean hands" intuition should be explained by a specific non-compromise condition rather than by any general moral-equality requirement—what matters is that the blamer not be specially entangled in the particular wrong being blamed, regardless of overall moral merit; and (5) that even when standing is severely diminished, the blamer's act can remain appropriate in a thinner, non-authoritative sense, so that loss of standing does not entail that blame is altogether out of order.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe is each? In particular, does the scalar view in (1) sit comfortably with the binary elimination condition in (2), and can (5) survive the cases where (2) seems to wipe standing out completely? How does (3) interact with (2) in institutional contexts? Rank the tensions by severity, specify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency for each, and then tell me which single change would clean up the most problems at once. I am not committed to saving any particular plank—just tell me what has to give.
