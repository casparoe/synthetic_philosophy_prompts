My philosophy tutor has delivered the following verdicts on moral-responsibility cases: in the classic Frankfurt scenario, where a counterfactual intervener stands ready but never activates and the agent acts for reasons they fully endorse, she holds the agent fully responsible; in a deterministic universe with no particular blocker but where every choice is physically inevitable, she holds the agent not responsible; in a case where an external device actually overrides the agent's final hesitation, producing an outcome that coincides with the agent's preference, she denies responsibility; in a situation where the agent freely entered a binding agreement that removes all subsequent options and then acts within those constraints, she affirms responsibility; and in a case where an unconscious neural reflex suppresses any contrary intention before it reaches awareness, leaving the agent with only one conscious course of action, she still affirms responsibility.

Next term she must judge a novel case: an agent deliberates freely and endorses performing an action, but an earlier unconscious reflex—unknown to the agent—has permanently disabled the only neural pathway capable of executing a contrary choice; had that reflex failed to fire, a separate dormant monitoring system would have activated to block any deviation, yet because the reflex did fire, the monitor remains inactive and the agent is never aware of either mechanism. Predict her verdict, identify at least two distinct principles that could consistently account for all her earlier rulings, state which principle you think she is implicitly employing and why, name the single further case that would best distinguish the competing principles, and present the entire response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with neither an introduction nor a conclusion.
