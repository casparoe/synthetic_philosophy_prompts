I am writing a paper for my philosophy of economics course on intergenerational justice in development policy. I noticed that several multilateral funding bodies apply a strict safe Pareto improvement test to infrastructure projects: no currently living resident in the target region may be made worse off, even temporarily, without compensation. At the same time, these bodies routinely approve projects that shift significant long-term ecological risks onto future, not-yet-existing populations, provided those risks do not violate the rights of anyone alive now. What philosophical commitments about value, moral standing, and the scope of distributive justice does this pairing presuppose? Must it assume a person-affecting restriction that excludes merely possible people from moral consideration, or could a weighted consequentialist framework reproduce the same practice? And if the implicit theory is a present-biased safe Pareto criterion, what concrete changes would project evaluation require if it were replaced by an intergenerational framework that treats identity-affecting risks symmetrically?
