I'm enrolled in an upper-division historiography seminar this term, and our recent discussions have circled around the familiar opposition between Leopold von Ranke's aspiration to present the past as it actually occurred and the sceptical arguments advanced by E.H. Carr and Hayden White regarding narrative construction, present-minded selection, and the absence of value-neutral representation. The course framing tends to present this as an either-or: either historical accounts correspond to a unique, unmediated past, or they are irreducibly shaped by the historian's conceptual framework and rhetorical choices. For my final essay, I want to defend a position that cuts through this binary.

Please argue that historical objectivity ought to be reconceived not as correspondence to a single correct narrative, but as modal invariance across all historiographically permissible reconstructions of a specified event-space. Specifically, defend the claim that a statement about the past is objective when it holds true in every narrative framing that remains consistent with the available archival evidence and with the causal mechanisms accepted within the relevant historiographical practice—even though different permissible frames may vary in emphasis, periodization, and explanatory priority. This allows one to accept Carr and White's insight that narrative form is constructed and that the historian's present conditions what gets included, without abandoning Ranke's core commitment to a mind-independent past that constrains representation. The argument should explain how this modal criterion distinguishes legitimate interpretive disagreement from mere relativism, and it should show that the constraint comes from the structure of the event-space itself rather than from an implausible demand for narrative transparency.

In doing so, address two specific objections: first, that modal invariance reduces to inter-subjective consensus among practicing historians; second, that the criterion is either too permissive, licensing contradictory narratives, or too restrictive, disallowing genuinely contested but well-supported interpretations. You should also clarify why this position is not merely a variant of standard critical realism or of inference-to-the-best-explanation in historical reasoning, but relies specifically on treating permissible narratives as elements in a modal space governed by consistency conditions rather than by predictive power or overall coherence alone. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a survey of the literature; I want a direct philosophical argument for this reconception, assuming familiarity with the Ranke-Carr-White debate but without requiring a rehearsal of its standard moves.
