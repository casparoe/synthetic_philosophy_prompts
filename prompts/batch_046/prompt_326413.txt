In a recent seminar on penal philosophy, an interlocutor invoked the familiar maxim that it is preferable for many guilty persons to escape punishment than for one innocent person to suffer it, using it to dismiss both retributive and deterrent justifications for the death penalty in a single sweeping move. I am convinced the slogan is carrying far more argumentative weight than its surface rhetoric allows, and that its apparent conclusiveness dissolves once we separate the distinct theses it might be expressing. Could you disambiguate the maxim into its precise possible readings? For each, specify whether it articulates an epistemic principle about the burden of proof in criminal adjudication, a deontic asymmetry that weights wrongful conviction more heavily than wrongful acquittal, a consequentialist calculus comparing aggregate social harms, or a specifically anti-retributive claim about the moral limits of state-inflicted suffering. Then assess, for each reading, whether it amounts to a near-platitude, a defensible substantive principle, or an overreach—particularly when we restrict attention to irreversible punishments and allow that error rates in capital cases may be empirically tractable rather than purely speculative. I am especially interested in whether any interpretation can genuinely single out execution as uniquely impermissible, as opposed to merely demanding a higher evidentiary bar for all severe sanctions, and whether the conclusion my interlocutor drew actually survives once we distinguish the risk of executing an innocent person from the broader problem of false positives in non-capital sentencing.
