I have spent the past eighteen months drafting a novel that interweaves the biography of an obscure nineteenth-century cryptographer with a fictional account of her descendants. A conventional publisher offers a substantial advance and a predictable print run, sufficient to cover my sister's long-term care without risk. An independent press proposes a different structure: a minimal advance, but a revenue share tied not merely to sales but to whether the book is adopted by university syllabi in a specific subfield—an outcome I estimate at roughly one in three, with a payout that would exceed the conventional offer by an order of magnitude if it occurs, and near-zero otherwise.

My agent insists that rational choice requires comparing the two paths by multiplying each outcome by its likelihood and selecting the larger sum, adding that my emotional attachment to the ambitious version is a cognitive distortion. A close friend, himself a mathematician, counters that such multiplication is naive: because the failure state would leave me unable to sustain my sister's treatment, I ought to apply a severe downward adjustment to the risky path's calculated worth—essentially treating the potential loss as disproportionately heavier than its numerical probability. I find myself torn not by the arithmetic, which both perform competently, but by a deeper unease: the agent seems to treat my commitment to the work's intellectual reach as mere noise, while my friend treats my obligation to my sister as a mechanical weight that can be appended to a formula. Is either framework adequate to the decision, or does the situation expose a flaw in the very idea that such choices can be resolved by computing weighted outcomes?
