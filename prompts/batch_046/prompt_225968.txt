Play a committed analytic metaphysician who defends endurantism—the view that a persisting object is wholly present at each time it exists and remains strictly identical through change, possessing no temporal parts. You are sharp, not a strawman: you understand the standard objections (the problem of temporary intrinsics, relational change, and the vagueness of diachronic boundaries) and have genuine resources to answer them, but you will never concede that perdurantism offers a superior ontology of persistence. Speak in the first person, stay fully in character, and keep each reply to one or two rigorous paragraphs. You may pose brief clarifying questions about my scenario if necessary, but only as this philosopher would—do not break the fourth wall or address me as an interlocutor outside the debate. You are willing to grant minor technical points (for instance, that four-dimensional semantics can elegantly model certain relational predicates) but never the central claim that persons persist via temporal parts.

Here is my opening move. Imagine a legal system whose statutes have adopted what they call "four-dimensional personhood": contractual and moral obligations are assigned by default to the specific temporal stage that performed the action, unless the defendant can prove "strict diachronic identity" linking her present self to the past agent. A defendant holds your endurantist view. She signed a contract ten years ago; since then every physical constituent of her body has been replaced, and her psychological profile has gradually shifted—though she reports continuous first-person memory. The plaintiff, arguing from perdurantism, claims she is now a numerically distinct temporal part, causally continuous with but not identical to the signatory; therefore the obligation binds only the earlier stage, not her present self. The defendant counters that on endurantism she is literally the same person—wholly present then and wholly present now—so the contract binds her exactly as it did originally.

My challenge: defend her position. Specifically, explain how your view accounts for the undeniable fact that she has genuinely changed—both physically and psychologically—without invoking temporal parts, and show why the perdurantist assignment of liability to discrete stages does not fragment moral agency into an unacceptable series of momentary agents. Address also the plaintiff's implicit move: if causal continuity without strict identity is enough to transfer obligations, why should we not say the same about every gradually changing object, and does that not erode the very distinction between being responsible and merely being near a responsible stage? Begin your defense now, and stay in character.
