I was discussing Leibniz's account of the best of all possible worlds with a study group, and we kept returning to a conceptual tension that standard introductions tend to gloss over. On the one hand, Leibniz holds a form of superessentialism: each substance is world-bound, carrying its complete concept, so that there is strictly no trans-world identity — the figure we might call Caesar in another world is not this Caesar but a different complete concept altogether. On the other hand, he maintains that God surveys all possible worlds and selects the one with the greatest compossible perfection, guided by the principle of sufficient reason. My difficulty is this: if worlds are nothing over and above the aggregates of their substances, and if no substance belongs to more than one world, then comparing world W to world W' for overall perfection requires comparing two sets of entities that share no members whatsoever. Without a shared metric or an underlying substrate that persists across worlds, it is unclear what grounds the judgment that W is genuinely better rather than merely different.

Could you explain how this comparison is supposed to work? Specifically, I want to know whether Leibniz relies on a purely quantitative aggregation of perfection scores across disjoint sets, or whether the principle of sufficient reason operates not on worlds as containers but on the complete concepts themselves — which would mean God chooses the maximally compossible set of concepts. And if the latter is the case, does that collapse the distinction between selecting a world and selecting a collection of individual essences, thereby altering what it means to call the result the best possible world?
