ok so I'm prepping for this design-philosophy meetup and I need to actually defend a view instead of just rambling. I want to argue that what makes every 'a' an 'a' isn't a geometric checklist (round bowl, vertical stem, etc.) or just family resemblance — both of those miss what I'm calling the "closure debt." The idea is that an 'a' makes a minimal topological commitment: it encloses negative space on the left with an open counter, then closes that space with vertical stress on the right. Style is just ornamentation layered on top of that debt; you can warp the bowl, tilt the stem, even go full geometric sans-serif, but if you flip the counter to the right or drop the closure you've made an 'o' or a 'q' or just garbage. I think this is why we tolerate wild stylistic variation but instantly reject forms that keep the curves yet violate the left-open/right-closed obligation.

Can you lay out the argument for this? Specifically, build the case that letterform identity is grounded in invariant topological obligations rather than feature clusters, and deal with the pushback that blackletter or certain handwritten scripts seem to violate the debt without breaking recognition.
