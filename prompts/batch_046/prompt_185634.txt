I'm a distributed-systems engineer who works on Byzantine-fault-tolerant consensus protocols and conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs), but I have no formal philosophy training. Restate the debate between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness—particularly the objection that global workspace theory accounts only for the former and leaves the latter unexplained—using only the vocabulary I actually use: local node state versus globally replicated state, consensus on message ordering, gossip protocols versus broadcast buses, read-only observers, the distinction between a value residing in a local register and being propagated to every replica, and whether "availability to all subsystems" is just describing a particular network topology. Cover three specific philosophical moves: (1) that access consciousness is essentially global availability of information, (2) that phenomenal consciousness involves qualitative character or first-person immediacy that outstrips such availability, and (3) that one could in principle have full global broadcasting throughout a system with no phenomenal consciousness at all. Be precise about which conceptual steps map cleanly onto distributed computing and which require you to distort either the engineering or the philosophy; do not smuggle philosophical assumptions in by giving them technical-sounding names.

Then deliver the residue report. For each of the three moves above, state whether it translates cleanly into network architecture, whether it requires a distortion (specify exactly what is being distorted and in which direction), or whether it has no counterpart in distributed systems at all. Pay particular attention to whether "first-person immediacy" can be reconstructed as "local execution state that never enters the replication log," and examine whether that reconstruction collapses because any local register is in principle readable by an observer with sufficient access rights—so that the distinction between "locally present" and "globally available" cannot do the work the philosopher wants it to do. Finally, conclude by judging whether the residue is a defect of the distributed-systems framework (it was never designed to model subjectivity) or whether it reveals that the original philosophical claim was less operationally specified than it appeared—specifically, whether "phenomenal consciousness over and above global availability" can be given content without circular reference to the very global-availability conditions it is meant to exceed.
