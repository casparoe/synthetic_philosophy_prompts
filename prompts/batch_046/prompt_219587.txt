Consider the long-running stall in debates over free will worth wanting within hierarchical, Frankfurt-inspired frameworks. Despite broad agreement that the Principle of Alternate Possibilities fails, that mere first-order desire satisfaction is insufficient, and that some form of second-order identification is necessary for moral responsibility, a sharp division persists. Mesh theorists hold that wholehearted endorsement—where an agent’s first-order desires align with her higher-order volitions without ambivalence—is fully sufficient for the freedom that grounds deep moral praise and blame. Sourcehood theorists maintain that without being the ultimate origin of the very psychological architecture that produces this endorsement—without what might be called authorial control rather than mere managerial control—the agent lacks the kind of self-authorship required for freedom that is genuinely worth wanting. The arguments have cycled for years: mesh theorists point to standard counterexamples to show that sourcehood demands are either impossible for finite agents or irrelevant to reactive attitudes; sourcehood theorists reply that mesh alone reduces freedom to internal governance without genuine ownership, collapsing the distinction between an agent who identifies with her motives and one who merely manages an inherited psychological structure. Meanwhile, both sides claim to be describing the same phenomenon—free will adequate to moral desert—yet neither concedes ground.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement about human psychology, nor a purely verbal dispute about the word freedom, but a case of cross-purposed inquiry masked by shared vocabulary: one camp asks what conditions make an agent a legitimate target of reactive attitudes and moral accountability, while the other asks what conditions make an agent the author of her life-narrative. I further suspect that a hidden normative premise is driving the stall—specifically, an undefended commitment to originary authority (the idea that moral desert requires being an uncaused cause of one’s will), which, if taken literally, is self-undermining for any finite being, and that neither side has articulated a clear distinction between adopted values (endorsed but not self-constructed) and constructed values (shaped through a process over which the agent exercises diachronic authority).
