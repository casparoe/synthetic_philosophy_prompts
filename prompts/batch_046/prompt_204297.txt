I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I recently witnessed an extended debate between two colleagues—I'll call them Sam and Taylor—that left me genuinely unable to referee them, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

The subject was whether consequentialist ethics is undermined by what philosophers apparently call cluelessness: the problem that, for any significant choice, we cannot predict the branching long-run effects well enough to identify which option maximizes welfare. Sam opened by arguing that this makes consequentialism not merely difficult but practically useless as a decision procedure. Because the causal tree from any act is too large and opaque, computing the best outcome is intractable, so we should fall back on deontological rules—such as "do not break promises"—that do not require forecasting. He introduced an analogy from software engineering: consequentialism is like running an unoptimized simulation of every future state, whereas rule-based ethics is like an amortized algorithm. You pay a high upfront design cost to establish the rule, but each subsequent decision is constant-time and avoids full computation. He named this strategy moral amortization.

Taylor replied that cluelessness is symmetric across frameworks. If we are ignorant about the distant effects of breaking a promise, we are equally ignorant about the distant systemic effects of rigidly keeping one in every context. She also argued that Sam's amortization analogy actually smuggles consequentialism back in at the meta-level: selecting which rules to amortize requires comparing the long-run outcomes of different rule-sets, which is precisely the intractable computation Sam claims to avoid. If we truly lack the knowledge to perform that comparison, we have no principled basis for preferring Sam's rules over any alternative.

Sam countered that Taylor conflates object-level and meta-level reasoning. At the object level, consequentialism demands a fresh welfare simulation for every individual act. At the meta-level, societies can select rules through evolutionary or cultural filtering—observing which norms correlate with stable, flourishing communities—without any individual agent computing the full outcome space. Thus the intractable work is outsourced to historical processes, not performed by the moral agent.

Taylor responded that outsourcing does not eliminate the need for justification. To defend a rule-set by appealing to survival or cultural success is implicitly to claim that survival correlates with welfare, which is an empirical thesis itself vulnerable to cluelessness. She also noted that Sam's "constant-time decision" framing collapses in precisely the edge cases that motivate moral philosophy—situations where rules conflict and there is no pre-computed answer. In those conflicts, one still must compute, so the problem has only been relocated, not solved.

They asked me to adjudicate, and I honestly could not. Please go through their exchange move by move: for each of Sam's and Taylor's arguments, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both sides are missing. In particular, is there a coherent way to combine amortized rules with occasional consequentialist override for hard edge cases, without collapsing back into the full cluelessness problem? I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—only for the reasoning itself, laid out clearly.
