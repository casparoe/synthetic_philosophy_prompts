I've been arguing with friends about whether you morally have to follow local conventions like tipping, queueing, and jaywalking—specifcally whether a visitor has an obligation to do it even when breaking the rule causes basicly zero harm. The stall is weird: we all agree on the empiracal facts (no one gets hurt if I skip tipping in Tokyo, queue norms in London are arbitary, etc.) and we all agree suffering is bad, but the 'yes you must' and 'no it's just etiquette' camps haven't moved in months. I think part of the issue is that one side is smuggling in a kind of practice-internal deontic authority—the idea that being inside a convention generates obligations that don't reduce to consent or harm—while the other side treats all convention-violation as strategic or aesthetic. Is the disagreement really about whether conventions can generate non-instrumental reasons? Or is there a hidden disagreement about what counts as participation in a practice? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, not just a survey of positions. And please say what argument or counterexample would actually change your mind on this, and where your reasoning is most likely to fall apart.
