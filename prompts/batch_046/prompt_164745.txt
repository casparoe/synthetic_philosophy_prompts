I am working through an argument about recursive legal self-reference for a seminar on normative dynamics, and I fear it now proves far too much. Here is the chain, step by step.

(1) Every legal system contains a meta-rule M that fixes the conditions under which any rule — including M itself — may be amended or repealed. (2) If M permits its own amendment, then there exists a possible successor rule M' that alters M's conditions of revision. (3) The transition from M to M' is legally valid only if M authorises it; yet once M' is in force, M no longer governs, so the authorisation that justified the transition is retrospectively withdrawn. (4) This produces a diachronic branching: either the transition was never valid (making M' a legal impostor) or M retains a kind of normative inertia that outlasts its official repeal (so M and M' co-exist in contradiction). (5) To avoid contradiction, one must deny that M can ever be amended — but then legal change is impossible. (6) Yet constitutional democracies routinely amend their foundational rules, so legal change is plainly possible. (7) Therefore, either all constitutional amendments are legally void, or every self-amending system harbours a hidden contradiction that undermines its normative authority. (8) The latter is intolerable; hence self-amendment is impossible, and all observed constitutional change is either illusion or lawless revolution.

I would prefer not to surrender (1) or (6), since I take legal meta-rules and actual constitutional change to be real. My suspicion is that the slip occurs either in (3), with its claim about retrospective withdrawal of authorisation, or in (4), where I invoke a metaphor of diachronic branching and normative inertia drawn from recent work on legal identity over time that I may be misapplying. Which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what does each option cost? If the argument rests on a confused premise — for example, if I am smuggling in an assumption about the temporal persistence of legal authority that the philosophy of law rejects — please say so plainly rather than forcing a diagnosis.
