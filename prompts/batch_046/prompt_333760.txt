Over the past year I have repeatedly entered collaborative projects with genuine enthusiasm, only to find my preferences reversing months later—yet defending those reversed preferences with equal conviction—so the problem of diachronic consistency has become personally urgent. Next Thursday I am facilitating a session for an interdisciplinary group of colleagues (behavioral scientists and applied mathematicians, with little formal exposure to decision theory) on money pumps and diachronic rationality. I need not the theory itself but a pedagogical architecture: in what order must the conceptual pieces be introduced so that the structural vulnerability to exploitation becomes visible before any notation appears? Which single concrete scenario—explicitly avoiding the familiar subscription-service or coffee-machine tropes—should carry the entire session, and what properties must it possess (intertemporal linkage, reversibility, observable stakes) to make the pump mechanically transparent rather than psychologically plausible? What misconceptions should I anticipate—likely that preference reversal is merely weakness of will, that rational agents never update, or that the argument illegitimately confuses consistency with rigidity—and precisely where in the sequence should each be neutralized? What diagnostic question, answerable without technical vocabulary, can I pose at the close to distinguish someone who grasps the structural constraint from someone who has merely acquired the vocabulary? Finally, which formal result—perhaps the connection between dynamic consistency and expected-utility representation—should I explicitly bracket as beyond the session’s scope, and what rationale should I offer the group for leaving it untouched?
