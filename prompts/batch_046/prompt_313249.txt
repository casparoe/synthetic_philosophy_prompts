I'm prepping a sylabus unit on church-state relations and I've been arguing with a collegue about whether Germany's church-tax system is actually more respectful of religious freedom than France's hard laïcité or England's lingering establishment. I want my students to write an essay weighing the three models—England's established Church with its symbolic crown appointments, Germany's Kirchensteuer collected by the state but only for registered members, and France's strict seperation with its recent tensions over religious dress in public schools. The controversy I want them to tackle: does administrative cooperation between state and church (like tax collection) inevitably compromise netural secularism, or can it be justified as a kind of fiscal pluralism that treats religious membership analogously to linguistic minority rights?

They should reach a conclusion—defend one of the three regimes, or propose a hybrid, but don't just say it depends on context. I'm especially interested in whether the German model avoids the establishment problem by making membership opt-in via tax registration, or whether that very mechanism makes the state an arbiter of religious belonging in a way France's stricter seperation avoids. Don't just summarize legal histories; engage with the normative question of whether neutrality requires distance or can accommodate structured cooperation. And please, no bibliographies—this is a conceptual exercise, not a research paper. I was thinking about this after reading some comparative constitutional stuff, but I don't want them citing specific court cases unless they happen to know them—just argue from the concepts.
