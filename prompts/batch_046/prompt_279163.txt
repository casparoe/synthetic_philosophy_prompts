I'm a high school student working through informal game-theory puzzles with a study partner, and I have noticed a recurring pattern for which I do not have an established name. In the standard analysis of a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defection is presented as the uniquely rational choice, because without future interaction there is no mechanism to reward cooperation. Yet I keep encountering situations in which two agents appear to cooperate anyway—not out of altruism, miscalculation, or expectation of reciprocity, but because each recognizes that the other is running a decision procedure that is structurally identical to its own. For example, in a classroom exercise my partner and I designed, pairs of students were given different representations of the same payoff structure—one expressed in grade points, the other in abstract tokens—and both still selected cooperation. Each student seemed to apply an explicit meta-rule: if I can verify that my counterpart's reasoning architecture is isomorphic to mine, then I should choose the action I want both of us to take, regardless of the representational format in which the payoffs are framed.

A second instance comes from a logic puzzle I constructed involving two artificial agents that share an identical update rule but carry different utility weights. Faced with a one-time choice between a dominant-strategy defection and a jointly suboptimal outcome, both agents select the cooperative option. When their reasoning is inspected, each explains that it derived the other's choice by substituting its own decision architecture into the counterpart's position, despite the divergence in underlying weights.

This phenomenon seems different from ordinary common-knowledge cooperation, since the agents neither share preferences nor anticipate future interaction; it also seems different from simple bounded rationality or error, because the reasoning is fully explicit and reflective. Is there an established concept—within decision theory or the philosophy of strategic reasoning—that captures coordination mediated specifically by transparent structural identity of reasoning processes rather than by aligned interests or repeated play? If such a concept exists, how precisely does it relate to the standard treatments of rational choice in one-shot interactions? And if my description fits nothing exactly, could you propose a term together with a precise definition and clear boundary conditions—for instance, specifying what should happen when the reasoning isomorphism is only partial, or when one agent suspects that the other is merely simulating rather than genuinely executing the same procedure?
