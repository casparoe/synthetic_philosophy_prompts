I am a journalist preparing a feature interview for a science-and-ideas magazine with a philosopher—let us call her Dr. Elena Voss—who has recently advanced a rehabilitated version of the Cartesian theater. Rather than treating consciousness as a passive screen, she proposes a "localized convergence" model in which predictive-coding circuits in prefrontal and parietal regions generate a single, revisable narrative line that serves as the functional equivalent of a central auditorium, but without a homuncular spectator. She contends that Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model correctly dissolves the naive theater but fails to account for temporal binding anomalies—specifically the color-phi phenomenon and postdictive motion perception—which, she argues, require one canonical sequence rather than parallel, competing drafts. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. Include at least one question addressing the homunculus objection and exactly how her convergence point avoids reintroducing an inner observer, together with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—namely, that the convergence point is a functional attractor state rather than a spatial location. Include at least one question on whether her model can accommodate Dennett's distinction between Stalinesque and Orwellian revisions of mental content, with a follow-up anticipating her likely claim that predictive coding naturally explains both as different temporal scales of error correction. Include at least one question pressing her on the subjective "now"—the felt present—given that her convergence is always predictive and therefore slightly delayed, and provide a follow-up suited to her probable answer that the "now" is a constructed boundary condition rather than an instantaneous snapshot. Beyond these three, include questions that press whether her account collapses into Bernard Baars's global workspace theory, how she handles split-brain cases without multiplying theaters, and whether accepting any convergence point forces her to reject distributed representation altogether in cognition. Write all questions in a polished, journalistic register appropriate for an intellectually serious audience, avoiding generic biographical or definitional prompts.
