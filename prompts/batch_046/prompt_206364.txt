I'm preparing the opening for a graduate seminar on causation in historical explanation, and I want a poem that stages a conceptual knot we've been circling rather than resolving it. The knot concerns overdetermination: in metaphysics, an event is causally overdetermined when two or more conditions are each independently sufficient—think of two assassins firing at once. In history, however, explanations of large-scale collapses—say, the disintegration of the Late Bronze Age palace economies around 1177 BCE—routinely invoke drought, seismic disruption, migratory pressure, and internal systems failure together. Yet historians rarely treat these as independent, metaphysically overdetermining causes. Instead they appeal to "systems collapse" or conjunctural fragility, where the causes form an interdependent network: removing one node may not prevent collapse because the system is already brittle, but removing all but one might also fail because no single factor was sufficient alone. So historical multi-causality sits in a strange middle space—more than a cluster of necessary conditions, less than true simultaneous overdetermination—and the explanatory narrative keeps circling back without isolating the decisive factor.

Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains that should shift subtly in meaning across their repetitions—spoken in the voice of a historian trying to account for that Bronze Age collapse. The form should do structural work: the returning refrains ought to enact the historian's recurring inability to assign unique explanatory weight to any single cause, while the rhyme scheme (A1bA2, abA1, abA2, abA1, abA2, abA1A2) should mirror the way explanations keep folding back into one another. Use iambic pentameter or a close approximation; do not resolve the tension into either fatalism ("it was inevitable") or epistemic surrender ("we can never know"). Keep the philosophical distinction accurate enough that someone familiar with debates between invasionist and systems-collapse historiography recognizes the problem, but let the poem stand on its own for a reader who knows nothing of the period.

The poem should be followed by a single section titled "Commentary on formal choices," roughly two paragraphs long, in which you explain how the villanelle's repetition and rhyme scheme track the logic of explanatory recurrence—why the historian keeps returning to drought, invasion, and institutional fragility without being able to rank them—and whether the final quatrain's coupling of the two refrains holds the tension open or lets it collapse into a single verdict. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
