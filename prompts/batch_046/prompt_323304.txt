I was arguing with some mates in the pub last week about Austin and Searle — well, more like I was losing the argument — and I realised I don't actually have a stable view on when saying something really does something. I'm especially stuck on what I'd call hollow performatives: utterances that have the right form and even a bit of social weight, but lack the usual institutinal backing, yet sometimes they still seem to land. Here is my gut on a bunch of cases, and I'd like a principle, or maybe a small set of competing ones, that captures the pattern.

Saying "sorry, I ate your yoghurt" right after I did it — that's a proper apology, works fine. A team lead telling an intern "you're fired" when HR hasn't touched it — feels like bullying, not a real dismissal, doesn't work. Two friends in their kitchen saying "we're breaking up" — feels like the relationship actually ends right then. But those excat same two people saying "we're married now" in the same kitchen with no registrar or witnesses — feels like playing house, not a real marriage. Writing "this is my final will" in a private notebook with no one around — just words on paper, no legal effect. Promising a friend "I'll definately help you move" when I already know I'm too busy — still a real promise, just one I'll probably break. A random bloke in the town square shouting "I hereby declare this a tax-free zone" — obviously nothing, doesn't work. But then there's the one I keep going back and forth on: a nurse (not a doctor) reading a chart and telling a patient "you're in remission." There's some authority there, but not the full kind, and I can't decide if the utterance actually changes the patient's medical status or just reports it.

So is there a principle — maybe about authority, uptake, or social recognition — that gets all of these? Or do I have to drop one of my verdicts, probably the marriage one or the nurse one? Please state each candidate precisely, say which case motivates each clause, test them on cases I haven't mentioned — like saying "I bet you a tenner" where gambling contracts aren't enforceable, or deleting a sent message as a kind of negative performative — and be honest about which of my gut feelings they can't save. Write the answer in British English, please, and don't just name-drop philosophers; actually build the principle out.
