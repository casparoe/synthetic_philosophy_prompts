Consider a textbook accreditation practice in which reviewers apply a "descriptive purity" filter to science texts—flagging normative language that appears in explanatory prose rather than in isolated "ethics boxes"—while applying no comparable filter to history texts, where normative framing is permitted throughout so long as it remains within an approved "civic values" rubric. At the same time, the board treats moral disagreements among reviewers as grounds for revising the rubric, but treats factual disagreements as grounds for consulting primary sources without altering the evaluation criteria. What conception of moral values does this dual treatment presuppose? Can it be reconciled with a Quinean naturalism that treats moral beliefs as part of a unified web of belief—continuous with empirical commitments yet lacking direct observational anchors—or does it depend on a sharp descriptive-normative boundary that such naturalism undermines? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and specify what would have to change in the accreditation process if moral values were treated, in Quine's fashion, as revisable through broad pragmatic considerations rather than cordoned off in domain-specific boxes.
