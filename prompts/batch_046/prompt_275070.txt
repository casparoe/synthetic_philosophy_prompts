I have recently found myself in the uncomfortable position of correcting a conceptual mistake I made in my own reasoning, and I want to do it rigorously by teaching the idea properly to others. Last month, during a discussion with colleagues in an interdisciplinary seminar I help coordinate—mostly architects, cultural geographers, and a few economists with no formal training in evolutionary biology—I noticed that several participants were using “costly signaling” and “the handicap principle” interchangeably with Veblen goods and conspicuous consumption, treating any expensive display as an evolutionary handicap. I realized I had made a parallel error in a personal conversation about charitable giving, assuming that high visibility and financial sacrifice automatically constituted a Zahavian handicap without asking whether the cost was genuinely correlated with an underlying quality that deception could not fake. Rather than merely correct them, I have volunteered to lead a single ninety-minute session for this group, using only a whiteboard and no slides, with the explicit goal of making the logic of the handicap principle feel inevitable rather than metaphorical.

Please plan the explanatory arc for this session, treating the biological concept—not its applications in design or philanthropy—as the object. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual piece must be in place before the next can land, particularly why the distinction between an index signal and a handicap signal needs to be established before any examples are introduced, and why fitness-cost correlation must precede discussion of human analogues. Second, identify the one carrying example that should bear the weight of the explanation—explicitly not the peacock’s tail alone, but a paired biological case that makes the deception-proof logic visible—and specify what that example must possess and what it must lack to serve this purpose. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form—I'm anticipating conflation with financial cost, intentional strategic design, and mere conspicuous consumption—and state exactly at which point in the sequence each should be headed off, not after the fact. Fourth, propose one concrete check at the end that would show whether they have grasped the mechanism rather than just memorized the vocabulary. Fifth, tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, whether formal game-theoretic derivations or specific debates about human evolutionary psychology, and say so explicitly.

Throughout, separate what is settled in evolutionary biology from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as the scope of handicap signaling in human cultural practices or the precise conditions under which index signals become handicaps—as the consensus view. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I need a pedagogical plan I can execute with the knowledge I have now.
