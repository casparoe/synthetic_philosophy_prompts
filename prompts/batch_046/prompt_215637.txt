I am preparing for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of life, and my study partner and I have reached an impasse that I would like to see worked through in dialogue form. She holds that contemporary objectivist accounts—those that tie meaning to objectively valuable activities combined with subjective engagement, sometimes discussed under the label of fitting-fulfillment—have quietly resolved the classical absurdist challenge by removing the demand for cosmic or transcendent justification. I counter that they have only relocated the problem: if the values that ground meaning are not themselves ultimately justified, then fitting one's life to them is arbitrary at a deeper level, and subjective fulfillment risks collapsing into mere preference-satisfaction. I want you to write a dialogue between two interlocutors—Mara, who defends a refined version of this objectivist position augmented by what she calls recursive value anchoring, and Jorin, who argues from a post-institutional absurdist stance that once traditional sources of narrative coherence have dissolved, any attempt to stabilize meaning through anchoring collapses into semantic saturation, the condition in which all available life-narratives have already been culturally instantiated, leaving only recombination without genuine novelty.

The exchange should be genuinely dialectical: neither speaker should be a strawman. Mara needs to explain how recursive anchoring stops the justificatory regress at the level of practical identity without smuggling in transcendence, and she must directly address Jorin's charge that this is merely bad faith disguised as coherence. Jorin should press the saturation claim—arguing that in a culture where every form of valuable engagement has been mapped and commodified, the objectivity Mara requires is undermined—and he must respond when Mara objects that saturation falsely assumes a completeness of cultural imagination. They should actually listen: when one introduces a distinction, the other should engage with it rather than shifting ground. The dialogue should treat both positions as serious attempts to answer whether human life can remain meaningful now that we no longer share a collective metaphysical framework, and it should avoid bibliographies, citations, or appeals to specific texts that would require verification.
