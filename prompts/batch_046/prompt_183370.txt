I'm trying to name a failure mode I keep encountering near discussions of rational agreement. Consider a deliberative setting — a scientific review board or an interdisciplinary policy panel — where all participants explicitly share the same prior framework, endorse Bayesian updating, and treat one another as fully rational. They disclose every piece of private evidence they possess. By the standard agreement result, convergence ought to follow; yet they remain divided. The division is not caused by hidden data or by anyone rejecting the shared rules outright. Instead, each faction interprets the other faction's failure to update as evidence that the other is misapplying the very framework everyone accepts — attributing the divergence to hidden bias, scope error, or faulty execution — and then discounts the opposing posterior on those grounds. Crucially, each faction applies this diagnostic only outward: no one considers that the persistent disagreement might indicate a flaw in the shared framework itself. The result is a self-sealing loop in which common knowledge of rationality is intact, yet convergence is blocked by reciprocal accusations of misapplication. I have observed analogues in debates over climate-model parameter choices and in clinical-guideline disagreements about risk thresholds. Is there an established concept — distinct from ordinary stubbornness, from hidden-information disagreement, and from the standard conditions that defeat Aumann-style convergence — that captures this specific dynamic? If not, what would be the precise boundaries of such a concept: in particular, does it require that both sides genuinely share the prior, or only that they profess to, and how does it differ from a simple disagreement about auxiliary hypotheses?
