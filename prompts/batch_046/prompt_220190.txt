Consider the distinction Douglas Hofstadter draws between strange loops—tangled hierarchies in which levels of organization feed back into one another, as illustrated by Escher's Drawing Hands—and the inviolate level, a base stratum that a system can observe but never alter or enter, analogous to the fixed rules of a formal system. Now apply this distinction to a specific conceptual puzzle in normative philosophy: a principle of distributive justice that asserts its own authority is itself subject to the very distributive standard it imposes, so that any attempt to justify the principle must invoke the principle. Explain why this creates a tangled hierarchy rather than a stable inviolate foundation, and clarify exactly where the analogy to Escher's drawing breaks down: in the artwork, the hands are simultaneously creator and created within a single representational plane, whereas in the normative case, the levels are temporal and institutional, separating rule-givers from rules. In your explanation, avoid treating the loop as merely vicious circularity; instead, articulate what structural feature—if any—might serve as a functional equivalent to an inviolate level within such a self-authorizing framework, and whether that feature necessarily collapses back into the loop.
