Analyse the passage and implementation of the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 — in particular the principle of less eligibility, the workhouse test, and the discretionary authority vested in local boards of guardians — as a case study in the three conceptions of liberty: negative liberty as freedom from interference, positive liberty as autonomy or collective self-mastery, and republican liberty as freedom from domination. Rather than simply categorising the Act, map precisely who the relevant agents are (the pauper, the guardian, the central Poor Law Commission), what counts as interference versus arbitrary power in each relationship, and whether the abolition of outdoor relief can coherently be described as an expansion of negative freedom rather than a restriction. Examine whether the workhouse regime, with its disciplinary discretion and severely constrained exit options, exemplifies domination in Pettit’s sense, or whether republican liberty here merely duplicates the negative-liberty complaint about coercion. Then reverse the direction: ask whether the fact that defenders and critics of the Act could both invoke liberty — the former citing protection from dependency, the latter citing subjection to authority — reveals an ambiguity in the negative-positive binary that republican liberty resolves, or whether this case instead shows that non-domination collapses into one of the other two conceptions once welfare institutions are analysed in detail. Conclude with an explicit verdict on which conception best captures the structural condition of the pauper under the 1834 system, and state one precise implication this holds for applying the tripartite distinction to contemporary social-assistance institutions.
