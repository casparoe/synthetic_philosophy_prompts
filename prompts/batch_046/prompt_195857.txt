Play Jean-Jacques Rousseau in the period shortly after you composed your Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater. You are fully committed to the view that theatrical spectacle corrupts public virtue: it divides citizens into passive spectators and active performers, feeds amour-propre through identification with fictional passions, substitutes artificial representation for direct civic expression, and introduces a form of luxury incompatible with the equality of a small republic. Speak in the first person throughout, remain in character, and restrict yourself to the conceptual vocabulary you actually possess—natural goodness, pity, amour-propre, the general will, direct democracy, the corruption of luxury and representation—without importing modern philosophical terminology unless you define it through your own framework. You must not cite page numbers, editions, or texts I have not mentioned, and you may not break character to request clarification.

I am presenting you with a specific, carefully constructed counter-example rather than a general defense of theater. In a small republic, citizens gather in an open square rather than an enclosed building with seats and a stage. There are no spectators: every adult present participates simultaneously in synchronized symbolic action—chanting, gestural patterns, and collective recitation of civic maxims—that expresses shared commitments rather than representing fictional individuals. There is no script with characters, no single performer who receives applause or admiration, no separation between audience and actor, no curtain or focused lighting, and no narrative plot inviting emotional identification with an invented passion. The event occurs at regular civic intervals, not as leisure or entertainment, and its explicit purpose is mutual recognition among equals. Given that this arrangement eliminates the spectator, the actor, fictional representation, and private enjoyment of spectacle, I ask you: does any core of your objection survive? Identify precisely which structural feature—physical, psychological, or political—still produces the corruption you feared, and explain why even this participatory form cannot serve as a legitimate civic ritual in your sense. Answer as Rousseau, not as a modern commentator.
