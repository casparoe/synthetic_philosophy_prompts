Write a deadpan consumer-review article of approximately 700 words, in the formal, exasperated register of a Wirecutter or Lonely Planet evaluation, assessing the "Grand Transfinite Hotel"—a hospitality chain that has implemented Hilbert's hotel literally. The property must feature a countably infinite ground-floor corridor of rooms (numbered 1, 2, 3, ...), a nightly "full-occupancy shift protocol" in which every current guest is moved to room n+1 to make space for new arrivals, and a "Real-Number Wing" that uses a Cantor-style diagonal assignment algorithm to ensure no two guests ever share an identical binary-sequence suite key, despite management's claim that the wing is already fully occupied. Include an ordinal-based loyalty program in which guests accumulate "omega points" redeemable for access to the omega-plus-one floor, served by an elevator that makes countably infinite intermediate stops and therefore never returns to the lobby. Outside, a small but vocal group of finitist protestors should picket the entrance, distributing leaflets that object not to the service but to the ontology: they deny that the hotel's "no vacancy" sign describes a completed totality rather than an indefinitely extendable potentiality.

The reviewer should accept all of this mathematics as working operational reality—never breaking character to call it absurd—but should treat every transfinite consequence as a legitimate customer-service grievance. Complaints should map precisely onto real philosophical and mathematical tensions: the violation of the Euclidean principle that the whole is greater than the part; the distinction between cardinal and ordinal growth (the loyalty points accumulate without reaching a "largest" tier); the problem of whether shifting every guest is a well-defined procedure or an infinite task that is never finished; and the diagonal argument's implication that any purported complete list of room assignments can be escaped. The voice must remain deadpan and sustained: the reviewer should genuinely wonder whether his own room number is well-ordered, should be annoyed that the refund policy for "continuity disputes" is undefined past the first limit ordinal, and should note, in passing, that the hotel's fictional night manager—Mr. Cantor—insists the Real-Number Wing has strictly more rooms than the ground floor, yet every room is still taken.

The satire should exaggerate actual features of infinity reasoning rather than inventing false ones, so that a reader familiar with Hilbert's hotel and Cantor's argument recognizes the mapping immediately, while a general reader understands the hotel is broken in specifically mathematical ways. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to external philosophical texts. Do not quote any real passage; if you mention a specific principle, paraphrase it without quotation marks. Keep the target on the practice of treating actual infinities as unproblematic completed objects in formal and applied contexts, not on any named living person. Ensure internal consistency: the reviewer never slips into finitism as a philosophical position, but only as a consumer preference—he wants a room he can actually reach.
