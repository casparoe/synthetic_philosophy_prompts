Write a taxonomy, structured as a decision tree, for positions on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, designed as a handout for an advanced metaphysics seminar. The tree must begin with a concrete shared commitment: that in a true proposition—say, that Socrates is mortal—the subject and predicate are genuinely unified rather than merely co-listed. From there, build branch nodes out of three diagnostic commitments phrased as questions a student can answer without knowing theory names: (1) whether this unity requires an explicit relating relation, a relator, over and above the terms themselves; (2) if a relator is required, whether it in turn needs further relating, accepting or rejecting the vicious regress; and (3) if no relator is required, whether the connection is internal to the terms or is eliminated altogether in favor of a non-relational or deflationary account of truth. Each leaf should carry a standard position label where one exists—such as Bradleyan, Russellian, nominalist or deflationary, trope-theoretic, or Aristotelian—and should be marked unnamed if that combination of answers is unoccupied, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The tree must explicitly flag any pair of named leaves it cannot genuinely separate, stating which shared answers cause the overlap. To prevent the exercise from being solved by encyclopedia recall, frame the splits around two underused test cases: negative propositions (e.g., Socrates is not mortal), asking how each leaf accounts for the unity of absence; and three-place relational propositions (e.g., Socrates is taller than Theaetetus), asking how each leaf accounts for the unity of order among multiple terms. Fix the size at eight to ten leaves. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The goal is to sort students by what they actually commit to about unity, absence, and order, not by which philosopher they name-drop.
