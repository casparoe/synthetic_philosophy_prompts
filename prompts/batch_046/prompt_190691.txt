A recent survey article on Bayesian epistemology summarised Richard Jeffrey's rule of conditionalisation in the following way: 'Jeffrey introduced his rule in the 1960s as a direct mathematical derivation from Bayes' theorem, designed specifically for cases in which an agent receives perceptual evidence that she does not fully trust. Instead of conditioning on a definite observation, the agent assigns fresh subjective probabilities to a partition of hypotheses and then applies Bayes' formula with these values substituted as weights. The rule imposes a global rigidity condition: every prior conditional probability, across the entire belief system, must remain exactly as it was, so that updating affects only the marginal probabilities of the partition cells. Because the revised probabilities for those cells are fixed solely by the agent's internal judgment about her own sensory reliability, Jeffrey's framework eliminates any need for objective likelihoods and renders Bayesian updating entirely subjective. For this reason, Jeffrey conditionalisation has become the standard method in machine learning for fusing data from unreliable sensors, and it also solves the problem of old evidence by allowing hypotheses to be reweighted even when no new observational data are available.'

This account strikes me as mostly coherent, but I worry that it mischaracterises the relationship between Jeffrey's rule and Bayes' theorem, overstates the scope of the rigidity condition, and draws conclusions about machine-learning practice and old evidence that do not seem warranted. Is this a fair summary?
