In discussions with colleagues, we have struggled to articulate when altering or removing such an entry counts as a justified ethical correction versus an unacceptable erasure of shared memory, particularly because our architecture preserves a complete audit trail even when the public-facing symbol is changed. I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of philosophers; I want to build the evaluation criteria from first principles.

Please lay out the desiderata that any adequate normative theory of commemorative ethics—specifically for statues and named public spaces—must satisfy. Identify the distinctions the theory must not collapse—such as that between being worthy of historical remembrance and receiving a specific form of public honor—and flag any pairs of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, perhaps involving democratic transparency, minority protection, and long-term historical preservation. Indicate which criteria are genuinely non-negotiable for any plausible view and which reflect contingent preferences of particular camps, and discuss whether framing commemorative objects as database entries with rollback capabilities introduces unique ethical obligations—such as maintaining archival integrity, providing public explanations for version changes, or preventing silent revision—that static, physical-world theories of monument ethics fail to capture.
