I'm in an advanced high school philosophy seminar, and we've been studying performative utterances—the idea that saying something can also be doing something, like promising, sentencing, or naming. But I've become fixated on an institutional practice in large online multiplayer games: players can file "reports" against others for toxic behavior, and on several well-known platforms, reaching a specific numerical threshold of reports within a short time window automatically triggers a temporary chat ban or account restriction, with no human moderator reviewing the individual case. This seems radically different from criminal or administrative law, where a citizen's accusation by itself carries no coercive force and a magistrate or authorized official must issue a formal order before any restriction applies.

So what philosophical view of speech acts and institutional authority does that automated gaming-report system actually presuppose? Does it implicitly treat a dispersed crowd of users as collectively exercising an illocutionary power—something like a distributed sentencing—despite lacking any conventional procedure that recognizes them as authorized officials? Or does it avoid being a genuine performative altogether by framing each report as a mere data input that only causally triggers an algorithmic response, with no institutional status-changing force? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory: must the practice assume that authority can be quantified and delegated to statistical thresholds, or can it be explained without any commitment to collective illocutionary force? And if we imagine transplanting this same logic into legal institutions—say, allowing accumulated citizen reports to automatically trigger temporary detention—would that reveal that the gaming practice borrows the language of judgment without relying on a coherent account of performative authority? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires philosophically from what merely fits it well, and say whether it is consistent with standard conditions on conventional procedures or demands an entirely different framework for understanding institutional speech.
