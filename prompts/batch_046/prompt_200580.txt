I'm preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on clinical ontology, and I keep encountering the dismissal that "dead is dead"—invoked, for instance, when colleagues reject any philosophical distinction between brain death and circulatory death, or when they argue that jurisdictions recognizing brain-based criteria are tracking precisely the same phenomenon as those requiring irreversible cardiac arrest. Before I reply, I want the phrase precisely disambiguated.

Could you lay out, as an explicit menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that "dead is dead" might be advancing in this debate? I have in mind at least the following possibilities, but I want you to state each with enough precision that I can see whether it actually does the work: (i) an ontological thesis that death is a unitary biological event with exactly one correct criterion, such that brain-dead and heart-dead patients are in identically the same state; (ii) a normative or legal-coherence thesis that clinical and statutory definitions ought not diverge, regardless of underlying ontology; (iii) an epistemic-modesty thesis that disagreements about criteria reflect only our present ignorance of a single underlying fact, not a genuine multiplicity; (iv) an eliminativist thesis that "brain death" and "circulatory death" are different descriptions of the same outcome despite different mechanisms; (v) a pragmatic thesis that, for all practical purposes—organ procurement, burial, grief—any remaining differences are negligible; and (vi) any further reading you think belongs on the list.

For each thesis, please indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; specify which of them actually support dismissing the brain-death versus circulatory-death distinction as philosophically unimportant; and identify which ones fail to support that dismissal, so that I can determine whether my interlocutors are relying on a strong claim or merely on a platitude. Finally, apply your disambiguation to one case I have not listed: a patient with irreversible loss of all brainstem function but sustained cardiac output via mechanical support, in a jurisdiction that recognizes both brain-death and circulatory-death statutes—does "dead is dead" entail that this patient is dead, that the statute is redundant, or neither?
