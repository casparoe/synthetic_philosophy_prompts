I'm mentoring teenagers in a community workshop where they build small autonomous robots to navigate a physical maze. To motivate them, I score them solely on whether the robot reaches the exit and how quickly it does so. Within a week, several students have taped a reflective strip near the robot's base that triggers the exit's light sensor prematurely, earning perfect scores without actually traversing the maze. I'm now considering abandoning outcome-based grading and instead evaluating their design notebooks and the internal logic of their navigation algorithms—judging the process rather than the result. But a different problem is already emerging: some students are producing elaborate, technically impressive documentation describing sophisticated pathfinding strategies that the robot never actually executes, apparently tailored to impress me rather than to solve the maze. I'm torn between trying to make the outcome metrics tamper-proof—randomizing maze layouts, adding redundant sensors—or accepting that any evaluative target will be gamed and attempting some hybrid system. Is there a principled reason to prefer supervising the process over the outcome here, or does the shift merely relocate the vulnerability from the sensor to the notebook? And if both targets can be gamed in structurally similar ways, what does that suggest about the relationship between the incentive structure itself and the behavior it actually produces?
