I have been discussing with colleagues in our philosophy of science reading group the extent to which Tetlock’s distinction between the inside view and the outside view can be applied retrospectively to institutional failures of judgment, particularly where expert knowledge is present but structurally misallocated. I should like you to analyse the decision to launch the Space Shuttle Challenger on 28 January 1986 through precisely this lens, but with a specific conceptual refinement: rather than treating the inside view as a single undifferentiated position, distinguish carefully between (i) the individual cognitive inside view of the Thiokol engineers who possessed direct data on O-ring erosion, (ii) the organisational inside view of NASA management, which operated within institutional incentives and hierarchical protocols, and (iii) the reference-class outside view available to an observer familiar with the historical base rates of catastrophic failure in high-reliability bureaucracies under launch-pressure conditions. For each layer, articulate what epistemic modesty would have required, whether it was in principle available to the actors, and where the theory predicts a failure of deference that the historical record either confirms or complicates.

Then, running the analysis in the reverse direction, consider what the Challenger episode reveals about the limits of the inside/outside view framework itself. Does the case suggest that epistemic modesty is not merely a cognitive failure but an organisational impossibility under certain incentive structures? Does the presence of technically correct expert dissent—subsequently overridden—indicate that the framework needs a supplementary account of authority and voice, or that the outside view was in fact inaccessible because the reference class was too sparse to generate reliable predictions? I want an honest account of what the theory explains (the misallocation of confidence, the neglect of base-rate reasoning), what it misdescribes (the role of managerial rhetoric in suppressing dissent), and whether any modification—perhaps one drawing on recent work on structural ignorance in institutional epistemology—would do better without merely retrofitting the theory to the outcome. Please do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I am interested in the conceptual architecture of your argument rather than its scholarly apparatus.
