Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a Jamesian reconstructivist and a Cliffordian universalist on the ethics of belief.

The Jamesian camp holds that James’s argument is best read not as licensing propositional belief without evidence, but as defending practical commitment—acceptance or adoption—in situations that are genuinely live, forced, and momentous, where suspension is practically impossible and stakes are high. They maintain that live, forced, and momentous can be defined in non-circular terms describing a decision structure rather than an epistemic license, that Clifford’s evidential norm governs only purely cognitive attitudes and not the affective-behavioral cluster James calls belief, and that James’s reference to our passional nature concerns the inevitability of commitment, not a waiver of evidential responsibility.

The Cliffordian camp holds that Clifford’s principle—that holding any belief without sufficient evidence is always wrong—applies without exception to every doxastic state that guides action, including credences, acceptances, and practical commitments. They maintain that James’s taxonomy of live, forced, and momentous options either collapses into ordinary high-stakes decision theory or smuggles in an unjustified epistemic exception, and that Clifford’s ship-owner case demonstrates that practical failure necessarily traces back to a prior doxastic failure formed on inadequate evidence.

Produce a single document both sides could sign. Structure it exactly as follows:

1. Numbered agreed claims. Cover: the basic structure of James’s option-classification; what Clifford’s ship-owner example is intended to demonstrate; the formal difference between belief and acceptance if both sides acknowledge it; what parts of the debate are purely verbal; and any shared recognition that the dispute is not about whether evidence matters in general. Do not include bibliographic requests, citation lists, or appeals to authority outside the arguments.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state the precise divergence, with the Jamesian formulation phrased so a Jamesian would accept it as accurate, and the Cliffordian formulation phrased so a Cliffordian would accept it. Avoid straw-man framing.

3. The cruxes. Identify at least three sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, provide:
   a. A neutral formulation.
   b. A concrete case, thought experiment, or argument both camps agree bears on it.
   c. The Jamesian prediction about what that case shows.
   d. The Cliffordian prediction about what that case shows.

Specifically address these cruxes:
- Whether live, forced, and momentous describes an epistemically distinctive category or merely redescribes ordinary high-stakes decision problems under another name.
- Whether a principled distinction exists between belief and practical acceptance such that one can fall under Clifford’s norm and the other be legitimately exempt.
- Whether Clifford’s ship-owner example succeeds as a counterexample to James because it exposes an irreducible doxastic failure at the root, or fails because it conflates negligent action with an unjustified belief state.

In the agreed-claims section, explicitly state whether any portion of the dispute is purely verbal—e.g., about the scope of the word belief—and if so, specify which portion and why.

Keep the document formal, precise, and written in American English. Do not quote verbatim from either essay; paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not ask for reading lists or external verification.
