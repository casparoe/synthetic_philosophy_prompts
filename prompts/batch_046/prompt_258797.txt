I am grading exam papers for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind that treats Dennett's critique of Cartesian materialism as a central case study. In addition to the standard texts, our discussions have introduced what I have called the lateral coherence constraint: the requirement that any parallel-processing account of consciousness must explain how simultaneous representational streams maintain sufficient consistency to guide unified action, without appealing to a central convergence point. The exam asked students to explain Dennett's distinction between the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model, to show how the latter avoids the homunculus fallacy, and to assess whether it preserves the temporal unity of experience in light of that constraint.

The exam question read: Explain Dennett's distinction between the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model of consciousness. In your answer, address how the latter avoids the homunculus fallacy and whether it can account for the apparent temporal unity of experience, particularly in relation to the lateral coherence constraint discussed in the course.

One student wrote the following. Dennett rejects the Cartesian theater, which is the assumption that sensory inputs converge at a single point in the brain—historically linked to Descartes' pineal gland—where they are displayed for an inner observer. He argues that neuroscience reveals no such convergence point, and that positing one invites an infinite regress of homunculi. Instead, the brain continuously produces numerous parallel narrative streams, or drafts, and consciousness consists in one draft achieving temporary dominance through distributed competition, a process Dennett likens to fame in the brain. This eliminates the homunculus problem because there is no central audience, only editorial processes. The model also accounts for temporal unity, since later drafts can retroactively revise earlier ones, meaning the subject never actually holds contradictory experiences. Dennett developed this framework in direct dialogue with Baars' global workspace theory, adopting its parallel architecture but stripping away the workspace itself in order to block any recreation of the theater. Because there is no canonical final draft, there is strictly no fact of the matter about what a subject is experiencing at any given moment—only about what they will subsequently report. Consequently, while the lateral coherence constraint requires consistency for action guidance, Dennett's model satisfies it purely at the behavioral level, leaving no intrinsic experiential coherence.

Deliver the assessment directly, without an opening summary that separates your overall impression from the specific criticisms.
