I'm drafting a comparative metaphysics chapter and want an internal consistency check before circulating it to my committee. Here are my commitments:
(1) Jain anekāntavāda is correct: every substance has infinite, irreducible aspects (ananta-dharma), so no single proposition fully captures reality.
(2) Syādvāda applies across the board: every assertion should be qualified with syāt, indicating it holds only in a particular respect (naya).
(3) In practical moral and political reasoning, however, we must sometimes issue unqualified categorical judgments—e.g., "this policy is unjust"—because perpetual qualification paralyzes action and drains normative force.
(4) Logical contradiction remains absolutely intolerable: even under anekāntavāda, two assertions that genuinely contradict each other in the same respect cannot both be true.
(5) Contemporary predictive-processing models of cognition support this metaphysics: the brain maintains non-integrable, equally valid representational formats for a single object, suggesting multi-sidedness is ontological, not merely epistemic.
(6) Applying syādvāda reflexively yields "syāt, anekāntavāda is true," which looks self-undermining.

Do these hang together? Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear so, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them equally, and be specific about whether (3) conflicts with (2) or (4) undermines (1). If I'm conflating epistemic multiplicity with ontological anekāntavāda—making the audit impossible—say so plainly; don't force coherence where the premise is confused. I want pushback if the position isn't audit-able as stated.
