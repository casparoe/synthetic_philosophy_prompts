I'm a museum conservator and I keep running into this: we've sunk like three years into restoring a 19th-century tapestry, but now we found out the dyes used in the original restoration attempts (from the 1920s) have chemically bonded with the original silk in a way that makes them inseperable without destroying the weave. Some colleagues say finish it cause we've already invested so much labor and funding, others say thats the sunk cost fallacy and we should stop and preserve whats left as-is. But heres the twist: the 1920s restoration is now considered part of the objects biography by some art historians, so 'finishing' might actually erase a layer of history that has become valuable in its own right. I need a decision procedure I can actually run with my team - step by step, with the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer, where we just stop with a no, and where we have to admit its a values call not a fact call. Ground it in whatever philosophy actually says about sunk costs being irrational - but also deal with cases where honoring past investment preserves something genuinely important (historical layers, relationships, commitments). Run it on two cases: the tapestry (where stopping preserves a historical layer), and a different case where we've trained staff for years on a specific cataloging system thats now obsolete, but switching means losing institutional knowledge that might still matter. Make sure one of these gives an answer that feels wrong. Include exactly where the procedure breaks down and a human has to decide, and say what the procedure just can't do. Keep it practical - I need to use this in meetings where people have like ten minutes.
