A common framing of the Knobe effect—both in experimental-philosophy literature and in broader discussions of moral psychology—treats the asymmetry between intentional-action attributions for harmful versus beneficial side-effects as analogous to a perceptual illusion such as the Müller-Lyer figure: just as two lines of equal length appear different because of surrounding context, two structurally identical actions (foreseen, caused, but not aimed at) appear differently intentional because of their normative valence. Proponents use this analogy to argue that the asymmetry reveals a distorting cognitive bias rather than a deep feature of the concept of intentional action, and that once the distortion is recognized it should be corrected rather than accommodated. Audit this analogy precisely: map the lines, the measurement procedure, the persistence of the effect despite explicit knowledge, and the proposed underlying mechanism onto the corresponding elements in the Knobe case; then identify where the mapping breaks down—for example, whether there is an analog to the objective equality of the lines independent of appearance, whether warning subjects about the asymmetry eliminates it in the way knowing about an illusion sometimes does not, and whether the “distortion” operates at the level of perceptual processing or at the level of concept application and normative reasoning. Finally, assess whether the conclusion that the Knobe asymmetry is a bias to be eliminated, rather than a datum that any adequate theory of intentional action must explain, survives once these disanalogies are fully on the table, and say what a better analogy—if any—would need to preserve.
