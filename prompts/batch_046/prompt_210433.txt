I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on libertarian political theory and applied ethics. Next week I am presenting an interpretation of Robert Nozick's discussion of animals in Anarchy, State, and Utopia, specifically the material excerpted as Constraints and Animals (pp. 35–42). My thesis is that Nozick's rejection of the spillover justification—according to which harming animals is wrong mainly because it risks brutalizing agents and making them more likely to harm persons—depends on an implicit proximity condition: the agent must stand in direct causal and psychological relation to the harm for the side-constraint question to arise meaningfully. I argue that if we take this proximity condition seriously, it generates a counterintuitive result for modern industrial agriculture: because supply chains psychologically distance consumers from killing, Nozick's framework makes the absence of direct side constraints for animals more internally consistent, not less. At the same time, I claim this creates tension with Nozick's entitlement theory, since systemic coercion within industrial farming might constitute an indirect rights-violation against human workers or affected communities, even while the animals themselves remain outside the scope of stringent protection.

My audience will include a faculty respondent who specializes in Nozick's entitlement theory and is known for defending the coherence of side constraints; a visiting moral psychologist who studies moral distancing and is skeptical of analogies drawn from direct physical interaction; and a graduate student in animal ethics influenced by Regan's rights-based approach, who will likely press me on whether my reading lets Nozick off the hook for factory farming. There will also be two other students who have only encountered the experience-machine passage and will be seeing the animals section for the first time.
