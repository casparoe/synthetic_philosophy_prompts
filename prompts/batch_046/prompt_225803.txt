The Grand Inquisitor appears in Ivan Karamazov's poem inside The Brothers Karamazov, confronting Christ in Seville after the Inquisition has burned heretics. He says Christ refused Satan's three temptations — turning stones into bread, jumping from the temple, and ruling the kingdoms — which meant Christ insisted people choose freely between good and evil. This is clearly epistemic paternalism. The Inquisitor assumes people suffer from false consciousness about freedom: they say they want autonomy, but revealed preference shows they always pick security when given the choice. So he substitutes the Church's judgment for theirs. He doesn't deny freedom exists, he just treats it as a luxury good that humans can't psychologically afford. Dostoevsky puts this in Ivan's mouth rather than his own, but the novel's structure — especially Alyosha's brief, emotional reply — suggests Dostoevsky accepts the Inquisitor's economic logic, even if he dislikes the Church's cruelty. The silent kiss Christ gives the Inquisitor is mainly a gesture of forgiveness, not a substantive rejection of the idea that people need authority to be happy.
