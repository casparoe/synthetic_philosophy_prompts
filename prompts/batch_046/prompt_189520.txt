I'm writing this for an epistemology seminar in which we've just covered the classic 1963 counterexamples to justified true belief. Compare Michael Clark's "no false premises" repair with the safety-condition repair associated with Ernest Sosa and developed by later epistemologists, but focus the comparison specifically on cases of "intervening luck" rather than the standard Gettier structure. Here is the modified case I want you to center: Smith justifiably believes, on the basis of strong evidence, that Jones—who has ten coins in his pocket—will get the job. Smith's reasoning depends on a false premise about Jones's qualifications. Unbeknownst to Smith, however, Jones is not selected because he is qualified; he is selected because of a bureaucratic error that assigns the position to the tenth applicant on a list, and Jones happens to be tenth. Meanwhile, Smith himself has ten coins in his pocket, so Smith's derived belief that "the person who gets the job has ten coins in his pocket" is true, but its truth seems to depend on a causal pathway entirely independent of his reasoning.

First, explain whether Clark's prohibition on false premises correctly classifies Smith's belief as lacking knowledge in this modified scenario, and whether that prohibition overgeneralizes by ruling out legitimate knowledge acquired through fallible but safe reasoning. Second, analyze how the safety condition—roughly, that a belief counts as knowledge only if it could not easily have been false—treats the same case: does it distinguish the accidental truth here from the accidental truth in standard Gettier cases, or does it classify both as knowledge, or neither? Third, argue which of the two repairs offers the more stable revision to JTB, paying special attention to whether either account can consistently separate "intervening" environmental accidents from the kind of internal reasoning failures that originally motivated Gettier. Keep the discussion conceptual rather than historical, and please do not provide a bibliography or list of citations.
