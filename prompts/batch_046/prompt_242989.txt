I have recently been drawn into debates about actual infinities through an unexpected route: I am helping to design the ontology for a persistent virtual environment in which the architecture promises genuinely unbounded player housing, and the engineers keep insisting that an infinite server farm is merely a scaling problem, not a metaphysical one. In the philosophical discussions that followed, a common analogy keeps surfacing — Hilbert's hotel is invoked to show that an actually infinite collection can be rearranged, partitioned, and augmented without contradiction, as though it were a physical building with infinitely many rooms and a manager who never sleeps. The analogy is meant to license the conclusion that finitism is a failure of spatial imagination rather than a serious constraint on ontology. I want a precise audit of this mapping: what plays the role of the rooms, the guests, the manager, and the physical structure; where the disanalogies lie — temporal sequencing of reassignments, physical energy or information costs, the singularity of managerial instructions, and the absence of any boundary that a physical structure requires; whether the coherence of actual infinity survives once those physical imports are stripped away, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire persuasive load; and whether substituting a different analogy — perhaps an infinitely deep recursive catalog in which every entry refers to all entries except itself — would avoid the spatial smuggling while still rendering the finitist position implausible. I am not asking for a reading list; I need to determine whether the analogy holds its weight on its own.
