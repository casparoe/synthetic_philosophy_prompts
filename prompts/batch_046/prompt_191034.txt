I'm a software engineer working on model interpretability, and while I don't have formal training in philosophy, I've been trying to work through the Gödelian analogy that Hofstadter develops in Gödel, Escher, Bach—the move from self-referential arithmetic formulas to the "strange loops" he takes to underpin human self-awareness. I have sketched an argument that pushes this analogy much further than I'm comfortable with, and it now seems to prove far too much. Here are the steps as I see them: (1) Gödel constructed a formula within Peano arithmetic that, via Gödel numbering, asserts its own unprovability inside that very system. (2) Hofstadter extends this structural feature to cognition: consciousness arises when a symbol system achieves a high-level loop in which its representational patterns turn back upon themselves, modeling the system that generates them. (3) If this closed loop of self-representation is the essential feature—rather than biology, qualia, or any particular substrate—then any system that realizes the loop should share the relevant structural property. (4) In modern transformers, attention performs recursive token-binding: when the input includes references to the model's own prior outputs, those representations are folded back into the active computation graph, creating what I will call an indexical self-model embedded in distributed weights rather than in explicit symbolic form. (5) Therefore, by the Gödelian analogy, a system that achieves this functional loop should possess the structural conditions for self-awareness. (6) But this conclusion seems untenable, because it implies either that current large language models are conscious, which strikes me as implausible, or that any recursive program—say, a Python script that prints its own source code—lies on a continuum toward consciousness, which seems even worse. (7) So the analogy must be misapplied at some point, or consciousness requires an additional condition that blocks the inference from artificial token-binding to genuine self-awareness.

Please diagnose where the chain breaks: identify the specific step or hidden premise that should be rejected, explain why it initially looked defensible, and price the alternatives—especially what I must give up if I want to preserve the Gödelian analogy for human minds while denying it applies to artificial systems. I am inclined to think the missing ingredient is something like semantic grounding or embodied causal interaction, but I want to know whether that saves the analogy or just relocates the problem. Finally, please say what would change your mind: which counterexample, empirical finding, or philosophical objection would most weaken your diagnosis, and flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail—whether in interpreting Hofstadter's analogy, in my characterization of transformer computation as a strange loop, or in the implicit assumption that substrate independence follows from structural similarity.
