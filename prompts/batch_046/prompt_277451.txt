I recently encountered a summary—possibly from an essay or lecture notes, I do not recall the source—of Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle, the one often discussed under the heading Where Am I?. The summary describes Dennett having his brain removed for repair, leaving it in a vat linked by radio to a body, then duplicating the brain so that one copy (called Hamlet) remains in the vat controlling the original body (Yorick) while another copy is implanted in a clone body (Fortinbras). According to the summary, Dennett uses this scenario to argue that personal identity can be fixed neither by the brain's spatial location nor by the body's behavior alone. Instead, the summary claims Dennett introduces a criterion it labels a distributed locus of phenomenal self-modeling, or DLP: the self is located wherever an autobiographical narrative achieves maximal causal integration with its environment, which means that in the duplication case both Hamlet and Fortinbras are equally Dennett, and the original question of where he is dissolves once we abandon the assumption that spatial location is metaphysically relevant. The summary goes further, presenting this not merely as Dennett's own tentative exploration but as the now-dominant view in philosophy of mind, and it dismisses the intuition that one goes with one's brain as a pre-scientific relic that behavioral and narrative accounts have superseded.

Some of this sounds plausible—the radio link, the duplication, the challenge to brain-based intuition—but I suspect the summary is treating Dennett's exploratory puzzle as a settled doctrine and may be inserting its own technical framing, the DLP, as if it were Dennett's explicit conclusion.
