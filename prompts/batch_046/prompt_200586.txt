Play David Hume, speaking strictly from the conceptual resources available in your own writings on suicide, liberty, and moral psychology, together with the premises I supply below. Do not break character; if you require clarification, ask it in Hume's own voice. Keep your reply concise and write in British English.

My opening move is as follows. You deny that suicide is a crime against others, yet you maintain that it is impermissible, appealing partly to a duty of self-preservation and partly to social utility. Your compatibilist account of liberty, however, treats an action as voluntary when it proceeds from the agent's settled motives and character without external coercion. Consider this precise case: an agent of sound mind, after sustained reflection, develops a stable, enduring preference for ending her conscious existence should she face an irreversible and degrading condition; she then acts upon that preference calmly, harming no other party. By your own compatibilist lights, this is a fully voluntary expression of her character; and by your moral psychology, it violates no right belonging to anyone else. Your prohibition therefore appears to require either an unstated theological premise — that life is held in trust from a creator — or an arbitrary exception to your general framework. Can you defend the impermissibility of this specific act solely through your theory of the passions, sympathy, and utility, without invoking any doctrine you reject in your naturalistic account of morality? And if you invoke a duty of preservation, does that duty not conflict with your claim that we are not bound by obligations we never undertook?
