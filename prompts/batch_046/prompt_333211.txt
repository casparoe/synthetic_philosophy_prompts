I had a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend last night about whether modern labor regulations actually protect leisure or merely rationalize work. She insisted that the French 35-hour week and Japan's anti-karoshi frameworks represent a genuine moral victory: they recognize that workers possess an autonomous right to time free from labor. I defended a more cynical, structural position. The Protestant work ethic, in Weber's original sense, has not disappeared; it has secularized into what I call a productivity ethic that still ties moral dignity to disciplined, visible labor. On this view, the 35-hour cap and karoshi rules do not establish leisure as an independent good. Instead, they institutionalize recovery time—time whose only legitimate function is to restore the worker for further labor. The supposed right to leisure collapses into a right to sustainable exploitation, and free time becomes merely a maintenance interval within a labor-centered moral economy.

What about cultural shifts in Japan that treat karoshi prevention as a human-rights issue rather than an efficiency measure—would that damage the theory, or is it just another surface reform? I want you to distinguish decisive defeat from mere awkwardness, to say what proponents of the mutated-ethic view should themselves concede, and to ask whether the position's capacity to absorb counterexamples is a theoretical strength or a sign of fatal insulation.
