A close friend of mine recently resigned from a tenure-track position in philosophy to keep bees in a remote valley. She explained that she had spent over a decade constructing ever more precise arguments about what gives human life value, only to feel she was polishing instruments in a room with no audience — producing elaborate justifications for importance that the universe itself never solicited. Now her days are spent with hives that do not understand her intentions, and she insists she is not pretending the bees belong to some hidden cosmic plan; she simply finds that the work demands her complete attention in a way that writing papers never did. Yet she worries she has merely exchanged one comforting fiction for another, and she asks whether she ought to keep searching for some deeper reason that her life genuinely matters, or whether that very demand is precisely the source of her earlier dissatisfaction.

She also mentioned a distinction she encountered between feeling genuinely fulfilled by an activity and being objectively warranted in that fulfillment — the idea that a life might seem satisfying from the inside yet fail to connect with anything that actually merits the satisfaction. Given that she has rejected the possibility of cosmic endorsement, she is unsure whether this distinction still applies to her, or whether abandoning the search for objective warrant is the only coherent way to live after recognizing that the world offers none. She wants to know how she should evaluate her choice: is beekeeping a more meaningful life, or just a more pleasant narrative? And if the difference between a meaningful life and a well-told story collapses once we stop demanding universal validation, has she solved her problem or dissolved it?
