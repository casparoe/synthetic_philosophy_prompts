As a journalist writing on institutional design and collective-resource governance, I have been following the rulings of a regional commons arbitration panel whose verdicts on proposed management schemes I have documented across several years. Their established record is as follows. First, they approved a mandatory catch-quota system with licensed vessel access for a coastal fishery in which stock depletion was directly observable and the costs of excluding non-compliant parties were low. Second, they rejected a voluntary contribution program for urban air-quality improvement on the grounds that pollution sources were too diffuse for reliable monitoring and that the benefits of cleaner air were effectively non-excludable. Third, they approved, despite substantial social resistance, a mandatory maintenance levy on commercial users of an open-source geospatial library, reasoning that precise user identification made exclusion technically straightforward and that the labor required to maintain the code was genuinely rivalrous. Fourth, they rejected a proposed extraction tariff for a transboundary groundwater aquifer because enforcement mechanisms across porous jurisdictional borders were judged inadequate. Fifth, they approved a tradable-use permit system for alpine grazing lands with clearly demarcated ecological carrying capacities. The panel now faces a proposal for a mandatory replenishment protocol governing a shared repository of annotated satellite imagery that serves both public emergency-response agencies and private climate-risk forecasting algorithms. The annotations are technically non-rivalrous in direct consumption, yet rapid extraction by commercial entities without corresponding updates has led contributing meteorological agencies to reduce both the frequency and the granularity of their submissions, producing a measurable but lagging degradation in predictive accuracy that affects all users; exclusion via API-key licensing is technically trivial, yet politically fraught because the underlying imagery was originally captured with public funding, and the depletion manifests not as a physical stock reduction but as a gradual erosion in data quality that becomes visible only after several update cycles have passed. Given this body of precedents, I want to know how the panel should rule if it wishes to be consistent with itself, which prior case offers the closest analog, and whether the record genuinely underdetermines the verdict. If the precedents do allow more than one coherent principle, please identify the competing rules that justify each outcome, say which verdict you think best coheres with the panel's history and why, state explicitly what governing principle the panel would be endorsing if it chose the opposite verdict, and describe one further hypothetical case that would actually distinguish between those two underlying principles.
