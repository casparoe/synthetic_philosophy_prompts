I'm putting together opening remarks for a graduate seminar on the ethics of belief, and I want a joke that actually engages the substance of the debate between William James and W.K. Clifford rather than relying on a generic philosopher-stereotype punchline. The joke should turn on James's claim that belief is permissible without sufficient evidence only when the option is living, forced, and momentous — especially his view that our passional nature may legitimately decide a live hypothesis that intellect alone cannot resolve — set against Clifford's insistence that believing anything upon insufficient evidence is always wrong everywhere and for anyone, because of the collective epistemic damage of accepting the unverified.

To keep it from being a recycled classroom anecdote, please frame the punchline around an invented but conceptually precise scenario: imagine both thinkers have been resurrected as co-advisors to a modern institutional review board evaluating a grant proposal to study a singular, non-replicable mystical experience recorded in a private nineteenth-century diary. The board must decide whether the experience's veridicality counts as a live option worthy of funded investigation, or as an unsupported claim that must be rejected to preserve scientific integrity. The joke should depend on how James's three conditions interact with Clifford's shipowner-parable reasoning about collective responsibility and the habit-forming danger of belief, and it should be funny precisely to someone who understands why James accuses Clifford of ignoring the reality of forced options, while Clifford accuses James of licensing intellectual recklessness. It should be fully self-contained, requiring no citations, bibliographies, or access to secondary literature, and it should make sense only to a listener who actually grasps the arguments rather than to someone hearing only the surface vocabulary.
