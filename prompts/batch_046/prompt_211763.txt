I have just finished a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about the unilateralist's curse — that peculiar decision-theoretic trap in which each agent in a group rationally chooses to act because they expect others to do so in any case, with the collective result being precisely the outcome every individual wished to avoid. We went round in circles applying it to familiar cases — open-source release of frontier models, unilateral geoengineering trials, the publication of dual-use synthetic-biology protocols — and I realised we were treating the agents as a flat peer group. My friend then proposed a structural twist that I think breaks the standard framing, and I want to test whether a philosopher whose account of human nature was formed well before modern institutional complexity could still make sense of it.

The twist, which I will call recursive unilateralism, imagines the curse operating not horizontally among equals but vertically through nested layers of authority: the individual researcher, the institutional review board, the sovereign regulator, and perhaps even intergovernmental bodies. At each tier the logic repeats with a different object. The researcher publishes because 'if I withhold, a rival laboratory will release regardless'; the board approves because 'if we block, a less cautious jurisdiction will authorise'; the state funds because 'if we abstain, a strategic competitor will seize the advantage'. Crucially, every layer sincerely believes it is exercising caution relative to the layer beneath or above it, yet the global risk profile escalates precisely because each layer's 'defensive' action validates the next layer's fear of being outpaced. Apply this specifically to the governance of frontier artificial intelligence, where epistemic humility about long-term risks is widespread but competitive pressure is relentless. Now imagine that David Hume — with his account of sympathy, convention, the artificial virtues, and the passions that he takes to govern human action — were presented with this layered structure. How would he diagnose the moral psychology of agents trapped at each level? Would he interpret their reasoning as a corruption of the conventional logic he finds in justice, or as an inevitable expression of the self-interested and social passions he describes? And would he regard the recursive structure as amenable to institutional redesign, or as structurally inevitable given his view of human nature?
