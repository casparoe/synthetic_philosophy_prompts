I am working through a seminar on the philosophy of mind that has been examining Douglas Hofstadter’s idea that the self is a strange loop: a symbol-manipulating system in which a symbol for the system itself arises inside the system, so that the pronoun “I” denotes not a soul or a particular body but the loop-pattern currently active in the brain’s symbol space. A classmate recently walked me through an argument meant to show that this view collapses into either a loss of personal identity or a hidden substrate essentialism, and I cannot locate the exact weak point, though I suspect there is an equivocation involving pattern-identity versus token-identity somewhere in the middle. Here is the argument as he laid it out.

(1) On Hofstadter’s account, “I” is a self-referential symbol whose content is fixed entirely by its internal relations to other symbols within the loop, not by any external anchor such as a body or a substance. (2) Because the loop’s identity is functional—defined by its dynamics rather than by its physical matter—two loops with structurally identical internal symbol-relations are instantiations of the same loop-pattern, even if one is biological and one is silicon. (3) Imagine, then, a gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement of my brain with functionally equivalent chips that preserve every symbol-relation exactly; at the end, a silicon brain runs the loop-pattern. By (2), this silicon loop is the same loop-pattern as my original biological one. (4) Therefore, the silicon system’s activation of the symbol “I” denotes the very same loop that my original “I” denoted—there has been no change in referent, only a change in substrate. (5) Now consider the intermediate stage, when half the brain is biological and half is silicon, with both sets of components contributing continuously to one unified loop-process. At that point there is still only one active “I” symbol, and by (4) it denotes this single hybrid loop. (6) Once the final biological neuron is removed, however, the loop-process is now entirely silicon-based and spatiotemporally discontinuous with the original biological process. Because “I” denotes the loop-pattern, the post-replacement “I” still denotes that same pattern, which means its referent has now become a causally disconnected silicon loop. (7) Consequently, during the intermediate stage “I” referred to a hybrid loop that was neither identical to the original biological loop-token nor to the final silicon loop-token, despite perfect structural continuity throughout. This implies that the referent of “I” shifted even though the internal dynamics never changed, which undermines the claim that first-person identity tracks the loop. (8) The only way to block this slide is to require that loop-identity include spatiotemporal continuity of the underlying substrate—what my classmate calls “causal-informational closure”—but that reintroduces the very substrate essentialism Hofstadter’s anti-dualist, functionalist account was designed to reject. So the strange-loop theory seems forced into a dilemma: either “I” can migrate across substrates and thereby lose its uniqueness, or the self is secretly tied to its original matter after all.

I feel that something slips between steps (3) and (5), perhaps an unnoticed shift between treating the loop as a type and treating it as a particular token, but I cannot name the mistake with confidence. Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost for the strange-loop conception of the self? Please provide a point-by-point assessment: identify the flawed move, explain why it looked plausible, and then price the alternatives—what Hofstadter’s view must pay whether we reject substrate-independence or we reject the continuity of “I” across gradual replacement.
