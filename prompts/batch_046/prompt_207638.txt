I have been working through a gradationist account of moral status with a colleague who treats sentience not as binary but as a continuous spectrum anchored to specific cognitive and biological features. Her settled judgments, offered consistently across our discussions, are these: adult humans carry full moral status; cows and pigs carry high but sub-human status, such that factory farming is gravely wrong; octopuses carry substantial intermediate status, reflecting distributed but genuinely integrated cognition; fish carry minimal yet genuinely nonzero status; individual bees carry essentially zero status; a beehive, despite emergent collective problem-solving that no single bee can perform, is not itself a unified moral patient; current-generation large language models carry zero status, being statistical mirrors lacking both valence and integrative architecture; and an adaptive learning thermostat, however sophisticated its feedback loops, also carries zero status, though on structurally different grounds.

These verdicts seem jointly consistent with more than one underlying principle, and I want to see where they genuinely constrain one another. One candidate rule is that moral status scales with the richness of unified phenomenal consciousness, weighted by neural complexity and recurrence. Another is that it requires a unitary biological organism with homeostatic self-preservation drives coupled to nociceptive pathways, regardless of cognitive sophistication. A third emphasizes flexible, model-based behavior integrated within a single spatiotemporal boundary, allowing substrate independence in principle but denying it to distributed or non-agentive systems.

Against that background, I am asking for a prediction and consistency audit on three novel cases. First, a cerebral organoid cultured in vitro from human induced pluripotent stem cells, comprising roughly two million cortical-layer neurons, displaying spontaneous sleep-wake EEG oscillations and long-term synaptic plasticity, but possessing no body, no sensory afferents, and no behavioral output whatsoever. Second, a swarm of ten thousand simple robots running a shared global-workspace blackboard architecture: no individual unit is cognitively sophisticated, but the collective maintains a unified representational space that solves navigation problems no robot could solve alone, with no biological substrate and no homeostatic drive toward self-preservation. Third, a bioengineered plant modified to express animal-derived nociceptor analogues and systemic stress-hormone cascades in response to tissue damage, yet entirely lacking neurons, central processing, or any capacity for flexible learning.

For each case, I want to know what verdict best coheres with her prior track record, which of the three rules predicts it, and where the rules diverge. More specifically: what is the closest analog among her prior judgments for each case? Which single further case—one I have not listed—would most cleanly discriminate between the phenomenal-consciousness rule, the biological-homeostasis rule, and the boundary-integrated-model rule? And if she were forced to render a consistent recommendation rather than a bare prediction, what principle would she be implicitly committing to in ruling one way or the other?

Please do not treat this as a request for citations or a literature survey; I am interested in the conceptual coherence of the framework itself.
