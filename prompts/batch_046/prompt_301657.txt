I work as a clinical ethics adviser in a psychiatric rehab unit, and we keep getting asked to sign off on whether a patient's reconstructed autobiography counts as a 'real' self or just a story they've picked up from therapy culture and Netflix docs. I know Dennett talks about the self as a centre of narrative gravity - not a thing but a point where stories converge and pull other stories in. But I need something I can actually run in about fifteen minutes during a case review, with no philosophy PhD required, and it needs to say clearly where the answer is factual and where it's just a value call about what kind of person we think they should become.

Can you write me a decision procedure - ordered questions, what counts as passing each one, where it stops with a no, where it dumps the decision back onto the clinical team to recieve it because it's values not facts. The twist is I want it to check for what I'm calling 'narrative inertia' (the story keeps itself going without external feed) versus 'borrowed momentum' (it collapses when the therapist or the TV series stops being present). I don't care if that's Dennett's actual term, it's the distinction I want tested.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a patient whose life story suddenly includes detailed trauma narratives that match a popular podcast series they binge-watched; (2) a patient whose story is fragmented but consistently pulls new memories and behaviours towards the same thematic centre even when therapists change; (3) one where the procedure gives you an answer that feels wrong - maybe a coherent but clearly imported story that technically passes all your inertia checks.

Also flag what the procedure can't do, especially around the fact that we're partly deciding what counts as a healthy self rather than describing one.
