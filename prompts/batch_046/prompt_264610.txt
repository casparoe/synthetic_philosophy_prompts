I am drafting a novel in which a head of state suspends constitutional protections to prevent secession, and I want to understand the moral architecture of such moments before writing the scene. Analyze Abraham Lincoln's 1862 suspension of habeas corpus—specifically the military arrest of Maryland secessionists, including Congressman John Merryman, without prior congressional approval—through the lens of dirty hands in politics. Set aside the constitutional debate, which I take as given. Treat the suspension itself as a first-order moral wrong committed for a greater good, and Lincoln's subsequent pursuit of retroactive legislative authorization as a second-order institutional maneuver. Within the framework associated with Walzer, does such retroactive cover constitute an acknowledgment of the moral stain or an attempt to wash it away? Lincoln defended the act by necessity, yet never, to my knowledge, admitted its intrinsic wrongness. Does this render the episode a case of hidden rather than acknowledged dirty hands, and what does that distinction do to the claim that the leader must publicly shoulder the burden? Map the agents with precision: does the stain adhere to Lincoln personally, to the officers who executed the arrests, or to the legislature that later ratified them? Finally, ask whether the dirty-hands problem applies to institutional betrayal—violating a constitutional oath one is sworn to uphold—or whether the theory was designed only for personal moral violations. Where does it remain silent?
