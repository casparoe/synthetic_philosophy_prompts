I am trying to reconstruct the implicit principle governing a gradationalist philosopher's assignments of moral status. Her settled verdicts across cases are these: adult humans possess maximal status; chimpanzees possess near-maximal status, treated as practically equivalent to humans in moral weight; pigs possess substantial but clearly inferior status; chickens possess minimal yet genuinely non-zero status; individual honeybees possess barely perceptible status, just above the zero line; a honeybee colony, despite solving problems no individual bee can solve, receives exactly zero collective status; earthworms receive negligible but technically non-zero status; a Portuguese man o' war receives status only as the aggregate of its constituent zooids, with no emergent collective standing; and thermostats—including those running adaptive feedback algorithms—receive unconditional zero status regardless of functional complexity.

Consider now a novel case: a cerebral organoid cultured from human stem cells that exhibits functional thalamocortical connectivity, sustained gamma oscillations, and calcium-wave responses analogous to nociceptive signaling, yet entirely lacks sensory periphery, motor capacity, evolutionary history, behavioral repertoire, or learning mechanisms.

Predict her verdict regarding the organoid's moral status. In doing so, articulate at least two competing rules that are equally consistent with her prior rulings—for instance, a phenomenal-valence rule, an integrated-cognitive-architecture rule, and an organismal-integrity rule—explain what each predicts for the organoid, provide your best-guess verdict with supporting reasoning, and identify one additional hypothetical case that would most effectively discriminate among these competing interpretations.
