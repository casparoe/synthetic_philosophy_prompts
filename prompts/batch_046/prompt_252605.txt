Construct a realistic clinical thought experiment—no longer than one paragraph, set in a standard neuropsychological testing room with no exotic technology—featuring a split-brain patient with a complete corpus callosum section. Engineer the case so that a theory of phenomenal access unity (one conscious subject with divided information streams) and a theory of phenomenal subject division (two conscious subjects) both predict the identical overt behavioral response on a bimanual classification task involving an ambiguous figure presented separately to each hemifield, yet deliver opposite verdicts on whether the patient undergoes a single, verbally reportable experience of cognitive conflict or two isolated, non-interacting affective states. The divergence must turn on one precisely isolable structural feature—such as the presence or absence of a shared error-monitoring signal accessible to the language-dominant hemisphere—rather than on bare stipulation. After presenting the scenario, derive each theory’s verdict with explicit reasoning, identify the feature that does the work, explain what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed or neutralized, and present the strongest objection a unity-theory defender could raise without appealing to hidden neural mechanisms that are in principle unobservable to the patient.
