A writer in a recent essay on biography and ethics summarizes Plutarch's Parallel Lives as operating through a mechanism she calls exemplar calibration: Plutarch selects extreme moral types—Alexander's unchecked ambition paired against Caesar's calculated restraint—so that readers adjust their own ethical metrics by measuring the distance between these subjects and themselves. She claims that Plutarch intended these pairings as direct hagiographic models for imitation, that his Greek-Roman structure was mainly a rhetorical strategy to render pagan virtue legible to Christian audiences, and that modern moral learning from biography is now broken because we have abandoned this calibration model—hagiography today serves communal identity rather than individual moral correction, leaving us without Plutarch's mechanism. Is this a fair account? Please separate what accurately reflects Plutarch's aims and method, what is a standard simplification, and what genuinely misrepresents either Plutarch's historical context or the relationship between classical biography and later hagiography. In particular, does her conclusion—that the mechanism is broken—follow from the accurate parts of her summary, or does it depend on the misrepresentations? Also state what would change your mind: which historical or conceptual consideration would lead you to reassess whether the Greek-Roman pairing was primarily rhetorical, and where your reasoning about the mechanism's failure is most vulnerable.
