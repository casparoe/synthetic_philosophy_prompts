I was arguing for several hours with a friend after a seminar on medieval Islamic metaphysics, and I have the uneasy sense that my own position on Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism is not as coherent as I presented it. Before I write this up, I want a consistency audit of the following package. First, I hold that Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalist core is correct: created things possess no intrinsic causal powers, God is the sole direct efficient cause of every particular event, and what we call natural causes are only occasions (asbāb) for divine action. Second, I take the doctrine of ʿādah—God’s customary regularity—to have real ontological weight, not merely epistemic force: God has freely bound Himself to create in stable patterns, and these patterns constitute a genuine modal structure in the world. Third, I therefore maintain that scientific explanation is truly explanatory rather than instrumentally useful—when physics articulates a law, it describes the actual structure of consistent divine creation, not a predictive fiction imposed on random events. Fourth, I defend human moral responsibility through the Ashʿarite doctrine of kasb (acquisition): an agent acquires an act by means of an internal volition that accompanies it, and this volition—though also immediately created by God—is genuinely the agent’s own in a non-derivative sense that grounds praise and blame. Fifth, I think occasionalism offers the best solution to the modern interaction problem: mental events and physical events are each directly caused by God in perfect correlation, eliminating the need for mental-to-physical causation and dissolving the causal exclusion worry. Sixth, however, I also insist that for moral responsibility to be more than illusory, the agent’s volition must contribute something causally or constitutively distinctive—something that distinguishes voluntary action from reflex or coercion—rather than functioning as just another occasion on a par with fire or medicine.

Please examine these six commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine logical or motivational tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but can be reconciled once the roles of ʿādah and kasb are properly distinguished, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each serious conflict, specify exactly what must be revised—whether my reading of kasb, my extension of occasionalism to mind-body correlation, or my claim about scientific explanation—and indicate the least costly adjustment that would restore consistency without abandoning the occasionalist framework altogether. I am not sure where the fault lies, so do not treat any one commitment as the obvious sacrificial lamb; trace the commitments precisely.
