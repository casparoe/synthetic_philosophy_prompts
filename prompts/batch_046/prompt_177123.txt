I am a high school student working through Derek Parfit’s teletransporter argument, and I think I grasp why he says personal identity is not what matters—what matters is psychological continuity, which can come in degrees and can branch. But I keep running into what seems like an accounting problem that the literature never addresses directly. Suppose the scanner does not copy my full psychological profile but only a partial one, and then reconstructs two bodies: one that inherits about sixty percent of my original psychology and one that inherits about forty percent, while my original body is destroyed. If psychological continuity is the real measure of survival, then together my two descendants seem to possess one hundred percent of my continuity, only distributed. That looks as though continuity has been duplicated and summed rather than merely transferred. I have started calling this intuition the “divergence debt”: the idea that when a mind splits, the total psychological continuity across all branches should not exceed what existed in the original, otherwise we have created survival-value out of nothing. Is there an established name for this kind of conservation constraint on psychological continuity? And if Parfit rejects it, does that commit him to the view that my survival can be multiplied without limit—so that the question “will I survive?” fails not because identity is absent, but because there is too much of me to go around?
