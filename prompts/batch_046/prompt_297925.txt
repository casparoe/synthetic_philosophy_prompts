I've been thinking about Hempel's raven paradox lately - you know, how seeing a green apple supposedly confirms that all ravens are black - and it got me wondering about detective fiction. I'm taking a course on logic and we just read some Sherlock Holmes, and I want to use the stories to illustrate confirmation theory without misleading anyone.

Read Conan Doyle's Holmes stories, especialy Silver Blaze, through the lens of Hempel's paradox and the philosophy of confirmation. Holmes is constantly treating absences and negative observations - the dog that didn't bark, the missing footprint - as decisive evidence. Does this commit him to a version of the raven paradox? That is, if observing a non-raven that isn't black confirms all ravens are black, then observing a non-barking dog should confirm... well, what exactly? And does Holmes's abductive method avoid the paradox by implicitly restricting the reference class, or does it just hide the problem?

I'm especially interested in whether the stories dramatize a tension between epistemic and ontological versions of the paradox - does Holmes care about what confirms his belief, or about what makes the world structured such that his belief is true? There's this idea I ran into (maybe I'm inventing it?) that detective fiction treats negative evidence as having inverse weight compared to positive clues, but Hempel's framework treats all confirming instances symmetrically. Does Holmes's practice actually depend on breaking that symmetry, and if so, is there a philosophical defense of that break? Don't just summarize the paradox and then list Holmes scenes. I want an actual reading: where the paradox illuminates a scene, where the scene shows the paradox missing something about real reasoning, and whether the stories end up vindicating Hempel or undermining him.
