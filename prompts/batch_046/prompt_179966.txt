Design a 90-minute seminar activity for approximately twelve philosophy graduate students that makes participants feel the force of Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, deliberately set outside the familiar gavagai and rabbit scenario. Instead, the activity should center on translating assembly instructions for an unfamiliar modular mechanical object—described hypothetically, with parts that can be arranged into a functioning structure—where two translation manuals are observationally equivalent yet assign incompatible internal functional descriptions to identical components (for example, one manual treats a given part as a lever and the other as a counterweight). Specify that the seminar room should be arranged so that pairs of students each receive one manual, assemble the same physical or diagrammed structure successfully, and then compare outcomes. The rules and material layout must be arranged so that the pairs discover through direct trial and structured comparison that both manuals predict every correct assembly step and every observable failure mode, leaving them unable to find an empirical test that distinguishes the manuals. Only after they have experienced this adjudication failure should the facilitator name the parallel to radical translation and the absence of a fact of the matter about meaning.

Include precise rules and a hypothetical description of the modular object and its parts; a non-monetary incentive or payoff scheme appropriate to a graduate seminar, such as collective debate privileges or points toward a shared analytical goal; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the assembly and comparison phases onto Quine's arguments about the underdetermination of theory by evidence, the difficulty of maintaining a sharp analytic-synthetic distinction in translation, and the inscrutability of reference. The debrief should also state frankly what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—for instance, that participants will invent hidden physical tests that smuggle in unacknowledged ontological commitments, or that they will judge one manual simpler based on prior mechanical intuition rather than behavioral evidence. Finally, explicitly admit which feature of Quine's global thesis—namely, the theoretical possibility of radically incompatible but empirically equivalent total translation manuals across an entire language—cannot be fully reproduced with a single modular artifact and must be supplied by the facilitator's framing and discussion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write in a formal, polished register.
