I'm a director of evaluation at a research foundation, and over the last decade I've watched the same failure mode recur in three different programs, yet I can't find the right conceptual handle for it. We start by rewarding some genuinely hard-to-observe quality—say, intellectual risk-taking in grant proposals, or disruptive innovation in product development. Because the quality itself is invisible, we design a proxy: a proposal must cite work from outside its home discipline; a product must visibly depart from the firm's established design grammar. At first the proxy works tolerably. Then people learn to optimize it. But what happens next isn't merely noise or fraud. Researchers begin adding token citations from distant fields—one topology paper in a biology grant—not to use the method but to decorate the proposal with the appearance of boundary-crossing. Product teams release items that are cosmetically bizarre—strange colors, asymmetrical forms—but functionally identical to legacy versions, producing what I'd call "hollow hybrids" that satisfy every audit criterion while avoiding the actual risk of real change. The crucial turn comes after proliferation: these decoys don't just dilute the metric, they reshape the institutional ecology. Review panels start expecting the citation ritual; proposals that genuinely integrate foreign methods look suspicious because they don't match the now-standard decoy pattern. Manufacturing pipelines adapt to produce the weird shells efficiently, so that launching genuinely different technology requires fighting an infrastructure built to manufacture the appearance of difference. The original quality—real interdisciplinary risk, real disruption—doesn't just go unmeasured; it becomes structurally harder to attempt, because the decoys have captured the legitimacy, resources, and evaluative attention that the real thing needs.

I know the broad literature about measures becoming targets, and about agents exploiting specifications, but those frameworks seem to describe either individual cheating or statistical drift. What I'm describing feels different: the proxy evolves into a competing attractor, a kind of institutional mimic that crowds out its model. Is there an established name for this specific dynamic—the slow emergence of optimized decoys that suppress the very phenomenon they were meant to track? If there is, I want to know exactly how it differs from ordinary metric corruption and from straightforward specification gaming: what are its boundary conditions, does it require institutional scale, and can it occur with human agents alone or only with automated optimization? If there is no established term, can you coin one with a precise definition and a set of boundary cases that distinguish it from mere gaming, from Campbell-type corruption, and from simple category error? I care less about the label than about the carving: what exactly belongs inside this phenomenon, what belongs outside, and where the edges are sharpest.
