I'm preparing a dataset that asks models to apply philosophical frameworks to well-known historical episodes without web lookup, and I'm particularly interested in whether Marx's paired analysis of exploitation and alienation can be disciplined by a concrete labor crisis rather than treated as an abstract moral vocabulary. Take the 1936-1937 Flint sit-down strike against General Motors as the episode. Assume familiarity with the basic chronology—workers occupied key plants, demanded union recognition and control over line speed, and eventually secured a contract that raised wages but preserved management's authority over production—but do not recount it as history.

Instead, use the strike to test whether Marx's technical distinction between exploitation (the extraction of surplus value mediated by the wage-form) and alienation (the fourfold estrangement from product, labor process, species-being, and fellow workers) holds together in practice. Map each dimension of alienation onto the concrete grievances and tactics of the strike: what did physically occupying the factory alter about the workers' relation to the goods they made, to the rhythm of labor, to one another, and to their own capacities? Then consider the settlement: did raising wages reduce exploitation in Marx's precise sense, or was this a case in which the formal subsumption of labor under capital remained intact while only the price of labor-power changed?

Finally, inject an unorthodox framing: treat the sit-down not merely as a bargaining tactic but as an attempt to reclaim species-being inside the factory gates. Does Marx's framework permit labor struggles to function as moments of de-alienation, or does it predict that any settlement within capitalism must reproduce both exploitation and alienation? Say where the theory explains the episode well, where it forces you to read the workers' motives through a grid they might not have recognized, whether the case supports treating exploitation and alienation as inseparable or as analytically independent, and where Marx's account is silent about features of the crisis—such as the role of state mediators or the specific technological layout of the assembly line—that actually determined how it ended.
