I'm developing a research program on the democratic boundary problem—specifically, who belongs to the demos—through an unconventional framing I call institutional maintenance loops: the recursive, practice-based processes through which shared political structures reproduce themselves over time. Rather than relying solely on affected-interest or citizenship criteria, I want to determine which entities are genuinely embedded in these loops in a way that generates normative standing for democratic inclusion. Given this framing, what are the main open questions and lines of work that should take priority for a researcher with roughly three years and a background in political philosophy rather than institutional design?

In particular, I see three candidate problems. First, whether future generations can be said to participate in maintenance loops despite their absence from present recursive practice. Second, whether non-human entities—particularly complex ecosystems or advanced artificial systems—can be embedded in such loops in a politically relevant sense without collapsing into metaphor. Third, how to design revisable boundary mechanisms that allow the demos to redefine itself over time without circular dependence on an already-fixed membership.

Please rank these in terms of conceptual importance and tractability, and defend the ordering with explicit criteria: which question is upstream of the others, which is most likely to yield clarity for democratic theory before requiring empirical institutional study, and which carries the greatest risk of being intractable within a purely philosophical framework. I am less interested in a broad survey than in a defended agenda—what deserves attention first, and why?

Additionally, please indicate what would change your mind: whether a demonstration that maintenance loops are reducible to standard affected-interest accounts, or a particular argument about the ontology of collective practice, would cause you to reorder these priorities or abandon the framing altogether. Respond in American English.
