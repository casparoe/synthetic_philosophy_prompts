hey, i'm writing this scene where a philosophy grad student argues with her brother about bootstrapping, and she lays out this chain that seems to prove she can know her whole tax situation just by glancing at her car. here's the argument, tell me where the leak is:

(1) i can see my car is in the driveway, so i know its there.
(2) if my car is there, then it hasn't been stolen and sold to cover hidden debts.
(3) so i know it hasn't been sold for hidden debts.
(4) if it hasn't been sold for hidden debts, then i don't have hidden debts of that kind.
(5) therefore i know i don't have hidden debts tied to my car.
(6) i can run the same reasoning for my bike, laptop, watch — every asset i can observe.
(7) once i've covered all observable assets, i know i have no hidden debts tied to anything i own.
(8) and if i have no hidden debts tied to anything i own, then i have no hidden debts period.
(9) so just by walking around my apartment, i can come to know i'm fully in the clear financially, without ever checking a statement.
(10) but that's absurd — i can't know my taxes are clean just by checking my mailbox... wait, i mean driveway.

she really wants to keep closure intact, and she thinks the problem is in (8), that it's an overgeneralization. but i feel like there's a sneaky shift between (4) and (5), or maybe in (2) where she treats a counterfactual like a deductive premise. which step actually breaks? and if we save closure, what do we have to pay — do we lose easy knowledge altogether, or just this weird pyramid?
