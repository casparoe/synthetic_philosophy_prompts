The philosophical literature on demarcating legitimate historiography from pseudohistory has proposed criteria ranging from falsifiability to institutional peer review, yet debates over concrete cases—Fomenko’s New Chronology, narratives attributing monumental architecture to ancient astronauts, and Atlantis reconstructions—remain frozen in cycles of accusation and restatement without advancing the underlying framework. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about evidentiary standards, but about a deeper divergence in what counts as an explanatory burden: one camp treats archival gaps as temporary deficiencies that pattern-matching across cultures can overcome, while the other treats those same gaps as ontological barriers that render trans-historical causal claims structurally invalid regardless of internal coherence. Is this diagnosis correct, or is the disagreement actually verbal—centered on whether “pseudohistory” denotes a methodological failure, a sociological label, or a normative judgment—or are the sides answering different questions, one descriptive and one prescriptive, under a single heading? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure, supported by what proponents of methodological and sociological demarcation actually claim about cases like Fomenko’s chronology, together with an honest assessment of whether a hidden empirical question about cognitive bias or institutional authority is doing the work. Finally, tell me what—if anything—would count as genuine progress: a formal distinction in the logic of historical explanation, an empirical finding about reasoning biases, a reconceptualization of archival absence, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
