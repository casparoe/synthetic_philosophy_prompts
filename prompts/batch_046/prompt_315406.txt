I've been chewing on Hofstadter's idea that the self is basically a strange loop and can persist in other brains — like he suggests with his late wife Carol's patterns living on inside his own head. But I want to push it in a weirder, more specific direction: what if the "I" isn't just a static pattern that gets copied over, but actually needs an active, live resonance between neural substrates? Picture a hypothetical setup where two people are linked by a sustained bidirectional interface — not a one-time upload, but a constant feedback loop where each brain is always adjusting to the other's dynamics. After years of this coupling, the loop itself becomes the primary site of self-reference; neither brain alone can maintain the "I," but together they do. I'm calling this the coupled-loop view, and it feels like it explains why people feel so hollowed out when a long-term partner dies, but also why a perfect brain-emulation might still miss something if it only copies static weights.

But I'm trying not to fall in love with my own sci-fi fantasy here. What would actually count against the coupled-loop position? I want the honest defeaters — not just "that sounds implausible." Would split-brain patients maintaining unified selfhood despite a severed corpus callosum be fatal, or just awkward? What about dementia cases where the "resonance partner" dies first and the survivor's personality seems totally intact? Or neuroscience showing that self-reference is localized enough that continuous coupling can't really change its locus? I also worry I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability — like, if I say the resonance is "subtle" or "non-functional," then no experiment could ever disprove it. Is that a real problem for a view of this kind, or is it okay for metaphysical claims? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely embarassing, say what defenders of the coupled-loop view would themselves concede is damaging, and tell me whether the position risks anything at all or if I've already insulated it too well. Also, if there's a conceptual mistake I'm making by treating selfhood like a distributed process rather than a singular one, I'd like to hear that spelled out — especially if it collapses the view back into standard psychological continuity.
