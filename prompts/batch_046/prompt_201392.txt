Read Christopher Nolan’s film The Prestige through Derek Parfit’s teletransporter argument and his claim that psychological continuity, not physical continuity or identity, is what matters for survival—together with the further idea that survival can come in degrees and can branch. The film literalizes this in Angier’s use of Tesla’s duplication apparatus: every performance produces a copy with complete memory and psychology, while the original falls through a trapdoor and drowns in a water tank beneath the stage. By the end, dozens of dead originals accumulate in storage, and the living copy continues, perhaps knowing he is only the latest link in a chain.

Treat the film as more than an illustration. Apply Parfit’s distinction between identity (a one-one relation) and survival (potentially one-many) to Angier’s repeated fission: in what sense, and to what degree, does the copy survive? Does the pile of dead bodies undermine the intuition that psychological continuity is sufficient, or does it reveal that continuity stripped of physical continuity becomes horrifying precisely because it severs survival from the value we attach to being the same person? Then turn to Borden’s parallel secret—he is actually identical twins living one shared life, distributing psychological continuity across two bodies from the start. Does Borden’s case support Parfit by showing that one biography can be lived by more than one body, or does it undermine him by showing that shared identity requires a unified life-narrative that Angier’s copies lack?

Conclude by saying what the film adds that the abstract argument alone misses: does the spectacle of deliberate, repeated self-annihilation for art expose something about why we resist treating survival as a purely quantitative or distributive good? And be honest about where the reading strains the film—where the horror is better explained by physicalist intuition, where by moral revulsion at the waste of lives, and where by Parfit’s own framework.
