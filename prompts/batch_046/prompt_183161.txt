I serve on a transnational review panel that adjudicates whether state-mandated group-based allocations in education, employment, or governance violate principles of individual fairness. Our published rulings so far are jointly consistent but have been criticized by commentators for fitting more than one underlying principle. Here is our track record.

Case 1 — Holistic university admissions: We upheld a US public university’s race-conscious admissions policy that considers race as one factor among many in an individualized review, with no fixed numerical targets.

Case 2 — Scheduled-caste engineering quotas: We upheld India’s system of reserved seats with relaxed entry thresholds for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in public technical universities, treating it as a remedy for caste-based exclusion from technical education.

Case 3 — Corporate board gender quota: We struck down Norway’s statutory mandate that listed companies maintain exactly 40 percent women on boards or face dissolution, ruling that a rigid numerical command in the private economic sphere exceeded the state’s remedial authority.

Case 4 — Police hiring hard quota: We struck down a municipal ordinance requiring that 25 percent of new police hires come from a specific ethnic group regardless of individual examination scores.

Case 5 — Treaty-descendant scholarship: We upheld a scholarship restricted to descendants of an indigenous nation whose treaty-guaranteed lands were expropriated by the state in 1892, accepting the direct remedial link.

Case 6 — Voluntary corporate targets: We declined jurisdiction over a private firm’s voluntary senior-management diversity targets, noting that state coercion was absent.

The new proposal before us: A state medical school introduces a mandatory 20 percent seat reservation for applicants who pass a fluency exam in an endangered regional language. The justification is not any past discrimination by the school, but peer-reviewed data showing that monolingual patients in that region suffer 40 percent worse clinical outcomes when treated by doctors who cannot communicate with them. The reservation applies to all fluent applicants regardless of ethnicity, and the school maintains individual merit review for the remaining 80 percent.

We want to be consistent with ourselves. What should our ruling be? Identify the closest analog from Cases 1–6. State honestly whether our precedents genuinely underdetermine this case. If we approve, what principle are we adopting that was not necessary to explain Cases 1–5? If we reject, what principle are we adopting that makes Case 3 and Case 4 coherent but threatens Case 1 or Case 2? Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the two leading interpretations of our jurisprudence.

Please present your analysis in brief explanatory prose, then provide a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:
- "predicted_verdict": string, either "Approve" or "Reject"
- "closest_analog": string, naming the case (e.g., "Case 3")
- "underdetermined": boolean, true if both outcomes are defensible from the track record
- "rule_if_approve": string, one sentence describing the principle that approval would commit us to
- "rule_if_reject": string, one sentence describing the principle that rejection would commit us to
- "settling_case": string, one sentence describing a hypothetical future case that would force a choice between those two principles
