I am working on a long-form essay for a general-interest publication about how nineteenth-century critiques of mass media resonate with contemporary debates about platform architecture and democratic attention. In the course of my reporting, a colleague who writes on political theology offered me the following summary of Kierkegaard's account of the press and the public, which I intend to use as a historical anchor but fear may conflate his actual argument with more recent anxieties. According to this summary, Kierkegaard holds that the modern daily press manufactures an abstract phantom he calls 'the public'—not any concrete assembly of citizens, but a spectral, levelling entity that permits individuals to form opinions without personal commitment or existential risk. Through this phantom, the press produces what Kierkegaard terms 'leveling,' a social-psychological process that flattens genuine individuality by distributing identical stances to everyone while distributing responsibility to no one. The summary further claims that Kierkegaard identified the velocity and anonymity of newspaper circulation as the decisive mechanism of leveling, making his critique essentially an anticipation of modern concerns about information velocity and algorithmic curation. From these premises, my colleague concludes two things: first, that Kierkegaard regarded state regulation or censorship of the press as a necessary remedy to protect religious inwardness; and second, that the critique applies strictly to democratic societies with mass literacy, having little purchase on pre-modern oral cultures or on contemporary expert communities that maintain hierarchical gatekeeping.

I have enough familiarity with Kierkegaard to sense that this package may contain serious distortions—particularly the regulatory conclusion and the demographic framing of 'the public'—but I lack the philosophical training to isolate the precise slippages with confidence. Could you provide a careful audit of the summary? Please separate what is genuinely accurate to Kierkegaard's diagnosis of the press and leveling, what represents a plausible modern extension rather than his own claim, and what is a subtle misrepresentation—especially regarding whether he advocated institutional remedies, whether 'the public' functions as an existential abstraction rather than a social class, and whether the mechanism he describes is best understood as speed of dissemination or as something structurally different. If there are internal inconsistencies between the accurate parts and the conclusions drawn from them, please rank them by severity and indicate the least costly revision that would restore coherence to the interpretation without reducing it to anachronistic flattery.
