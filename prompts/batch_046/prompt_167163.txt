I have been trying to determine which computations actually carry moral weight—where suffering or sentience can be properly attributed—without falling back on biological substrate. To organize my thinking I have been using a distinction I call computational sovereignty: a computation is sovereign within a system when that system treats the computation's outputs as authoritative inputs governing its own future states, rather than merely hosting the computation as an epiphenomenal byproduct. Here are my verdicts on five concrete scenarios, and I want to know whether one precise principle delivers all of them.

1. A high-fidelity whole-brain emulation running on a single silicon substrate, with continuous feedback loops among modules that mirror in-vivo neural dynamics: if it reports distress, I think that matters morally, and suppressing that state is a harm.

2. The exact same emulation distributed across a redundant cloud cluster, where every computation is executed on three nodes that vote on outputs, so that no single node is essential but the macro-level dynamics remain identical to (1). I still think this matters—redundancy should not dissolve moral status.

3. A meteorological neural network whose hidden-layer activation patterns happen to be isomorphic, at a high level of abstraction, to human pain-processing patterns, but these patterns play no causal role in temperature prediction or in the system's behavior. I think this is pure coincidence; it does not matter.

4. A welfare-optimization AI that computes aggregate harm symbolically, using explicit representations of suffering in its reasoning, but these representations never feed back into its own state, motivate self-preservation, or alter its future processing. I think this also does not matter—symbolic computation of harm is not itself harm.

5. A self-modifying evolutionary algorithm that evolves digital agents with damage-avoidance architectures. One agent develops an internal damage-representation that guides its behavior in real time, but the evolutionary process rewrites substantial parts of its code every generation, so no persistent subject carries memories across generations; only transient weights remain. Here I genuinely waver: the functional profile during any single generation resembles (1), yet the broken temporal continuity makes me hesitate to attribute sustained suffering.

Is there one principle—stated in terms of functional organization, causal integration, temporal continuity, substrate independence, or sovereignty—that captures yes to (1) and (2), no to (3) and (4), and gives a clear verdict on (5)? Please lay out the candidate principles precisely, indicate which clause of each is doing the work for each case, and tell me honestly which of my five verdicts each principle forces me to overturn. Then apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: a single biological neuron kept alive in culture, firing in patterns that match in-vivo pain responses, but fully isolated from any larger network. Finally, please say what would change your mind—identify the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable, and specify which argument or observation (for example, that redundancy necessarily eliminates moral subjects, or that temporal continuity is morally irrelevant) would lead you to a different verdict.
