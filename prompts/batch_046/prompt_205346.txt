hey — writing up a chapter on decision paralysis and I keep running into a phenomenom that doesn't quite match Buridan's ass but feels adjacent.

Here's teh pattern. Someone faces two options that are genuinely symetric in every respect that matters — same payoff, same cost, no hidden leanings. They don't freeze; they break the tie with something totally arbitrary: a coin flip, picking the left-hand item, asking a random friend. Fine. But right after, they start acting like the arbitrary mechanism actually revealed a preference they already had. They build reasons why the chosen option was always slightly better, spot flaws in the rejected one they never noticed before, and talk about the choice as expressing their true self. It's not just feeling good about the outcome; it's retroactively inventing preference out of perfect indifference.

Two quick instances. A colleague picking between two citation formats for her diss — she rolls a die, then spends twenty minutes defending that format as "more readable" despite admitting they were identical. Or me at lunch yesterday: two sandwiches, I grab the closer one, then tell my friend I must have wanted turkey because I was "in a protein mood," even though I had zero preference thirty seconds earlier.

So: is there an established concept for this specific post-arbitrary-break move? I know Buridan's ass covers the paralysis before choice, and it's near rationalization, but rationalization usually needs some prior tension or discomfort. Here there was zero conflict — pure symetry, random resolution, then fabricated preference. What's the nearest named idea, how exactly does this differ, and if there's no dedicated term, can you coin one with boundary cases (e.g., does it cover reasoned-but-trivial tie-breakers, or only literally random ones)?
