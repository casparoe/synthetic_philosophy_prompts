I serve as chair of the trustee board for a national public archive that holds the complete administrative records of a state-sponsored resettlement program that displaced rural communities between 1950 and 1975. The board must vote on whether the archive’s new public charter should contain a formal acknowledgment of collective guilt—specifically, whether the institution, speaking on behalf of the present citizenry, should declare itself metaphysically and politically guilty for the harms documented in its own holdings.

The board is divided along conceptual rather than merely partisan lines. One faction, drawing on Jaspers’s distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, argues that attributing guilt to present citizens or to the archive itself confuses categories: criminal guilt requires individual perpetrators, moral guilt is non-transferable across generations, and political guilt attaches to the state apparatus that enacted the policy, not to a custodial institution that merely preserves its traces. They propose replacing “collective guilt” with “institutional custodial responsibility,” framing the archive’s obligation as forward-looking and structural rather than backward-looking and guilt-laden. The opposing faction contends that metaphysical guilt—solidarity with victims that transcends individual agency—does license a collective acknowledgment, and they have proposed extending Jaspers’s framework with a fifth category they call “archival guilt”: the specific obligation borne by institutions that physically house the evidence of harm, an obligation that is neither purely political (it does not depend on state continuity) nor purely metaphysical (it is tied to the material fact of preservation, access, and display). They argue that without this acknowledgment, the archive risks a form of epistemic violence by presenting the records as neutral artifacts.

Please draft the board’s decision memo recommending one of these positions—approve the guilt acknowledgment with precise framing, reject it in favor of custodial responsibility, or approve a hybrid—giving honest weight to the strongest categorical objection on the losing side and addressing whether “archival guilt” is a coherent addition or a category mistake. Lead with your bottom-line recommendation in the very first sentence, then lay out the reasoning; do not begin with a restatement of the problem or with preliminary remarks, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
