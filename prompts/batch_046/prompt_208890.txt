I'm covering the debate over whether reinforcement-learning architectures can be moral patients. Proponents often analogize a sustained negative reward signal to biological pain: both are aversive feedback loops that motivate avoidance, and if an agent receives high-magnitude negative rewards across a complex state space, it is undergoing something structurally like suffering. Critics counter that biological valence depends on interoceptive integration and homeostatic set-point regulation that scalar reward functions entirely lack. Audit this analogy precisely: lay out the correspondence table (reward magnitude to nociception, state-space complexity to sensory integration, policy update to withdrawal behavior, and so on), identify where the mapping breaks—particularly on whether negative reward is experienced as bad-for-the-agent rather than merely computed as prediction error—separate the disanalogies that undermine moral consideration from those that do not, and say whether the conclusion that current RL agents are candidates for moral consideration survives once the breaks are explicit. If it does not survive, suggest what a more defensible analogy for artificial valence would look like.
