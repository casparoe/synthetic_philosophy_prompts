I'm taking a graduate seminar on value pluralism and hard choices, and our instructor has asked us to advise a fictional Civic Memory Arbitration Board that resolves conflicts among incommensurable public goods—material preservation, phenomenological authenticity, dignified accessibility, and artistic autonomy—without having ever published an explicit hierarchy. The board's precedents are these: in Case A, they permitted removal of original ironwork from a historic facade to install seismic reinforcement, ruling that preservation of life outweighed material authenticity; in Case B, they blocked replacement of a deteriorating mural with a high-fidelity digital replica, holding that direct encounter with the original medium was non-substitutable; in Case C, they mandated full wheelchair accessibility for a memorial plaza despite the disruption of its intended procession geometry, treating dignified access as a threshold condition rather than a tradeable good; and in Case D, they rejected adding explanatory audio guides to an abstract sculpture garden, arguing that interpretive framing violated the artist's specified intent for ambiguous, unguided experience, even while acknowledging this limited engagement for some visitors. These four rulings are jointly consistent with more than one underlying principle.

The board now confronts Case E: a proposal to convert a decommissioned industrial water tower into a "silent observatory"—a space with no signage, no guided tours, no digital interpretation, and only minimal code-compliant accessibility modifications, designed to force an unmediated, solitary encounter with the structure's scale and decay. Proponents claim this honors aesthetic autonomy and historical rawness; opponents argue it effectively excludes visitors with cognitive and sensory disabilities, abandons the board's prior commitment to dignified access, and renders the tower's industrial history invisible without some interpretive scaffold. How should the board rule if it seeks consistency with its own track record? Identify the closest analog among Cases A through D, articulate the two or more candidate rules that could explain the board's prior verdicts, specify what each rule predicts for Case E, offer your best recommendation with reasons, state what principle the board would be adopting with each possible choice, and describe one further hypothetical case that would actually force the board to choose between those competing rules.
