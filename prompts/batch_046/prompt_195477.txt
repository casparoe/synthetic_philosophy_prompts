Consider the following judgments made by an epistemologist about whether a belief counts as knowledge in each scenario. First, direct visual perception of an object under normal conditions: yes. Second, the fake-barn case, in which one sees a genuine barn in a region filled with convincing facades: no. Third, a true belief formed by reading a stopped clock that happens to display the correct time: no. Fourth, explicit deductive inference: the subject knows premises A and B, recognizes that they entail conclusion C, and comes to believe C by working through the proof: yes. Fifth, deduction through a false but inessential intermediate: the subject knows premise A, erroneously accepts an intermediate lemma L that she takes to be necessary, derives C via L, yet C is true and in fact follows from A alone by a valid route she does not recognize; she has no independent access to C: no.

Now consider this new case. A researcher believes that a particular compound exhibits oxidation state X. She formed this belief by consulting an automated knowledge-base that aggregates primary studies and displays an explicit reasoning chain: study S reports feature F, feature F implies oxidation state X, therefore X. Unknown to her, the aggregation pipeline contains two independent algorithmic errors. The first misclassifies study S, so that the intermediate feature F is actually false—the study showed F′, which does not imply X. The second error is a numerical-approximation glitch that, purely by coincidence, maps F′ back onto X in the final output. The researcher accepts the displayed chain and relies entirely on the database conclusion; she does not inspect the intermediate classification. She also performed a direct laboratory assay that happens to confirm X, but she regards this merely as a redundant check and does not treat it as an independent evidential basis for her belief. Crucially, if oxidation state X were actually absent, the two algorithmic errors would not cancel, and the database would have returned a negative result; and her direct assay, had she relied on it, would also have shown absence.

Predict whether she knows that the compound has oxidation state X. Then identify which theoretical framework—Nozickian tracking conditions, a modal-safety requirement, a no-false-lemmas condition, epistemic closure, or some hybrid—best systematizes her five earlier judgments, noting precisely which of those frameworks diverge on this new case. Give the reasoning behind your preferred prediction, and specify a further hypothetical case, distinct from all those listed above, that would discriminate between the two leading frameworks you have identified.
