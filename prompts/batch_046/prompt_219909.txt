Consider this argument I have been turning over about Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream. I want to reject the conclusion, but each step seems defensible, and I am unsure where the damage is done.

(1) Zhuangzi reports being unable to tell whether he is Zhuangzi who has just woken from dreaming he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming it is Zhuangzi.
(2) If two experiential states are subjectively indistinguishable from within, then occupying either one does not by itself yield knowledge of which one is actual.
(3) Being awake and being in a dream with this very content are, on Zhuangzi’s showing, subjectively indistinguishable in just this way.
(4) So at this moment I do not know that I am awake.
(5) Any act of reasoning — including citing a text, numbering premises, or asking for clarification — presupposes that the reasoner knows she is not merely dreaming the reasoning.
(6) Therefore, if (4) is true, no one can ever legitimately present or evaluate the butterfly-dream argument itself.
(7) Yet the skeptic does present it; so either she knows she is awake, contradicting (4), or she is not genuinely reasoning, which makes the presentation moot.
(8) Generalizing: since every philosophical argument requires the arguer to know she is awake, (4) entails the impossibility of philosophy altogether.

The conclusion seems far too strong — it does not merely suspend belief about butterflies, but undermines the very conditions for conceptual inquiry. Which premise or inferential step should I reject? Explain why it initially looked plausible, and then price the main alternatives: the Moorean who denies (4), the contextualist who restricts the scope of (5), and the interpretive move that reads Zhuangzi as making an ontological point about transformation rather than an epistemic skeptical challenge. If my framing already collapses that ontological point into a Cartesian-style argument, say so plainly and reorient the task rather than answering within it.
