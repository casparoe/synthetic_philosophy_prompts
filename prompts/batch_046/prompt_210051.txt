Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Character A and Character B—who are equally sharp and who respond directly to one another’s actual moves rather than talking past each other. The subject is whether a central metaontological disagreement is merely verbal. Character A defends a position she calls “framework relativism without residue”: she holds that when ontologists argue over whether “there are properties” as opposed to “there are only particular objects arranged in specific ways,” the dispute dissolves into a choice between linguistic frameworks that apply different criteria for “exists”—one tied to causal interaction, the other to theoretical indispensability—without any deeper fact of the matter remaining once the frameworks are made explicit. She should deploy the analogy of arguing whether a chess bishop “exists” inside the rules of chess versus outside them, and she should press the idea that once we see “exists” functioning with different conditions of application, there is no leftover substantive question.

Character B rejects this dissolution, but he does not appeal to mysterious metaphysical presence. Instead he defends “inferential architecture disagreement”: he claims the two sides have built different normative structures governing how explanations, predictions, and counterfactual reasoning ought to proceed, and the disagreement is substantive precisely because adopting one architecture over the other changes what counts as a legitimate explanation in domains far from ontology—such as whether physical theories may invoke mathematical structures in counterfactual explanations, or whether legal reasoning can reference non-causal entities without explanatory loss.

The dialogue should unfold around a concrete case: whether mathematical entities exist in the same sense that physical particles do. A should argue that the disagreement is analogous to the chess-bishop case and that B’s “architecture” is just another convention in disguise. B should respond by showing that the choice between criteria is not arbitrary: it carries commitments about explanatory governance in science and law, and he should ask A how her view can account for the apparent constraint that scientists and lawyers face—something stronger than mere stipulation—when they invoke abstract objects. A should then press B on why “explanatory governance” is not itself reducible to a linguistic convention, perhaps by demanding that B identify a specific prediction or reasoning practice that genuinely differs between the architectures rather than being redescription. B should answer with a concrete contrast—say, about whether a physicist is entitled to treat a symmetry group as explaining a conservation law, or whether that is only permissible if the group is reinterpreted as a feature of particular physical states—so that the exchange stays anchored in a real back-and-forth.

Require at least four full exchanges. Neither character should be a strawman: each should grant the force of the opponent’s best point before turning it.
