As a member of the clinical ethics panel at a university hospital, I must circulate a draft opinion before Thursday's meeting on a withdrawal-of-care dispute. A 34-year-old patient has remained in a disorder of consciousness for eight months after anoxic injury; repeated neurobehavioral assessments and functional imaging reveal no command-following or awareness-related activity. Her family refuses discontinuation of artificial nutrition and hydration, not on religious grounds, but on an epistemological one: they argue that the panel's inference from absent behavioral response to absent subjective experience commits the same error highlighted by the problem of other minds. Because direct access to another's consciousness is impossible, they claim that behavioral silence cannot warrant treating her as lacking an inner life, and that epistemic humility therefore demands indefinite preservation. They ask the panel to formalize this limitation rather than proceed with withdrawal. Draft the panel's written opinion: it should recommend a specific, time-bound course of action (for example, honoring the refusal for a defined review period, proceeding with withdrawal subject to judicial oversight, or another concrete option), treat the family's philosophical argument seriously rather than dismissing it as outside medical jurisdiction, and honestly address the strongest consideration supporting the position you reject.
