I've been working closely with my advisor this semester on a project in moral psychology, and she's walked me through her judgments on several classic cases as we try to map her implicit framework. So far her verdicts run like this: on the Darley and Batson Good Samaritan study, where rushing seminary students passed a distressed confederate, she says this doesn't demonstrate an absence of compassion—the time pressure and competing role obligation were overwhelming situational suppressors of an otherwise present disposition; on Milgram's obedience experiments, where participants continued shocking despite protests, she says this does reveal a genuine deficit in moral courage, because an authority structure is not sufficient excuse for directly harming another, and continuation reveals a failure of character rather than mere situational influence; on a prominent philanthropist documented as generous in anonymous giving but verbally abusive to domestic staff, she denies this is evidence of the virtue of generosity at all, arguing that true virtue requires coherence between public and private domains and that selective kindness coupled with hidden cruelty indicates strategic preference or emotional gratification; on a surgeon who is extraordinarily calm with frightened patients but chronically impatient with her family, she rejects domain-specific patience, holding that the clinical composure is professional role-performance while the domestic behavior reveals the absence of a genuinely integrated disposition; and on an activist who risked imprisonment for civil rights yet routinely cheats on taxes, she says this undermines attributing global justice or integrity, since moral excellence in high-profile, identity-defining domains paired with petty dishonesty elsewhere suggests emotional investment or strategic identity rather than unified character.

Next week we are to discuss a case closer to home: a graduate student in our program who, during a sudden lab crisis involving potential data corruption and professional liability, made correct split-second ethical decisions under extreme time pressure and personal risk, but who in calm everyday interactions is consistently dismissive of junior colleagues' concerns, avoids even minor interpersonal corrections in her private relationships, and has been observed to prioritize social harmony over truthful feedback in low-stakes settings. I need to predict my advisor's verdict—does this student possess the relevant virtues, and is the crisis performance evidence of genuine character, professional role-performance, or something else? More importantly, I want to know which implicit rules best fit her pattern: perhaps a global-trait view that allows situational suppression for omissions but not for harms or private failures; a coherence-based view that treats private and everyday behavior as the true diagnostic of character; or an interactionist account that distinguishes trait-relevant situations from role-defined ones. If more than one rule accommodates her past five judgments, where exactly do they diverge on this new case, and what further hypothetical—say, a variant involving omission versus commission, or a crisis outside one's professional domain—would best settle which rule she is actually running?
