I serve on the interdisciplinary ethics and funding review panel of our university's Center for Distributed Cognition, and we are required to render a decision by the final Friday of next month on a proposal titled "Synthetic Fugue: Testing Colony-Level Mind in Distributed Robotic Systems." The principal investigator contends that her synthetic colony of modular robots replicates the organizational dynamics central to the well-known Ant Fugue thought experiment, in which the ant Aunt Hillary participates in a collective whose cognitive status remains philosophically unsettled. Specifically, the PI proposes three conditions she terms the Hillary criteria—functional caste differentiation without central control, system-wide counterfactual adjustment to localized damage, and problem-solving behaviors that resist decomposition into the intentional states of individual modules—and she argues that satisfying these conditions demonstrates that the collective functions as a unified cognitive subject rather than a mere aggregate. On that basis, she requests that the system be granted ethical clearance and funding as a single research subject with unified welfare interests, rather than being regulated as thousands of separate agents. A significant minority on the panel objects that these criteria, even if fully met, establish only sophisticated distributed computation and fail to secure the kind of subjective unity—sometimes characterized as a center of narrative gravity—that distinguishes a genuine mind from an elaborate mechanism; they further warn that misclassifying the system risks both conceptual confusion and inadequate ethical protection for the individual modules. Please draft the panel's decision memo in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, recommending either approval, approval with specific conditions, or rejection, with a concrete implementation timeline; the memo should engage the PI's argument about the Hillary criteria and the ant-colony analogy seriously, address the strongest objection concerning subjective unity and the center of narrative gravity, and explain clearly why the panel's recommendation stands despite that objection.
