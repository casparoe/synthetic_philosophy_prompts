I have been commissioned by a local historical society to write a one-act play about a community union organizer who died eighteen months ago. She left behind extensive journals in which she insisted that her private emotional life should never be invoked to explain her public commitments, arguing that such psychologizing reduces principled conviction to personal pathology. My co-writer proposes opening the play with an invented scene in which she collapses in private grief over an estranged friendship, framing her subsequent militancy as a reaction to intimate betrayal rather than to structural injustice. The society's board—several members of whom were her public adversaries—enthusiastically endorses this framing, claiming that audiences require emotional access and that biographical interpretation necessarily constructs interiority. They also observe that she left no immediate family to contest the portrayal. I feel strongly that fabricating this specific inner moment does a kind of harm, yet I cannot clearly say whether the wrong is to her, to the audience, or to historical understanding itself, and I am being told that my concern for literal accuracy is anachronistic for dramatic art. Should I resist the invented scene, and if the resistance is principled rather than sentimental, what exactly grounds it?
