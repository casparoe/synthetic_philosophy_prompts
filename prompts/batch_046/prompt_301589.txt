I am a high school student who has been reading in philosophy of value, and I want to understand why a particular debate seems permanently frozen despite widespread agreement on the central cases. The debate is between absurdists in the tradition of Camus—who hold that the human drive for meaning is structurally frustrated by an indifferent universe, yet insist that we can live with this tension through a kind of sustained revolt—and contemporary hybrid theorists of meaning in life, who claim that a life is meaningful only when it combines subjective fulfillment with objective worth, and who often cite sustained scientific discovery or moral commitment as paradigmatic examples. In my reading group, we have noticed something strange: both sides accept that a person can feel deeply fulfilled while engaged in objectively worthless pursuits, and both sides accept that objectively valuable pursuits can leave one feeling hollow. They also share the same illustrative cases—the scientist who finds no joy in her work, the artist who produces nothing of lasting value, the activist who burns out—yet they classify these cases differently, with the absurdist treating them as manifestations of the absurd and the hybridist treating them as straightforward failures of meaning. The disagreement does not appear to turn on any empirical fact about psychology or cosmology; it also does not seem to be merely verbal, since both sides use meaning-evaluative language in a normative register rather than in a purely descriptive one. My working hypothesis is that the stall is caused by a hidden meta-explanatory divergence: the absurdist demands that an account of a life provide a kind of existential reconciliation with futility, whereas the hybridist demands a causal-explanatory story linking subjective attachment to objective value, and neither side recognizes that they are applying different standards for what counts as a successful explanation of a life-evaluative judgment. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a substantive disagreement concealed beneath the surface—perhaps about whether objective value can exist independently of a valuing subject, or about whether the feeling of fulfillment is itself a kind of evidence for meaning? Alternatively, is the question ill-posed because it conflates descriptive psychology with normative evaluation, or could a precise conceptual distinction—say, between meaning-as-significance and meaning-as-coherence—actually resolve or dissolve the impasse? I am not asking for a survey of positions, for reading recommendations, or for bibliographic references; I want a structural diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, supported by what each side actually says about the shared cases, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a formal distinction, an empirical finding about human flourishing, or a reframing of the central question—could genuinely move the debate forward.
