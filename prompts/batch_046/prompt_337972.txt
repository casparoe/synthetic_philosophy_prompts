I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly longform stuff on how we think about progress and historical narratives, and I was arguing with a friend over coffee about whether you can actually teach people to resist Whig history without just giving them a lecture. She's running a workshop — about 16 people, mix of grad students and curious outsiders, two hours in one sitting — and we want an activity that makes them feel teh specific pull of writing the past as progress toward the present. Not generic presentism, but what I'm calling "present-anchor bias": the habit where we don't just misread old norms, we rearrange causation so the present becomes a hidden attractor state that every prior event was secretly orbiting.

Can you design a structured simulation or game — rules, roles, maybe hidden payoffs — where the group has to construct a collective narrative of change from 1700 to 1950? The incentives should reward coherence, moral clarity, and "relevance to now," so they naturally start compressing dead ends into necessary stepping-stones and making past actors sound like proto-liberals working toward our current norms. They shouldn't hear the term "Whig interpretation" or any named theorist until the debrief. I want them to do the building themselves, present their timeline, and only then discover they've all inserted the same retrospective inevitability.

Then include a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto parts of the idea — the causation-compression, the moral teleology in hindsight, the erasure of contingency — plus an honest note on what's predictable to go wrong when you run it with real people, and which feature of Whig history (maybe the self-congratulatory part) the game just can't reproduce and I'd have to supply verbally. No bibliography or citations, just make it runnable and keep the rules doing the work, not the facilitator's explanation.
