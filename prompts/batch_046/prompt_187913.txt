Consider the following summary of a passage from Buridan's discussion of voluntary choice: when the intellect judges two options to be exactly equal in value, the will is not compelled toward either, but neither does it possess a reason to prefer one. Rather than selecting arbitrarily, the will can suspend its act of election, leaving the agent in indecision. Buridan illustrates this with the familiar case of an ass standing between two identical bales of hay, suggesting that without a superior faculty capable of breaking the symmetry, a being governed solely by appetite might starve rather than choose.

How should I understand this in light of Buridan's distinction between liberty of contradiction and liberty of contrariety? Specifically, does the will's ability to refrain from choosing demonstrate liberty of contradiction—since it can either act or not act—while the absence of preference shows that liberty of contrariety requires some objective difference in the goods presented? And is the ass meant as a direct analog for human volition, or rather as a contrast case showing that pure appetite lacks the rational capacity for suspension?
