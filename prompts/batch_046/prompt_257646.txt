Contemporary development-aid frameworks classify states as "developed" or "developing" and make funding contingent on institutional reforms—representative elections, independent judiciaries, market liberalization—treating these arrangements as appropriate for every society regardless of local social structure. Reconstruct the implicit theory of political progress and civilizational capacity that makes this conditionality appear rational rather than arbitrary. Must it presuppose a hierarchical view of societies arranged by readiness for self-government, comparable to the framework John Stuart Mill used to justify British administration in India? Or is it compatible with a flat, universalist liberalism that denies any such stages? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say what an aid architecture built on Mill's actual commitments—his recognition that Indian village communities contained functioning indigenous governance and his insistence that outsiders should not forcibly reshape local social customs—would have to alter about its criteria and conditions.
