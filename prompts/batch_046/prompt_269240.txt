I am applying for a six-week summer philosophy fellowship open to high school seniors, and I need a two-page research proposal that turns a half-formed classroom idea into a precise, tractable project. In my ethics course we discussed the classic distinction between guilt cultures and shame cultures, and I became fixated on a question that the usual discussions seem to bypass: can a society deliberately choose which moral emotion dominates—say, by restructuring its legal punishment system, its school curricula, or its religious rituals to privilege action-oriented remorse over identity-oriented disgrace—or is the dominance of one emotion over the other fixed by deeper structural conditions such as economic organization, cosmological beliefs about the self, or linguistic categories? I want the proposal to state this question with exactness, to explain why existing anthropological descriptions and normative philosophical evaluations leave the institutional-choice problem open, and to propose a method that actually makes progress within a six-week window without requiring empirical fieldwork or a citation-heavy literature review that I cannot complete without library access.

More specifically, the proposal should identify why the standard alternatives—large-scale comparative history on one side and pure conceptual argument about which emotion is rationally superior on the other—fail to settle whether cultural engineering is possible or stable. I intend to defend a middle path: conceptual analysis of three hypothetical institutional-redesign scenarios, testing each for internal consistency and for hidden presuppositions about personhood, agency, and social recognition. The deliverable should be a framework that distinguishes features of moral emotion that are institutionally malleable from those that are structurally embedded, together with a reasoned judgment on whether a culture that successfully engineered a shift toward guilt-based regulation would be morally improved or merely differently organized in its practices of blame. The proposal must also specify what would count as failure—for instance, if the framework collapses into the trivial claim that all cultures change slowly, or if I cannot distinguish emotional dominance from mere emotional expression—and it should keep the scope realistic: conceptual work only, no bibliography or reading list requested, with references to be added later, and a final paper of roughly four thousand words. I was discussing this with a friend who studies cognitive science, and she suggested that my framing might secretly depend on a particular view of the self that I have not defended; I would like the proposal to flag that risk explicitly rather than hide it.
