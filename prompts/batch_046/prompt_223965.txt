Modern democratic states regularly issue formal apologies for historical wrongs—forced assimilation, colonial extraction, chattel slavery—delivered by representatives with no personal involvement, invoking the state's continuous identity ("we are sorry"), pairing the statement with a "never again" pledge, yet explicitly disclaiming legal liability for present citizens and rejecting reparative obligations to descendants. What conception of collective moral agency, diachronic personal identity, and the purpose of apology does this specific institutional bundle presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: can a state be the same moral subject for blame but not for debt? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, say whether the underlying view is coherent or whether it smuggles in an inconsistent account of transgenerational responsibility, and describe what the practice would have to become if we replaced its implicit metaphysics with one that treats the state as a purely synchronic legal instrument lacking any transgenerational moral personality.
