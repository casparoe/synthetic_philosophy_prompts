You are an expert philosopher of value and an experienced seminar facilitator; think carefully and begin with a brief brainstorm of three concrete ways the heterogeneity problem for hedonism manifests when people are forced to compare qualitatively different enjoyments without appealing to domain-specific qualities. I have recently become personally struck by this problem: I find myself unable to coherently rank the pleasure of solving a difficult proof against the pleasure of a long run, and I want my students to feel that same conceptual pressure rather than hear it described. Design a 75-minute classroom activity for about 14 advanced undergraduates in a philosophy of well-being seminar. The deliverable should be a structured deliberation with small real stakes—pairs jointly allocate a fixed budget of points representing welfare units across six experience cards (physical, intellectual, social, aesthetic, competitive, restorative)—governed by the rule that they may defend any allocation using only the unmodified word pleasure and lose a token if they invoke intensity, duration, nobility, or domain names. Before anyone names the heterogeneity objection, the rules should force participants into cross-domain trades that feel arbitrary, and some pairs should deadlock. Include the setup, pairing rules, payoff scheme, timeline, and a debrief that maps moments of deadlock or hidden metric-smuggling onto parts of the problem—specifically whether pleasure names a unified natural kind or only a family-resemblance cluster. Be explicit about what participants should come away seeing, be frank about which feature of the objection the activity cannot reproduce (likely the metaphysical claim that no common phenomenal property exists at all, which the facilitator must supply), and predict exactly what will go wrong the first time—such as students inventing covert scales or dismissing the framing as artificial—along with how to handle it.
