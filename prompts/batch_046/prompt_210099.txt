I have recently found myself unable to withdraw from an expensive, multi-year professional commitment that no longer serves my goals, not because the remaining costs are recoverable, but because leaving now would seem to erase the person I became while enduring it. This experience has led me to question whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Please argue that the standard economic analysis applies only to fungible resources and systematically misclassifies identity-constituting commitments—such as socially embedded obligations, narrative continuity, or the preservation of a practical self—as mere predictive errors. You should defend the position that when past investment has structured an agent's present practical identity, continuing can be rational, or even obligatory, even when forward-looking utility alone does not justify it, and that discontinuing solely on prospective grounds can constitute a form of self-alienation rather than rational correction. In doing so, distinguish sharply between errors of forecasting and legitimate loyalty to one's past choices, and address the strongest counter-objection: that any appeal to narrative identity is indistinguishable from status-quo bias or affective attachment dressed up as principle. Reach a clear verdict on whether there are conditions under which honoring sunk costs is not only permissible but required.
