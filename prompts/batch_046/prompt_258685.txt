Consider the divergence in tax-compliance architecture across liberal democracies: in some systems, revenue agencies pre-fill returns from third-party reports, treat minor discrepancies as correctable clerical errors, and rely on broad norms of voluntary cooperation; in others, taxpayers must self-declare under opaque risk-scoring audit regimes, with severe civil and criminal penalties for underpayment. What implicit answer to Glaucon's challenge—whether people are just only when they cannot escape detection, as in the myth of the ring of Gyges—does each design presuppose? Does the cooperative model assume an intrinsic moral motivation that survives invisibility, or merely that transparency makes defection detectable? Does the punitive model presuppose that, absent credible threats of punishment, citizens are naturally inclined to defect? Reconstruct the implicit moral psychology and theory of institutional legitimacy in each case; distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; and note whether either system treats the same individual as both a strategic defector and a trustworthy partner in different administrative contexts. Then ask what a tax institution built on an explicit diachronic-rationality framework—one that treats present filing behavior as shaped by expectations about one's future self—would have to alter, and whether that alteration pulls the design toward cooperation or toward suspicion.
