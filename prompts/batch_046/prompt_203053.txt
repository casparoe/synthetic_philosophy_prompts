Here's an argument I cooked up for a seminar on narrative identity and now I can't find the exit. (1) If the self is just a center of narrative gravity — the point where autobiograpical stories converge — then selfhood is entirely about where those stories cluster. (2) But stories can cluster around non-biological nodes: a family saga, a nation-state, or an AI trained on one person's diaries that keeps writing in first-person. (3) Imagine a "legacy server" that preserves not just memories but decision-rules and emotional valences, updating the narrative so smoothly that outsiders can't detect a gap. (4) If narrative continuity is sufficient, then either the dead person migrated into the server or the server became that person. (5) Yet we don't think dead people survive as hardware, and we resist saying the server is literally the same person — it lacks consciousness and embodied concern. (6) So the theory either forces information-immortality or denies identity survives even small disruptions. (7) That proves too much. Which step should I reject, and why did it seem fine? I'd like to keep (1) and (5) if possible — what do Dennett-style, phenomenological, and animalist views each have to pay for that?
