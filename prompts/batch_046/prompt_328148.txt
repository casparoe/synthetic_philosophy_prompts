At a recent interdisciplinary seminar in philosophy of history and comparative sociology, two colleagues—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—debated whether collapse is an empirical category or a normative judgment disguised as description, taking the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya southern lowlands, and Rapa Nui as their principal cases. Vasquez argued that what archaeologists call collapse is more accurately institutional metamorphosis: in the Eastern Mediterranean, palace economies dissolved into decentralized household production and iron-working networks, yet Greek, Phoenician, and Levantine linguistic and kinship structures persisted; in the Maya lowlands, monument-building and dynastic courts disappeared from the Petén, yet Maya languages, calendrical practices, and religious life continued in the northern Yucatan; and on Rapa Nui, deforestation and the end of moai transport accompanied a shift in ritual authority rather than demographic extinction or cultural discontinuity. From these observations she advanced a continuity criterion: if linguistic, religious, and kinship practices survive alongside demographic persistence, then no collapse has occurred—only a transformation that appears catastrophic solely from the bureaucratic vantage point that values centralized state capacity. Chen replied that Vasquez's criterion is descriptively empty and covertly normative: it treats biological and cultural survival as sufficient for social success, ignoring that societies frequently define themselves by specific organizational capacities—writing systems, long-distance tribute networks, monumental logistics—whose irreversible loss constitutes failure even by internal standards. He proposed instead a reversibility test: if the pre-crisis organizational form cannot be reconstructed from post-crisis institutions without substantial external input or centuries of redevelopment, then functional redundancy has been destroyed and collapse is the appropriate term. Applying this, he noted that Linear B writing vanished and was not reconstructed in Greece for centuries; the Classic Maya calendrical and dynastic apparatus collapsed in the south and was never restored there; and Rapa Nui's inter-island navigation and megalithic logistics were lost without indigenous recovery. Vasquez countered that Chen imports teleology by treating societies as having essential functions, and that his reversibility test merely encodes an archaeological preference for centralized complexity. Chen responded that Vasquez's continuity criterion is equally value-laden, privileging persistence over the distinctive forms of life that made these societies what they were, and that without reference to functional loss the concept of collapse loses all discriminating power—everything becomes metamorphosis. They asked me to referee, but I could not determine whether either framework escapes normative loading, or whether the disagreement is ultimately about facts or about which social features we deem essential. Please adjudicate point by point: where Vasquez is right about continuity and metamorphosis, where Chen is right about functional redundancy and irreversibility, and whose position is stronger overall. Specifically, address whether either framework can distinguish collapse from mere change without smuggling in normative assumptions about essential social features; identify what both parties are missing—perhaps a third criterion, a conceptual distinction they overlook, or a reason to think the debate is misframed; and say whether there is any coherent sense in which a society both survives and collapses.
