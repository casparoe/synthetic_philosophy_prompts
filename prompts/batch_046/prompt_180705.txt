I'm drafting assessment guidelines for a state forensic psychology board, and I've been asked to translate the compatibilist account of moral responsibility into a structured, reusable evaluation protocol. The examiners need to determine whether a defendant's criminal conduct was "free enough" to support culpability—particularly in cases involving addiction or compulsive behavior—without asking clinicians to adjudicate metaphysical determinism directly. Rather than a generic symptom checklist, the board wants a procedure that actually operationalizes the philosophical distinction between sourcehood and responsiveness-to-reasons, converting those conditions into observable criteria that can be evaluated in roughly ninety minutes. To prevent the framework from collapsing into a standard clinical rubric, I've introduced a third filter I'm calling "counterfactual stability of the agent's value hierarchy": whether the defendant's core evaluative commitments would have persisted under a minimal counterfactual perturbation, such as a brief delay, an altered social cue, or a modest increase in the cost of acting. I need this written in American English.

Please construct the step-by-step decision procedure: the ordered questions the examiner must ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stopping rules that yield a negative determination, and clearly marked points where the examiner must make a non-delegable value judgment rather than a factual finding. The protocol should specify which distinctions must survive simplification—such as the difference between acting from one's own reasons and merely being caused by them—and which can be set aside. Then apply the procedure to two hypothetical defendants: first, an individual with severe alcohol use disorder who committed theft to obtain alcohol immediately following a brief but genuine period of sobriety planning; second, an individual with obsessive-compulsive disorder who performed a physically harmful ritualistic act that they describe as alien to their values but necessary to avert catastrophic anxiety. Include at least one case where the procedure produces a result the board will find uncomfortable, and be explicit about what the protocol cannot do—for instance, whether determinism globally undermines responsibility, whether the defendant "deserves" punishment in a retributive sense, or whether the presence of a mental disorder automatically excuses the conduct.
