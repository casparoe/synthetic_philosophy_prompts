You direct a small foundation that awards annual “Integrity in Practice” citations to professionals—engineers, nurses, civil servants, auditors—who performed actions that were ethically costly. You receive nomination packets containing the nominee’s own account, witness statements, and institutional records. Your board wants a reusable decision procedure to determine not whether the action was correct, but whether it carried moral worth: whether the agent acted for the right reasons rather than merely in accordance with morality from inclination, self-interest, or accident. The board has no patience for metaphysics; they need a step-by-step protocol a non-philosopher can run in about thirty minutes per packet, with explicit stopping rules, points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and an honest flag for cases in which the philosophical distinctions collapse under practical scrutiny.

Design the procedure. Ground it in the distinction between motivating reasons and explanatory reasons, the counterfactual stability of a moral motive (would the agent still have acted if inclination and self-interest pointed the other way?), and the problem of overdetermination (whether the presence of a non-moral motive eliminates moral worth or merely accompanies it). The deliverable should include:

1. A preliminary filter that eliminates nominations in which no ethically significant choice was present.
2. A sequence of tests, in order, with for each: what counts as passing or failing evidence in the packet, what ambiguity looks like, and a reliability warning where the test is likely to misfire.
3. A decision matrix mapping test results onto four outcomes: full moral worth; correct action with ambiguous or partial worth; correct action without moral worth; and indeterminate.
4. Explicit “judgment nodes”—steps at which the procedure stops and requires the board to make a value call rather than apply a rule.
5. A brief note on structural limitations: two kinds of case or evidence for which the procedure should not be used at all.

Then apply the procedure to three nomination packets:

A. A structural engineer refused to certify a bridge design she judged unsafe, knowing it would destroy her career at the firm. In her statement she says she acted because “the public has a right not to be killed by negligence.” Internal emails, however, show she had been negotiating an exit to a competitor for months, and this refusal accelerated her departure to a substantially higher-paying position.
B. A nurse manually ventilated patients through three consecutive nights during a hospital power outage. She describes feeling “called to care for the vulnerable,” but payroll records confirm she received triple overtime, and a colleague’s statement says she complained repeatedly that the pay was not worth the exhaustion.
C. A whistleblower reported long-concealed environmental contamination. He has years of documented public advocacy for stricter regulation, but private messages reveal he stood to inherit property that would plummet in value if the contamination were covered up; in his interview he admits, “I would have done it anyway, but I’m not sorry about the inheritance.”

For each case, run the procedure step by step, state the outcome, and flag any step where the protocol’s answer conflicts with an intuitive assessment of the agent’s character. Finally, identify two structural limitations—types of nomination or forms of evidence for which the board should suspend the protocol and decide by direct deliberation rather than by checklist.
