You serve on your country's National Policy Evaluation Council. The Minister of Social Development has proposed that the government formally adopt a ten-year strategic objective: to rank in the top five of the annual World Happiness Report, and to restructure domestic well-being policy evaluation around the report's core metric—the Cantril Self-Anchoring Striving Scale—rather than around composite indices of material conditions or capabilities. The Minister argues that subjective life evaluation is what ultimately matters, and that the report's explanatory framework (GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, and corruption perceptions) gives clear policy levers. Opponents on the Council argue that the ladder measures evaluative life satisfaction rather than happiness proper, that cross-national comparison is distorted by linguistic framing and cultural response styles, and that making the rank an explicit target invites Goodhart-style distortion: agencies might optimize for reported scale values rather than for welfare, especially since the report partly explains rankings through variables like generosity that can be influenced by survey framing. There is also concern that the report's reliance on global retrospective evaluation misses experienced affect and adaptive preference formation—people in severely constrained circumstances sometimes report high satisfaction precisely because expectations have adjusted downward. Draft the Council's decision memo recommending whether to approve the proposal, reject it, or approve it with conditions. Your memo should take a clear position, address whether the Cantril ladder genuinely measures a construct a state ought to maximize, consider whether cross-national ranking is methodologically sound enough to serve as a policy target, and honestly present the strongest argument for the side you reject—including the risk that abandoning subjective metrics altogether cedes well-being policy to purely economic indicators.
