Consider a reconstructed track record of Albert Camus's judgments on capital punishment, assembled from his well-known essay opposing the death penalty and from his public positions during the post-war purges in France. Across four cases, his verdict is consistently against execution, though the explicit reasoning shifts slightly. Case one: a common murderer convicted after killing in a crime of passion; Camus argues that execution is a premeditated murder committed by the state and violates the limit that rebellion itself places on violence. Case two: a political assassin executed for killing a government minister; he holds that execution transforms the assassin into a martyr and that justice must not mirror the violence it condemns. Case three: a wartime collaborator convicted of actions that led directly to the deaths of resistance members; he refuses to endorse execution even for grave moral crimes, insisting that long imprisonment preserves both judgment and the possibility of human change. Case four: an accidental killer convicted under a strict-liability statute that carries death; he objects that execution is irrevocable and therefore disproportionate regardless of culpability.

Now consider a new penal proposal: a jurisdiction introduces "permanent civic nullification." The subject is a perpetrator of a mass-casualty attack who, after decades of imprisonment, has shown documented remorse and engaged in rehabilitative work. Rather than being executed, he is permanently stripped of all legal personality and social recognition, confined in isolation, and excluded from all human community. The status is technically revocable only by a unanimous decision of a future international ethical body that does not yet exist, making it practically irrevocable; the body remains physically alive and is not killed by the state.

If one aims to render a verdict consistent with Camus's four earlier judgments, what should the conclusion be about civic nullification? Identify the closest analog among the four cases and justify the choice. Then articulate the competing principles that could jointly explain the four negative verdicts—such as an absolute prohibition on state killing, a prohibition on irrevocable punishment, and a prohibition on measures that deny the human capacity for change—and state what each principle predicts about nullification: does it count as acceptable, as execution by another name, or as worse than execution? Give your best-guess recommendation with the reasoning for preferring one principle, and specify what further principle is being adopted if the verdict is acceptance versus rejection. Finally, name a single further case—perhaps a terminally ill prisoner who voluntarily requests execution, or a form of temporary, fully revocable exile—that would distinguish the winning principle from its closest rival.
