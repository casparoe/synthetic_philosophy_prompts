Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a phenomenal uniformist and a functional heterogeneity pluralist on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—specifically, on whether all pleasures share a common phenomenal quality or whether they form a heterogeneous class unified only by functional or intentional features.

Structure the output as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should include shared commitments about the diversity of pleasure-types (gustatory, intellectual, aesthetic, malicious, relief-based), about the inadequacy of simple intensity-based or location-based unifications, about the unreliability of naive introspective comparison across modalities, and about the need for an explanation of why these disparate states are all called "pleasures."

2. A disagreements table. For each camp, provide the precise formulation of its position phrased in wording that its own proponents would accept. The uniformist should defend the existence of a single, introspectively accessible phenomenal quality—call it Q—present in every pleasure, not reducible to liking, arousal, or satisfaction. The pluralist should defend that pleasure is a family-resemblance or functional-kind category lacking any shared phenomenal substrate, and that attempts to isolate Q confuse attentional salience or linguistic convention with qualitative identity. Include also the exact points where they part ways on whether cross-modal phenomenal comparison is coherent and on how to interpret dissociation cases.

3. The cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I take the central cruxes to be: (a) the cross-modal attenuation problem—whether there is a phenomenal comparison point that survives attenuation across sensory, cognitive, and emotional domains, such that one can rank the "pleasure" of a mathematical insight against that of a cool breeze without invoking non-phenomenal criteria; (b) the linguistic-practice crux—whether first-person reports of "this feels good" across disparate domains track a shared property or merely a shared evaluative language game; and (c) the dissociation crux—whether cases of anhedonia with preserved motivation, or of motivated pleasure in harm (schadenfreude lacking warmth or relaxation), genuinely separate the putative phenomenal quality Q from functional role, and what each separation implies for the opposing view.

4. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or hypothetical evidence that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's advance prediction. For example, both should accept the relevance of a blinded cross-modal matching task in which subjects judge phenomenal similarity between pleasures drawn from different domains using only introspective comparison. The uniformist should predict that subjects will exhibit a non-random clustering consistent with detection of Q, even if attenuated; the pluralist should predict that clustering will track functional or semantic similarity rather than any shared phenomenal mark. Likewise, both should address the case of intense schadenfreude reported as pleasant but lacking relaxation: the uniformist should predict that closer phenomenological analysis reveals Q in a masked or cognitively mediated form, while the pluralist should predict that analysis reveals only a complex evaluative attitude with no residual shared feel.

Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for a collaborative position paper. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts or authors. Ensure that formulations are neither vacuous nor slanted, and distinguish substantive disagreements from merely verbal ones (for instance, whether "feeling" is being used in a technical phenomenological sense versus an ordinary-language sense).
