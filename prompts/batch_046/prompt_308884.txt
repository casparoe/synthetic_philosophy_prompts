Recently I have been forced to decide whether to complete an expensive, multi-year professional certification that I have come to deeply dislike, which has made the logic of sunk costs acutely personal rather than merely academic. I overheard an extended argument between two colleagues—Maya and Elias—about the underlying principle, and I report it below as an uninvolved bystander.

Maya began with a strict, forward-looking claim: rational decision-making must weigh only future costs and benefits, so allowing unrecoverable past expenditures to influence present choice is always an error in reasoning—the sunk-cost fallacy—no matter whether the domain is financial, professional, or relational.

Elias responded with a structural counterexample. He asked Maya to consider a founder who has spent a decade building a cooperative enterprise that now runs at a persistent loss. Closing it would recover capital but would also dissolve the network of mutual obligations and shared practical identity that the original investment made possible. The past expenditure, he argued, did not merely create future benefits; it established present commitments that carry normative weight independently of any payoff.

Maya granted that abandonment might carry hidden future costs—reputational damage, broken trust, or psychological regret—but insisted that if one genuinely strips away all such forward-looking considerations, persisting is nothing more than sentimentality. What people call loyalty to past commitments, she said, is typically an aversion to admitting error, misdescribed as virtue.

Elias then sharpened his position by appealing to constitutive reasons. In some roles—he mentioned long-term caregiving or collective stewardship—the commitment partly defines the agent's practical identity. To evaluate whether to continue purely by weighing future utility, he argued, is already to misunderstand the rational structure of the commitment; its value is partly constituted by not being subjected to instrumental calculation. Therefore, applying the sunk-cost framework here begs the question against non-instrumental rationality.

Maya countered that even internal coherence, moral self-image, and psychological continuity are themselves items in a forward-looking calculus, since they determine future states of the agent. Once all consequences—including internal ones—are incorporated, evaluation remains future-directed, and the fallacy stands: people systematically overvalue continuity because they confuse identity-maintenance with the avoidance of regret.

They reached an impasse. I want an adjudication rather than a synthesis. Specifically: is Elias correct that there exist genuinely non-instrumental commitments for which the sunk-cost label is conceptually inappropriate, or does Maya's reduction to an expanded forward-looking evaluation—including internal states—absorb those cases without residue? What specific point is each party right about, and what, if anything, are both missing regarding the conditions under which honoring past investment genuinely alters the rational calculus rather than distorting it?
