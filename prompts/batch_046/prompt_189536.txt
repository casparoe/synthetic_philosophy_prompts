In my philosophy of knowledge seminar, discussion of Gettier-style counterexamples has settled into an impasse that feels structurally different from ordinary theoretical disagreement. We all accept the canonical structure—an agent forms a justified true belief through reasoning that depends on a false premise, yet the belief happens to be true for reasons unrelated to that reasoning—and we all agree that such beliefs fall short of knowledge. The fracture appears over what the shortfall actually consists in. One faction, working with virtue-theoretic commitments, maintains that the agent lacks knowledge because she does not merit epistemic credit: her cognitive success is not properly attributable to her competence but to environmental luck. Another faction, operating within a modal framework, contends that the belief fails because it is neither safe nor sensitive—across nearby possible worlds it is false—irrespective of whether the agent exercised skill. Both camps render identical verdicts on textbook cases, yet they diverge on constructed variants, such as a radiologist who correctly diagnoses a tumor because a hidden software artifact in the imaging system happens to cancel out her misinterpretation of the actual scan. The virtue-oriented participant tends to ask whether her interpretive competence causally produced the correct result; the modal-oriented participant tends to ask whether the belief remains true in sufficiently close worlds where the artifact behaves differently.

What I find puzzling is that this disagreement has persisted for months without movement, despite full agreement on the formal and psychological properties of these variants. I am trying to diagnose whether this is a substantive dispute about the nature of knowledge, a verbal disagreement over whether the word "knowledge" ought to track epistemic credit or modal robustness, or rather two groups answering different questions—one about the normative standing of the believer, one about the metaphysical stability of the belief—under a single heading. Is there a concealed empirical or formal issue doing the hidden work, perhaps about how we individuate cognitive processes versus belief-forming environments, or are both sides anchored in bedrock intuitions about what makes belief valuable that neither can argue for? Given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction, a redesigned thought experiment, or a formal result that could genuinely advance the debate, or should we treat the impasse as permanent because the underlying commitments are incommensurable? I want a structural diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of what, if anything, would count as progress.
