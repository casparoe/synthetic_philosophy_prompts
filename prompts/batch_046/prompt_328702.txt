I am a PhD candidate in metaphysics and philosophy of art, and I serve on my university museum’s Acquisition and Integrity Committee. We must decide whether to approve the donation of a proposed contemporary bio-sculpture constructed from a metabolizing bacterial-cellulose substrate: according to the artist’s maintenance protocol, the living material degrades and is fully replaced with chemically identical but numerically distinct matter every eight to ten years, while the macroscopic form and internal structural organization are preserved by design. Our charter requires that acquired works maintain substantial material continuity with the artist’s original execution, yet the donor seeks tax-credit valuation tied specifically to the identity of the “original” object. Half the committee treats this as routine part replacement—no different in principle from repairing or re-casting clay—while the other half insists that total biological turnover dissolves material identity, so that each replacement produces a numerically distinct artifact rather than the same persisting work.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in ontology and the philosophy of art. Before drafting, think carefully and begin with brief brainstorming on the key distinctions that bear on the case: persistence conditions for artworks, essential versus accidental parts, whether form or matter grounds identity in artifacts, and how the classic statue-and-clay framework applies when the “clay” is living tissue that metabolizes rather than inert material that merely erodes. Then draft a formal decision memo addressed to the committee, recommending approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions. The memo should present clear reasoning that both the donor and the skeptical committee members could actually respond to, include an honest and fair treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and avoid requesting bibliographies, citations, or external reading lists. Write in a polished, institutional voice suitable for a university review board, with an explicit actionable bottom line.
