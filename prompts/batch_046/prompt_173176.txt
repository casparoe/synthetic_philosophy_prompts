I'm delivering the opening remarks at the retirement symposium for Dr. Elena Vasquez, a historian whose career has been defined by counter-hagiography—biographies that systematically resist narrative smoothing and moral exemplification. The audience is split between her academic colleagues, who will catch every allusion to Plutarch's Parallel Lives and to the modern critique of hagiography, and former students now teaching high school humanities, for whom this needs to land without jargon. I want a ten-minute address that treats the moral use of biography as a live conceptual problem, not decorative background. The structural frame I'm proposing—call it calibrated disanalogy—is that biography teaches not through imitation or rule-extraction, but by forcing us to map precisely where a subject's incentives, character, and circumstances fail to align with ours; the gap, not the overlap, is where reasoning happens. This should do real structural work: it explains why honoring Elena requires refusing to turn her (or her subjects) into exemplary figures, avoiding the reference-class error of treating exceptional lives as representative moral templates, which is precisely the failure mode her scholarship avoids. Please keep the distinction sharp between what is historically settled—Plutarch's comparative pairing, the pedagogical function of hagiography—and what remains genuinely contested, including whether disanalogy rather than analogy is the primary moral mechanism in reading lives. Do not present calibrated disanalogy as established consensus; mark it explicitly as the speculative framing I'm testing. The tone should be formal, intellectually precise, and fully accessible to the non-specialists present.
