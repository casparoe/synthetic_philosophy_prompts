I have been working with my university registrar to redesign the automated taxonomy for cross-listed courses, and the classification algorithm has begun to look philosophically puzzling. The system assigns every course to exactly one distribution bucket—Humanities, Natural Sciences, Social Sciences, or Formal Reasoning—using a two-step rule: first, identify the root discipline named in the course title; second, check whether an "of X" modifier points to a field outside that root. If it does, the course is reassigned to X's bucket. So Philosophy of Biology and Philosophy of Mathematics are routed to Natural Sciences and Formal Reasoning, respectively, even when taught by philosophy faculty with no empirical or quantitative component. By contrast, History of Science remains in Humanities, Mathematics for Biologists stays in Mathematics, and no reverse case—say, Biology of Philosophy—exists to test the symmetry. The modifier seems to dissolve the root category in some disciplines but leave it intact in others, and I cannot tell whether this is a pragmatic administrative compromise or the symptom of a hidden ontology.

I was discussing this with a friend who works in comparative logic, and she suggested the pattern resembles an institutional version of the old categorization problem raised in the white horse dialogue associated with the School of Names: if you combine a kind term with an additional determination—color, or in our case a disciplinary modifier—have you named the original kind or an entirely new reality? Does our registrar implicitly treat philosophy-of-biology as a distinct reality from philosophy in the same way that dialogue asks whether white-horse is distinct from horse? Or is the asymmetry—why History survives its modifiers while Philosophy does not—better explained by a conventionalist view of institutional boundaries, where the name merely tracks administrative convenience rather than metaphysical kind?

What philosophical commitments about universals, properties, and the relation between names and realities does this tagging practice strictly presuppose, and where is it merely underdetermined? Reconstruct the implicit theory of categorization that makes the current rules seem natural, distinguish what the practice requires from what happens to fit it well, and assess whether different parts of the system—say, the treatment of History versus Philosophy, or the museum database's parallel rule that keeps White Marble Bust under Sculpture rather than Mineralogy—presuppose consistent views. Then tell me what would have to change in the registrar's rules if we replaced this implicit ontology with a straightforwardly extensional account, where a course belongs to every distribution whose defining conditions it satisfies, and indicate which of our current administrative distinctions would collapse under that replacement.
