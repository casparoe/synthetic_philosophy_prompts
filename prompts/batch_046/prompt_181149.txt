As a novelist presently drafting a philosophical essay for a literary quarterly, I should be grateful for your comments upon the following notes and tentative line of argument. I wish to explore whether cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions—particularly concerning moral responsibility and personal identity over time—might be illuminated not solely through the usual anthropological or psychological lenses, but through the formal properties of narrative itself. The empirical observation, familiar enough, is that respondents from different linguistic and cultural backgrounds diverge systematically on thought-experiments involving free will and causal determinism. Rather than treating these divergences as direct windows onto competing metaphysical doctrines, I propose that they correlate with deep structural preferences in storytelling: cultures favouring linear, agent-driven plots with singular climactic resolution tend to elicit stronger compatibilist or libertarian intuitions about choice, whereas cultures whose traditions favour cyclical, distributed, or collective narrative architectures tend to weaken or redistribute intuitions about individual agency. I then ask whether such patterns suggest that at least some portion of what philosophers classify as intuitive disagreement is in fact an aesthetic disagreement about the coherence of a life-story, disguised in metaphysical vocabulary. I should welcome your assessment of three points in particular. First, does this framing misrepresent the actual findings of cross-cultural experimental philosophy, or does it remain within the bounds of what a non-specialist might reasonably infer? Second, is the analogy between narrative form and metaphysical intuition philosophically substantial, or does it collapse into a mere metaphor once pressed? Third, I reach the tentative conclusion that if narrative architecture genuinely shapes these intuitions, then philosophical argument alone may be insufficient to resolve them; one might instead need a form of aesthetic reconciliation. Is this conclusion warranted, or does it overreach? I ask only for conceptual and argumentative feedback—no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations are required, and I should prefer your remarks to remain accessible without recourse to specialised archives.
