Exam question: Some philosophers defend the Dissolution Thesis—that every apparent moral conflict can be resolved by applying a correct hierarchical ordering of obligations, leaving no genuine dilemma but only errors in ordering. Critics reply with the Residue Argument: even after one acts for the best, an appropriate normative and emotional remainder (agent-regret, a surviving claim from the overridden obligation) shows the conflict was real. Now consider a refined challenge: suppose the ordering principle is not merely unknown to the agent but structurally indeterminate—there is no fact of the matter about which obligation ranks higher. Does this revive the Residue Argument, or does it reduce the conflict to an epistemic gap? Evaluate, paying particular attention to whether agent-regret can be rationally appropriate when the conflict stems from structural indeterminacy rather than from a known but painful trade-off.

Student answer:

"The Dissolution Thesis holds that a single hierarchical principle resolves all moral conflicts, so genuine dilemmas are impossible; apparent conflicts are just failures to apply the ordering correctly. The Residue Argument, used by defenders of dilemmas, claims that after correct choice we still feel agent-regret and recognize that the overridden obligation retains a claim on us—this moral remainder proves the conflict was real. I believe the Residue Argument succeeds only if we reject every hierarchical principle; if any principle exists, even an unknown one, then one duty genuinely cancels the other and no remainder can survive. Turning to structural indeterminacy—the case where there is no fact about ranking—I think this actually undermines the Residue Argument. If the ordering is indeterminate, there is no 'correct choice,' and agent-regret is irrational because agent-regret presupposes that the agent knowingly violated a determinately stronger duty. What remains is purely epistemic regret about our inability to discern the principle. Nevertheless, structural indeterminacy does show that dilemmas are ontologically real, since both obligations remain unranked. So the result is split: dilemmas exist, but they do not generate agent-regret; they generate only cognitive frustration. Thus the refined challenge saves the ontology of dilemmas while dissolving the phenomenological residue."
