Ok so I've been playing around with this idea that care ethics, taken seriously, forces us to drop the idea that moral status is an intrinsic property of agents altogether. Instead obligations track what I'm calling "vulnerability-proximity"—basically a non-transitive gradient where your duty to X depends on how embedded you are in the specific dependancy relation with X, not on X's capacities or on any universal principle. I think this means care ethics isn't just a supplement to justice frameworks but actually undermines them at the root, since justice assumes you can abstract away from particuar relations and still have obligations.

But I want to stress-test it. What would have to be true for this vulnerability-proximity view to be wrong? Not just the standard critiques—like that it collapses into nepotism or that it's parochial—but which specific intuitions about distant strangers, which instutional design problems, and which results from other areas (say, about moral status in bioethics or about the logic of obligations) it stands or falls with? Would a committed care ethicist concede that the failure of transitivity is fatal, or could they absorb that? And is there any observation or argument that would actually kill the view rather than just make it awkward? Rank the defeaters for me, fatal to merely annoying. Also, I personally find the distant-stranger objection kind of weak—what would actually move me is if it turned out that relational embeddedness can't do the normative work I'm asking it to do without smuggling in capacity-based criteria.
