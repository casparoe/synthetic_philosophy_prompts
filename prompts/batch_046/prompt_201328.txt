I'm studying the dynamics of peer disagreement in epistemology, particularly the space between conciliationism and steadfastness, and I've run into a recurring maneuver that doesn't seem to fit either category cleanly. Consider two philosophers with identical training and evidence access who disagree about whether a particular theory of consciousness applies to non-human animals. Rather than lowering her confidence in the theory or defending its universal scope, one party responds that the theory was always intended only for human phenomenology, and that her peer's animal counterexamples therefore miss the target entirely. Yet in other discussions where no peer is challenging her, the same philosopher applies the theory broadly to animals without hesitation. A similar pattern appears in economic methodology: when confronted with data that a market model fails in developing economies, a defender will carve out a domain restriction—the model holds only for mature markets—but will then invoke the model's universal validity in non-adversarial settings. In both cases, the boundary is drawn precisely where the counterevidence sits, not by any independently motivated principle.

What strikes me is that this allows the agent to appear conciliatory—she concedes the particular case—while remaining fully steadfast about the underlying claim, because she denies that there was ever a genuine disagreement about the core theory. The restriction is not a pre-existing auxiliary hypothesis that was always part of the framework; it is generated post-hoc by the location of peer objection, and it shifts if the disagreement moves. Is there an established name for this specific configuration? If so, how exactly does it differ from an ordinary scope restriction, from an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis, and from defensive maneuvers like the motte-and-bailey, where one retreats to a genuinely defensible core? If there is no dedicated concept, I would like a precise coinage with a definition that captures its distinctive feature: that the domain boundary is not independently justified but is produced by the geography of disagreement itself, allowing simultaneous apparent conciliation and substantive steadfastness.

Please answer in American English. I am not asking for citations, a reading list, or bibliographic references—only the conceptual carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges lie, and whether the nearest existing labels fit well enough to borrow them.
