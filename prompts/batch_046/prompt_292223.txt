I'm working on a chapter for my dissertation in philosophy of medicine, specifically on whether informed consent is best understood as a single normative threshold or as a cluster of independent conditions—epistemic, volitional, and temporal—that can fail separately. To test my own commitments, I've been collecting intuitive verdicts on concrete cases, but one cluster remains resistant to systematization, and I want to see whether a coherent principle can accommodate them all or whether I must abandon one.

Here are my judgments. In a psychiatric research protocol, a principal investigator describes a study drug using technically accurate language drawn from regulatory labeling, but deliberately sequences the disclosure so that the rare but serious adverse event is embedded in the middle of a dense paragraph on pharmacokinetics, knowing that participants with limited health literacy will miss it. I think consent here is defective—not fraudulent in the manner of an outright lie, but still failing an epistemic condition. In an emergency department, a family is asked to consent to a craniotomy for their unconscious relative and is told, truthfully, that delay means near-certain death while immediate surgery offers a reasonable chance; they agree in under two minutes. I think this is fully valid consent, despite the extreme temporal pressure, because the urgency is natural rather than manufactured. In a corporate wellness program, employees are told that participation—including biometric screening—is entirely voluntary, but internal evaluation metrics (unknown to them) tie promotion eligibility to participation rates. They sign the form. I think this is invalid, though I cannot tell whether the defect is deception, structural coercion, or both acting together.

Then there are competence and temporal alignment cases. A patient with bipolar I disorder, currently in remission and with full decision-making capacity, signs an advance psychiatric directive authorizing specific interventions should she enter a manic episode and refuse treatment. I think this is valid, provided the directive is sufficiently specific. But consider a patient with early Alzheimer's who has pronounced good days and bad days. On a good day she consents to a living will that activates only during future bad days. I keep oscillating: part of me thinks this is valid because she is competent now and directing her future care; part of me thinks consent must apply to the present decision-making self, and authorizing actions over a future incompetent self is not consent but something closer to delegation or even an abandonment of present autonomy.

There's also the case I keep returning to: a pediatrician tells parents that a particular vaccine is safe and recommended, which is true in the regulatory sense, but omits mention of a one-in-fifty-thousand adverse event that she knows the parents, if informed, would find decisive. The parents consent. Is this omission on a par with the researcher's strategic sequencing, or is it closer to acceptable clinical simplification? And if I say it is invalid, do I have to condemn every case where a physician tailors disclosure to avoid overwhelming the patient?

So my questions are these. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing epistemic manipulation from volitional interference, and both from temporal misalignment—that captures all of these verdicts? Which of my judgments is the outlier that should be dropped in order to preserve simplicity? And what does your best principle predict for a case I have not mentioned: a competent adult consents to an experimental procedure after being told all material risks in plain language, but the researcher knows the subject is participating primarily because she is romantically involved with the principal investigator and wishes to please him? Is the consent valid, and does the answer depend on whether the researcher is aware of that romantic motivation?
