I'm preparing a forty-five-minute museum lecture for an educated general audience—no background in history of science—on Johannes Kepler's relationship to astrology, and I want to avoid the familiar caricature that he was a modern astronomer who simply mocked superstition. Instead, I want to teach the more precise conceptual distinction he maintained between mathematical astronomy and what he called celestial physics: the claim that planetary bodies exert genuine but non-deterministic physical influences on terrestrial phenomena—especially weather patterns and human temperament—through causal mechanisms he analogized to magnetism, rather than through the deterministic fate-prophecy of popular horoscopes. The lecture should build around his horoscope practice for figures such as Albrecht von Wallenstein, using it not as a failed prediction but as a concrete case that shows how Kepler interpreted celestial configurations as indicators of probable tendencies and temperamental dispositions without claiming inevitability. Plan the explanatory sequence: the dependency order in which the ideas must be introduced so that the audience understands why Kepler could reject deterministic astrology while still practicing a form of celestial interpretation; the specific feature the Wallenstein example must possess and the feature it must lack if it is to carry this distinction rather than collapse back into fortune-telling; the misconceptions the audience will predictably arrive with or form during the talk—such as the belief that science and astrology were already separated in his era, that Kepler dismissed all astrological influence, that his laws of planetary motion contradicted his astrological commitments, or that celestial influence necessarily equals destiny—and the precise point in the arc where each misconception should be headed off; the check I can use at the end to determine whether they have grasped the difference between a probabilistic physical correlation and a deterministic prophetic claim, rather than merely recalling that Kepler cast horoscopes; and what I should explicitly leave out at this level—such as the technical mathematics of his harmonic cosmology, the full structure of astrological houses, or modern statistical critiques of astrological correlation—while saying so openly. Keep the plan formal and structured, suitable for a journalist constructing an explanatory feature rather than delivering the lecture itself.
