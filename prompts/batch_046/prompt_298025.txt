Exam prompt: In his later philosophical essays, especially those shaped by Advaita Vedanta, Erwin Schrödinger defended what he called the "unity of consciousness." Explain how he moves from Vedantic metaphysics—particularly the relation between Atman and Brahman and the concept of maya—to a claim about quantum mechanics, specifically about entanglement and the observer-system boundary. Then address two further issues: first, whether he takes this unity to resolve the measurement problem, and if so, by what mechanism; second, whether he believes this view supports any form of personal immortality or survival of individual identity after death. Be precise about the difference between a universal, undifferentiated consciousness and the persistence of a personal self.

Student answer:
Schrödinger draws on the Upanishads to argue that the multiplicity of individual minds is maya, an illusion concealing one undivided consciousness that corresponds to nirguna Brahman. He treats quantum entanglement as a physical parallel: just as entangled particles are described by a single state vector regardless of spatial separation, so too are apparently separate observers ultimately expressions of one Atman. From this he concludes that the measurement problem is dissolved rather than solved—there is no genuine collapse, because the distinction between observer and observed is itself part of the illusion of multiplicity, and what appears as collapse is actually the universal consciousness recognizing its own previously hidden unified state. Finally, he takes this to entail a qualified personal immortality: since the individual self is ultimately identical with Brahman, death is not annihilation but the return of one's personal Atman to the universal source, preserving individual identity in a transformed, non-bodily mode.
