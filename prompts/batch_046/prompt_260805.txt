I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a friend about Schlick’s view that life acquires meaning only through playful, autotelic engagement rather than through purpose-driven labor. We kept returning to a scenario that splits the difference: imagine an activity that is internally structured exactly like Schlickian play—voluntary, rule-governed, absorbing, and pursued for its own sake—yet sustained only because an external algorithmic reward mechanism makes it instrumentally necessary for the agent’s survival or social standing. My friend insists this collapses Schlick’s distinction; I think it actually clarifies it, but I want students to work it out themselves rather than recite the thesis. Please draft one essay exam question for an upper-level philosophy course, answerable in 90 minutes, that requires students to reconstruct Schlick’s argument for the meaning-conferring nature of play, apply it precisely to this hybrid scenario, and defend a substantive position on whether the distinction survives. Give me a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points: state each point, the marks it carries (out of 20 total), and a single line describing how candidates typically miss it. The question should not ask for citations or bibliographies, and it should test whether a student can do more than summarize Schlick’s well-known thesis.
