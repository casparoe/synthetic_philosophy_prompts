I'm a journalist who covers the philosophy of science and the history of archives, and over the past few months I've found myself returning to the same tension in seminars and conversations with historians: Ranke's old ambition to reconstruct events as they actually were versus the scepticism of Carr and Hayden White, who argue that historical writing is always shaped by narrative emplotment and prior ideological commitments. Most treatments of this debate treat it as settled or cyclical, but I think there's an open conceptual question hidden inside it—one that hasn't been properly framed because neither side has seriously engaged with modern archival science's account of how records are produced, selected, and preserved.

My half-formed idea is to inject the concept of provenance-layer contamination—borrowed loosely from data forensics and digital preservation—into the debate. If we treat an archive not as a transparent window onto past events but as a layered artifact shaped by bureaucratic selection criteria, preservation budgets, and cataloguing logic, then Ranke's ideal seems to face a new kind of challenge: not just that historians narrate, but that the sources themselves carry structural biases that may be indistinguishable from the historian's own emplotment. At the same time, I want to ask whether this contamination is uniformly destructive, or whether some layers are correctable in ways that would preserve a modified Rankean objectivity while accepting White's critique of naive transparency.

I'm pitching this as a proposal for a six-month interdisciplinary research fellowship—something between a focused dissertation prospectus and a workshop grant—aimed at scholars in philosophy of history and archival studies. I need the proposal to contain: a precise statement of the question (does provenance-layer contamination constitute an in-principle barrier to Rankean reconstruction, or a surmountable methodological obstacle that sharpens rather than dissolves the ideal?); an explanation of why existing positions leave this open, specifically that Rankeans tend to assume unmediated access to sources while White treats all mediation as narratively homogeneous; the actual method I would pursue, perhaps a conceptual framework for distinguishing correctable selection bias from irreducible emplotment applied to a single case study of administrative records; a realistic month-by-month plan; what a successful result would look like versus what would count as failure; and an honest assessment of scope risk given the six-month window. The deliverable should be useful to people actually deciding whether to fund philosophy at the boundary of humanities and data provenance, and it should not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—I will handle those separately. Please write this as a polished, formal proposal outline that treats the framing itself as part of the argument for why the project is worth doing.
