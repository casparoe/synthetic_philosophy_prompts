Construct a realistic, one-paragraph institutional scenario—set in a workplace or public hospital, not a trolley problem—engineered so that a distributive theory of leisure rights (modeled on the structural logic of the 35-hour week and treating rest as a non-rivalrous entitlement subject to collective aggregation) demands a harsher institutional response to an individual worker than a rehabilitated Protestant-work-ethic framework that treats labor as a bounded vocation with sabbatarian limits. The scenario must show karoshi-level harm arising not from individual ambition but from the systemic logic of leisure aggregation—specifically, from shift-coverage or productivity-maintenance dynamics that make one worker’s refusal to overwork increase the burden on colleagues in a way that threatens collective leisure quotas. Constraints: the leisure-rights rule must be genuinely optimal in general, not obviously bad; the case must isolate a single structural feature (the non-fungibility of rest across persons, or the free-rider dynamics of chronological equality); and neither framework may invoke perfect predictors, exotic physics, or information unavailable to ordinary managers. After presenting the scenario, state the specific action or sanction each theory recommends, show the step-by-step reasoning, identify the feature doing the work, explain what happens if that feature is removed—does the case collapse back into a standard individual-versus-collective welfare conflict?—and describe how a defender of leisure rights could attack the case without denying the feature, for instance by distinguishing chronological equality from well-being equality or by denying that leisure can be aggregated like income.
