I'm presenting to a philosophy-of-mind and applied-ethics working group next Thursday. The audience is mostly physicalist philosophers of mind who are skeptical of robust phenomenal continuity and narrative-identity conditions for personhood, plus two reproductive bioethicists who reject antinatalism on practical grounds and worry that asymmetry arguments are weaponized to justify coercive population policies. My thesis is an interval-sensitive revision of Benatar's asymmetry: I argue that the standard asymmetry—where the presence of suffering is bad but the absence of pleasure is not—depends on an unstated assumption that there is a continuous phenomenal subject spanning the interval in question. For beings with genuinely discontinuous consciousness—whether hypothetical artificial agents that are instantiated only episodically, or human cases of severe anterograde amnesia lacking any narrative bridge—there is no single bearer who misses pleasure during the gaps, so the absence of pleasure does not carry the usual asymmetrical weight; meanwhile, harm remains localized to each instantiated interval, which means the procreative calculus for such beings differs structurally from Benatar's. I am not defending artificial reproduction, but using these edge cases to show that Benatar's argument smuggles in a continuity condition it never defends.
