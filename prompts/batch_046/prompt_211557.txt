Recently, in my work assisting with the interpretability of large language models, I found myself drawn back to Donald Davidson’s framework of radical interpretation and, in particular, the principle of charity. Last week, two colleagues—both philosophers of mind who have read Davidson closely but disagree sharply about his scope—fell into a prolonged argument over dinner about whether charitable interpretation can be applied to the outputs of an artificial system. I was present only as a listener, and I remain genuinely unsettled about where the line falls, so I would like a careful adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded, without any signal of which side I favor.

The first colleague argued that when a language model gives contradictory answers about causal relations across different prompts, Davidson’s method requires us to maximize coherence and rationality in our interpretation: we should treat the contradictions as context-sensitive distinctions rather than as bare failures of belief, because interpretation itself collapses if we do not begin by assuming the speaker is largely rational and largely correct about the world. The second colleague objected that radical interpretation is specifically a theory of interpreting agents who hold beliefs and have intentions; since the model lacks mental states entirely, applying charity here is a straightforward category error—like attributing beliefs to a parrot or to a thermometer. The first colleague replied that Davidson’s project is fundamentally about making sense of linguistic behavior, not about verifying an internal mental architecture; if we abandon charity for statistical systems, we lose the only methodological tool we have for distinguishing meaningful variation from mere noise. The second colleague then countered that this dilutes Davidson into triviality: charity is tied to the principle of humanity and to the attribution of rational agency, and without that metaphysical commitment, “charitable interpretation” of a model is not interpretation at all but merely smoothing over errors to make the system seem intelligent. The first colleague conceded that agency matters for full-blooded interpretation but insisted that the methodological point survives regardless—without assuming some baseline coherence, we cannot learn anything substantive from the system’s outputs, and we are left with nothing but prediction.

Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about each specific claim? In particular, address whether charity requires actual belief-holding, whether it can be applied purely methodologically to non-agential systems, and whether abandoning charity for AI outputs necessarily leaves us unable to interpret them in any meaningful sense. Also, identify what both parties are missing—perhaps about the distinction between interpretive charity and predictive smoothing, or about whether Davidson’s framework was ever intended to cover artifacts rather than speakers, or about what it means for a contradiction to be “context-sensitive” in a system without a unified point of view.
