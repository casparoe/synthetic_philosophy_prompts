I want to defend a specific, somewhat unorthodox diagnosis of the Gettier problem, and I want an honest audit of its exposure rather than a victory lap. The position is this: Gettier cases do not merely show that justified true belief is insufficient for knowledge; they reveal that knowledge requires what we might call modal resilience of justification. On this view, a belief counts as knowledge only if the process that justifies it is structurally insulated from the particular failure mode present in the nearest worlds where the proposition is false—meaning the justification would not have yielded this belief in those worlds—while remaining stable across nearby worlds where the mechanism functions but the environment shifts slightly. This is not Nozick-style tracking applied globally to belief-world relations, nor is it virtue epistemology in normative dress: it targets the internal architecture of justification itself and requires no claim about cognitive competence or manifest excellence, only that the justification is not accidentally connected to the truth through a hidden false premise. I take this to solve standard Gettier structures—Smith’s job and the ten coins, the stopped clock, the fake-barn cases—without reducing them all to a single false-lemma diagnosis.
