Here are my judgments about what revealed choice behavior discloses about underlying utility or preference, and I want to know whether they cohere around a single principle, or whether some must be abandoned.

First, consider a consumer who, in every pairwise comparison, selects product A over product B, yet reports identical satisfaction after being given B alone and expresses no regret. I take this to show that A is genuinely preferred to B; the absence of regret with B does not erase the revealed ranking, because preference is measured by comparative choice, not by retrospective affect.

Second, in an experimental setting, subjects choose gamble G (a 90% chance of $100) over gamble S (a certain $80), but when asked to state their reservation price, they value S above G. Here I am inclined to say that both measures reveal something real—choice reveals a behavioral disposition, pricing reveals an evaluative judgment—and I resist collapsing one into the other. The tension is not resolved by declaring one measurement erroneous.

Third, a municipal council votes directly for policy P over Q by a clear margin. When the same council uses a ranked-choice procedure with an irrelevant decoy R introduced, Q prevails. I judge that the direct vote reveals the collective preference, while the ranked-choice outcome is an artifact of the aggregation mechanism, not a counter-preference.

Fourth, an individual in the grip of a well-documented addictive cycle repeatedly selects substance use over abstention, but after sustained recovery describes every such selection as not truly mine and consistently endorses abstention as what they wanted all along. I think the post-recovery, reflective endorsement reveals the true preference, and the addicted selections should be discounted as preference-revealing.

Fifth, employees in a firm choose a flexible-hours arrangement far more frequently than a salary-increase option when the two are offered in separate, isolated menus. Yet in an anonymous ranking task, the majority rank salary higher. I take the actual adoption behavior as the more reliable indicator of preference, treating the ranking response as distorted by social-desirability bias.

These judgments seem to pull in different directions: I defend choice data against retrospective satisfaction in case one, defend both choice and pricing in case two, defend direct voting against aggregation artifacts in case three, reject choice data in favor of reflective endorsement in case four, and defend usage data against explicit ranking in case five. Is there a unified account of revealed preference—perhaps one that distinguishes between first-order dispositions, second-order endorsements, and institutional framing—that saves all five verdicts? If not, which verdict is the outlier that a simpler, more parsimonious theory should overturn? Please also state what your proposed principle predicts for an unlisted case: a person who consistently ranks option X above Y in every survey, but whose every observed action, even in private and without social observation, selects Y.

Please present the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and be explicit about any verdict of mine that the principle cannot accommodate without ad hoc modification.
