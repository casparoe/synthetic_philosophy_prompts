Analyze the 1973 American Psychiatric Association vote to remove homosexuality from the DSM-II, together with the subsequent appearance of ego-dystonic homosexuality in DSM-III (1980), as a case study in Ian Hacking's looping effects. Do not simply retell the political narrative; instead, treat activists and clinicians as participants in a two-stage loop. First, examine the strategic alteration of clinical self-presentation by gay activists and allies in the years preceding the vote—how changing the empirical profile presented in clinical settings affected the data available to the classifiers. Second, examine the institutional response that replaced the broad category with a narrower residual diagnosis centered on distress about orientation rather than orientation itself. Through this lens, address what the episode reveals about whether looping requires institutional recognition to complete, or whether intentional interference from outside the clinical framework can short-circuit or redirect the loop. Then run the analysis in reverse: identify precisely where Hacking's framework is silent. Does it explain why the residual category persisted through later DSM revisions despite minimal clinical uptake, and what does that persistence suggest about the inertia of diagnostic architecture operating independently of looping dynamics? Finally, state whether this episode confirms that interactive kinds are uniquely vulnerable to strategic manipulation, or whether it shows that such manipulation only succeeds when it eventually receives institutional codification.
