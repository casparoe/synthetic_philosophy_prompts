I have been working through variants of the teletransportation problem with a reading group in my philosophy of mind seminar, and I find that my intuitive judgments about survival and identity across these scenarios form a pattern I cannot fully articulate. I am trying to determine whether there is a coherent principle behind them, or whether reflective equilibrium forces me to abandon one or more verdicts. Here are six concrete cases and my reactions.

In the standard case, a scanner on Earth destroys my body atom by atom and transmits the information to Mars, where an exact physical and psychological replica is assembled. I feel that I survive in the relevant sense—that it is appropriate for me to anticipate the replica's experiences—even though ordinary bodily continuity is absent. In a second scenario, the scanner produces an identical copy on Mars without destroying the original on Earth. Here I am certain that I do not survive as the copy; we are two distinct people who share psychology but not identity. Third, consider gradual replacement: over ten years, each biological neuron is replaced by a synthetic equivalent that preserves all functional dynamics and causal relations among mental states, until no original tissue remains. I think I survive continuously through this process. Fourth, there is the delayed-reconstruction case: a complete snapshot is taken at age thirty, but due to technical failure the copy is not built until I have lived naturally to age seventy and died. Despite perfect psychological similarity, I feel the reconstructed person is not me; the long gap breaks the continuity required for survival. Fifth, a branching malfunction creates two perfect copies on Mars and Venus. I find that I should be equally concerned for both, yet I am reluctant to say I survive as both, since that violates the logic of identity; at the same time, I resist saying there is a hidden fact about which one is really me. Sixth, there is a variant I have not seen discussed in the standard literature: a distributed-network reconstruction. The scanner destroys my body, but instead of assembling a localized organism, it instantiates my mental dynamics as patterns distributed across non-localized processing nodes, with no spatiotemporal contiguity between successive states and no embodied causal interaction with a physical environment—only structural relations among mental contents are preserved. Despite the preservation of all psychological relations, I am inclined to deny that this is survival, because something essential about embodied, causally integrated continuity seems to be missing.

Is there one principle—whether psychological continuity, bodily continuity, a hybrid account, or something more nuanced—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should be overturned, and what does the best available principle predict for a further case I have not listed: specifically, a modular-transfer scenario in which mental functions are migrated into a synthetic substrate not neuron-by-neuron but in discrete functional blocks, with temporary periods of unconsciousness between each integration? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each of its clauses, apply it to the modular-transfer case, and be explicit about which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate.
