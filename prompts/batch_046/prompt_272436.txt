After a recent procedure left me conscious but entirely without proprioception for nearly an hour, I have been unable to shake Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment: the figure suspended in void, blindfolded, limbs spread, certain of his own existence yet ignorant of any body. Write a poem of about thirty lines in blank verse, divided into three movements, spoken in the voice of that suspended figure. The first movement should establish the classical intuition—immediate self-certainty in the absence of all bodily knowledge—through regular iambic meter that evokes an implicit bodily order. The second movement should fracture meter and syntax, using enjambment and suspended clauses to enact the disappearance of every sensory frame. The third movement should quietly reintroduce a hidden orientation—perhaps a residual sense of having just been dropped, or of falling toward an unseen ground, or a minimal spatial index that the "I" cannot fully purge—so that the poem's return to rhythmic regularity feels less like recovery and more like a smuggled premise. The philosophical work should be done by the form itself: the reader should feel that the self-certainty of the second movement depends on the very framing the argument claims to exclude. Do not resolve the tension; end with the frame still present but unacknowledged by the speaker. The result should be recognizable to someone who knows the argument, and should treat the hidden-frame objection with enough precision that it functions as philosophy rendered in verse rather than mere illustration.
