I've come around to the view that if you're being epistemically modest, you should basically always take the outside view over the inside view when you're evaluating your own philosophical reasoning. Like, if I think I've got a knockdown argument against some form of materialism, the modest thing is to ask how often philosophers think they've got knockdown arguments and turn out wrong, instead of trusting the internal feel of the reasoning. I'm not sure if I'm being to rigid here, but I want to know what would actually count against this stance. Not just the generic sometimes-you-should-trust-your-gut reply, but concrete things: are there cases where the inside view carries information that base rates can't capture because the argument has some specific structural feature? What about anlogies from forecasting tournaments or clinical diagnosis where over-relying on outside-view stats actually hurts performance? And is there a formal result somewhere showing that strict outside-view dominance collapses into a self-undermining skepticism about philosophy itself? I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, including which ones a committed outside-view defender would concede and which they'd dismiss. Also, does this view risk being unfalsifiable -- if every time I feel convinced I just say that's my bias talking -- and is that a problem for a normative epistmic claim?
