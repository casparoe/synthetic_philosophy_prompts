I've mostly bought into the Dennett-style view that the self is just a center of narrative gravity — not a Cartesian ego, but the fictional protagonist our brains spin up to keep intentions and memories in line. But I also think there's something genuinely first-personal about experience that this story doesn't fully dissolve; I don't think I'm a zombie, but I don't think I'm a little soul inside either. So here's my hybrid: the self is a useful fiction generated by narrative integration, but it carries real causal and epistemic weight because it structures how we actually process information, and the first-person perspective is an irreducable feature of that structure rather than an illusion to be explained away. What would actually count against this? Not just the standard "who's the audience for the narrative?" objection, which I think misses the point. I want to know what kind of empircal finding in neuroscience or psychology would make this view untenable. What philosophical argument — about persistance conditions, the transparency of mental states, or social recognition — would force me to drop either the narrative-gravity half or the irreducable-first-person half? For each escape route, is it live or merely logically possible? And be honest: is this position well-insulated by qualifications, or am I just helping myself to both sides of the debate? If the framing is confused — like if "center of narrative gravity" and "irreducable first-person feature" are actually incompatible in a way I'm not seeing — just say so plainly instead of playing along. I want an honest map of how exposed this actually is.
