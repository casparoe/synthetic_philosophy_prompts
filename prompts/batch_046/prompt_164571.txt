I'm trying to determine whether Marx's analysis of machinery yields a consistent principle about when automation is liberating versus alienating. My verdicts are these. First, capital-owned factory robots that displace workers and intensify surplus extraction strike me as paradigmatic of real subsumption: the worker becomes an appendage to the machine. Second, collectively owned automation that reduces necessary labor strikes me as the technical precondition for communism. Third, a gig-economy driver who owns his car but is paced, routed, and evaluated by proprietary algorithms strikes me as formally independent yet really subsumed—the capital controls the labor process without owning the truck. Fourth, an artisan using computer-numeric tools under her own design authority strikes me as genuinely aided, not subsumed, despite the technology's sophistication. The tension is between the third and fourth cases: both involve formal ownership of means, yet I judge them differently. Is there a principle—perhaps centered on who directs the labor process and whether automation replaces human judgment or extends it—that captures all four verdicts? If not, which one should I overturn? State the principle precisely, identify the case that motivates each clause, give its verdict on the wavering case, and say honestly which of my four judgments it cannot accommodate.
