Please argue for the view that Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential experiments do not meaningfully threaten free will, but do so without relying on the familiar conscious-veto defense. Instead, defend the position that the experiments mislocate the intentional act: the readiness potential tracks not the formation of an intention, but the ballistic execution of a pre-authorized, generic motor schema that was settled when the subject adopted the experimental frame—namely, the prior commitment to perform the task whenever an internal urge arises. On this account, genuine agency resides in the framing decision (accepting the protocol and instituting the conditional policy "when I feel the urge, I will move"), which lies outside the EEG measurement window and is therefore invisible to the readiness-potential timeline. Develop the argument by clearly distinguishing (i) the authorization of a conditional action policy, (ii) the triggering event that activates the schema, and (iii) the execution that the RP anticipates. Then address the objection that this merely pushes agency back to an earlier, equally unconscious moment, and explain why the framing commitment differs in kind from the execution it governs—particularly with respect to its temporal scope, its representational content, and its susceptibility to revision. In constructing this argument, separate what is empirically settled about the neuroscience of voluntary movement from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of action and cognitive scientists, and avoid presenting any disputed interpretive claim about Libet's data or its philosophical implications as though it were the established consensus.
