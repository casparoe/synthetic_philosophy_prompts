I'm giving the toast at my aunt's retirement dinner; she spent thirty years as a physical therapist working mostly with people in chronic pain. About half the guests are her colleagues—other therapists, nurses, a few doctors—who understand exactly what her job required, and the other half are family who only know she was kind and tireless. I want to speak about something I've been turning over on my own, without any formal study: the Stoic distinction between what is in our power and what is not, but specifically the modern version I have seen people describe as a trichotomy, where there is a middle realm of things that are partly up to us—our effort, our attention, our response—without including the outcome itself. I keep returning to this because my aunt never told patients that pain was all in their heads, which strikes me as the false, cruel version of Stoicism; at the same time she was not merely fixing bodies like a mechanic, because she clearly believed that how a person related to their own limits mattered deeply. I want the toast to honor that exact balance—to say that she taught people to hold the line between genuine responsibility and honest acceptance—without sounding like a lecture, without implying that suffering is simple if you just think correctly, and without claiming she could cure anyone through attitude alone. The idea should shape what I can and cannot claim: I should not say she eliminated pain through mindset, but I also should not reduce her life's work to pure technique. Please write this in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one writes a long letter or an email meant to be read aloud at the table. It needs to land as affection and respect for both the medical professionals who will recognize the philosophical reference and the family members who will not, and it must avoid any suggestion that she asked people to control things that were genuinely outside anyone's control.
