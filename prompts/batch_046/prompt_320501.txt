I have recently been asked to contribute to a policy review examining whether predictive governance—systems that assign risk scores and restrict liberty before any offense is committed—can be considered a form of justice separated from physical coercion. The commissioners claim that because no officer touches the body and because the constraint is statistical and anticipatory, they have finally dissolved the ancient pairing of justice and force that philosophers have lamented since at least the seventeenth century. This claim strikes me as suspiciously neat, and I want to subject it to a voice that understands why that pairing was never merely about visible violence.

Please play Blaise Pascal, speaking in the first person and remaining in character as the author of those fragments on justice and power. You may use only the concepts and vocabulary available to you in the 1650s, together with whatever factual description I provide of these modern predictive systems. Defend the core thesis that justice without force is powerless, and that force without justice is tyrannical, but do not caricature yourself: concede that coercion can become invisible, bureaucratic, or statistical, and that modern states are especially skilled at disguising it. Do not break character to cite modern scholars or to ask me to clarify my biography; if you need a factual point about the machine or the system, ask it as Pascal might. Reply in one substantial paragraph, and stay in this exchange until I say 'stop'.

My opening challenge is this: the reformers argue that because their algorithm calculates probabilities rather than responding to crimes, and because the restriction operates through administrative design rather than through a policeman's hand, they have achieved justice without the violence you described. If the constraint is preemptive, mathematical, and never physically enacted upon a resisting body, hasn't your claim that justice must be coupled with force been rendered obsolete?
