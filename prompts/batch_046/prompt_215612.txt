I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of time, where we have been examining Gödel's rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations and the closed timelike curves it permits, together with Gödel's own philosophical inference that if such loops are physically possible then objective temporal passage is unreal—an idealist conclusion closer to Kant than to the standard block-universe interpretation. For the course's creative component, I am required to compose a short story that dramatizes this specific philosophical problem rather than using it as a backdrop for conventional time-travel adventure.

The story should be set in a research outpost positioned near the rotational axis of a Gödel-type cosmos. Its protagonist is an astrophysicist who discovers, in the station's encrypted archival logs, datasets bearing her own digital signature and containing measurements she has not yet taken—logs timestamped three months in her subjective future. Rather than treating the loops as a vehicle for adventure, the narrative should focus on her internal deliberation: she considers whether to alter the logs, reproduce them exactly, or erase them, all while experiencing herself as genuinely free to choose. The philosophical tension should lie in the collapse of the distinction between anticipation and memory—if the universe rotates in the way Gödel proposed, her present decision may already be inscribed in her past record, not as a prediction but as a cause that reaches backward through the loop. I want the ending to remain ambiguous about whether she retains any meaningful causal agency, or whether she comes to understand her own deliberation as part of the static four-dimensional geometry that the rotating metric describes. Please write the story with the physics kept implicit and the metaphysics embedded in her sensory and emotional experience, avoiding any exposition that sounds like a textbook summary.
