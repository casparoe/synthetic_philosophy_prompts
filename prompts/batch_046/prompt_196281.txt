Since taking on an advisory role with our municipal infrastructure board, I have found myself replaying a debate that occurred between two colleagues—Mara, a reliability engineer, and David, a philosopher of science—during a closed session on liability for a recent water-treatment failure. They were not arguing about legal blame per se, but about whether an omission can genuinely cause harm in a metaphysically robust sense. I report their exchange as a bystander with no settled view.

The case: A primary chlorination unit failed. A redundant backup existed but was never activated because the failure-detection sensor malfunctioned. Contamination spread for several hours before manual intervention occurred.

Mara’s first move: Omissions are not events; they are absences, and absences lack causal powers. The backup was never activated—there is no event there—so it cannot be part of the causal chain. The actual causes are the primary unit’s failure and the sensor’s malfunction; everything else is a counterfactual shadow.

David’s reply: That conflicts with ordinary and institutional practice. We hold a physician responsible when she omits an antidote, and we treat that omission as a cause of death. If omissions never cause anything, then our entire framework of attributing responsibility for failures to act collapses into incoherence.

Mara’s counter: Responsibility and causation come apart. We blame the physician for violating a duty, but the metaphysical cause of death remains the toxin’s presence and the absence of neutralization. The omission explains why no countervailing cause was present, but explanation is not causation; counterfactual dependence—if she had acted, the patient would have lived—is not sufficient for genuine causation.

David’s next move—introducing what he called a “structured omission”: In engineered systems, an omission is not a bare absence but a configured gap within a causal network. The backup’s activation was designed as a specified causal link. When that link fails to occur, it is not like darkness in a room with no bulb; it is like a broken gear in a machine. The omission has causal relevance because the architecture assigns it a functional role, making its non-occurrence a failure mode with its own explanatory and causal weight.

Mara’s response: That confuses the model with the territory. The reliability diagram includes the backup as a node, but ontologically only physical processes exist. The unimpeded spread of contamination is fully explained by the primary failure and the physical properties of the water. Calling the absence of backup activation “a cause” is like saying darkness is caused by the absence of light: it reifies a gap in our representation rather than identifying a real causal power.
