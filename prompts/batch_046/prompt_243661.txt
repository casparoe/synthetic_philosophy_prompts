As a student in my philosophy of science course, I have been working through Nelson Goodman’s discussion of “grue” and the problem of projectible predicates, and I have noticed a recurring defensive pattern in scientific practice that feels structurally analogous but is not quite the same as the standard examples in the literature. Here is the pattern. A quantitative model is introduced with explicit conditions of applicability—for instance, it predicts diffusion rates in homogeneous, non-reactive media. When it fails in a heterogeneous environment, defenders do not revise the internal equations; instead, they append a scope restriction, saying the model was never intended to apply there. After a subsequent failure in systems with active feedback loops, another restriction is added. Each restriction is individually plausible, but together they seem to tailor the model’s domain of applicability in much the same way that “grue” tailors the temporal extension of a predicate—adjusting boundary conditions post hoc to insulate the core claim from counterexample. Is there an established name for this specific phenomenon: the gradual accumulation of individually defensible scope restrictions that protect a theory without altering its internal structure? How precisely does it differ from introducing an auxiliary hypothesis in the Duhem-Quine sense, given that the adjustments occur at the level of applicability rather than at the level of theoretical premises? And if no standard term exists, should we classify this as an extension of Goodman’s problem of projectibility from properties to model domains, or as a distinct failure mode—perhaps a form of conventionalist immunization—that requires its own conceptual analysis? I would particularly value clarification on where the boundary lies between legitimate domain specification and the kind of gerrymandered protection strategy Goodman warned against.
