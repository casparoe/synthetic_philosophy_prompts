I am taking an upper-division seminar in philosophy of mathematics and logic, and our final session concerns how Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems are repeatedly invoked outside mathematics—to license claims that human minds transcend algorithmic formalization (the Lucas-Penrose line), that any ethical or legal system must contain undecidable propositions, or more generally that formalization is inherently doomed. I want to close our discussion with a poem that takes this analogical overreach seriously but corrects it structurally, letting the verse form itself do the philosophical work.

Please write a Petrarchan-style sonnet—fourteen lines, octave with interwoven rhyme (ABBA ABBA), sestet with a flexible scheme (CDECDE or CDCDCD), and a decisive volta around line nine—on what Gödel's results do and do not license beyond arithmetic. In the octave, establish the precise technical picture: a consistent formal system F, effectively axiomatizable and strong enough to represent primitive recursive arithmetic, contains a Gödel sentence G_F that is true in the standard model yet unprovable within F, with the result originally requiring ω-consistency and later weakened by Rosser to mere consistency. Let the rhyme and rhythm mirror the tight pairing of provability and truth that the theorem separates without collapsing them. At the volta, pivot sharply to the seductive misapplication: treating human cognition, moral reasoning, or legal frameworks as if they were a single fixed formal system with immutable axioms, then claiming Gödel proves such domains necessarily harbor truths that only non-formal intuition can grasp. In the sestet, dismantle the analogy with equal precision: human reason is not surveyed from within one effectively axiomatized system; we can revise premises in ways F cannot do to itself; and the second theorem states only that F cannot prove its own consistency, not that consistency is unprovable in principle. The closing couplet should state, without triumphalism, exactly what survives once the analogy collapses—not a license for mysticism, but a disciplined humility about the limits of any single formalization.

The poem should be rigorous enough that a philosopher of logic recognizes the distinction between the first and second theorems, the Rosser improvement, and the specific failure of the anti-mechanist argument (that the mind is not a single consistent formal system accessible to itself in the way Gödel's construction requires). Avoid reducing the poem to a sermon or a list of errors; instead, let the turn at the volta carry the burden of showing why the misapplication feels compelling and exactly where it quietly breaks. Someone who does not know Gödel should still encounter a coherent, serious poem about the gap between proof and truth, and about the dangers of exporting a technical result into domains where its conditions no longer hold.
