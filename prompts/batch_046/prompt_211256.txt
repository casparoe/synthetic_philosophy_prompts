You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral philosophy and institutional design. Before you answer, briefly brainstorm the major families of desert theory—control-based, contribution-based, luck-egalitarian, and hybrid institutional accounts—and note which ones treat opportunity-luck as cancelable rather than constitutive of merit.

Consider the following evaluation practice, now used in several large public research-funding agencies: grant renewals are scored with a “risk-calibrated merit” protocol. Reviewers do not rate outcomes (publications, patents, data sets) in raw terms. Instead they compute a “desert-weighted contribution index” that discounts achievements by the resources and prior institutional support the researcher enjoyed, while crediting ambitious failed experiments at a non-zero rate, provided the failure was not caused by negligence or recklessness. The explicit rationale is that merit should track what the researcher “really contributed” net of brute opportunity-luck; yet anyone whose failure stemmed from incompetence is excluded entirely, even if the negative results were scientifically valuable.

What theory—or cluster of theories—of desert and moral merit does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit commitments: is desert comparative or absolute? Does it depend on control over outcomes, or on the value of the attempt itself? Can institutional luck genuinely be “subtracted out” of achievement without also undermining the moral relevance of outcome-luck? Assess whether the protocol is internally coherent, or whether it quietly combines incompatible assumptions—for example, treating resource-luck as correctable while treating success-luck as irrelevant to blame and credit.

Finally, describe what would have to change in the evaluation rules if we replaced the protocol’s implicit theory with a straightforwardly consequentialist funding model, in which only expected future social value matters and past failure or success carries no independent moral weight.
