I serve on the board of a charitable trust capitalized in 1952 with profits from a plantation-supply firm that operated across Southeast Asia during decolonization. Today the trust funds medical scholarships. At a recent meeting, one trustee proposed adopting an explicit moral amortization schedule: each year we would compute the present value of historical harm—estimated from extracted labor and displaced communities—against the present value of scholarships granted, and restrict spending to the net surplus. Another trustee called this a grotesque quantification of injustice, arguing that wrongs of this kind are not debts that can be retired like a mortgage but enduring structural stains that require separating governance from the founders' descendants and transferring control to representatives of the affected regions. They asked me to break the deadlock. Is there a coherent way to weigh past exploitation against present institutional good, or does any attempt to balance them on a single ledger compound the original wrong? And if one framework is more defensible, does it point toward retaining the fund with an accounting mechanism, restructuring governance through separation, or some other course?
