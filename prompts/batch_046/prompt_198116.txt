Write a polished, formal dialogue between two philosophers—designated only as K and M—who are debating whether superrational coordination is justified in the Platonia dilemma. K should defend the view that structurally identical agents across causally isolated worlds ought to treat shared reasoning as a correlation device strong enough to justify cooperation, even in the absence of causal feedback; in particular, K should invoke the idea that perfect transparency of reasoning architecture functions like a logical escrow that binds counterfactual copies without requiring empirical observation of them. M should reject this, arguing from an evidential-decision-theoretic standpoint that without observable correlation or causal linkage, treating hypothetical copies as strategic partners amounts to an unjustified metaphysical wager, and should press the point that the real-world Luring Lottery—where readers submitted postcards bearing entry counts, with a prize divided among all submissions—shows that even human agents with near-identical reasoning architectures fail to achieve low-number coordination when strategic misrepresentation is possible. The exchange must be genuinely dialectical: each speaker should respond to the specific argument just made by the other, not simply restate a prepared position. Both characters must be presented with equal intellectual competence; neither should be reduced to a strawman. Have them directly address whether the Luring Lottery's empirical noise—strategic inflation of entry counts and the resulting runaway collapse of the prize—undermines Platonic superrationality, or whether Platonia's assumption of perfect reasoning transparency removes precisely the noise that makes the lottery fail. The dialogue should remain unresolved, but the tension should be sharpened and precisely located rather than dissolved.
