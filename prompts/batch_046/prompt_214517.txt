Design a runnable, 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of approximately ten students that transforms Kavka's toxin puzzle from an abstract intuition-pump into an experienced structural tension. The activity should employ real but small social stakes rather than monetary ones—specifically, a collective negotiation over whether to adopt a mildly inconvenient group norm (for example, a mandatory silent writing exercise at the start of every remaining session) that can be unanimously revoked after adoption—so that risk aversion does not collapse the paradox into a trivial cost-benefit calculation. Internally frame the negotiation as a contract for a hypothetical cognitive-enhancement protocol: students are assigned to one of two roles, Principal (who offers a tangible collective reward—control over the seminar's final discussion topic—conditional on a genuine shared intention to accept the norm) or Agent (who must form that intention while knowing the norm can be suspended immediately after the reward is secured). Specify the rules governing pre-commitment, the mechanism for verifying intention without observing future compliance, the three-phase timeline (negotiation, verification and reward, suspension decision), and the debrief that maps each phase onto the puzzle's logic: the rationality of intending to suffer, the rationality of not suffering once rewarded, and the instability of intending an action one already knows will not be performed. The debrief should also identify the misconception students are likely to form—namely, that the paradox is merely about weakness of will—and show exactly where in the sequence to correct it. Include an explicit, honest note on which feature of Kavka's original scenario—its solitary, non-coordinated character—this collective version necessarily fails to reproduce, and instruct the facilitator on how to supply that missing element verbally. The framing device should remain internal to the rules rather than an explicit analogy, so that participants discover the structural parallel themselves during the debrief; avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or external sources.
