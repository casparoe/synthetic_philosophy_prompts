I have recently become concerned that the mental vocabulary I habitually use to explain my own decisions—speaking of my “beliefs” and “desires”—might be as scientifically obsolete as talk of phlogiston or humors, which led me to take eliminative materialism seriously. Yet I now face what seems like a fatal reflexive difficulty: if beliefs genuinely do not exist, then it appears that no one can believe eliminative materialism itself to be true, which would make the view self-undermining in exactly the way global skepticism is often said to be. Is this objection sound, or does it depend on a misunderstanding of what eliminativists actually reject and of whether their own theoretical commitments can be cast in non-folk-psychological terms? Please distinguish carefully between what is broadly settled in contemporary philosophy of mind and what remains genuinely contested among specialists—particularly regarding the precise scope of the eliminated categories and how eliminativism applies to its own assertions—and do not present any disputed interpretation of those issues as the consensus view.
