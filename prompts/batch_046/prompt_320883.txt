At a recent colloquium on ancient political philosophy, two colleagues—both specialists in Aristotle—debated the interpretation of natural slavery in the Politics. I am writing to ask you to adjudicate their exchange. Please go through each move and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification; then say who is right overall about the central interpretive question, and identify what both interlocutors are overlooking. Do not treat this as asking for your own independent essay on Aristotle; evaluate the arguments exactly as they stand.

Colleague A opened by arguing that Aristotle's account of the phusei doulos in Politics I is best understood as a normative-teleological classification rather than an empirical description of actual captives. On this view, the claim that some people are marked for rule from birth is not a biological prediction but a metaphysical statement about the distribution of rational form within the household, or oikos. The natural slave lacks the deliberative faculty, to bouleutikon, not as a defect like blindness, but as a formal orientation analogous to how a hand, when separated from the body, is no longer a hand in the primary sense. Thus, the slave's practical wisdom is present but oriented pros hen—toward the master's reason—much as the hand's motion is oriented toward the soul's command when attached. Aristotle's references to barbarians and to bodies marked for servitude are rhetorical illustrations of functional hierarchy, not ethnographic or anatomical claims.

Colleague B replied that this reading evacuates the specifically natural character of Aristotle's argument. If the distinction were purely functional and normative, Aristotle could have said that some people ought to be ruled without invoking physis, birth marks, or comparisons to domesticated animals. The text explicitly treats slavery as an extension of the soul-body relation within the polis: just as the soul naturally rules the body, so the master naturally rules the slave. This suggests a substantial, not merely relational, difference in the soul's form. Moreover, if the slave's rationality is genuinely present but merely oriented toward another, then we face a metaphysical puzzle Aristotle never resolves: is this orientation accidental (making slavery a contingent social arrangement) or substantial (making the slave a different kind of human being)? Colleague B insisted that Colleague A's pros hen solution borrows from the Metaphysics without warrant, since there Aristotle discusses homonymy for parts separated from wholes, not for souls in hierarchical union. Without an explicit account of shared or parasitic form, the normative reading collapses into either a morally unacceptable essentialism or a vacuous functionalism.

Colleague A responded that Colleague B conflates Aristotelian nature with modern biological essentialism. For Aristotle, physis is always an internal principle of motion and rest oriented toward an end. To say that nature distinguishes the bodies of free and slave is no more empirical than saying nature distinguishes the acorn's potential for oakhood. The comparison to animals in Politics I.5 concerns the presence or absence of authoritative practical reason, not racial or anatomical taxonomy. Furthermore, the slave's soul is not parasitic on the master's in any metaphysically mysterious way; it is complete in its own order, like the vegetative soul is complete in a plant, but it lacks the authoritative part that would make it capable of self-rule. The master and slave form a single practical unit not through shared form but through complementary teleology—each achieves its proper good only in relation to the other.

Colleague B countered that this complementary teleology is precisely what needs defense. If the slave achieves its proper good only through being ruled, then the slave's rationality, however incomplete, must still be capable of recognizing that good—otherwise there is no teleological orientation, only coercion. But if the slave can recognize the good of being ruled, then it possesses sufficient deliberative capacity to ask whether that arrangement is genuinely just, which undermines the claim that it naturally lacks authority. Colleague B concluded that Aristotle's argument works only if we assume that the slave's rational deficiency is so profound that it cannot even question its own subordination; yet in that case, the pros hen analogy fails, because a hand does not need to understand the body's plan in order to serve it. The slave, being rational (even if defectively so), occupies an unstable middle ground that Aristotle's metaphysics of soul cannot comfortably accommodate.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of the four moves above, indicate whether it is clearly right, mostly right with reservations, ambiguous, mostly wrong with some merit, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify your rating. Then state which colleague, if either, succeeds in resolving the central tension in Aristotle's account—between the normative-functional and the substantial-natural interpretations—and note what important consideration both have neglected.
