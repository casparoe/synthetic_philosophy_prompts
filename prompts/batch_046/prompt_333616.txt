I'm presenting a seminar defense of the claim that the well-documented gender-equality paradox—where societies with stronger gender-egalitarian institutions and norms display more, not less, occupational gender segregation—should be interpreted as exposing a normative trilemma rather than resolving the nature-culture debate. My thesis is that autonomy-maximizing social structures relax external constraints on preference formation, which allows small initial differences in preference distributions—whether rooted in socialization, biological variance, or interaction effects—to be amplified by competitive labor-market sorting. The result is that three goals standard equality discourse treats as compatible—procedural autonomy (minimizing gendered socialization pressure), distributive parity across occupations, and representational fairness in elite sectors—are in structural tension; pursuing one to its limit undermines the others, and conventional metrics conceal the trade-off. I am not arguing for biological determinism, nor am I denying social construction, but I am insisting that the paradox forces us to abandon the implicit assumption that lower segregation is always the correct indicator of greater gender justice.
