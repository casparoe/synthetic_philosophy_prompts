In my graduate seminar on the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism, I have been tracking the implicit commitments of a particular non-cognitivist framework—one that treats moral predicates as expressing non-cognitive attitudes of condemnation or acquiescence rather than describing states of affairs—through its verdicts on embedded contexts. The framework has consistently ruled as follows. First, on an unembedded claim that the practice of extracting information through prolonged sensory deprivation is morally impermissible, it holds that the sentence expresses direct moral condemnation and is not literally truth-evaluable. Second, on the negation—that prolonged sensory deprivation is not morally impermissible—it denies that the speaker expresses condemnation, taking the utterance instead to express a contrary practical attitude of tolerance or acquiescence, again without descriptive truth conditions. Third, on a conditional—if prolonged sensory deprivation is impermissible, then the state must not authorize it—it holds that the antecedent does not carry expressive force in isolation, while the compound as a whole expresses a practical commitment linking conditions to action rather than describing a relation between propositions. Fourth, on a propositional-attitude report—Dr. Arendt believes that prolonged sensory deprivation is impermissible—it allows that the embedded clause contributes to the descriptive content of the belief ascription, so the overall sentence is truth-evaluable, though the speaker herself expresses no moral stance. Fifth, on a disjunction—either prolonged sensory deprivation is impermissible, or it is a permissible instrument of state security—it treats the utterance as expressing a divided or unsettled practical orientation, not as a standard truth-functional compound.

I now wish to apply this framework consistently to a new construction: the concessive counterfactual Even if prolonged sensory deprivation were not morally impermissible, the state ought still to abandon it on purely evidentiary grounds, in which a moral predicate is embedded within a counterfactual concessive clause whose main clause offers a non-moral, evidentiary recommendation. Predict whether, on this framework, the speaker expresses moral condemnation of the practice, whether the overall sentence is assessable for descriptive truth, and which of the five precedents supplies the closest analog. The track record seems jointly compatible with at least two competing rules—one holding that expressive force is strictly confined to top-level unembedded positions and is fully neutralized in any subordinate clause, and another holding that expressive force can be preserved through presuppositional or implicational projection even within embedded contexts, being blocked only in transparent descriptive reports such as belief ascriptions. For each rule, say precisely what it predicts about this concessive case, identify which principle the framework would be adopting by siding with one prediction over the other, and specify one further linguistic construction—different from those already listed—that would decisively adjudicate between these two rules.
