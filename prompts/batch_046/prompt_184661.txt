I run a grad-level political ethics workshop, about 10 people, one 90 minute session. Design an in-class role-play or structured deliberation that makes the dirty-hands problem feel real, but don't do the standard leader-does-bad-thing framing—thats been done to death. Instead, inject the distributed version: citizens who authorize an immoral but necessary act without carrying it out, and then live with the moral residue. Split the group: half are decision-makers facing a concrete fictional crisis (small point stakes representing institutional trust), half are an authorising electorate that votes to approve or block. The rules should force them to discover thier own that authorizing feels different from doing but still leaves a kind of taint. Include the payoff scheme, roles, timeline, and a debrief that maps moments onto Walzer's core idea and onto the complicity twist. Tell me what will predicably go wrong the first run and be honest about which feature—probably the long-term psychological weight over years—the activity can't reproduce. They should come away seeing that moral residue leaks into the structures that approve the act, not just the agent who pulls the lever. No lecture up front, and don't ask for advance readings.
