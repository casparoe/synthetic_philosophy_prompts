Please argue that introspection is structurally reliable for what can be called phenomenal valence tracking—the first-person registration of affective intensity and directional quality—while being systematically unreliable for etiological self-attribution, the identification of the causal sources and sustaining conditions of those same states, and that this failure is not a contingent cognitive limitation but arises because etiological framing requires a third-person causal schema incompatible with the first-person mode of presentation. Defend the further claim that recognizing this asymmetry undermines a common formulation of the transparency argument for self-knowledge—namely, the inference from immediate phenomenal access to authoritative knowledge of mental content—without collapsing into general skepticism about first-person authority. Address the objection that this view illegitimately imports externalist assumptions about mental content into philosophy of mind, and show why the structural unavailability of etiological access should be treated as an architectural feature of introspection rather than a remediable ignorance. Construct the argument through conceptual analysis alone; do not invoke specific texts, named authors, or empirical studies, and avoid treating the distinction between phenomenal and etiological access as a settled taxonomy rather than a framing device that must earn its keep within the argument itself.
