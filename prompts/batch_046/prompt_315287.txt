I've been going over some imaginary cases with a friend, and I keep coming back to the same pattern, but I'm not sure it's consistent.

Here are my gut verdicts. One: an art historian I trust tells me Turner's late seascapes have a sublime turbulence the earlier ones lack; I haven't seen them, but I take her word and form the belief. That feels fine, almost like picking up a scientific claim. Two: an untrained friend tells me a modern sculpture is moving, and I haven't seen it either. I feel I shouldn't just accept that; I withold judgement until I see it myself. Three: I'm in front of a painting but the lighting is dreadful, so I can't make out the colours properly; an expert beside me says the palette is harmonious, and I believe her. That seems legitimate too. Four: a museum guide tells a group a conceptual piece is profoundly ironic, and everyone nods along without really grasping it. I think they're all faking an aesthetic response they don't have, so that's not okay. Five: a musicologist describes the emotional arc of a symphony I've never heard, using technical language. I understand the description and come to believe it's melancholic then triumphant, but somehow this feels different from case one — more like learning a plot summary than gaining aesthetic knowledge — and I'm genuinely unsure whether I should count it. Six: two equally qualified critics disagree sharply on whether a novel is elegant or pretentious, and I side with the one whose taste already matches mine. That feels like bias, not testimony, so not okay either.

So my verdicts are roughly: cases one and three yes, case two and four and six no, case five I'm flip-flopping on. Is there a principle that captures this? Maybe something about testimony filling in for poor perceptual access rather than substituting for my own evaluative reaction? So perhaps the line is about whether the property is one I could verify with my own senses if conditions were right, but I'm not convinced that saves case five since I could just listen to the music. There's also the hard case I keep going back and forth on: I'm blind, and a sighted friend describes the visual rhythm of a Mondrian and tells me it's balanced. I come to believe it, but I can't tell if I've gained genuine aesthetic knowledge or just second-hand information. What should I say?
