Design an in-class simulation for an upper-level political-philosophy seminar (twelve students, one 90-minute session) that makes participants experience how modern nations are not merely rediscovered by archives but are partly constituted by the very bureaucratic practices meant to document them—specifically through what I call retroactive genealogical stitching, the projection of census, linguistic, and museological categories backward onto pre-modern social formations that possessed no equivalent collective identity. Use a fictional multi-ethnic region and staged archival fragments spanning 1800 to 1950: census ledgers that change classification schemes over time, school curricula that introduce standardized languages, museum labels that assign ethnic origins to artifacts, and transcribed folk narratives that are edited to emphasize national continuity. Divide the group into three rotating roles: Archivists, who may classify populations only with the categories available in 1950; Historians, who must construct an unbroken national narrative back to 1800 using only the Archivists' classifications and the source fragments; and Critics, who observe, identify moments of anachronism, and award coherence scores according to explicit rules you should provide. Specify a concrete timeline in phases, non-monetary payoffs tied to narrative coherence versus detected stitching, and interaction rules that prevent the Historians from inventing categories outside the archival record. The debrief should map specific turning points in the simulation onto Hobsbawm's analysis of invented traditions and onto Anderson's census-map-museum triad, showing how instruments designed to describe a pre-existing nation end up producing the continuity they claim to find. Include an honest assessment of which feature of actual nation-building the 90-minute format cannot reproduce—namely, the multi-generational sedimentation of these categories into lived self-understanding—and identify what will predictably fail on the first run, such as students treating the fictional fragments as transparent historical data rather than constructed artifacts, together with a practical remedy. The idea should emerge from the conflict between roles and the constraints of the source material, not from facilitator explanation afterward.
