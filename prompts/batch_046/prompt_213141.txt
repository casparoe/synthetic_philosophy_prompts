Here is an argument I have been turning over that starts from Putnam-style Twin Earth reasoning and Burge-style social externalism, then pushes both into moral vocabulary, and ends up seeming to dissolve cross-cultural moral disagreement into mere verbal divergence. I want to keep the externalist premise for natural kinds and the intuition that moral disagreement is genuinely substantive, but I cannot tell which step is the illicit slide.

(1) For natural kind terms like "water," semantic externalism holds: Earth-speakers refer to H2O and Twin Earth-speakers to XYZ, with reference fixed by the environment rather than by internal psychology.
(2) Burge extends this to mental content generally: the concept JUSTICE is partly fixed by the linguistic practices of one's community, not solely by individual reasoning.
(3) Now imagine a "Moral Twin Earth" where local practices fix "cruel" to actions that cause unnecessary suffering, but "necessary" is defined relative to collective social harmony rather than individual dignity.
(4) An Earthling and a Twin Earthling could be phenomenally identical in their reasoning, both sincerely uttering "This punishment is cruel," yet refer to different properties—excess relative to dignity versus excess relative to harmony.
(5) If externalism holds for moral terms, then the content of their beliefs is determined by these external social facts, not by any shared reasoning they can access.
(6) So they are not genuinely disagreeing about one proposition; they express different propositions with the same phonetic form, just as Earth-water and Twin-water do.
(7) But then cross-cultural moral criticism—condemning an outside practice—becomes conceptually impossible: the critic literally lacks the concept that the practice instantiates.
(8) This is wrong; moral disagreement feels real and not merely verbal.
(9) So either social externalism fails for moral terms, or step (5) misapplies the division of linguistic labor, or there is a hidden indexical that saves shared reference despite divergent environments.

Where does the argument actually break? Is the mistake treating moral terms like "water," or is the real error in (5)'s move from community-fixed reference to belief content? If I reject (2) for moral vocabulary, must I abandon social externalism entirely, or can I restrict it without ad hoc boundary-drawing? And if I keep (2) but block (7), what does that do to the idea of moral deference—do I end up committed to a kind of moral elitism where only insiders can truly understand the terms?
