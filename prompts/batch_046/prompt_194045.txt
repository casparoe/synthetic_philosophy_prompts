I have been tracking a recurring asymmetry in how working scientists and philosophers invoke Dennett’s three stances—physical, design, and intentional—around the question of what makes a pattern genuinely explanatory rather than merely heuristic, and I want to know whether this configuration already has a name in the philosophy of science literature.

Consider two concrete cases I keep encountering. In evolutionary biology, a researcher defends a functional claim—say, that a particular mating display operates as deception—at the design stance, and is then pressed for the underlying genetic or neural mechanism. The absence of that mechanism is treated as a temporary lacuna; the design-level pattern is still taken as real and worthy of further investigation, not as falsified. In cognitive science or artificial intelligence, a researcher defends attributing a belief-like state—perhaps that a navigating system represents an obstacle—purely on the grounds that the intentional stance yields accurate predictions. When critics ask for the design-level architecture that implements this representation, the researcher replies that predictive success at the intentional stance is self-sufficient, and that demanding a design-level mechanism is an illegitimate reductionist move. Yet if the same researcher proposed a design-level feature—say, a specific learning algorithm—without showing that it produces successful intentional-level predictions, the claim would be dismissed as unsupported. Meanwhile, physical-level explanations are rarely permitted to justify intentional-level attributions upward; one does not say, “the neurons fired this way, therefore the system believes P,” without an intervening design or intentional story. So the burden of justification seems to flow downward (design claims are expected eventually to bottom out in physical mechanisms) but not symmetrically upward or laterally (intentional patterns need not be anchored in design features, and design patterns need not be justified by intentional predictive success).

Is there an established concept for this uneven distribution of explanatory burden across the stances? How exactly does it relate to Dennett’s own view that a pattern can be real at one stance without being reducible to another, and to discussions of explanatory pluralism or autonomy? If there is no standard term, please propose one with explicit boundary conditions—for instance, does the asymmetry hold for artifacts as well as organisms, and does it collapse when the intentional attribution is clearly metaphorical rather than predictive?
