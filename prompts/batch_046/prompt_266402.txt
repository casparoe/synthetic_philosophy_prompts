Write the desiderata that any adequate theory treating the self as a center of narrative gravity must meet if it is to explain personal persistence without reducing the self to mere fiction or to Cartesian substance. Specify the phenomena the theory must accommodate: the maintenance of practical commitments across episodic amnesia, the integration of conflicting first-person reports following hemispheric disconnection, and the retention of moral responsibility when an agent's autobiographical narrative has been therapeutically reconstructed. For each phenomenon, state the distinction the theory must preserve—between being the subject of a narrative and being its author, between narrative coherence and narrative veridicality, between gradual revision and wholesale replacement. Then state the constraints from cognitive neuroscience it cannot contradict, particularly the distributed and predictive architecture of autobiographical memory, and flag any pair of requirements that appear jointly unsatisfiable, such as the demand for a genuinely unified center of gravity and the demand that this center survive radical narrative revision without loss of identity. Identify which single desideratum does the most work in excluding rival accounts, but do not provide a scorecard of named theories. Present everything in continuous prose alone: no headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter.
