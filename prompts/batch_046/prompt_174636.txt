I've been working through some alignment-literature notes with a study group, and we keep returning to HCH — Humans Consulting HCH — as an idealization of deliberation. The usual story is that factored cognition captures how good reasoning decomposes into subproblems, with each node consulting copies of itself so that justification flows upward without the root needing direct access to every detail. That makes sense as a model of distributed epistemic labor, but I'm struggling with a conceptual issue that seems under-discussed in the standard treatments. Real deliberation isn't just solving pre-given subproblems; the framing of those subproblems — which distinctions you draw, which variables you treat as exogenous — is often the result of preliminary, holistic judgment. There's a reflexive dependency: your choice of decomposition depends on anticipatory hunches about the overall answer, and those hunches are themselves shaped by how you've decomposed things so far.

If HCH is meant to idealize deliberation structurally, not just computationally, then it seems to presuppose that the factorization can be fixed independently of the reasoning it enables. But in many philosophical and practical cases, the factorization is precisely what's at stake. So my question is: where should we draw the line between the justification structure that HCH genuinely captures — the upward flow of warranted sub-answers — and the framing dynamics it necessarily abstracts away? In particular, can the idealization accommodate cases where the crux of deliberation is not the answer to any sub-question, but the decision of how to factor the problem in the first place? I'd like an explanation that doesn't just say "HCH is an approximation," but actually locates the conceptual boundary between modular justification and holistic framing, especially when the framing choice itself is reflexively entangled with the answers being sought.
