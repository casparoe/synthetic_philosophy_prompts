I have been trying to work through Rawls's separateness of persons objection with a colleague, and she offered the following gloss, which she said captures the heart of the argument in A Theory of Justice: "Rawls claims that utilitarianism treats a society as though it were a single individual with one continuous consciousness. Just as a rational person might accept the loss of a finger to preserve the rest of the body, utilitarianism permits the suffering of some citizens so long as the total social welfare increases. The error is metaphysical—utilitarianism implicitly posits a collective social mind that experiences aggregate pleasure and pain, and since no such super-subject actually exists, there is no legitimate subject to bear the net gain. This also explains why Rawls rejects utility monsters: if one person's capacity for satisfaction were vast enough, utilitarian logic would license extreme deprivation for everyone else, which proves that the framework cannot respect the boundaries between persons."

I find the summary persuasive in places, yet I am uneasy about two moves: first, that Rawls's objection depends on denying a collective consciousness, and second, that the utility-monster scenario is part of his separateness argument rather than a separate line of critique. Before I commit to this reading, I would like an accuracy audit. Which parts of my colleague's account are faithful to Rawls's actual reasoning about interpersonal comparison and institutional design? Where does she conflate structural features of moral argumentation with metaphysical claims about group minds? And does her conclusion—that without a super-subject the objection dissolves—misunderstand what Rawls means by taking the separateness of persons seriously in the construction of basic institutions?
