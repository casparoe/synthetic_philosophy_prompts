A review essay I am considering for my graduate seminar offers the following characterization of the cognitive-penetration debate. Its author claims, first, that the central philosophical question is whether conceptual content can enter into the representational content of perceptual experience itself, rather than whether cognition exerts merely causal influence upstream or downstream of experience. Second, she attributes to defenders of impenetrability—working in the tradition of Pylyshyn—a concession that attention and belief-formation are cognitively mediated, coupled with an insistence that the non-conceptual outputs of early visual processing remain informationally encapsulated. Third, she presents the hollow-face illusion and expert-novice differences in color-contrast judgments as decisive empirical demonstrations that prior knowledge alters the phenomenal character of seeing, not merely subsequent interpretation or report. Fourth, she argues that predictive-coding architectures render the impenetrability thesis untenable, on the grounds that if the visual system minimizes free-energy using conceptually structured priors, then conceptual content shapes perceptual representations at every hierarchical level. Before I assign this, I want an accuracy audit. Which parts of this summary would a careful defender of impenetrability accept without objection? Where does it risk conflating epistemic penetration with perceptual penetration, in the sense recently distinguished by Macpherson and others? And does the appeal to predictive processing genuinely undermine informational encapsulation, or does it change the subject by substituting a neurocomputational model for the original epistemological question?
