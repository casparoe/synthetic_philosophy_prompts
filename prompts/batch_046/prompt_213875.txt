I recently joined the ethics advisory panel for a multinational cooperative that must integrate secular humanist, Catholic social-teaching, and Islamic finance principles into one governance charter, and next month I will defend our draft framework at a public hearing. The thesis is that moral compromise across incommensurable value systems should not be treated as split-the-difference bargaining or a Rawlsian overlapping consensus, but as a form of normative gains-from-trade: each tradition retains full sovereignty over the domains where its commitments are most internally coherent and socially costly to abandon—its comparative moral advantage—and in exchange grants structured deference to other traditions in domains where its own commitments are thinner or more contested, generating a cooperative surplus of institutional legitimacy rather than a bare modus vivendi. The audience will be about thirty people: liberal political philosophers committed to public reason and state neutrality; communitarian critics who regard liberal compromise as dissolving thick moral identities; legal scholars who specialize in religious arbitration and jurisdictional pluralism; and two economists who will instinctively translate the argument into Pareto-efficiency language. I need a prep sheet, not a defense. List the objections in the order they will actually arise—beginning with the liberal neutralists, then the communitarians, then the legal scholars, then the economists. For each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary that group would actually use, indicate whether it targets the trade analogy itself, the institutional application, or a hidden normative premise, and specify what I should concede up front, what I must hold without qualification, and where my reply is weakest. Include the single question I am hoping nobody asks—likely from the economists or the legal scholars—and draft a concise reply of about two minutes that does not invoke empirical data or technical legal expertise I do not possess. Finally, identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend explicitly rather than hope they overlook, and suggest one voluntary concession that, if offered early, actually strengthens the framework’s credibility with this particular room. Write in American English, keep the tone formal, and do not ask me for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
