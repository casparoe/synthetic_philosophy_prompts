I serve on my university’s Joint Committee on Secular Governance and Student Association Recognition. We must reach a decision by 15 June on whether to grant formal recognition to the Collective for Structured Ambiguity, a student group that describes its weekly gatherings as simultaneously political deliberation and ritual observance, and that explicitly refuses to classify itself as either religious or secular. Our current framework requires every recognised association to declare itself faith-based—making it eligible for chaplaincy funding and religious accommodation—or secular—making it eligible for political-society funding and access to university debate forums—with no hybrid category permitted. The collective’s request therefore forces the committee to apply either the principle of French laïcité, which insists on a strict public separation and refuses institutional recognition to entities that blur the religious-political boundary, or the Anglo-American multicultural model, which extends recognition and resources to self-identified cultural and religious groups but typically requires clear categorisation for equitable distribution.

The collective argues that both frameworks commit a form of categorical violence by imposing a binary that misrepresents their practice; they claim that their refusal to choose is itself a meaningful exercise in pluralist citizenship rather than an administrative evasion. Some members want to reject the application outright on the grounds that recognition requires administrable boundaries and that ambiguity invites arbitrary resource allocation. Others want to create a provisional “post-secular” category. Others contend that the institution’s commitment to pluralism requires accommodating practices that resist the very taxonomy the institution uses to deliver recognition.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—together with a defined time frame for implementation and for review. The reasoning should take seriously the collective’s claim that both laïcité and multiculturalism depend on an unjustifiable binary, but it should not merely restate either model’s textbook definition. Instead, it should engage the conceptual tension between negative neutrality (the exclusion of ambiguous religious-political expression from public institutional recognition) and positive pluralism (the inclusion of groups through categorised rights). You should also address the strongest argument for the position you reject: if you recommend rejection, explain why administrative coherence and non-arbitrariness outweigh the claim to ambiguity; if you recommend approval or a hybrid category, explain why the university’s pluralist commitments require accommodating practices that undermine the classification system upon which fair distribution rests.
