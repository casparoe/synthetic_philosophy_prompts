I'm trying to determine whether guilt or shame is the more fitting moral emotion, and whether a society can deliberately adopt one as its dominant framework. My intuitions across cases are mostly consistent but pull in opposite directions on the design question. A surgeon who kills a patient through fatigue feels guilty, reports the error, and drives institutional reform; shame would misclassify a correctable mistake as a stain on her identity. A politician caught fabricating credentials feels shame and withdraws; guilt alone seems inadequate because the failure is about who he is, not merely what he did. When someone realizes they have neglected their partner's needs for years, guilt motivates repair; shame would likely drive concealment. But when an old private slur is unearthed, the public response is shame, and I think that is disproportionate—guilt for the harm caused would be more appropriate, yet shame dominates. These cases suggest a principle: guilt fits failures that are reparable and behavior-specific, while shame fits failures that reveal a defective social identity. But then I consider two cultural designs. One community decides all moral failures—betrayals, harms, deceptions—will be processed through collective guilt rituals with no public shaming; another reserves shame for violations of sacred communal identity and assigns guilt to interpersonal harm. The first strikes me as possibly unstable for deep betrayals; the second strikes me as coherent. So my questions are these. Is there a single principle that captures which emotion is fitting in each case, that explains why the surgeon should not be shamed and why the credential liar should not merely feel guilty, and that also predicts whether a culture can rationally choose its dominant emotional regime? If so, state it precisely and say which of my verdicts it must overturn—particularly my sense that the all-guilt community is unstable. If there is no single principle, present the two best candidates, show what each saves and sacrifices, and say whether either supports what I have been calling emotional constitutionalism: the idea that a culture can legitimately design its moral-emotional architecture, selecting guilt or shame as primary instruments, provided it respects some boundary conditions. I am especially interested in the case I keep reversing on: a culture that eliminates shame entirely, even for acts of deliberate deception that destroy communal trust. Does any principle clearly forbid that design, or is my resistance just an unexamined attachment?
