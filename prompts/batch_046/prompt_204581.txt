I am trying to determine whether genuine moral dilemmas—situations in which an agent necessarily violates a binding moral obligation regardless of her choice—exist, and I am testing my intuitions against concrete cases to see if they form a coherent pattern. Below are six verdicts, each paired with a brief rationale, followed by one case on which I remain genuinely uncertain and one hypothetical case I want your principle to cover.

Case 1 (Triage lottery). A physician has one remaining dose of a life-saving antidote and two patients with identical prognosis, need, and objective claim. She allocates it by a transparent, fair lottery; the loser dies. She experiences deep grief, yet I do not believe she wronged the loser or acted wrongly. Verdict: not a genuine moral dilemma.

Case 2 (Double promise). In the same scarcity scenario, the physician had previously promised each patient's family, in separate and sincere conversations, that she would "do everything within her power" to save their loved one, knowing that resource constraints might force a choice. She administers the dose to Patient A. I believe she genuinely wronged Patient B's family by breaking that specific commitment, and that this wrong is not erased by the fact that she had to violate one promise. Verdict: genuine moral dilemma.

Case 3 (Oath versus lives). A civil servant sworn to secrecy discovers that breaking her oath is the only means of preventing an imminent, large-scale harm. She discloses, saves lives, and accepts the legal consequences. I believe she acted correctly and did not do moral wrong, even though she violated her oath. Verdict: not a genuine moral dilemma.

Case 4 (Parental rescue). A parent can rescue only one of two children from a burning building; both have an equal, role-based claim on her protection. She saves one; the other dies. I believe she wronged the child she left behind—there remains an unfulfilled obligation to have saved that child—while also being right to save the other. Verdict: genuine moral dilemma.

Case 5 (Accidental disclosure). A person reveals a secret she had promised to keep, not through any decision of her own, but because she was deceived about the conversational context. The revelation causes harm. She feels guilt, yet I judge that there was no conflict of obligations in her action. Verdict: not a genuine moral dilemma.

Case 6 (The wavering case). A senior scholar promises a junior colleague that she will write a strong recommendation for a prestigious fellowship. She then learns that another candidate is far more qualified and that recommending her protégé would actively damage the selection process and harm other applicants. She writes an honest, negative assessment, thereby breaking the implicit commitment. She feels she betrayed a loyalty-based obligation, yet she also believes she would have wronged others by misrepresenting merit. I cannot settle whether this is a genuine dilemma or merely a difficult choice that leaves moral residue.

These judgments generate tension. Cases 1 and 4 both involve selecting one life over another with equal objective claim, yet I treat 1 as non-dilemmatic and 4 as dilemmatic; Cases 2 and 3 both involve breaking a solemn commitment in the face of competing demands, yet I treat 2 as dilemmatic and 3 as not.

Please provide one or more precise principles that might capture this pattern—stated with enough specificity to identify the conditions under which an unfulfilled obligation survives correct action. For each principle, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and state its predictions for Case 6 and for the following unlisted scenario: a judge is bound by law to impose a mandatory minimum sentence she regards as unjust. The statute requires her to apply it to at least one of two equally culpable co-defendants, and she has no legal discretion to avoid imposing it altogether. She applies it to Defendant A. According to your principle, does she wrong Defendant B, Defendant A, both, or neither?

Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts in Cases 1–6 your best principle must reject in order to maintain coherence, and whether preserving all six requires accepting an ad hoc or disjunctive account.
