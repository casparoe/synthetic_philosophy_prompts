Write the opening remarks, approximately five minutes when spoken, for the inaugural meeting of a local civic network titled Civic Attention and the Absence of Parties. The audience is mixed: half are experienced community organisers who need practical guidance on running meetings and setting agendas, and half are scholars of political philosophy who will recognise conceptual references and expect precision rather than simplification. Structure the entire speech around Simone Weil’s argument — developed in her writing on political parties — that any collective body tends to generate a shared passion that suppresses individual attention to truth, and that this is not a failure of leadership but an inherent tendency of partisan organisation. Because the network intends to coordinate local action without becoming a party or permanent faction, use Weil’s framework to set explicit structural boundaries: what the group can and cannot promise about loyalty, what it must protect regarding dissent, and what it should treat as a warning sign that coordination has become conformity. For the philosophers, engage seriously with the distinction between attention as a disciplined, receptive orientation toward reality and the collective enthusiasm that replaces it; for the organisers, translate this into operational terms — for example, how an agenda should be framed, how disagreement should be recorded, and what a chair should listen for. Include one concrete procedural test that a meeting chair can apply in real time without philosophical training to detect when attention is being displaced by collective passion. Write in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts or editions, and do not ask the audience to consult further works. Acknowledge explicitly where the Weilian framework reaches its limit and practical judgment must take over, and ensure the speech delivers concrete guidance on meeting structure rather than abstract inspiration.
