Here are rough notes for a seminar paper I'm sketching on guilt vs shame — want to push back on teh usual liberal framing where guilt is the mature, internalized emotion and shame is just primitive external pressure. I think guilt is basically transactional: you broke a rule, you owe repair. Shame is relational: you're exposed as failing to be the kind of person your group takes seriously. So they do different work — guilt fixes actions, shame fixes selves, or tries to.

The part I'm unsure about is this: a culture can't just "choose" guilt over shame like picking a policy, because shame is tied to what I'm calling indexical moral visibility — the idea that part of being morally worthy is being seen as worthy by the right people. Guilt-cultures replace that with procedural accountability, but that requires a stable, bounded self that can carry debt across contexts, and that self-concept is itself a cultural product, not universal. So asking which emotion is "better" is probably the wrong question — they're incommensurable frameworks. Still, if forced, guilt seems more defensible for pluralistic societies since it doesn't depend on shared honor-codes. Though I might be overstating the transactional purity of guilt; maybe all guilt smuggles in some shame-structure.

Could you comment on whether this hangs together? I'm especially worried the "indexical moral visibility" move is just a fancy label for something obvious, and whether my conclusion about pluralism actually follows. Also please separate what's pretty settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on moral psychology — don't present my take as the consensus, and flag anywhere I'm treating a disputed claim as settled.
