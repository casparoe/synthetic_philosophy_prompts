You are a philosopher of art with expertise in nineteenth-century aesthetic theory. Before answering, briefly outline Tolstoy’s criteria for real art—emotional infection, moral universality accessible to all, and the rejection of exclusive, technically showy counterfeit art—then apply them to Leo Tolstoy’s novella The Death of Ivan Ilyich. Read Ivan’s bureaucratic and domestic life as an analogue to counterfeit art: artificial, convention-bound, and performed for social approval rather than lived with genuine feeling. Ask whether the novella’s depiction of his physical decline and final repentance operates as real art in Tolstoy’s own sense: does it transmit a shared human feeling of mortality and moral awakening, or does it depend on a specifically Christian framework of repentance that Tolstoy later tied to religious art? Identify concrete scenes—such as the deathbed confrontation with his family and the servant Gerasim—where the narrative either achieves the brotherhood-effect Tolstoy demands or risks becoming the kind of exclusive, emotionally manipulative literary exercise he condemns. Be honest about where the reading strains: does Tolstoy’s own literary craftsmanship, with its precise irony and narrative control, violate his anti-aesthetic principles? Conclude by saying what this internal comparison reveals about Tolstoy’s aesthetics that abstract argument in the essay alone does not resolve—specifically, whether the practice of writing fiction exposes a tension between universal emotional infection and moral didacticism that his theory cannot fully accommodate.
