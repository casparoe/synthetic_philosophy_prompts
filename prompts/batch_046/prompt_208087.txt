For a feature I am preparing on how classical moral frameworks survive contact with modern neurotechnology, I want to stage a rigorous counterfactual reconstruction. Take Jeremy Bentham—the architect of the felicific calculus and a relentless critic of qualitative distinctions in moral reasoning—and imagine he is confronted with the contemporary philosophical problem of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, specifically the claim that suffering occupies a register incommensurable with pleasure once a certain intensity is crossed, and that this incommensurability is not dissolved but complicated by brain-computer interfaces capable of inducing sustained synthetic euphoria of arbitrary intensity. How would Bentham have responded? Would he have read the lexical threshold as a failure of quantification—arguing that synthetic pleasure can be precisely weighted and therefore that no lexical barrier holds—or would he have found in the incommensurability thesis an implicit concession that the qualitative distinctions he spent his career attacking are, after all, unavoidable? Please construct the answer as a serious philosophical reconstruction rather than historical costume drama: reason from Bentham's actual commitments to quantitative comparison, to his suspicion of metaphysical entities in ethics, and to his view that pleasure and pain are the sole masters of human action. Do not supply bibliographies, cite page numbers, or appeal to secondary literature I could not access offline; I need an argument that stands on conceptual coherence alone.
