I have come to defend a particular interpretation of sphexishness—the mechanical rigidity observed in organisms such as the sphex wasp, which repeats an invariant behavioral loop even when the environment has rendered it maladaptive—not as a mere deficit of flexibility but as revealing a deeper architectural boundary. On my view, if a system's conduct can be exhaustively predicted from a finite-state mapping between environmental triggers and motor outputs, without any internal recursive model of its own future states, then attributing agency or free will to it is not a false empirical claim but a conceptual confusion, analogous to attributing digestion to a thermostat. The rigidity is therefore diagnostic: it indicates the absence of the representational structure that makes the concept of agency intelligible in the first place. I am asking what would genuinely count against this position. I want more than the standard reply that rigid architectures sometimes produce complex or adaptive-looking results; I want to know which specific empirical findings, formal results in cognitive architecture, or conceptual distinctions would show that mechanical rigidity is compatible with some minimal form of agency rather than conceptually excluding it. Which defeaters should I take as fatal—say, a demonstration that recursive self-modeling can emerge from fixed loops under specific conditions—and which are merely awkward adjustments? I also want an honest assessment of whether my position has been insulated from counterexample by definitional fiat, and if it has, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vulnerability that renders the view unfalsifiable in a damaging way. Please rank the considerations from decisive to merely inconvenient, and indicate which ones defenders of this view would themselves concede as damaging.
