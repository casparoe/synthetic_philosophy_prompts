I was at this weird meetup about synthetic biology and my two friends got into it over whether this lab-grown protocell thing counts as alive. Its not one of those viral debates everyone has heard—this thing uses a silicon-phosphorus membrane instead of lipids, and it replicates through a kind of template copying that isnt DNA or RNA. But it does maintain homeostasis and metabolize. Anyway, they went back and forth and I couldn't tell who was actually winning.

Maya said it's clearly alive: it has metabolic closure, it repairs its own boundary, and it reproduces with heritable variation. She said if we found something exactly like it in a Martian subsurface lake we'd be calling it life instantly, so our hesitation is just because we watched engineers build it in a clean room. Leo pushed back hard—he said life isn't just a checklist of processes, it's membership in a continuous evolutionary lineage that goes back to LUCA or at least some common ancestor. He pointed out that viruses fail the metabolism test and we still argue about them, but at least they have genetic material shaped by evolutionary history. This thing was designed top-down by a grad student last year; it has no phylogenetic tree, no deep history of selection. Maya answered that evolutionary history is a contingent fact about Earth life, not a definition—if life on Earth had started with silicon membranes, we'd still call it life. Leo said she's smuggling in an analogy: Earth life shares chemistry because chemistry constrains evolution, but this protocell is an artifact, like a very convincing robot that mimics digestion. Maya then said that by Leo's standard, a mule is arguably not alive because it's a dead-end hybrid with no evolutionary future, which sounds absurd. Leo said that's a cheap shot—mules are alive because they're embedded in living lineages, even if they don't reproduce, whereas this protocell is embedded in nothing biological.

They turned to me and asked who was right about what, and whether there's something both are missing. I honestly don't know—can you go through their exchange point by point? I'm especially curious whether the "designed vs evolved" distinction holds up, or if that's just an intuition pump that collapses once you press on it, and whether either of them is unfairly leaning on how weird the chemistry is.
