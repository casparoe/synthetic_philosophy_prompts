Write the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes, formal but warm—for the retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of science whose career has centered on whether scientific progress can be understood as movement toward truth through the concept of verisimilitude. The audience is split between specialists in philosophy of science, who know that formal quantitative measures of verisimilitude collapsed under technical objections in the 1970s, and family members, graduate students from other fields, and working scientists for whom the word itself is unfamiliar.

The philosophical idea must shape what you can and cannot say. You must explain why “getting closer to the truth” is philosophically treacherous—truth is binary, yet science clearly improves—without ever treating that phrase as an innocent description. You must honor Vasquez’s lifelong dedication without pretending that the quantitative program she helped develop ultimately succeeded, and you must do so without naming specific critics, citing papers, or offering a bibliography or reading list.

To navigate this tension, structure the speech around the following injected framing: scientific progress is not convergence on a hidden point called “truth,” but a form of epistemic cartography. Theories produce increasingly constrained and detailed maps of what is possible; improvement consists in eliminating false paths and sharpening the resolution of the map, not in measuring distance to an unknown destination. Use this metaphor to acknowledge, for the experts, that quantitative verisimilitude failed without dwelling on its technical demise, and to show the non-specialists why Vasquez’s intuition—that science aims at something like truth—remains vital even if the metric she pursued does not. Ensure the family members can follow the emotional arc—devotion to a hard problem, graceful acceptance of its limits, and celebration of a different kind of achievement—while the philosophers recognize that you have replaced a failed formal program with a qualitative, topographical alternative that preserves the core intuition behind her work. Do not include citations, footnotes, or references to specific texts.
