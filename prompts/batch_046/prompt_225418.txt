Here is an argument about pattern-identity across brains that I have been turning over and now find suspicious, because it seems to dissolve the boundary between killing a person and damaging part of one.

(1) If a self is a high-fidelity dynamic pattern of symbolic relationships rather than a substance tied to one brain—call this the patternist thesis—then the self can in principle persist wherever that pattern is functionally reconstructed with sufficient structural correspondence.

(2) Define a distributed instantiation as a case in which the pattern is partitioned across two distinct brains, B and C: B realizes the narrative-memory loops, C realizes the evaluative-deliberative loops, and a continuous real-time synchronization protocol maintains the cross-references that make them one functional system.

(3) In such a case, the full pattern is present and operational, merely spatially dispersed; therefore, by (1), there is exactly one self instantiated across B and C together, not two.

(4) Now suppose B is destroyed. Because the synchronization protocol has kept the loops in C continuously updated, C retains a substantial portion of the pattern—analogous to the way a brain retains identity after losing one hemisphere—so the self is not destroyed; only one substrate-node has been lost.

(5) But if destroying B is not homicide, then B, despite being a conscious, suffering biological entity, lacks the moral protection that attaches to persons, because it is merely a limb of the distributed self.

(6) Conversely, if we insist that B does have independent moral standing, then we must deny that the distributed system in (2) was really one self; yet then we owe an account of why spatial contiguity inside one skull is identity-constituting while functional synchronization across two skulls is not.

(7) So the patternist view seems forced either to deny moral protection to constituent brains or to abandon its own criterion for persistence.

The conclusion feels wrong—surely B's destruction is a grave harm regardless of synchronization—but I want to keep (1). Where does the argument slip? Is it an equivocation on "the pattern is present," a hidden assumption about recoverability, or something about how moral status attaches to biological entities? If I must choose between (1) and (5), I prefer (1), but I want to know exactly what that choice costs me.
