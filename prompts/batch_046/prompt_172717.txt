In debates about Jain epistemology, anekāntavāda—the doctrine of many-sidedness—is often illustrated with the parable of the blind men and the elephant: each touches a different part, reports a different shape, and is partly right about one reality they all fail to grasp whole. The analogy is used to license epistemic humility and to argue that conflicting philosophical or religious claims are like the blind men’s reports, each capturing one aspect of a single, shared object. I want a strict audit of how far this holds inside Jain logic rather than as generic pluralism. Give the correspondence table—what plays the elephant, the blind men, the tactile reports, and the implied observer who knows it is an elephant? Then show where the mapping breaks, especially with respect to syādvāda (conditional, “in some respect” predication) and naya-vāda (standpoints of consideration): does Jainism permit an absolute “elephant” that transcends every naya, or does the analogy smuggle in a syncretic or theistic framework that anekāntavāda rejects? Note also that Jain logic allows explicit contradiction across respects—something can be true in one mode and false in another—whereas the blind men merely suffer incomplete description. Does the analogy’s usual conclusion, that we should tolerate all views as partial truths, survive once that distinction is in place? If the image fails, propose a better analogy for the relation between partial standpoints and an object with infinite, non-summable attributes. Answer in American English.
