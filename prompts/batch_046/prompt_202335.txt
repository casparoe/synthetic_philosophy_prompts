I'm working through a graduate seminar on philosophy of biology, and we've reached an impasse over how etiological (selected-effects) and causal-role (systemic-contribution) accounts of function handle cases where evolutionary origin and present-day operation genuinely pull apart. Most standard illustrations—hearts, eyes, kidneys—are too tidy, because the trait's selection history and its current systemic contribution align neatly. To push the issue, consider the following constructed but biologically plausible scenario: a modular bacterial metabolic pathway originally evolved under selection pressure for oxidative-stress detoxification, then was co-opted in a derived lineage to serve as the primary route for ATP synthesis, and now operates in both capacities simultaneously depending on local cellular redox conditions. The physical structure hasn't bifurcated; the evolutionary narrative points to stress detoxification; the physiological narrative assigns it two distinct system-level jobs. I want to understand not which theory "wins," but how each account's internal conceptual machinery processes this kind of functional multiplicity. Please respond as exactly five numbered points, each one to two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. (1) How does the etiological account decide whether both the ancestral stress-detox role and the derived energy-production role count as proper functions, and what does it say about the ancestral role if current selection primarily maintains the pathway for ATP synthesis? (2) How does the causal-role account distribute functional ascriptions when one physical module makes two non-redundant contributions to different organism-level capacities, and does it allow one contribution to be "more functional" than the other? (3) What conceptual criterion—if any—does each account deploy to distinguish genuine functional multiplicity from mere contextual modulation of a single underlying function? (4) How do the two accounts differ on whether the pathway can malfunction with respect to one contribution (say, stress detoxification) while performing the other (energy production) flawlessly, and does malfunction require failure of all assigned roles or only the "proper" one? (5) What does this divergence reveal about whether biological function is fundamentally a historical property, a present-tense relational property, or an irreducibly plural concept that requires both frameworks?
