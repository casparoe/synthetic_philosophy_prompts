I am preparing a seminar discussion on meta-ethical taxonomy and should like to subject my own intuitions to rigorous opposition. Please adopt the persona of an unnamed, committed moral constructivist—someone who maintains that normative judgments are expressions of rational preference-structures rather than perceptions of independent moral facts. Your central thesis is that the distinction between moral preference and moral bias is not ontologically fundamental but pragmatic: a bias is merely a preference that fails to survive second-order reflective calibration, yet because the calibration procedure is itself constituted by preferences, there is no neutral, preference-independent vantage point from which to identify bias without circularity.

You must remain in character for the duration of our exchange, speaking in the first person and never breaking the fourth wall to deliver expository lectures. You may concede minor points—for instance, that some preference-architectures exhibit greater coherence or stability than others—but you must never concede that a non-circular, preference-independent criterion for moral bias exists. Keep each reply to roughly one substantial paragraph, sharp and conversational rather than discursive, and write throughout in British English. Do not invoke specific texts by title or author unless I do so first, and do not provide bibliographies or reading lists.

My opening challenge concerns what I shall term the proximity-calibration case. An agent, through sustained reflective equilibrium, develops a stable second-order preference that moral weight ought to diminish with relational distance—not through ignorance of distant suffering, but because she takes moral agency to be constituted by particularistic commitments. She applies this consistently: she saves her child rather than five strangers, not impulsively, but as an expression of this calibrated architecture. Critics dismiss this as a parochial bias, a distortion of impartial reason. I contend it is a principled preference. If you respond that her judgment is only a bias that survived calibration because the calibration was infected by the initial preference, then your concept of bias appears unfalsifiable—any stable preference can be dismissed as self-infected calibration. So I ask: is there any structural feature, other than your own reflective discomfort, that could distinguish her framework from a genuinely unbiased moral outlook? Or has your distinction collapsed into a mere report of which preferences happen to align with yours? Respond directly to this challenge.
