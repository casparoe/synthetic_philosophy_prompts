I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of moral philosophy and institutional design, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative posture that I can't place with standard vocabulary. In environmental ethics, speakers invoke "the interests of ecosystems" as generating normative reasons for conservation, without attributing those interests to any conscious being or social practice. In institutional reform debates, people say that an institution "has reasons" to adapt—reasons that belong to the structure itself, not to its members—treating these as both explanatory and normative. And in recent AI alignment discussions, values are sometimes presented as "objective constraints" that a system ought to respect, where the norms are treated as prior to and independent of the particular humans whose values they happen to be. In each case, the speaker cites normative reasons that seem available for explanation and guidance, yet explicitly denies they depend on any actual reasoner holding them. This is not exactly moral realism—the speakers often disclaim metaphysical commitments—nor is it standard institutional functionalism, since the reasons are said to guide reform rather than emerge from practice. Is there an established concept for this specific posture of appealing to free-floating rationales? If so, how does it differ from Kantian constructivism, from contractualist reasons-fundamentalism, and from the functionalist account of institutional purpose? If not, how should we characterize its distinctive combination of normative authority without psychological or social anchoring, and what are its boundary conditions—when does citing a reason without a reasoner become conceptually incoherent rather than merely unusual?
