Take the Thermidoran coup in July 1794 — Robespierre gets dragged out of the Convention, the Terror ends, and within days he's guillotined alongside Saint-Just and Couthon. Analyze this episode through the lens of revolutions devouring their children, but don't just recycle the standard Saturn-and-children line that gets pasted into every undergrad essay. Instead, map the coup onto the three interpretive frames people usually use for the Revolution: class struggle (was this the bourgeois Convention reaserting control over the sans-culote radicals?), ideology (did the rhetoric of virtue and corruption make Robespierre's fall inevitable once he lost control of the narrative?), and contingency (how much turned on Robespierre's specific decison to not name names in his last speech, or on the accidental alliance between the Plain and surviving Girondins?). For each frame, say what it explains well about the coup and where it feels forced. Then tell me whether this case supports the idea that revolutions have a structural tendency to consume their own, or whether it's actually an anomaly that only looks inevitable in retrospect — and what that does to the broader thesis.
