I hold that Nietzsche’s remarks on diet, digestion, climate, and bodily regimen—scattered across Ecce Homo, his correspondence, and the late notebooks—constitute not autobiographical eccentricity but a coherent “gastronomic ethics” continuous with his critique of ascetic ideals and his physiology of culture. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? Rank the defeaters by decisiveness: textual indications that he regarded these prescriptions as purely private or ironic; philosophical arguments that bodily regimen cannot bear the normative and cultural weight the view assigns it; historical or biographical context that severs the link to his broader ethics; and the possibility that defenders have insulated the view by reinterpreting every counterexample as metaphor. I want an honest assessment of which of these a committed proponent should concede as genuinely damaging, which can be dismissed, and whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—plus whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind.
