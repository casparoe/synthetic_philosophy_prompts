Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for philosophical positions on whether clinical pain asymbolia undermines the claim that pain is necessarily bad. Start with a root question answerable without literature knowledge: do you treat first-person pain reports in asymbolic patients as authoritative evidence that pain is present despite the absence of withdrawal, avoidance, or emotional display? From there, branch through three diagnostic questions, each framed as a claim about a concrete case: (1) whether the missing motivational response indicates an absent affective quality, an intact but blocked conative pathway, or a dissociation between sensory representation and practical reasoning; (2) whether the badness of ordinary pain resides primarily in its sensory phenomenology, in its representational content about bodily damage, or in its disruption of agency and long-term planning; (3) whether "badness without aversion" is conceptually coherent, or whether it collapses into either non-bad sensation or hidden aversion. Each leaf should carry a position name if one is standard (e.g., pure representationalism, pure imperativism, hybrid affective-representationalism) or be marked "unnamed/unoccupied" with a one-sentence diagnosis of why the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Explicitly inject one non-standard hybrid leaf—treating asymbolic pain as derivatively bad via a violated normative expectation of self-protection rather than via intrinsic quality or direct motivation—and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate. Note which feature of the clinical case does the diagnostic work, and describe what happens to the taxonomy if asymbolia is reinterpreted as misreporting rather than dissociation. Keep every node phrased so that a respondent can answer it by reflecting on the scenario rather than by recognizing an author's label.
