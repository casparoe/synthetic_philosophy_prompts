I'm trying to map my own judgments about Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles by working through variations on Black's spheres and a few adjacent thought experiments. Here are my verdicts on specific cases; I want to know whether they hang together under one principle, or whether consistency forces me to abandon one.

Case 1 — Black's original scenario: two iron spheres of identical size, temperature, and surface texture in an otherwise empty universe, separated by two meters. Verdict: there are two spheres. They share every intrinsic property, and the separation relation is symmetric, so they share every relational property as well. The identity of indiscernibles therefore fails for concrete physical objects.

Case 2 — The same two spheres, but now a distant star sits three meters from one sphere and five from the other. Verdict: still two spheres, but now they differ in a non-symmetric relational property. This doesn't rehabilitate the principle; it merely shows that relations can distinguish when they are asymmetrical.

Case 3 — Three spheres arranged in a perfect equilateral triangle in an otherwise empty universe, all otherwise identical. Verdict: three spheres. Each bears exactly the same relational profile to the others (two neighbors at distance d). So symmetric relational networks do not collapse multiplicity into unity.

Case 4 — A homogeneous, boundary-less expanse of water—no containers, no internal structure, uniform density and temperature everywhere, extending without limit. Verdict: there are not "many parts" of water here; any division into regions is arbitrary and lacks ontological weight. So qualitative identity across regions does seem to entail the absence of distinct individuals.

Case 5 — Two possible worlds that are perfect qualitative duplicates except that in W1 sphere A occupies region R1 and sphere B occupies R2, while in W2 they are swapped. Verdict: W1 and W2 are the same world. I reject primitive thisness or haecceity; permuting indistinguishable objects does not generate a distinct possibility.

Case 6 — A single sphere exists alone for eternity, is annihilated, and is replaced by a qualitatively identical sphere in the same location (assuming location can be defined without other objects). Verdict: I am genuinely divided. If there is causal continuity I lean toward sameness; if the replacement is discontinuous I lean toward difference. But I worry that appealing to causal history smuggles in a hidden qualitative difference, which would make my verdict in Case 1 unstable.

My questions: Is there a single principle—perhaps appealing to discreteness, boundary conditions, causal continuity, or the ontology of objects versus continuous stuffs—that delivers all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should I overturn: my acceptance of distinct spheres in symmetric configurations (Cases 1 and 3), my denial of arbitrary parts in the homogeneous fluid (Case 4), or my rejection of haecceity (Case 5)? Please also state what your best principle predicts for an unlisted case: a universe containing exactly two left-handed and two right-handed gloves arranged with perfect rotational symmetry, where chirality is intrinsic but each chirality-type is duplicated, so that every glove shares its full qualitative profile with another. Finally, please separate what is settled in the philosophical literature on the identity of indiscernibles from what remains genuinely contested among metaphysicians who work on the topic, and do not present any controversial interpretation—such as a particular structural-realist or trope-theoretic reading—as the consensus view.
