I was recently discussing with colleagues in a course on comparative historical sociology the problem of forecasting state failure using what Tetlock and others call the outside view—aggregating base rates across reference classes rather than relying on inside narratives. We were applying this to imperial decline, specifically constructing a synthetic reference class of large, territorially contiguous agrarian-bureaucratic polities that experienced rapid fiscal-military overextension followed by peripheral tribal infiltration, and asking whether modern quantitative forecasters should weight this class more heavily than narrative-driven case studies when estimating the resilience of contemporary federal structures.

This led me to wonder about a counterfactual: if Ibn Khaldun, whose cyclical theory of state formation and dissolution centered on asabiyya, the erosion of group solidarity through sedentary luxury, and the inevitable conquest by fresh tribal forces with stronger cohesion, were presented with this modern forecasting apparatus, how would he evaluate its legitimacy?

Specifically, I am not asking for a bibliography or citation list, but rather a reasoned reconstruction of his likely stance on three interlocking issues. First, would he accept that his own qualitative cycle—Bedouin vigor giving way to urban decadence and eventual overthrow—could be operationalized into a quantitative reference class with explicit base rates for time-to-collapse without destroying the causal mechanism he thought essential? Second, given his skepticism about astrology and his preference for observable sociological dynamics, how might he regard the modern superforecasting emphasis on calibration and reference-class aggregation as either a fulfillment or a betrayal of his methodological commitments? Third, there is a niche conceptual tension I want to inject: some contemporary forecasters propose weighting reference classes not by superficial institutional resemblance but by mechanistic homology—matching underlying causal pathways, such as fiscal extraction crises coupled with elite fragmentation, rather than surface features like monarchy versus republic. Would Khaldun likely argue that this mechanistic weighting actually recovers the spirit of his asabiyya analysis, or would he insist that the qualitative, almost biological vitality of group feeling cannot be captured by structural homology across radically different historical contexts?

Please construct a plausible view, noting where he might concede that quantitative base rates reveal patterns his narrative method missed, and where he would likely resist reducing cyclical renewal to probabilistic forecasting. I want this reconstructed without appeals to modern scholarship he could not have read—just reasoning from the conceptual architecture he actually developed.
