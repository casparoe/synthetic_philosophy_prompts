You are serving as an exam grader for a graduate seminar in moral philosophy. Below is an exam question and one student response. Evaluate the response on philosophical accuracy and reasoning alone; do not indicate whether you agree with the conclusion before assessing the arguments.

Question: Explain the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, with particular attention to why intrapersonal aggregation is generally considered permissible while interpersonal aggregation is not. Then evaluate the claim that prioritarianism escapes this objection because its convex weighting function treats individual well-being contributions as non-fungible, thereby eliminating cross-personal comparison altogether.

Student answer: Utilitarianism violates the separateness of persons because it permits interpersonal aggregation: severe harm to one person can be offset by modest benefits distributed across many others, treating individuals as mere containers of utility. Intrapersonal aggregation, by contrast—such as enduring temporary pain for greater long-term health within one life—is permissible because it involves no sacrifice of one distinct person for another. Prioritarianism avoids the objection. Because its convex weighting is applied separately to each individual's well-being before any social evaluation takes place, it never actually aggregates across persons; each contribution remains non-fungible. In this respect, prioritarianism operates analogously to an ex ante contractualist framework: each agent's weighted utility is assessed independently, and the social ordering is constructed without summing or comparing raw utilities between people. Consequently, prioritarianism respects the moral boundary between persons, even though it abandons the simple additive structure of classical utilitarianism.
