I'm drafting an analysis for my science-and-ideas column on a tension I've noticed between Nozick's entitlement theory and the contingency of historical chains. I want to road-test an argument that seems to collapse entitlement into historical determinism. Here is the chain I have in mind:

1. Under Nozick's entitlement theory, a distribution is just if every holding traces back to just original acquisition or to just transfer from someone already entitled.
2. The Wilt Chamberlain argument shows that liberty upsets patterns: if people freely exchange resources with Wilt Chamberlain, the resulting inequality is just despite breaking an initial egalitarian pattern.
3. Now inject historical contingency: for any actual entitlement chain—say, a property title tracing back to a nineteenth-century land grant—there exists a nearby counterfactual world in which a minor divergence (a missed voyage, a different election outcome, an early death) breaks the chain at its origin.
4. Because historical inevitability is false, no entitlement chain is counterfactually robust; every link depends on events that easily might not have occurred.
5. Therefore, to claim that a present holding is just, one must implicitly treat the chain leading to it as historically inevitable—otherwise the justification appeals to sheer accident.
6. But treating any specific chain as historically inevitable contradicts the standard view that history is highly contingent.
7. So either we accept that no current holding is fully justified (since every chain contains contingent breaks in nearby worlds), or we abandon the contingency of history.
8. The latter is implausible, so entitlement theory seems to prove that justice in holdings is impossible.

Where does this argument go wrong? Which premise should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost—either for Nozick's framework or for the critique of historical contingency? I am particularly interested in whether step 5 commits a modal fallacy by confusing actual justification with counterfactual robustness. Please diagnose the failure, explain why the rejected step initially looked plausible, and price the alternatives. In your response, state explicitly how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the specific point about which you are least sure—especially whether the counterfactual fragility of entitlement chains is a genuine philosophical problem or a red herring.
