I serve on the neuroethics review board of a university-affiliated medical center, and we must reach a decision by next Thursday’s meeting on a proposed pilot protocol for an adaptive, closed-loop deep brain stimulation system designed for severe, treatment-resistant anhedonia. The device continuously monitors real-time activity in the ventral striatum and nucleus accumbens, then autonomously adjusts stimulation parameters to maximize sustained positive affect ratings submitted through a patient-facing digital interface. The research team contends this is a refined form of conventional neuromodulation—functionally analogous to adjusting an antidepressant regimen—arguing that it restores a dysregulated reward circuit rather than altering it artificially. A substantial faction of the board, however, is concerned that the algorithm does not merely correct baseline function but actively restructures the patient’s reward predictions—what they describe as endogenous reward restructuring—potentially producing a state in which the patient reports high subjective well-being while objective social engagement, occupational functioning, and autonomous decision-making quietly deteriorate. They frame this risk as institutionalized wireheading: a therapeutic mechanism that severs the usual connection between well-being and actual life conditions, compounded by the protocol’s proposed value-lock safeguard, which freezes optimization targets to the patient’s pre-implantation stated preferences and thereby may prevent the patient’s present self from authentically revising those values.

Please draft the board’s formal written opinion—approximately two to three pages in institutional prose—for circulation before Thursday’s session. The document should open with an explicit, actionable recommendation: approve the pilot outright, approve with concrete conditions and monitoring milestones tied to a specific review timeline, or reject, together with the ethical threshold that drives the decision. Your reasoning should engage seriously with the wireheading objection, distinguishing adaptive reward optimization from conventional pharmacotherapy in terms clinicians can apply, and should address the value-lock problem directly—whether anchoring the algorithm to pre-treatment preferences genuinely protects autonomy or instead creates a frozen-preference trap that blocks legitimate value change. You must also treat honestly the strongest consideration on the opposing side, not merely acknowledge it in passing. If you recommend approval, specify enforceable conditions—such as mandatory independent functional-assessment checkpoints at defined intervals, a sunset clause requiring demonstrated preservation of objective life-functioning metrics, or restrictions on the algorithm’s autonomy to alter reward-weighting without clinician override. If you recommend rejection, identify precisely which ethical boundary the protocol crosses and why less restrictive alternatives are insufficient. The memo should be usable by clinicians, ethicists, and patient advocates without bibliographic apparatus, since the board needs an immediate decision framework rather than a literature review.
