I am in the final stages of drafting a biography of a nineteenth-century public-health reformer whose sanitation engineering reduced urban mortality by orders of magnitude, but who also designed racial segregation protocols for hospital wards that he understood as scientific hygiene. Every evaluative sentence I write—crediting his epidemiological rigor, condemning his racial taxonomy—seems to risk either anachronistic moralism or evasive neutrality. To clarify my own methodological commitments, I need a diagnostic taxonomy, structured as a branching decision tree, that sorts the actual positions historians take on whether present-day moral and conceptual standards can be escaped when judging past actors.

The root node should ask whether the historian’s own temporal situatedness makes normative evaluation of past actors unavoidable, or whether such evaluation is a bracketable methodological error. From there, build at least five branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete claim or case rather than as a label. The nodes should cover: first, whether recognizing long-term structural harm that was conceptually unavailable to the actor—for instance, the institutionalization of racial medicine—requires applying categories the actor could not have possessed; second, whether reconstructing an actor’s internal reasons necessarily amounts to excusing those reasons; third, whether a stable distinction exists between describing a past normative system and evaluating it; fourth, whether a historian can maintain what I term a chronologically parallaxed stance, holding the actor’s framework and the present framework in simultaneous view without collapsing either into the other; and fifth, whether the magnitude of harm alters the obligation to judge, or whether consistency demands uniform application regardless of scale.

The leaves should include strong presentism; hermeneutic historicism, in which evaluation is bracketed in favor of internal reconstruction; dual-perspective evaluation; methodological agnosticism; and at least one logically possible but rarely defended combination—for example, a view that insists on present moral judgment while denying that present conceptual categories are legitimate tools for that judgment. For each leaf, provide its designation and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Please also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, particularly where they diverge only in rhetorical framing rather than in practical historiographical method. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I intend to use this as a personal diagnostic instrument in a methodological appendix, and I need to apply it without consulting external sources.
