Write a dialogue between two equally competent metaphysicians—one defending David Lewis’s counterpart theory and one defending a Kripkean view of transworld identity—debating the modal status of a specific hypothetical subject, call her M. In the actual world, M is a concert pianist whose self-conception and professional identity are deeply tied to perfect pitch and to a particular neural auditory pathway established in infancy. In a nearby possible world, an almost imperceptible variation in that same infancy pathway leaves her without perfect pitch, though her psychological continuity, autobiographical memories, and social relationships remain otherwise intact. The counterpart theorist should argue that the auditory architecture is essential to M, so the nearby-world individual is merely a counterpart, not M herself; she should also defend the claim that counterpart relations are grounded in qualitative similarity and can therefore fail across long modal chains, addressing what happens if one constructs an intermediate chain of worlds in which each adjacent pair differs by only one minute neural feature, but the cumulative difference between the first and last world is drastic—so that direct counterpart relations may be absent even when indirect ones link the extremes. The transworld-identity theorist should counter that perfect pitch is an accidental property, that it is M herself who exists in that world lacking it, and that the counterpart theorist’s reliance on similarity relations is either arbitrary or secretly presupposes the very transworld identity it attempts to deny; he should press the point that if only indirect counterpart relations connect the extremes of the chain, then counterpart theory cannot explain our robust intuition that M “could have turned out very differently” without being numerically replaced. Both characters must respond directly to each other’s actual arguments—no talking past one another—and the dialogue should conclude without either side conceding defeat, but with the precise metaphysical crux clearly exposed: whether modal intuitions about personal possibility are better explained by a single individual bearing accidental differences across worlds or by a network of similar but numerically distinct world-bound counterparts. The dialogue should be technically precise but accessible to someone with an upper-level background in metaphysics, and it must not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to obscure literature.
