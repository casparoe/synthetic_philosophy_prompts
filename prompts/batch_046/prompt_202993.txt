As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and epistemology, I recently came across a provocative argument—circulated in an unpublished working paper and discussed among researchers—that seems to collapse first-person authority by combining two familiar principles. The author accepts the KK principle, that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p, and applies it to the thesis of luminosity: if one is in a mental state such as feeling cold or being in pain, one is in a position to know it. The argument runs as follows. (1) Luminosity, if true for any mental state M, implies that being in M puts one in a position to know one is in M. (2) By KK, knowing that one is in M entails knowing that one knows that one is in M, generating an infinite regress of nested knowledge states. (3) The author then introduces what he terms a cognitive stack limit: empirical work in social cognition suggests that explicit, recursive representations of mental states—e.g., I think that you think that she thinks...—rarely extend beyond four levels without cognitive breakdown. (4) Therefore, satisfying the full KK regress for any luminous state would require a representational architecture that exceeds our psychological capacity. (5) Hence, luminosity cannot hold for any mental state that triggers this regress, which includes basic sensations and beliefs. (6) The conclusion drawn is that first-person authority is not merely limited but eliminable: if we cannot satisfy the knowledge conditions for self-attribution, mental-state ascriptions must be grounded solely in third-person behavioral criteria. The author treats this not as a reductio but as support for a strong form of eliminativism about inner life. I suspect the error lies somewhere between (3) and (5)—perhaps conflating explicit recursive representation with implicit or dispositional knowledge, or sliding from a psychological limit on articulation to an epistemic impossibility—but I cannot isolate it precisely. Which premise should be rejected, and at what cost? Would abandoning (3) preserve luminosity without undermining KK, or does the argument instead reveal that KK must be restricted to avoid absurd demands on cognitive architecture?
