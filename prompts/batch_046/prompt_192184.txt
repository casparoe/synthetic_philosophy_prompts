I just came away from a long argument with a friend about whether an old philanthropic charter is secretly a philosophical manifesto. She insists that the rules of the Northbridge Arts and Civic Trust — a foundation whose charter, adopted in 1923 and never amended, governs all grantmaking — are an institutional expression of G. E. Moore’s view that beauty and friendship are the sole intrinsic goods and that they must remain categorically separated. I think they are arbitrary bureaucratic partitions that any number of value theories could accommodate. I want an outside, philosophically precise reconstruction.

You are an expert in early analytic ethics, with particular attention to G. E. Moore’s Principia Ethica, his account of intrinsic value, the principle of organic unity in aesthetic goods, and his treatment of personal affection as an independent intrinsic good. Before answering, please take a moment to brainstorm: list each structural feature of the charter I describe below, identify the specific view of value or of these goods that each feature strictly requires, and flag every point where the rules are philosophically underdetermined so that rival theories could license them. Then think step by step: distinguish what the practice genuinely presupposes about the nature of beauty and friendship from what it merely fits well with; assess whether the separation and weighting rules are coherent with Moore’s specific claims or with other pluralist, hierarchical, or monist positions; and determine whether the practice rules out competing theories or is compatible with several.

Here is the practice in concrete detail. The Northbridge Trust divides every grant proposal into exactly two evaluation tracks. Track One is scored for contribution to Organic Unity — understood as the kind of aesthetic value Moore discusses, where the value of a complex whole is not proportional to the sum of the values of its parts. Track Two is scored for contribution to Personal Affection — understood as the direct value of intimate friendship between specific persons, independent of any further consequence. The charter contains a strict separation clause: no proposal may receive funding if it serves both tracks simultaneously, regardless of how highly it scores in each. Furthermore, the evaluation rubric assigns exactly fifty points to each track, with no mechanism for context-sensitive reweighting. Last year, a proposal for a neighborhood mural designed both to achieve formal aesthetic unity and to serve as a regular gathering place for isolated elderly residents was rejected solely because it violated the separation clause, despite receiving near-perfect scores in both tracks.

What philosophical commitments does this institutional design presuppose? Does the separation clause require that beauty and friendship are incommensurable goods, or could it be justified by administrative tractability, conflict of interest, or institutional mission alone? Does the fixed fifty-fifty weighting presuppose that they carry equal intrinsic weight, or only that they are evaluated on independent dimensions? Must the charter be read as endorsing Moore’s specific thesis that organic unity and personal affection are the only intrinsic goods, or is it compatible with a broader pluralism that recognizes other intrinsic values — or even with a monist view that treats both as substitutable instrumental contributions to well-being? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value embedded in these rules, say whether its parts are mutually consistent, and tell me exactly which provisions — the separation clause, the weighting scheme, the rejection of hybrid proposals — would have to change if the underlying metaphysics of value were replaced by one on which all goods are ultimately fungible contributions to a single measure of welfare. In particular, address whether the practice’s insistence on non-combination reflects any deep metaphysical claim about the nature of intrinsic goods, or whether it is merely a contingent design choice that different value theories could preserve or abandon for different reasons.
