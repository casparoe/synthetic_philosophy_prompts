Analyze the Bay of Pigs invasion of April 1961 as a case study in inside-view versus outside-view reasoning and epistemic modesty. The salient facts are these: in April 1961, the U.S. government, under President John F. Kennedy, supported a CIA-trained force of Cuban exiles (Brigade 2506) in an amphibious landing at the Bay of Pigs, with the strategic aim of triggering a broader anti-Castro uprising inside Cuba. The operation was expected to remain plausibly deniable, to receive limited direct U.S. air support, and to face weak resistance from Cuban forces. Instead, the landing was quickly contained by Castro's military, air cover was withheld, the exile force was decimated or captured within seventy-two hours, and the failure severely damaged U.S. credibility.

Your analysis should run in both directions—using the theory to illuminate the episode, and using the episode to test the theory.

First, apply the framework to the original planners. Identify the inside view available to Kennedy and his senior advisors: specific intelligence assessments about Castro's domestic popularity, the combat readiness of the exile brigade, the likely behavior of the Cuban air force, and the belief that direct U.S. involvement could be concealed. Then identify the outside-view reference classes that were available at the time—historical base rates for U.S.-backed paramilitary invasions, for regime-change operations led by exile forces without sustained great-power air support, and for military actions in which political leaders restrict operational options for diplomatic reasons. Say what epistemic modesty, interpreted through an outside-view lens, should have compelled the planners to conclude about the reliability of their own forecasts. Be explicit about who counts as the decision-maker, what counts as evidence, and what the theory predicts they should have done differently.

Second, apply the framework recursively to retrospective analysis. Political scientists and historians commonly explain the failure by citing groupthink, overconfidence, and an inside-view fixation among the planners. Treat this retrospective diagnosis as itself a belief subject to inside/outside evaluation. What is the inside view of the analyst—access to declassified records, awareness of specific cognitive biases like groupthink, the narrative coherence of attributing failure to psychological error? What outside view applies—say, the base rate at which post-hoc explanations of policy failures invoke "groupthink" or "inside-view error," or the historical frequency with which retrospective analysts correctly identify the decisive causal factor in complex bureaucratic decisions? Does the claim that "inside-view reasoning caused the Bay of Pigs" survive its own outside-view check, or does it risk being a just-so story? This second-order layer is central to the prompt.

Where does the theory remain silent—perhaps on how to weigh moral and political responsibility when an outside-view forecast conflicts with a specific, credible expert assessment?
