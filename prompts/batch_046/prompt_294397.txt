As a retired physicist who has spent the last several years working through philosophy of time and metaphysics in a small study group, I have become preoccupied with James Thomson’s lamp and the broader family of supertask paradoxes. In my scientific training, infinite sequences were always treated as limits rather than as completed processes executed in finite duration, and I find the philosophical debate about whether a lamp toggled at intervals of 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, … minutes can have a well-defined state at the two-minute mark both fascinating and frustratingly under-specified. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific papers; what I need is a conceptual sorting instrument I can apply to my own intuitions and to those of fellow discussants who, like me, come from technical backgrounds.

Please construct a decision tree—built from logical distinctions rather than from named theories—that takes a respondent from the single starting commitment “I accept that an infinite sequence of discrete actions can in principle be completed within a finite interval of time” to a specific position on the status of the lamp at the deadline. Each internal node should be phrased as a concrete claim or case distinction that someone could answer without already knowing which philosophical label it leads to; for example, whether the lamp’s state at the limit point is determined by the limit of its prior on/off sequence, whether the switch mechanism retains causal efficacy at the exact moment of completion, or whether one distinguishes kinematic possibility (compatibility with the geometry of the trajectory) from dynamic possibility (compatibility with the governing laws of the mechanism). The tree must include leaves for at least the following positions: the view that the scenario is impossible because it entails a contradiction about the lamp’s state; the view that the state is genuinely indeterminate at the limit; the view that it possesses a definite state determined by some non-limit property of the mechanism; a view that invokes temporal parts lacking well-defined persistence conditions at the limit point; and at least one “unnamed” combination that your branching logic permits but that no prominent author defends. For every leaf, provide the position’s common name if it has one (otherwise mark it “unnamed”) together with exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—what it must deny or accept that strikes pre-philosophical common sense as strange.

In addition, I want the tree to incorporate a niche distinction drawn more from physics and mathematics than from standard metaphysical taxonomies: include a branch asking whether accepting a non-Archimedean ordering of temporal moments resolves the paradox, and whether any impossibility involved is logical, physical in the sense of violating a dynamical law, or metaphysical in the sense that material objects simply do not instantiate determinate properties at limit points. Please flag explicitly any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate—where different answer patterns converge on the same position, or where one named view occupies more than one branch. Finally, identify all combinations of answers your tree generates that correspond to no named view, and for each empty cell briefly diagnose whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored in the literature.
