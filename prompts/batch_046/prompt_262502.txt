Maya said she basically only reads like eight substacks and a few old blogs, and she doesn't feel guilty. Her prior that some random twitter thread is noise is like 90%, so filtering hard is rational — it's not bias, it's efficiency. She said an epistemic bubble is only harmful if you can't escape it, but she can click any link; she's just choosing not to. She doesn't think outsiders are malicious, just likely wrong.

Leo said that's missing the actual mechanism. Once you build what he called a credibility recursion loop, your inside sources rate each other highly and outsiders get near-zero weight not because you've evaluated them, but because they don't cite the inside crew. Your prior doesn't stay independent; it gets infected by the loop itself. So that 90% isn't a stable belief, it's an artifact.

Maya came back: every field does this. Peer review is literally credibility recursion. The difference is external anchors — experiments that can fail. Her circle anchors to predictions and bets, not vibes. If they're consistently right about real-world stuff, that's tracking, not circularity.

Leo said but in practice the "real world" gets interpreted through the same loop. When a prediction fails, they update slowly inside shared framing or blame institutional interference — there's no falsification path that originates outside. He said she's not in a bubble (missing info) but in an echo chamber, because outsiders aren't just unseen, they're pre-emptively assigned near-zero credibility via prior weighting. He called it closure by prior weighting.

Maya said she doesn't discredit outsiders as evil, just as noisy, so it's a bubble not an echo chamber per the usual distinction. Leo said that distinction collapses once your prior weighting does the discrediting for you — functionally identical.
