Take the 1960s–1980s split-brain research programme led by Sperry and Gazzaniga — specifically the scientific and philosophical controversy over whether commissurotomy produces two distinct conscious subjects or one divided consciousness — and analyse it through the concept of epistemic sovereignty: the idea that the left hemisphere's verbal reports do not merely describe experience but monopolise the institutional authority to determine what counts as the patient's mental life, thereby relegating right-hemisphere behavioural outputs to the status of unconscious reflex. How well does this framing explain the researchers' interpretation of their data, the resistance from philosophers who defended unitary personhood, and the asymmetry in how each hemisphere's responses were evaluated? Conversely, what does the episode reveal about the concept itself — does it illuminate a genuine feature of consciousness attribution, or does the split-brain case show that epistemic sovereignty collapses into familiar problems of first-person authority and behavioural interpretation?
