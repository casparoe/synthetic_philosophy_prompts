Consider the analogy, common in discussions of psychiatric classification and Ian Hacking's looping effects, that constructing a diagnostic manual like the DSM is like drawing a map of an independent territory. Realists invoke it to insist that good classification must match a pre-existing landscape of mental disorders; constructivists invoke it to argue that publishing the map changes the territory—patients, clinicians, and institutions begin to navigate by it, so the landscape is partly constituted by the cartographic act itself. Audit this analogy systematically: specify the correspondences (what plays the role of the cartographer, the territory, the scale, the legend, and the traveler?), identify precisely where the mapping holds and where it collapses, and determine which features of geographic mapping are quietly imported into psychiatric taxonomy that the target either lacks or radically transforms. In particular, examine whether the analogy obscures a problem of resolution—epidemiological surveys, structured clinical interviews, and lay self-identification all deploy the same diagnostic "map" at radically different granularities, with no fixed scale factor—and whether Hacking's looping effect operates less like rivers reshaping in response to a map and more like a legend that rewrites its own symbols as travelers learn to read them. Does the map-territory framing genuinely support the realist-constructivist stalemate, or does it smuggle in assumptions about spatial independence, single-scale representation, and observer-neutrality that neither side should accept? If the analogy fails at structurally important points, propose a more apt analogy and defend why its correspondences survive the audit that this one does not.
