I have just come away from a long argument with a close friend about consciousness, narrative, and whether there is any 'place' where experience comes together. We were discussing Dennett's multiple drafts model as an alternative to the Cartesian theater — the rejection of a single central observer for whom all mental content is displayed — and my friend kept pressing a particular objection: if every experience is just one of many competing drafts, with no authoritative premiere, then introspection itself must also be just another draft among others, not a privileged report on the rest. I agreed, but then we got stuck on whether that means the feeling of 'being present right now' is itself merely a retrospective editorial revision rather than an immediate illumination.

I want to close this discussion with a poem of roughly 14–16 lines, free verse but with a distinct structural turn around the middle (around line 8 or 9). The poem should not merely name-check the theater and the drafts; it should enact the idea structurally. Perhaps use interrupted syntax, shifting pronouns, or a refrain that mutates each time, so that no stanza serves as the definitive center. The turn should be the moment when a single, unified voice seems to emerge — the pull of the Cartesian theater — only to be dispersed by the very mechanism that seemed to produce it. Crucially, the poem should leave open whether that dispersion is a loss or a liberation; it should not resolve into either a sermon about fragmentation or a hidden restoration of unity, since that is precisely where our argument stalled.

Keep the imagery concrete rather than abstract — no lists of brain regions, no direct citations.
