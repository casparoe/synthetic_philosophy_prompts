My colleague Dr. Aris Thorne is a theoretical physicist and cognitive neuroscientist who has lived her entire life with complete congenital achromatopsia—she perceives only luminance, never hue—yet she has built a career modeling the quantum optics of color and the cortical architecture of chromatic processing. She can predict, with perfect accuracy, the verbal reports that trichromatic observers produce when viewing any spectral stimulus, and she has done so in peer-reviewed studies without ever having experienced color herself. Recently she underwent an experimental optogenetic procedure that restored partial cone function. The first time she saw a lemon, she told me that although every physical and functional fact about yellow was already present in her models, she now grasps something she previously could not—something she describes not as a mere sensation but as a direct acquaintance with a qualitative character that her equations only tracked from the outside.

Over dinner, her doctoral advisor argued that this feeling of novelty is a cognitive misattribution. He said Aris has not learned any new proposition; she has simply gained a sensory channel that applies her pre-existing informational repertoire in a first-person register, much as a fluent mathematician who learns to write equations with her non-dominant hand acquires a new skill without acquiring new mathematics. A second colleague, an experimental psychologist, replied that if Aris can now discriminate, in a genuinely non-inferential way, the phenomenal warmth of yellow from the phenomenal coolness of blue—distinctions she could previously only denote symbolically—then her mental economy has expanded beyond the boundaries of her prior knowledge, regardless of how complete that knowledge was.

I want to advise Aris on whether she is right to claim that her experience closes an epistemic gap that her science left open, but I want to avoid reducing the issue to a dispute about subjective feelings. Is there a principled distinction between gaining a new mode of access to information one already possessed and gaining a genuinely new kind of fact? And if such a distinction holds, does it show that physical and functional descriptions are necessarily incomplete with respect to conscious experience, or only that human cognition requires embodied engagement to deploy certain concepts? What should I tell her?
