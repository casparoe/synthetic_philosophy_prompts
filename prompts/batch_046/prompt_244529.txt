As a journalist covering the boundary between physics, biology, and philosophy of science, I routinely encounter press releases and preprints claiming that some parameter—a cosmological constant, a biochemical rate, or a neural threshold—is "fine-tuned" for life, consciousness, or complexity. I need a reusable editorial decision procedure that I can run in under forty minutes without a philosophy background, that fixes whether I should frame such a claim as empirical science, as a philosophical argument requiring contextual framing, or as a category error that should not be reported under scientific authority. The procedure must be grounded in the actual logical structure of the fine-tuning argument—parameter-range comparison, the problem of probability measures over unbounded spaces, and the distinction between explaining a value and selecting for an outcome—not in popular shorthand.

Please construct an ordered flow of questions in plain language: the sequence I must follow, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the exact point where the procedure terminates with a clear "do not frame as fine-tuning," and the explicit judgment nodes where editorial values or institutional judgment must replace the checklist. Include a small set of quick diagnostic tests—conditions or sample sentences I can apply in minutes—to distinguish genuine empirical parameter sensitivity from post-hoc range selection, and to flag when the comparison range is defined solely by the outcome being explained. The procedure should specify what expertise is required at each step, where I must seek sign-off from my editor rather than decide alone, and what to do when I lack the quantitative background to assess a range claim.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases: (a) a cosmological study arguing that the cosmological constant falls within one part in 10^120 of a life-permitting window, with no discussion of multiverse hypotheses or unknown dynamics; (b) a neuroscience claim that cortical firing thresholds are "tuned" specifically for conscious integration, where the permitted range is defined entirely by the presence of consciousness itself; and (c) a biochemical report that water's viscosity is precisely set for snowflake formation, where the authors acknowledge that alternative physical laws are conceivable but dismiss them without argument. Include at least one case where the procedure directs me to a framing I will find journalistically unsatisfying, and explain precisely why the checklist forces that result.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which philosophical disagreements it leaves unresolved despite its appearance of neutrality, which hidden commitments it smuggles in through its framing choices, and the specific kinds of claim for which it offers no guidance at all.
