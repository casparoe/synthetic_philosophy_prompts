I have been studying Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist argument in the Tahāfut, where he denies that fire burns cotton through any intrinsic causal power and insists that God creates the blackening and separation directly at each instant, with the contact of fire serving only as an occasion. Reading this against modern philosophy of science, I notice that both Humean regularity theories and structural realist accounts of law reject necessary connections and treat natural regularities as patterns without hidden productive machinery. This suggests a reconciliation I find hard to resist: Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is not a pre-modern obstacle to science but its only fully consistent metaphysics, since it removes the philosopher's redundant assumption that correlations require underlying causal powers to be rationally intelligible. If that is right, then scientific explanation does not fail under occasionalism; rather, occasionalism completes science by recognizing that invariant patterns need no further ontological ground. So my question is this: if Hume and Al-Ghazālī both deny necessary connections in nature, why do philosophers of science still treat occasionalism as a threat to scientific realism rather than as its natural foundation? And should we conclude that Al-Ghazālī's real error is not the denial of secondary causation, but his failure to see that science never required causation in the first place—making his theology an unnecessary supplement to an already self-sufficient regularity theory?
