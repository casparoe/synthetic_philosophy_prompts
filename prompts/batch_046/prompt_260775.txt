They were arguing about whether Malthus can be rescued as 'right but early' or whether he's just flat wrong. (1) She said that his Essay made a predictive claim about imminent crisis that clearly failed, and that calling it 'early' is just a post-hoc rescue—if you can always push the date back, the theory becomes unfalsifiable in a Popperian sense. (2) He replied that long-run structural theories don't falsify like weather forecasts; the core mechanism—exponential population growth outpacing linear resource growth—wasn't disproven, it was merely delayed by innovations he couldn't have foreseen. (3) She hit back that without specifying exactly when innovation counts as a legitimate delaying factor versus an ad hoc exception, he's not defending Malthus, he's defending an empty shell that can absorb any counter-evidance. (4) He countered that we can specify conditions—say, sustained periods where agricultural productivity growth falls below population growth—and that climate models work the same way, being evaluated over centuries rather than decades.

Can you go through these four moves and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right? Also, what are both of them missing about how long-run predictions actually get rescued or abandoned in practice? Please separate what's fairly settled in the philosophy of science about falsification and temporal scope from what's genuinely contested—don't present either of their positions as the obvious consensus.
