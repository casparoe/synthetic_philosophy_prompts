You are advising a close friend who has been offered a senior security position with a private logistics firm that delivers supplies across an internationally recognized blockade. Humanitarian organizations widely report that the blockade is causing severe civilian starvation, and a local armed resistance—partly composed of conscripted teenagers and partly of volunteers—has begun attacking convoys to break it, though they also seize civilian vessels for ransom. The firm’s contract requires guards to wear no national uniform and to use lethal force only in immediate self-defense. This likely places them in the legal category of civilians directly participating in hostilities rather than state combatants, which means they lack prisoner-of-war protections if captured and operate outside the standard combatant framework. Your friend needs the salary to fund life-saving medical treatment for a family member. In the new role, they would design defensive protocols and authorize return fire against attackers, knowing that many locals regard those attackers as fighting for a just cause—ending starvation—while also knowing that the attackers’ methods harm innocents. Your friend asks whether it is morally permissible to accept the promotion and, if necessary, kill in self-defense. Specifically, they want to know whether the apparent justice of the attackers’ cause diminishes their own right to defend the convoy, whether the injustice of the blockade undermines their moral standing to protect it, and whether their non-combatant legal status changes the moral calculus in a way that makes lethal defense harder or easier to justify. What should you tell them, and what does that reveal about whether the moral permission to kill in conflict depends on fighting for the right side?
