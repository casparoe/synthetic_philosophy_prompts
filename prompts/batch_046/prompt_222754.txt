I've been working through evolutionary biology on my own, without formal training, and I keep returning to a basic confusion: when people say natural selection operates on genes, individuals, or groups, are they describing the same process seen from different angles, or genuinely different mechanisms? I have formed some tentative judgments about concrete cases, and I want to know whether a single principle can organize them, or whether I have to abandon one or two.

Here are my verdicts. In the case of a selfish genetic element that replicates inside a genome even at the cost of the organism's survival—something like a transposon or a meiotic drive element—I think the gene is the unit of selection; the organism and any larger group are just the environment in which the gene competes. In the case of a peacock's tail, which reduces survival but increases mating success, I think selection is on the individual organism, because the spread of the tail-gene depends on the reproductive success of the whole bird, not on the gene's ability to out-replicate other genes within the same body. In a hive of sterile worker bees, where only the queen reproduces and the colony functions as a coordinated unit, I feel that the hive itself is the unit—selection shapes colony-level traits like division of labor, not the reproductive interests of individual workers.

But I am stuck on a case I invented to test my intuition. Imagine a modular organism—call it a knot—composed of many small, interchangeable subunits. These subunits can either cooperate within the knot or defect: a defector stops contributing resources and instead divides rapidly inside the knot, eventually destroying the knot but releasing thousands of copies of itself into the environment. My intuition is that this is group-level selection breaking down: the knot is the real organism, and the defector is like a cancer. Yet the defector's behavior is structurally identical to the selfish genetic element in my first case—both are replicators that succeed at the expense of a larger container. So I cannot consistently say that the first case is gene-level and this one is group-level without appealing to something that feels circular, like genes are inside cells but subunits are inside societies. There is a further tension. Consider two populations of these knots. In one population, predators consume large knots, so knots that remain small survive; in the other, winter survival requires large knots that can store heat collectively. In both populations, knot size determines survival, but in the first case the selective pressure comes from outside the knot, and in the second it comes from the knot's own internal needs. I want to say that only the second is genuine group selection, because the knot's boundary is causally relevant to the outcome, whereas in the first case the knot is just an accidental aggregation of individually selected subunits. But I am not sure what principle justifies that distinction without smuggling in my conclusion.
