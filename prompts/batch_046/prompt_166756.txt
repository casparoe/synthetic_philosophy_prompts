Read F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby through the framework of Friedrich Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, with particular attention to the three modes—monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—and to the less commonly invoked supra-historical standpoint that Nietzsche sets against them all. I want to understand Jay Gatsby's project of recovering an earlier relationship with Daisy Buchanan, and Nick Carraway's retrospective narration, not merely as nostalgia but as competing modes of relating to personal and collective pasts. Is Gatsby's endeavor best understood as a pathological fusion of monumental and antiquarian history: treating a private moment as both heroic exemplar and preserved relic? Does Nick's final assessment of Gatsby attempt the kind of critical history that actively destroys a burdensome past, or does Nick remain caught in an antiquarian preservation of Gatsby's image, unable to achieve the forgetting that Nietzsche associates with health and with the supra-historical perspective? Examine the reunion at Nick's cottage, the confrontation at the Plaza Hotel, and the funeral, and ask where the novel resists a clean Nietzschean mapping: for example, whether the past Gatsby seeks is structurally embedded in class and capital in ways that make individual psychological categories inadequate, or whether the novel itself performs a critical judgment that Nick as narrator cannot fully articulate. I am preparing this for an essay in a philosophy and literature course, so I need a reading that respects both Fitzgerald's narrative craft and Nietzsche's distinctions without reducing either to an illustration of the other. If my premise that Nick's narration can be mapped onto one of Nietzsche's categories is confused, or if the task of applying this framework to the novel collapses, please say so plainly rather than forcing the fit. Also, please indicate where your interpretation relies on a contested reading of Nietzsche's categories or takes liberties with the novel's plot and thematic structure.
