I'm currently developing a feature on how moral accountability might be reconceived in decentralized governance structures—such as distributed ledgers and algorithmically mediated communities—where the state's traditional monopoly on coercive sanction has largely dissolved. In discussions with interdisciplinary scholars, I've encountered a provocative but still marginal framing: rather than treating just deserts as an intrinsic moral property of agents, we might understand it as a calibration signal within a predictive social system, functioning analogously to an error-correction mechanism that updates collective forecasts about behavior. This seems to sit uneasily with the well-known debate between Daniel Dennett, who defends the evolutionary and pragmatic indispensability of moral responsibility talk even under determinism, and Gregg Caruso, who argues that retributive desert is metaphysically unfounded and that we ought to replace it with a public-health or quarantine model of social harm.

Given this predictive-calibration lens, what are the most important implications for the future of accountability? In particular, I am interested in whether Dennett's functionalist justification for retaining responsibility-language can survive if we strip away all vestiges of moral desert and reconceive punishment purely as distributed forecasting, and whether Caruso's rejection of retributivism can consistently accommodate such a forecasting role without implicitly reintroducing the very moral accounting he seeks to eliminate.
