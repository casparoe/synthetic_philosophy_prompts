I'm presenting a seminar paper titled Adiaphorization Without Exception to a mixed audience of historians of modern Germany, comparative genocide scholars, and a political theorist who writes on administrative ethics. My central claim is that Zygmunt Bauman's analysis in Modernity and the Holocaust—specifically his argument that bureaucratic rationality produces adiaphorization, the separation of moral evaluation from organizational action—undermines rather than supports the uniqueness thesis central to the German Historikerstreit. I argue that if the Holocaust arose from generic features of modern bureaucratic organization rather than from specifically German pathologies or a singular European-Christian antisemitism, then its structural logic is reproducible and therefore comparable to other modern bureaucratic atrocities. I further distinguish causal specificity, which concerns the particular historical conditions of 1941-1945, from moral singularity, which treats the event as incomparable in kind; my position is that Bauman's modernity thesis supports the former but collapses the latter, and that defending uniqueness on moral grounds actually protects modern administrative institutions from the institutional critique Bauman intended. The audience will include historians committed to the uniqueness thesis who fear that comparability equals relativization, Bauman scholars who want to preserve the Holocaust's moral urgency as a singular warning about modernity, comparative-genocide researchers who think I am not going far enough in drawing structural parallels, and the administrative-ethics theorist who will worry that distributing causal responsibility across bureaucratic systems dissolves individual moral accountability.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I need the objections ordered by how damaging and how likely they are, each phrased in the vocabulary of the specific interlocutor who would raise it—so the historian's worry about relativization and national memory, the Bauman scholar's defense of singular moral urgency, the comparative scholar's push for stronger analogies, and the ethicist's concern about responsibility diffusion. For each objection, provide a concise two-minute reply, flag the weakest point in that reply, and indicate what I should concede up front. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks—likely whether my framework implicitly demands treating Stalinist terror or colonial administrative violence as morally equivalent to the Final Solution in a way that flattens the specific role of racial ideology. I also want a brief note on the softest point in my own position that I should defend actively rather than hope they overlook: my reliance on a distinction between procedural modernity and instrumental modernity that I have not fully defended elsewhere, and that a skeptical historian could treat as an ad hoc rescue of Bauman from his own apparent commitments.
