Consider the philosophy-of-science literature on the underdetermination of theory by evidence. The familiar terrain includes Quine-Duhem holism, the local-global distinction, and debates over whether underdetermination threatens scientific realism. A less explored but increasingly relevant frontier concerns what might be called computational underdetermination: in fields such as climate modeling or quantum chemistry, several formalisms are empirically adequate, yet only one is tractable with present algorithms and hardware, so theory choice is driven partly by resource constraints rather than by evidential discrimination alone. Suppose you are advising a small conceptual research group—philosophers without laboratory or computing access, funded for three years—whose brief is to clarify the foundations of scientific reasoning rather than to conduct empirical work. Which open questions at the intersection of classical evidential underdetermination and this tractability-driven variant deserve their priority? Rank your selections and defend the ordering: for each question, explain why it is upstream of others, how tractable it is with philosophical tools alone, and what kind of outcome—conceptual clarification, formal framework, or targeted case study—would most alter whether we treat tractability-based selection as a benign practical limit or as a distinct epistemic problem.
