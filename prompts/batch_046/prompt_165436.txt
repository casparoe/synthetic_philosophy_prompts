In debates over the International Military Tribunals at Nuremberg and Tokyo, a frequent analogy compares the proceedings to retrospective architecture: the victors are like surveyors who, after a building has collapsed, draft new structural codes and then prosecute the fallen building’s architects for violating standards that did not exist when the foundations were laid, while ignoring identical flaws in structures they themselves designed. The analogy is used to claim that the trials amounted to victors’ justice rather than law. Audit it fully: lay out the correspondence table (victors as surveyors, defeated leaders as architects, the Charter as the new code, the collapsed order as the building, and so on); identify precisely where the mapping breaks—especially regarding the distinction between nullum crimen sine lege and nullum poena sine lege, and whether the Charter created genuinely new substantive offences or merely supplied jurisdictional machinery for pre-existing customary norms; examine whether the analogy smuggles in the false premise that international humanitarian law in 1945 was an empty slate rather than an evolving customary practice; and say whether the illegitimacy conclusion survives these disanalogies, or whether a sharper comparison—say, to domestic courts articulating previously implicit constitutional principles—carries the argument more honestly.
