I'm advising a couple new democracies on whether to write strong judical review into their constitution -- letting unelected courts strike down laws the legislature actually passed. I need a decision procedure I can run with clients in about two days, not a philosophy essay. The principle I want to operationalize is that review is only legit when there's a specific kind of democratic failure: not just "the legislature got rights wrong," but a structural blockage where the legislative process itself can't fix the violation because of how political incentives align, plus an exhaustion check that no lower-cost democratic remedy exists.

Write me a reusable flowchart-in-words. Ordered questions my team can ask in sequence. For each: what counts as a sufficient answer, where we stop with a hard no, and where we hand off to the client because it's values not facts. Fix my role (constitutional design consultant), the stakes (millions affected, I sign off), and the constraints (two-day turnaround, clients aren't philosophers, I don't have local political science expertise). The procedure has to handle hard cases: a legislature with a solid rights record but weak minority protection; a technically competent court that was stacked by the previous authoritarian regime; economic rights that legislatures chronically underfund; and cases where court and legislature genuinely disagree about what equality requires.

Then run it on three proposals:
1. A brand-new legislature with tight party discipline, proposing strong review over labor laws using a vague "dignity at work" standard.
2. A trusted old legislature paired with a judiciary appointed by the prior regime; the proposal is weak-form review only.
3. One case where the procedure says "include strong review" but my gut says it's wrong -- show me exactly how it gets there, flag why my intuition is probably misfiring according to the logic, and say what I'd be giving up by overriding it.

Also be honest: where does this pretend to be mechanical but is actually a judgment call dressed up? Does it secretly assume courts are more rational? What if the blockage is real but the court is corrupt? And tell me flat out what the procedure can't do -- like settle whether economic rights belong in the constitution at all.

Keep it casual, no bibliographies or "see Waldron 1999" stuff, I can't verify citations in the field anyway. And if you can, build in one small typo naturally -- I keep writing "counter-majortarian" and it's embarrassing.
