I've been digging into Wittgenstein's hinge stuff in On Certainty and landed on a specific take: hinges aren't just beliefs with really high confidence or psychological fixed points, but what I'd call non-propositional scafolding — functional prerequisites that don't carry truth-values in the ordinary sense, since they make evidence and belief-formation possible in the first place. From this I think it follows that there's no meaningful line between "rationally revising a hinge" and "just losing your grip on reality"; it's definately always the latter, because any pressure that looks like counter-evidence gets interpreted inside the hinge framework and therefore can't reach it from the outside. I also think this is local rather than universal — different people or cultures can run on different hinges without anyone being irrational, since there's no external tribunal that can adjudicate between them.

What would actually count against this? I want the arguments and cases that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be, from fatal to merely awkward. Include things like whether there are real hinge revisions that don't look like madness (scientific revolutions, religious conversions), whether the "scafolding" metaphor collapses once hinges can be explicitly articulated and defended, and whether my view has just insulated itself from falsifcation by defining every challenge away. I'd also like to know what a committed hinge epistemologist who shares my view would themselves concede as counting against them, versus what they'd dismiss as missing the point. And be honest: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind?
