I am an undergraduate philosophy student currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on value theory and the philosophy of well-being. Next Thursday I am delivering the opening remarks—approximately five minutes—at a workshop titled The Common Thread: Pleasure, Reward, and Value, co-sponsored by our philosophy and neuroscience departments. The audience is small, about fifteen people, and deliberately mixed: half are philosophers of mind and ethics who are intimately familiar with the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and half are cognitive scientists and graduate students in affective neuroscience who work with reward prediction errors and mesolimbic circuitry but have limited patience for purely conceptual taxonomy.

Please take the role of an expert in philosophy of well-being and an experienced academic speechwriter. Before drafting the remarks, spend a moment thinking carefully about the structural constraints: the philosophical idea must shape what I can and cannot say, not merely decorate the introduction; the speech must land for the specialists without losing the scientists; and I must not rely on citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts that an audience without web access could not verify.

The central conceptual task is this. I want to frame the workshop around a specific, somewhat unconventional proposal—let us call it the modal-profile view—which I developed in seminar. On this view, pleasures do not share a uniform phenomenal quality (they are heterogeneous in feel) and they do not map neatly onto a single neural substrate (they recruit overlapping but distinct circuits). What they share, instead, is a particular functional role in practical reasoning: each presents itself as a reason to continue or repeat the activity that produces it, regardless of how it feels. I need you to present this framing with enough precision that philosophers can see exactly where it engages the heterogeneity objection, and with enough clarity that neuroscientists can see how it relates to their models of reward.

Crucially, the speech must honestly treat the strongest version of the heterogeneity objection: if pleasures share no introspectible commonality, hedonism risks collapsing into an empty formalism that merely re-labels good experiences as pleasures without explaining what makes them good. The speech should concede the force of this worry without conceding that hedonism is dead. It should also avoid asking anyone to read particular papers or providing a reading list.

Please begin with a brief explanatory note outlining your structural choices—specifically, why you ordered the philosophical moves as you did, how you balanced accessibility with rigor, and where you deliberately left an opening for audience pushback. Then provide the speech itself, written in a formal but not stiff register suitable for an academic workshop opening.
