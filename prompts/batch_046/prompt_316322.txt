I am trying to settle my own view on whether dreams are experiences in the robust, first-person sense—whether there is genuinely “something it is like” to dream—or whether they are better understood as post-hoc narratives constructed upon awakening, as Norman Malcolm suggested, against Daniel Dennett’s more permissive treatment of dream reports as tracking real mental events during sleep. Rather than a survey of the literature, I want to test my intuitions in reflective equilibrium: I will present my verdicts on a range of cases, and I ask you to articulate the principle, if any, that unifies them, to apply it to an unlisted case, and to tell me honestly which verdicts must be sacrificed for the sake of coherence.

Here are my judgments.

First, a standard vivid REM dream—being pursued through an unfamiliar city with a distinct emotional tone and sensory detail. I judge this a genuine experience: there was something it was like for the sleeper during the episode.

Second, a sleepwalking episode in which the subject walks to a kitchen, opens a drawer, and returns to bed, with complete amnesia upon awakening. I judge this not to be an experience: there was no conscious subject undergoing the events, only behavior.

Third, a so-called “blank dream,” in which the sleeper reports upon waking that she definitely dreamed but can recall no content, imagery, narrative, or affect—only a bare conviction that mental activity occurred. Here I genuinely oscillate. Sometimes I think there must have been a minimal experiential quality, perhaps an undifferentiated presence or pure affect, since the conviction demands some source. At other times I think the conviction itself is confabulated, and there was no experience at all.

Fourth, a lucid dream in which the sleeper realizes she is dreaming, deliberately decides to fly, and feels herself doing so. This seems clearly experiential. Yet I also feel it differs from the standard REM case: the presence of critical reflection and volition makes it feel more like waking experience grafted onto sleep. This makes me wonder whether lucidity reveals the true nature of all dreams (they become experiences only when attended to) or whether it transforms the phenomenon into something categorically different.

Fifth, a nested false awakening: the subject dreams she wakes up, examines her clock, and then actually wakes up, reporting both layers vividly. I judge both layers experiential. But the first layer is ontologically “false”—there was no clock, no real waking. This raises a tension: does the falsity of represented content undermine experiential status, or is experience independent of veridicality?

Sixth, a hypothetical neurological scenario: a patient in deep slow-wave sleep exhibits cortical activation patterns typically associated with narrative processing, but upon forced awakening reports nothing and denies any mental life. If neural decoding later reveals complex symbolic sequences present in the brain, should we say the patient had an unconscious experience? I am inclined to say no—experience requires some form of accessibility to the subject, even if only retrospective—but then I worry that this makes experience depend on reportability, which seems wrong.

These verdicts pull in different directions. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on first-person accessibility, on the presence of an integrated narrative self, on specific neural integration patterns, or on some combination—that captures all six judgments? Please state the principle precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and apply it to a case I have not listed: a waking subject in a sensory-deprivation tank who reports vivid dream-like imagery but retains full awareness that she is lying in the tank. Which of my verdicts must be abandoned if we prioritize simplicity over saving every intuition? And does the Malcolm–Dennett disagreement map cleanly onto your principle, or does the principle reveal that they were asking different questions?

Please provide your analysis in American English.
