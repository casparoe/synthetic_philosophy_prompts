At a philosophy of science reading group, two members argued about Otto Neurath’s claim that his ISOTYPE picture language offers a theory-neutral, intersubjective basis for unified science. The first said pictograms escape theoretical loading because they lack propositional syntax: an icon pairing a factory silhouette with worker figures can record a quantitative production relation without committing the observer to any particular causal or economic theory, which is precisely why Neurath believed pictures could bridge ideological divides. The second replied that every design decision—choosing silhouette over cross-section, uniform scaling over proportional distortion, isolating objects against blank backgrounds—embeds ontological judgments about which entities are fundamental and which relations matter, and that Neurath’s own physicalist and planning-oriented commitments visibly shaped which variables he made graphically salient. The first answered that these are merely pragmatic conventions of representation, not semantic commitments; just as a coordinate system can be rotated without altering the physics, the same ISOTYPE chart can be read within different theoretical frameworks. The second countered that if the pictures are that freely reinterpretable, they cannot carry the determinate empirical content Neurath needs them to have, leaving only a choice between hidden theoretical commitment and semantic emptiness. Please adjudicate step by step: whether the syntax argument genuinely establishes pictorial neutrality, whether the design-choice objection undermines it, whether the “pragmatic convention” reply saves neutrality or collapses into the emptiness charge, and whether any coherent middle position preserves Neurath’s intersubjective ambition without requiring an impossible neutrality.
