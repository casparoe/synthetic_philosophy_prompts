In the wake of my partner’s death and my concurrent, entirely virtual collaboration—conducted through asynchronous correspondence and shared digital archives—with an art historian I have never met in person, on an exhibition concerning grief and visual beauty, I have been attempting to articulate a coherent philosophical position on intrinsic value. I find myself returning to G. E. Moore’s ethical writings, in particular his claim that friendship and the contemplation of beautiful objects are among the greatest goods in themselves, and that certain combinations of them constitute organic unities whose value exceeds the sum of their parts. Before presenting these thoughts to a reading group, I want a rigorous consistency check on the specific package I now hold.

Here is where I stand:

(1) I accept Moore’s thesis that true friendship and the appreciation of beautiful objects are both intrinsically valuable, and that when they occur together they form an organic unity—call it aesthetic fellowship—such that the combined state has a value strictly greater than the value of the friendship plus the value of the aesthetic experience taken separately.

(2) I hold that for friendship to contribute this specific value, the friends must possess direct, mutual awareness of each other’s good qualities and must take genuine pleasure in them; without this cognitive-affective engagement, what remains is mere companionship or cooperation, lacking the intrinsic worth characteristic of true friendship.

(3) I maintain that aesthetic appreciation is complete in itself even in total solitude: a person contemplating a beautiful object alone realizes the full intrinsic value of that contemplation, with no deficit arising merely from the absence of others.

(4) I also hold that the peak realization of the organic unity described in (1) necessarily involves shared aesthetic attention—two friends jointly attending to a beautiful object—so that solitary appreciation, however complete on its own terms, falls short of the highest possible value.

(5) I believe that mediated or virtual relationships—specifically, the kind of collaborative curatorial work my colleague and I are conducting through digital exchange, without physical co-presence or direct observation of personal character—can fully instantiate both the friendship required by (2) and the shared aesthetic attention required by (4).

(6) I reject evolutionary debunking arguments against the intrinsic value of friendship and beauty, because on Moore’s non-naturalist account causal origins are irrelevant to normative status; at the same time, I think that the near-universal cross-cultural recognition of these goods provides defeasible epistemic support for trusting our judgments about them.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, rank them by severity, specify the cheapest revision that would resolve it, and state precisely what that revision costs. Then identify the single revision—modifying or abandoning one commitment—that would clean up the largest number of tensions at once. Finally, point out whether any commitment that looks isolated or problematic is actually saved by its interaction with another commitment in the set, and explain how.
