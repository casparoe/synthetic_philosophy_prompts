I'm writing a feature for a science-and-ideas audience on whether experts can rationally keep a specific forecast after receiving evidence that their reasoning is globally unreliable. I want a decision tree—not a quiz about labels—that sorts readers into positions on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia by having them answer concrete judgment questions about a single scenario.

The scenario: a climate modeler has published a regional temperature projection. She then learns two things: peer-reviewed audits show her statistical pipeline has failed in 15 percent of past analogous studies, and she is severely sleep-deprived, with field-specific studies linking such deprivation to overconfidence. She re-examines her current model and finds no identifiable error.

Build a tree with six to eight branch nodes. Each node should be a concrete claim or case judgment that a reader can answer without knowing philosophical jargon—phrased as yes/no or choose-A/B/C. Move through this logical space in order:

1. Do the pipeline-failure data and the fatigue evidence defeat this specific projection, or do they only undermine her general reliability?
2. Can she rationally apply a meta-cognitive "tag" to the belief—treating it as likely wrong—while still acting on it for decision-making?
3. Is epistemic defeat globally transmitted across her entire doxastic system, or can it be compartmentalized by domain, time-slice, or modeling framework?
4. If she acknowledges global unreliability but her first-order inspection still supports the projection, is retaining confidence ever rational?
5. Must her confidence be revised downward proportionally to the strength of the higher-order evidence, or can first-order reasoning "bracket" that evidence?

The leaves must include conciliationism (revise downward in line with the unreliability evidence), steadfastness (retain the first-order judgment if inspection supports it), level-splitting (it can be rational both to believe P and to believe that believing P is irrational), global revisionism (all beliefs, including this one, must be downgraded), and a compartmentalized-defeat view (defeat is indexed locally—say, to this model framework or temporal slice—so retention in tagged domains is permissible; flag this if it is unnamed or underdeveloped in the literature). Include at least one combination of answers that yields an unoccupied leaf, and briefly diagnose why it might be empty: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

For every leaf, provide one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—pay special attention to the compartmentalized and level-splitting positions. At the end, flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate on this scenario; if steadfastness and compartmentalized defeat converge here, say so explicitly and name the further case that would split them (for example, a situation where first-order reasoning also looks shaky but the agent retains confidence only in the tagged domain).

Keep the audience in mind: educated general readers of a science publication, not philosophy specialists. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The instrument should work for someone who knows only the scenario and the branch questions.
