Having spent three decades as an experimental physicist before turning to philosophy in retirement, I have been trying to systematize my intuitions about metaphysical grounding and fundamentality—intuitions shaped less by formal study than by thinking about reduction in the laboratory. Here are six verdicts I hold with some confidence, though I suspect they do not fully cohere.

First, that a sample of sodium chloride dissolves in water is fully grounded in the ionic lattice structure of NaCl together with the polar charge distribution of H₂O molecules; nothing further is required. Second, that a particular protein functions as an enzyme is grounded not merely in its static three-dimensional conformation but in that conformation being situated within a chemical environment that supplies substrates; the relational context is part of the ground. Third, that an atomically precise computer simulation of that same protein, running on silicon hardware and reproducing every molecular motion, does not genuinely catalyze anything; despite structural identity, the functional property is absent, which suggests that grounding requires actual physical instantiation of the relevant processes rather than mere structural correspondence. Fourth, that a synthetic enzyme engineered de novo—without any evolutionary history—nonetheless genuinely catalyzes its target reaction, and its functional property is grounded entirely in its present physical structure and immediate environment. Fifth, that a mineral crystal formed by chance in a hydrothermal vent, possessing by thermodynamic accident the exact same conformation and binding the same substrate, does not genuinely function as an enzyme; its interaction is accidental, not grounded in any functional or organizational fact. This conflicts with the fourth case, since both lack evolutionary origin and both possess the correct structure. Sixth, that the anti-correlation of spins in an entangled pair is not grounded in the individual spin states of the two particles taken separately; the correlation appears either fundamental or grounded in a joint state that is not reducible to the parts.

Is there a single, coherent principle of grounding and fundamentality that delivers all six verdicts, or close? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and predict its verdict on an unlisted scenario—specifically, whether the property "being a clock" in a quantum computing device that uses entanglement-decay intervals as its tick is grounded in the hardware substrate or in the relational dynamics of decay. You should also state honestly which of my six verdicts the principle must overturn if that is the price of simplicity. If no single principle can accommodate them without contortion, I prefer the simpler account together with an explicit statement of which intuition I should abandon. I am not asking for references or a survey of the literature; I want the principle, its costs, and its predictions.
