A fellow doctoral student in jurisprudence sketched an argument about recursive constitutional closure that I cannot fully dismantle, though its conclusion—that no legal system can coherently institute a supreme self-amending rule—seems to condemn every modern constitution. The argument turns on a hypothetical Rule of Recursive Closure (RRC): any constitutional provision may be amended, RRC included, provided the amended rule-set remains logically consistent. (1) Suppose RRC is replaced by RRC*, which permits future amendments that violate consistency, but only on the condition that RRC*'s own authority is preserved. (2) Under RRC, the replacement is valid only if the post-amendment rule-set is consistent; yet RRC* licenses future inconsistencies, so the replacement appears to violate RRC's consistency constraint. (3) Hence, judged by RRC, the transition to RRC* is unauthorized. (4) But once RRC* is in force, it retrospectively authorizes its own adoption, since preserving RRC*'s authority is precisely its defining condition. (5) So the same act of constitutional change is both unauthorized and authorized, producing a contradiction unless we treat RRC as immutable. (6) If RRC is immutable, however, the system contains an unamendable core, contradicting RRC's claim to cover every provision. (7) Therefore any legal order that tries to institute fully recursive self-amendment collapses into either an authorization gap or an unamendable core. (8) Modern constitutional democracies all presuppose such recursive closure, so they are conceptually defective. I want to preserve (1) and (8) if possible, since they describe actual constitutional practice and its normative aspiration. Where does the argument fail—likely in the inference from (2) to (3), in the contradiction drawn at (5), or in a hidden equivocation between the consistency of the rule-set and the consistency of the transition event itself—and what does rejecting that step cost? In particular, must I accept that legal authority is discontinuous across amendments, or can the paradox be dissolved by distinguishing the temporal index at which a rule evaluates a change?
