Consider the Cuban Missile Crisis during the forty-eight-hour window of October 26–27, 1962, when President Kennedy received Nikita Khrushchev’s private message of October 26—offering withdrawal of Soviet missiles from Cuba in exchange for a US non-invasion pledge—followed, on the morning of October 27, by Khrushchev’s public broadcast adding the removal of American Jupiter missiles from Turkey. At this same juncture, EXCOMM was divided between advisors urging immediate acceptance of the private terms and others demanding air strikes or a hard-line response to the public demand; Kennedy, meanwhile, authorized a U-2 reconnaissance flight over Cuba that was shot down on October 27, killing Major Rudolf Anderson and yielding photographic confirmation of continued Soviet construction at missile sites. Analyze this episode through the lens of Good’s theorem on the value of information—specifically, the Savage–Good result that for a rational agent with stable preferences who updates by conditionalization, freely acquired information cannot reduce expected utility, since the agent may always discard it and choose as before.

First, discipline the mapping precisely. Identify the decision-maker: is it Kennedy as unitary executive, or EXCOMM as a collective body with heterogeneous preference orderings? Specify the options at that node—accept the private deal immediately, delay action to await verification of withdrawal, escalate to air strikes, or treat the public Turkish demand as a separate bargaining chip. Specify what counts as the “free information” in this setting: is it the October 27 U-2 photographs alone, or also the sequence of Khrushchev’s contradictory messages? Then apply the theorem: given the available intelligence, does Good’s framework recommend continued information-gathering (further flights, more diplomatic signaling) before committing to a response, or does the time pressure and risk of escalation place the decision outside the theorem’s scope?

Second, examine where the episode tests the theory’s boundaries rather than confirming it. The U-2 flight was not costless in the theorem’s sense: it was observable, risked killing a pilot, and could have triggered Soviet retaliation—so information acquisition itself altered the strategic environment. Moreover, Khrushchev’s belief about whether Kennedy possessed further photographic evidence, and Kennedy’s belief about Khrushchev’s belief, introduced common-knowledge complications that a single-agent decision tree does not capture. Does the crisis therefore reveal that Good’s result needs a strategic, multi-agent extension—one in which the “value” of information depends on whether its acquisition is observable and on whether other agents update their own strategies in response? Or does it show that the theorem remains silent precisely where we need it most: on preference divergence within the decision body, on the ethics of risking lives to reduce epistemic uncertainty, and on whether an agent should gather information when delay itself is a move in a bargaining game?

Finally, say where the theory is genuinely silent rather than forcing it to speak. Does Good’s framework have anything to say about the moral weight of Anderson’s death as part of the “cost” of information? About whether Kennedy’s eventual decision—to accept the private deal and secretly concede on Turkey—was rational in the theorem’s sense, or whether rationality here requires a game-theoretic, not merely decision-theoretic, evaluation? And what does the case predict for an analogous strategic crisis in which information is genuinely free and unobservable, but the opposing party is also a rational information-gatherer?
