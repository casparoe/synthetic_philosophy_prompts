I'm working through some issues in philosophy of science for an advanced seminar, and I keep running into a tension between classical accounts of scientific realism and the kind of theories we now actually build. On Popper's view, a false theory T2 is closer to the truth than T1 if T2's true logical consequences contain those of T1 and T2's false logical consequences are a proper subset of T1's. On Niiniluoto's view, one defines a metric space of possible worlds, computes the distance between the actual world and the worlds in which the theory holds, and derives truthlikeness as a normalized inverse of that summed distance. For each account, lay out its core mechanics precisely. Then apply both to a concrete case: a deep predictive model—say, a neural network used for causal forecasting—whose content is not a set of propositions with logical relations but a high-dimensional weight matrix that approximates an input-output mapping. Does Popper's framework collapse because there is no clear deductive closure from which to separate true and false consequences? Does Niiniluoto's metric presuppose a well-defined state-space representation that such a black-box approximator may fail to determine uniquely? After mapping the correspondences and disanalogies between each philosophical machinery and the features of algorithmic models, argue which view—if either—offers a more defensible notion of approximate truth in this setting, and identify the specific structural feature that drives their divergence when faced with non-propositional theories.
