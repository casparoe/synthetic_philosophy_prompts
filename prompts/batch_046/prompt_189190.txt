Below are notes for a short essay I am drafting on Buridan's ass and the structure of preference under perfect equilibrium. I argue that the paradox depends on conflating two distinct capacities: comparative evaluation, which ranks options, and executive selection, which activates a choice mechanism. Call this the comparative/executive distinction. On my view, perfect equilibrium is not a failure of reason but a saturation of comparative evaluation; the ass's paralysis reveals not irrationality but the absence of an executive trigger. I then suggest that sustained equilibrium might constitute a positive state—what I term attentive indifference—governed by a second-order preference to preserve balance rather than break it. I worry, however, that this either smuggles in a hidden first-order preference for stasis or collapses into standard arbitrary tie-breaking once we explain how the agent ever exits the state.

Please comment on these notes with particular attention to three issues. First, is the comparative/executive distinction conceptually stable, or does any executive mechanism necessarily presuppose some comparative evaluation that reintroduces the paradox? Second, does attentive indifference describe a genuinely distinct attitude, or is it merely indecision with a normative gloss? Third, if one accepts the framework, what would have to be true about agency for an agent to rationally remain in perfect equilibrium indefinitely—would this require revising standard assumptions about practical rationality, or can it be accommodated within existing decision-theoretic frameworks without loss?
