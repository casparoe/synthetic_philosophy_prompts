I am a high school student drafting an essay for an interdisciplinary history course on whether macrohistorical explanations for national wealth—institutions, geography, or culture—can ever satisfy causal standards rather than merely correlational ones. Instead of rehearsing familiar debates, I focus on a problem I have not seen named: because history offers only a single realized timeline, any counterfactual claim about colonial institutions or geographic endowments must be constructed from the same archives that interpret the outcome, creating what I call counterfactual circularity in single-track history. I apply this to the Korea division and the Nogales border cases, arguing that treating them as natural experiments smuggles geographic assumptions into institutional conclusions, and I ask whether synthetic-control methods applied to colonial borders genuinely escape this circularity or merely formalize it. Would you comment on whether my reasoning holds together, whether my proposed term captures something distinct from ordinary identification problems in economics, and whether my critique unfairly ignores recent methodological advances?
