I'm preping a unit on demandingness objections and I'm struck by this weird consistency in how liberal democracies organize things. Tax codes let you deduct charitable giving up to some modest cap — in the US it's tied to AGI — but accountants routinely warn high earners not to 'overdo it,' and if someone actually lives on near-minimum wage and gives away the rest, we treat them as unstable or cultish. Meanwhile welfare and labor policy are built on the idea that people need carrots and sticks to pursue their own interest, not that they'll voluntarily maximize overall welfare. Moral education praises generosity but draws a sharp line between being good and being excessive, with the latter usually pathologized. So what philosophical commitments about obligation does that whole pattern presuppose? Is it quietly assuming consequentialism is false because it's too demanding, or that there's a hard distinction between required and supererogitory action, or just that psychology needs guardrails? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually needs that view or is compatible with a consequentialist who accepts practical constraints, and tell me what a tax, welfare, and moral-education system built on an unqualified demandingness-accepting view would have to change. Don't just summarize Singer — I want the instutional logic unpacked.
