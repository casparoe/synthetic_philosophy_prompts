Here are notes for an essay I am drafting on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and the problem of comparing pleasure and pain. The view holds that reducing suffering has lexical priority over increasing happiness, but only until suffering falls below some threshold T; once suffering is below T, the two goods become commensurable on a single scale.

I want to argue that this position hides a deeper incommensurability problem. I distinguish scale-incommensurability—no shared metric between pleasure and pain at all—from what I call threshold-incommensurability: a local metric exists within each region (above or below T) but not across the boundary. My claim is that LTNU needs global comparability just to fix the location of T, since setting T requires comparing states in which suffering is just above T with enormous pleasure to states in which suffering is just below T with only modest pleasure. If pleasure and pain are scale-incommensurable, T is arbitrary. But if they are only threshold-incommensurable, LTNU collapses either into pure lexical priority (if cross-threshold comparison is never legitimate) or into standard aggregative utilitarianism (if it is always legitimate).

I also want to examine the reply that T is fixed not by comparative weighing but by an independent moral principle—say, a side-constraint against allowing extreme suffering. I suspect this saves LTNU only by abandoning its lexical structure and turning it into a deontological constraint.

Please comment on whether the distinction between scale- and threshold-incommensurability is coherent, whether the argument that LTNU requires global comparability succeeds, and whether there is a defensible way to set T without either smuggling in a hidden cardinal metric or giving up the lexical claim. I am particularly interested in whether a defender could reject the premise that value comparison must be transitive across the threshold, and what that rejection would cost the view.
