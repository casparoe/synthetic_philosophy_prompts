You are preparing a speaker for the Q&A following a philosophy seminar entitled "Ontic Fragmentation and the Dissolution of the Sorites". The speaker defends the view that vagueness is neither semantic imprecision nor epistemic ignorance, but an ontological feature of properties: a property is composed of discrete determinacy-conditions, and borderline cases occur when some but not all conditions for determinately having (or lacking) the property are satisfied, producing a metaphysical status of "fragmentary instantiation" that is genuinely neither F nor not-F. In the classic baldness sorites, removing one hair never eliminates a determinacy-condition, yet cumulative loss eventually removes enough conditions to shift the metaphysical status discontinuously; hence the tolerance principle fails not because a sharp boundary is hidden, but because property-parts do not transmit across the series. The audience is a research seminar in philosophy of language and metaphysics at a UK university: three supervaluationists in the tradition of Fine and Keefe, two epistemicists following Williamson who insist every predicate has sharp unknown boundaries, one dialetheist who accepts true contradictions at the boundary, and several graduate students sympathetic to contextualist or degree-theoretic approaches in the manner of Raffman or Shapiro. Draft a Q&A prep sheet. List the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as each camp would phrase them. For each objection provide: (i) a concise reply suitable for two to three minutes; (ii) what should be conceded immediately; (iii) what must be held firm; and (iv) an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Include the one question the speaker hopes will not be asked, with a reply clearly marked as such. Finally, identify the weakest point in the speaker's own framework—the interaction between fragmentary instantiation and compositionality for complex properties—that they should be ready to defend actively rather than hoping it is missed.
