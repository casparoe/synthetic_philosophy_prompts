I am trying to work out what I actually believe about the justification of deductive inference, using reflective equilibrium on concrete cases. Here are my verdicts, which I want tested for coherence.

First, when someone sincerely accepts that if P then Q, accepts P, and comes to believe Q by seeing that the premises entail it, I take her to be fully justified in believing Q, with no further mental step required.

Second, in Carroll’s dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, the Tortoise accepts the premises and even grants that the conclusion is true, yet refuses to move from one to the other without an explicit further premise licensing the inference. My gut says the Tortoise is not irrational, but he is being perverse: he may withhold assent to the inference-step, though if he genuinely wishes to reason he must eventually stop demanding licences.

Third, a child who reasons that all birds fly, Tweety is a bird, therefore Tweety flies, without being able to state any logical rule, strikes me as justified in her belief, provided she is disposed to apply the pattern consistently. The justification seems to lie in her cognitive disposition, not in an explicit grasp of a rule.

Fourth, a computer executing a formal proof-checker strikes me as not justified in any belief at all; there is causal processing, but no epistemic standing.

Fifth, consider someone who accepts modus ponens in mathematics but refuses to apply it in moral reasoning, claiming that ethical conclusions never follow mechanically from premises. This strikes me as coherent: justification is domain-relative, and the licence to infer depends on the kind of claim at issue.

Sixth, imagine a philosopher who insists that every application of an inferential rule requires a separate meta-premise stating that the rule applies to this particular case. She applies modus ponens only after adding that modus ponens applies here. I think this generates a vicious regress, and her position is unstable: either she eventually accepts an unlicensed application, or she never reaches a conclusion.

Seventh, and this is the case I keep returning to: a speaker accepts P or not-P, notices that P or not-P together with not-Q entails Q, and therefore derives Q via explosion, all while sincerely denying that explosion is a legitimate move in ordinary reasoning. I feel she is being inconsistent—she cannot selectively invoke logical principles only when they suit her—yet I also want to say that her mistake is pragmatic or contextual rather than a simple failure of logical knowledge.

Is there a single coherent principle behind these seven verdicts? I want a definite answer, not a menu of options left for me to choose between. If the best account saves cases one, three, and five but forces me to reject either two or six, say so explicitly and defend the choice. Please also state what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: what should we say about someone who applies deduction flawlessly in private thought but systematically denies that others are ever justified in drawing the same inferences?

Write in British English, maintain a formal tone, and take a committed stance that gives reasons rather than merely surveying possibilities.
