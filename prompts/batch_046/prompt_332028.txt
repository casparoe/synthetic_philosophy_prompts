I've been studying the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for eternalism, and I want to check whether my diagnosis of presentism's failure is correct. The usual Andromeda-paradox objection shows that two inertial observers assign contradictory "present" slices to distant regions, which presentists try to escape by retreating to a local or point-present ontology: only events causally connected or spatiotemporally adjacent to the observer exist. But it seems to me that this retreat doesn't solve the problem; it merely relocates it. Even for a local region, an accelerating observer continuously shifts through instantaneous inertial frames, and if the boundary of what exists is frame-relative, then the ontological status of nearby events—say, the atoms composing the observer's own measuring apparatus—would have to flicker in and out of existence as the observer's velocity changes. Since special relativity denies any privileged foliation that could stabilize this boundary, presentism appears to entail not merely the absence of a global "now," but a dynamically unstable ontology at every scale. Am I right that this "local flickering" objection is fatal, and that the block universe is therefore the only interpretation that avoids ontological discontinuity for extended or accelerating bodies?
