I run a monthly rationalist-adjacent workshop on AI alignment and philosophy of technology—usually 12 to 16 people, 90 minutes, mostly graduate students and engineers who have read the standard sequences but have not done formal philosophy. I want an in-session activity that makes the group feel, in real time, why decomposing a complex alignment task through iterated amplification might preserve local competence while draining global alignment, specifically through what we might call structural abstraction: the process by which each distillation layer strips normative context that the evaluator at that layer is no longer equipped to detect, because their own judgment has been progressively shaped by the previous layer’s outputs.

Design a structured game with rules, roles, and a payoff scheme using only points or social reputation (no money). The setting should be a simulated recursive teaching chain of four levels: one Director who holds the full normative goal; two intermediate Decomposers who must break the task into sub-tasks and write one-sentence distillation prompts; and Workers who execute only those prompts without seeing the original goal. Use a concrete task that requires balancing incommensurable values—for instance, allocating limited medical resources across groups with different needs and different capacities to advocate for themselves. The rules must force each Decomposer to evaluate sub-outputs using only criteria they can articulate in the distilled prompt, not by appealing back to the original moral framework. The payoff should reward Workers for fidelity to the prompt and Decomposers for producing prompts that generate technically correct outputs, with no direct reward for preserving the Director’s original normative intent.

Include a precise timeline (opening framing, three rounds of decomposition and execution, the reveal, and the debrief), the debrief mapping each moment onto the amplification/distillation cycle, an honest note on which feature of the actual process the activity cannot reproduce—likely the scaling dynamics and gradient properties of neural networks—and a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run, specifically that one group will try to smuggle context into the prompts, together with instructions for how to handle that without breaking the simulation. The activity should be runnable without props beyond paper and pens, should not require participants to read papers beforehand, and should make the problem emerge from the rules and incentives rather than from anything the facilitator explains afterward. Specify exactly what participants should come away seeing about the relationship between decomposed competence and intact alignment.
