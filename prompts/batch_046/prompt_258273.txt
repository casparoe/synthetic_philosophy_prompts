I run an upper-level philosophy seminar on self-reference, strange loops, and hierarchical structures—about 12 students, one 90-minute session. We have discussed Douglas Hofstadter’s concept of an inviolate level and how Escher’s image of two hands drawing each other violates it, but the students still treat the case mainly as a visual curiosity rather than as a structural problem. I want an in-class simulation—structured as a role-play with real, small-stakes choices—that makes them feel the force of a tangled hierarchy: specifically, the moment when a level that should be meta becomes object-level, and authority collapses into circular dependence rather than dissolving into mere confusion.

Design the activity. Specify the roles or groups (no more than four), the rules that create the loop, a payoff or scoring mechanism using non-monetary stakes (for example, accumulated points that determine a final collective outcome such as which group authors the session’s concluding statement), and a timeline that fits 90 minutes. The rules themselves—not your post-hoc explanation—should force participants into the loop: they should try to assign authority, discover that every assignment depends on recognition by the very level being assigned to, and only then learn the names strange loop and tangled hierarchy.

Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the breakdown of the inviolate level and onto the structure of Escher’s image, predicts what will go wrong the first time (for example, one group seizing de facto authority, participants inventing an external arbiter, or the reflection mechanism being ignored), and says honestly which feature of the idea—perhaps the visual immediacy, the infinite regress, or the absence of any causal origin—the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply.

One constraint: inject a specific structural twist not found in the standard Hofstadter/Escher discussion—perhaps a mechanism by which a group can temporarily break the hierarchy, but only by being demoted into the level it tried to govern, or a scoring rule authored by the very participants being scored—to keep the task from being answerable purely from pretraining patterns.
