I'm attempting to map my intuitions about when a society or individual should relate to the past monumentally, antiquarianly, or critically — in the sense of Nietzsche's three modes of historical engagement — onto concrete cases that fall outside the usual cultural examples. My verdicts: A young physicist who keeps a portrait of a dead mentor in her lab and asks, in moments of creative block, what problem he would have tackled next, seems to be using history monumentally and healthily. A municipality that preserves every building from its founding in original condition, forbidding renovation even when structures become uninhabitable, seems antiquarian in a life-denying way. A professional historian who spends decades cataloguing every minor bureaucratic record from a defunct empire, producing no narrative, evaluation, or present connection, strikes me as similarly unhealthy, though the failure feels different from the municipality's. A political movement that systematically destroys monuments and rewrites curricula to erase national memory, then finds itself unable to sustain any collective long-term project, seems critically overreaching, with a different pathology. A therapist who encourages a client to reject every family tradition as oppressive, leaving the client unable to commit to relationships or projects, strikes me as misapplying critical history to personal life. But I am genuinely torn about a digital archive that algorithmically surfaces either the most heroic or the most shameful episodes from a community's past, calibrated to each user's preference, with no stable curatorial narrative binding them together: one part of me sees this as a mature synthesis of all three modes, another part sees it as a collapse into incoherence that serves none of life. Is there a unified principle — perhaps about whether a given historical mode is subordinated to present action or has become autonomous — that captures verdicts one through five? Which clause of the principle explains each case? What does the principle predict about the archive case, and does accommodating my ambivalence about it require an ad hoc exception? Finally, if simplicity demands that I drop one of my five verdicts rather than complicate the principle, which should go, and why?
