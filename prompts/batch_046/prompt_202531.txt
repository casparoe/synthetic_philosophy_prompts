Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme, with two refrains that alternate through the five tercets and converge in the final quatrain—on the separation of jus ad bellum and jus in bello in just war theory, specifically the moral equality of combatants: the thesis that soldiers on opposing sides possess symmetrical rights to kill and be killed regardless of whether their cause is just, provided they observe in bello constraints. Use refrain A (line 1, returning at lines 6, 12, and 18) to voice the in bello equality—e.g., We stand as equals in the line of fire—and refrain B (line 3, returning at lines 9, 15, and 19) to voice the ad bellum asymmetry—e.g., Your cause is wrong; mine alone is right. The form should do conceptual work: the two judgments must circle through the poem without synthesis, and in the closing couplet, where the refrains stand side by side, they should remain unreconciled, dramatizing that the license to kill is distributed symmetrically precisely when the moral judgment of the cause stays one-sided. In the middle tercets, introduce an epistemic turn: the soldier rarely possesses certain knowledge that his own cause is just, yet the separation assigns him and his enemy identical permissions on the basis of a cause-distinction he may not be able to verify. Let this generate tension rather than resolution—the compulsory repetition of the refrains should suggest that the separation is maintained structurally, not settled by argument. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the doctrine recognizes the separation of ad bellum and in bello judgment, but it should not resolve the paradox; instead, let the rhyme and refrain leave the reader with the unease that moral equality is granted where moral knowledge is weakest. Write in formal American English, with diction appropriate to philosophical verse, and avoid turning the poem into either a pacifist sermon or an endorsement of war.
