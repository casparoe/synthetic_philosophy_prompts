I'm working through a problem in practical reason with a colleague, and I keep returning to a line of reasoning that seems to undermine the very idea of indirect normativity through idealized deliberation. The thought is that if what I ought to do is determined not directly by normative facts but by what my ideally rational counterpart would choose, then we face a dilemma about what that counterpart must preserve. Here is the argument I sketched, and I find myself unable to locate the weak link without giving up something I want to hold onto.

(1) Indirect normativity holds only if, for any agent A and action φ, A ought to φ exactly when A's fully idealized deliberative counterpart, call her A*, would choose φ. (2) For A* to be genuinely A's counterpart rather than a generic moral oracle, A* must satisfy a retention condition: she preserves those commitments that constitute A's practical identity—those without which A would not recognize the choice as her own. (3) Now suppose A's practical identity includes a corrupt but deeply integrated commitment, for instance that the interests of members of group G carry less weight. (4) Because stripping this commitment away violates the retention condition, A* retains it; consequently A* endorses disadvantaging G. (5) By (1), it follows that A ought to disadvantage G, which is plainly wrong. (6) To avoid this, we replace the retention condition with a reconstruction condition: A* does not preserve A's actual commitments, but rather the practical identity A would possess after correct moral development. (7) But "correct moral development" just means development toward the values that an ideally rational agent ought to possess. (8) Hence (1) now states that A ought to do whatever an agent with correct values would do, which is direct normativity disguised as an indirect route; the deliberative counterpart adds no independent normative work. (9) Furthermore, if we attempt to break the circularity by defining "correct values" as those that survive ideal deliberation, we trap ourselves: the ideal deliberator is constituted by correct values, and correct values are constituted by the ideal deliberator. (10) So indirect normativity either licenses corruption by honoring corrupted practical identities or collapses into direct normativity, and in the constructivist variant it is viciously circular.

I am inclined to reject (6) rather than (1) or (2), because I want to preserve both the indirect structure and the idea that idealization cannot erase the agent entirely. But I cannot see how to reject (6) without either smuggling direct evaluation back in or accepting that some agents are simply bound to corrupt choices. Where exactly does this reasoning go wrong? Which step should I surrender, and what does surrendering it cost for the broader project of grounding norms in idealized deliberation?
