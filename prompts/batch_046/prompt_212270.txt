I am a high school junior presenting my symposium paper, “Institutional Truncation: A Pedagogical Variant of Infinitism,” at the regional Inter-School Philosophy Symposium next month. My thesis is that the epistemic regress problem is resolved not by self-evident foundations or by holistic coherence, but by recognizing that justification is genuinely infinite; however, within institutional contexts—classrooms, laboratories, peer review—the chain is pragmatically truncated by authoritative “provisional stopping points.” These are beliefs assigned temporary basal status not because they are intrinsically basic, but because the institution designates them as operationally terminal, while leaving them permanently open to further challenge. I argue that both foundationalism and coherentism misinterpret this pragmatic truncation as metaphysical termination, and that infinitism alone respects both the open-endedness of reasons and the practical necessities of knowledge practices.

The panel consists of five judges: two committed foundationalists who hold that direct empirical observation supplies intrinsically basic beliefs that halt the regress; two coherentists who maintain that beliefs justify one another through mutual support in a web with no linear terminus; one infinitist sympathizer who accepts infinite chains but fears my institutional twist collapses into conventionalism or authority-based relativism; and a moderator skeptical of meta-epistemological debates who will press me on whether this distinction alters any actual pedagogical or scientific practice.
