The exam asked: "Aristotle treats akrasia as a conflict between reason and appetite where the agent retains correct knowledge but fails to activate it, unlike Socrates who denies this is possible. Meanwhile, behavioral economists use the quasi-hyperbolic β-δ model to formalize weakness of will. Does this model actually explain Aristotelian akrasia, or does it just rename the phenomenon? And does framing time-inconsistent preferences as mere preferences miss the normative conflict central to Aristotle's account?"

Here's one student answer. Give it a letter grade and point-by-point feedback: what's right, what's subtly off (there are a couple slips that'd pass a quick read), and where the argument actually breaks down. I need specific comments to justify the score, not just a vibe check.

Student answer:
Aristotle says akrasia happens when appetite pulls you away from the right practical syllogism. You still have the universal premise—like 'sweet food is bad for my health'—but the particular premise 'this cake is sweet' takes over because desire is stronger. He rejects Socrates' claim that no one acts against knowledge, since for Aristotle knowledge can be present but 'tied down', like someone asleep or drunk. So there's a real internal battle, not just ignorance.

The β-δ model captures this by applying a discount factor β to every future reward uniformly, then using δ for exponential decay after that. This makes preferences reverse over time: at t=0 I pick the larger later reward, but at t=29 I switch to the smaller immediate one. That's exactly the akratic pattern—planning to diet but eating the cake.

But the model doesn't really explain akrasia, it just describes it with equations. Worse, it treats both preferences as equally legitimate, just indexed to different times. Aristotle thinks the rational preference is normatively better, not just another preference. Also, since each temporal self has its own utility function, the β-δ framework actually solves the problem of comparing utility across different selves—we don't need a single scale.

Overall, hyperbolic discounting gives a nice formal picture, but it misses the 'tied knowledge' point and treats the conflict as descriptive rather than normative.
