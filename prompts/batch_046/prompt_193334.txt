My study group has been going in circles on dispositions and we cooked up an argument that seems to kill all intrinsic causal powers. Here it is, step by step:

1. If a property is genuinely dispositional, then in every metaphysically possible world where it is instanciated and the stimulus conditions hold, it produces its manifestation. Otherwise it's just an acidental correlation.
2. Take any putative intrinsic disposition D — like fragility or solubility. We can always construct a double-fink: a first-order fink that blocks D, paired with a second-order mask that suppresses that fink only when some hidden background condition C is absent.
3. Because C can be made to fail in some worlds but obtain in others, there is always some world where D is present, stimulus conditions are met, and the double-fink chain still blocks manifestation.
4. So no intrinsic property can satisfy the condition in (1).
5. That leaves two options: either there are no real dispositions, or dispositions are purely relational — they depend on the global absence of interfering fink-chains.
6. The relational option makes causal explanation circular. We explain why the sugar dissolved by citing its relational profile, but that profile just encodes the absence of blockers, which is what we needed to explain.
7. Therefore the argument proves too much — it wipes out intrinsic dispositions only to replace them with a circular global relationalism.

Which step should I reject? I'd really like to keep (1) since it captures the intuition that dispositions aren't accidental, and I'd prefer not to bite the bullet on (6). Is the mistake thinking double-finks are always metaphysically possible? Or is (1) just too strong? If I weaken (1), what exactly am I giving up?
