After a long and somewhat heated discussion last night, my friend summarized Gödel's incompleteness theorems and their philosophical reach more or less as follows. He said that Gödel's first theorem establishes that every consistent formal system rich enough to encode basic arithmetic necessarily contains true statements that are unprovable within that system. From this he inferred that any rule-governed domain—constitutional interpretation, scientific methodology, even ethical reasoning—suffers an analogous structural blind spot, so long as its rules can apply recursively to themselves. He then invoked the second theorem to claim that no such system can ever demonstrate its own consistency using only its internal resources, which means any project that tries to ground legal doctrine or moral theory in a self-validating framework is necessarily doomed. He went further still: since human beings can apparently grasp the truth of a Gödel sentence for a given formal system, while the system itself cannot, this shows that human cognition is not itself a formal system, and therefore Gödel directly refutes mechanistic theories of mind. His final conclusion was that attempts to formalize jurisprudence or ethics are not merely difficult but fundamentally futile, because there will always be correct judgments that lie permanently outside whatever axiomatic rule-set we construct.

I suspect this is too sweeping, but I am unsure exactly where the overreach occurs. Which parts are faithful to what Gödel actually proved—particularly regarding what it means for a system to be rich enough to encode arithmetic, and regarding the precise conditions for the second theorem? Which parts conflate logical incompleteness with epistemic or institutional limits, and which import a separate philosophical thesis about human minds that needs its own defense? Most importantly, if we correct the subtle misrepresentations, does his conclusion that formalization in law and ethics is futile actually follow, or does it depend on treating legal and ethical reasoning as formal systems in the specific technical sense that Gödel's result requires? I would appreciate an accuracy audit that separates the legitimate extensions from the misapplications, with special attention to whether the anti-mechanist move is a standard interpretation or an independent argument that has been smuggled in.
