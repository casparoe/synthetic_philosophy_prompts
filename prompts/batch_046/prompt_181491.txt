I've been messing around with this framing for performance legitimacy in places like Singapore and China — not the tired version where people just say 'well GDP went up so they accept the regime,' which everyone already shoots down with corruption or inequality stats. I'm calling it adaptive thermostatic legitimacy: the idea that authority isn't bought with a one-time prosperity deal but maintained through continuous, visible calibration to social pressure — tweaking housing rules, adjusting censorship thresholds, fine-tuning welfare — all without ever giving up formal decision rights. The claim is this creates a genuinely distinct moral category, not a broken democracy but a different stability mechanism where consent gets manufactered through responsiveness rather than representation.

What would actually count against that? Not just 'they jail dissidents.' I want the serious defeaters: what empirical patterns would break it — maybe calibration failing and legitimacy collapsing anyway, or democracies showing the same adaptive capacity? What conceptual problems hit it, like whether manufactured responsiveness can ground real moral authority or if it just collapses into manipulation? Rank them for me — fatal vs awkward — and tell me which ones a defender of this view would themselves concede hurt the case. Also check whether it's been qualified into unfalsifability, like 'it only works in small city-states with specific cultural preconditons,' and if so whether that's actually a problem for a normative view like this.

I'm especially suspicious of corporate-governance analogies here — treating the state like a responsive firm feels like it undermines the moral distinctness — so if there's an argument along those lines that really damages the position, highlight it. That's probably the thing that'd actually move me.
