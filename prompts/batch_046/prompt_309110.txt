I'm trying to clarify my own view on when a society has genuinely collapsed rather than merely changed form, particularly in light of recent archaeological debates about framing. I've been discussing this with friends in a reading group and keep returning to the same set of cases, but my verdicts feel somewhat inconsistent, and I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them or whether I need to abandon one or more. First, the Late Bronze Age breakdown around 1177 BCE strikes me as a real collapse: interregional trade, palatial administration, and writing systems like Linear B vanish across a vast region, and the recovery of comparable integrative complexity takes centuries. Second, the Classic Maya abandonment of the southern lowlands in the ninth century feels different—more like a political reconfiguration. Writing, calendrical knowledge, and religious practice continue in the northern lowlands and highlands, so despite the dramatic demographic contraction in the Petén I resist calling it collapse. Third, Easter Island strikes me as collapse rather than transformation: the ecological base is destroyed through deforestation, moai construction ceases, population crashes sharply, and the prior social scale seems genuinely unrecoverable by the inhabitants themselves. Fourth, the thirteenth-century dispersal of the Ancestral Pueblo from Chaco Canyon and other large centers feels like adaptive migration rather than collapse—cultural continuity into modern Pueblo societies is direct, the abandonment appears responsive to drought, and the architecture was never rebuilt not because knowledge was lost but because the social need had shifted. Fifth, however, I am inclined to call the dissolution of Western Roman imperial administration in the fifth century a collapse, even though Latin, Christianity, and local populations persist; the integrative infrastructure—standardized law, long-distance taxation, coordinated military logistics—disappears and is not replaced by an equivalent system for centuries, which seems to matter more to me than mere demographic or linguistic continuity.

The tension is obvious: the Maya case involves loss of political centers and demographic contraction yet I call it transformation, while Rome involves broad cultural and demographic continuity yet I call it collapse; and Easter Island involves ecological destruction and demographic crash somewhat like Chaco, yet I treat them differently. I have been wondering whether what actually distinguishes them for me is something like a "reversibility gap"—not merely loss of complexity, but loss of the institutional knowledge and ecological conditions required to restore the previous scale—combined with whether the society's own members experienced the change as an irreversible rupture rather than as a deliberate dispersal. But I am unsure whether that distinction can save all five verdicts without becoming ad hoc, especially since I seem to weigh institutional rupture more heavily in Rome than in the Maya despite both being political dissolutions.

Can you articulate a precise principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures my pattern? For each candidate, state which clause accounts for which case, what it predicts for a case I have not listed—say, the decline of the Khmer hydraulic network around Angkor in the fifteenth century, or the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991—and be explicit about which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate. If none can save them all, tell me which verdict I should overturn and why, in light of the reversibility and institutional-rupture distinctions I have sketched.
