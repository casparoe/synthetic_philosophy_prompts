I have been thinking, mostly on my own, about whether our beliefs ought to align precisely with what physicists call objective chance. The idea — which I gather is sometimes named the Principal Principle — seems straightforward: if the world genuinely assigns an event a particular probability, then a rational person ought to hold that same degree of belief, unless they possess some special inside information. Yet whenever I raise this with others, the discussion stalls. One side insists that deferring to physical chance is a basic rule of rationality; the other side insists that belief must answer to the evidence we actually have, and that 'objective chance' is merely a feature of nature, not a command to the mind. We recycle the same examples — decaying atoms, weighted coins, weather forecasts — and agree on all the mathematics, but nobody moves. I have started to wonder whether we are truly debating one question. Perhaps some of us are asking whether rationality requires us to conform our minds to the world, while others are asking whether 'objective chance' can even be defined independently of our scientific models. To probe this, I have imagined a peculiar case. Picture a sealed chamber in which a quantum event is about to occur, but hidden inside is a mechanism that will randomly select — only after the outcome is observed — which scientific framework, standard quantum mechanics or a rival interpretation, correctly describes what happened. There appears to be a first-order objective chance for the decay, yet there is also a second-order uncertainty about whether the very notion of objective chance applies inside the chamber. If I am told to defer to the physical probabilities, am I deferring to the decay rate calculated within quantum mechanics, or to my own ignorance about which theory governs the box? The two camps in my conversations treat this as merely another coin-flip puzzle, yet to me it feels like an entirely different problem. So I want to ask: is the long-standing disagreement about deference to objective chance actually a single debate, or are there several distinct disputes — about rational norms, about metaphysics, about the boundaries of admissible evidence — being fought under one name? Is the stalemate verbal, substantive, or a sign that the question itself is ill-posed? And if you can diagnose it, what — a fresh conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a change in how we frame the problem — could genuinely advance matters, rather than leaving us to repeat the same atoms and coins?
