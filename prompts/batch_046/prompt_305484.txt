Having spent three decades in condensed-matter physics before turning, rather late, to philosophy of rationality, I have been drafting an essay on whether the Löbian obstacle is genuinely fatal to any agent that takes its own reasoning seriously. As I understand the formal result, if an agent applies a global reflection principle—roughly, that whatever it rationally believes is true—together with minimal logical-closure conditions, then Löb’s theorem in doxastic logic forces it into logical omniscience and, in effect, into believing every proposition it can formulate. This seems to show that a fully self-trusting rational agent is impossible.

In my draft I propose that the difficulty dissolves if we replace global self-trust with what I call an epistemic federal structure, modeled on how scientific communities actually operate. Rather than one monolithic belief system, the agent is divided into jurisdictionally bounded modules—say, a statistical-inference unit and a formal-derivation unit—each of which applies a local reflection principle restricted to its own domain. A meta-coordinator allocates problems and checks for cross-module inconsistencies, but it does not apply reflection to the union of all beliefs, and it deliberately lacks logical closure over inferences that span modular boundaries. Because the Löbian derivation requires a single, globally closed belief set, a federated architecture with locally bounded reflection should, I think, evade the result without merely collapsing into generic bounded rationality.

The essay argues three things. First, that the standard obstacle smuggles in an unrealistic monolithic conception of belief. Second, that local reflection within modules is sufficient for the kind of self-correction we actually value in scientific practice. Third, that the meta-coordinator can remain systematically agnostic about propositions that lie outside any single module’s jurisdiction, thereby blocking the step that generates omniscience.

I would be grateful for comments on whether my reading of the formal result is accurate—specifically, whether the theorem applies only to globally closed systems or whether it can be reconstructed across federated ones with a sufficiently powerful meta-coordinator. I am also unsure whether my analogy to scientific peer review is illuminating or misleading: does peer review function as a local reflection mechanism, or does it implicitly presuppose global trust in the community’s collective rationality? Finally, I wonder whether my proposal is genuinely distinct from existing models of bounded rationality, or whether it merely renames them with architectural language. Suggestions for sharpening that distinction, and for identifying any hidden commitment—perhaps to logical omniscience at the meta-level—would be especially welcome.
