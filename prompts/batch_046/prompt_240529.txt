I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of labor and find myself caught between Weber's framing of work as a moral calling and modern institutional attempts to protect leisure—specifically the thirty-five-hour week and the recognition of karoshi, or death by overwork, as a structural social harm rather than merely an individual failure. To work through this tension conceptually, I want you to write a short story set in a near-future city-state called Meridian, governed by a doctrine called chronometric justice: every citizen holds a positive entitlement to exactly thirty-five hours of protected leisure per week, measured not as absence from labor but as a metaphysical good with its own weight in civic life. Work remains culturally encoded as virtue inherited from an older Protestant ethic, yet employers now face criminal liability when an employee dies from overwork—unless that worker has executed a virtue waiver, a formal legal instrument that trades leisure rights for elevated social standing. The protagonist should be an adjudicator in the Bureau of Temporal Equity who must resolve the case of a celebrated architect who died after laboring ninety hours to complete a public cathedral, leaving behind both a valid virtue waiver and internal correspondence showing that the employer actively encouraged the overwork. Rather than delivering a clean legal verdict, let the narrative explore whether leisure can be genuinely protected if it remains structurally subordinate to work-as-virtue, and whether a right to leisure retains any meaning once it can be waived for prestige. Keep the philosophical conflict internal to the characters' reasoning; avoid didactic exposition. The story should read as if narrated by someone who has lived inside this logic, not observed it from outside.
