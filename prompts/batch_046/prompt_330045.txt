I am preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a modified teleosemantic account of mental content. Her position can be summarized as follows. First, the content of a representational state is fixed jointly by the evolutionary selection history of the producer mechanism and by the normal exercise conditions of the corresponding consumer mechanism; she defines those normal conditions not in statistical terms but in terms of the organism's homeostatic range, so that a consumer operates properly only when it contributes to maintaining that range. Second, she claims this joint-fixation framework explains misrepresentation without invoking dysfunction simpliciter: a state can be properly produced yet misrepresent when the consumer operates outside the homeostatic range, making misrepresentation a relation between producer and consumer rather than a failure of either alone. Third, she argues that artificial neural networks trained by gradient descent lack mental content not because they lack evolutionary history, but because they possess no integrated consumer mechanisms selected for homeostatic contribution, and she denies that redesigning such networks to include feedback loops tied to energy or temperature regulation would be sufficient to confer content, because the selection history would still be design-based rather than evolutionary.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate these claims. I want at least one question pressing on how her homeostatic-range condition avoids the classic indeterminacy worry—whether a frog's state represents a fly or a small dark moving dot—given that both may fall within the same homeostatic range; at least one question on whether her producer-consumer joint fixation makes misrepresentation overdetermined or, conversely, impossible in organisms too simple to have distinct consumer mechanisms; at least one question on whether a gradient-descent system could be redesigned with genuine evolutionary selection for homeostatic feedback loops and thereby acquire content, and what her view predicts about that case; and for each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The remaining questions should probe other pressure points in her view, such as whether her framework can accommodate representational content in plants or in collective entities, or whether her rejection of statistical normal conditions undermines the empirical tractability of her account. Do not include generic biographical questions, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
