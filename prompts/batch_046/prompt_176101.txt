I work as a senior backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been asked to lead a forty-five-minute internal seminar for about fifteen colleagues—mostly senior developers and one product manager—on functionalism and the challenge of multiple realizability. The audience understands computation, distributed systems, and formal verification well, but none of them have read philosophy of mind. I want them to grasp why the claim that mental states are defined entirely by their functional roles, rather than by whatever physically realizes them, is philosophically attractive and yet genuinely problematic, not merely a restatement of "the brain is like hardware running software."

To avoid the tired brain-computer analogy, I want to build the session around a single carrying example drawn from engineering: a distributed transaction coordinator—specifically a two-phase commit protocol—executing the same logical commitment procedure across three radically different physical substrates: bare-metal x86 servers in one data center, ARM-based virtualized containers in a second region, and a simulated test environment running on a quantum annealer that is never used in production. The philosophical hinge I want to hang on this is whether the coordinator's "commit decision" is the same state-analogue in all three realizations, and whether the fact that the underlying physics, energy consumption, and failure modes differ should alter our judgment about what that state actually is.

Please do not explain functionalism to me; plan how I should explain it to them. Specifically: what idea must be in place before the philosophical claim even makes sense to an engineer, and in what order should the components be introduced so that the dependency structure is transparent? Does my distributed-commit example carry the idea properly—what must it have and lack to serve as the central analogy—and if it fails, what should replace it and why? Which misconceptions will this audience form, and in what order will they arise: for instance, that functionalism is just substrate independence in software, that multiple realizability entails that any physical system can realize a mental state, or that I am implicitly arguing our cluster is conscious? At what precise point in the sequence should I head off each misconception? Finally, provide one closing question that would reveal whether they have understood the philosophical tension rather than merely mapped it onto an engineering pattern, and state explicitly what parts of the broader debate—such as phenomenal consciousness, specific historical positions, or technical literature—I should set aside for this audience and time frame and acknowledge as left out.
