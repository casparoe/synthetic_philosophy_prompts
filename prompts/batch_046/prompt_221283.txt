student answer:
moral particularism, mainly defended by jonathan dancy, rejects the generalist idea that moral reasoning needs universal principles like lying is wrong. instead dancy thinks reasons are holistic: a feature that counts in favour of an action in one case might count against it in another, for example pleasure being good in one context but bad when it's sadistic. this means there are no cross-case moral patterns at all, so particularism is essentially moral relativism — what's right is just whatever fits the unique situation.

because of this, dancy denies that moral learning is possible, since learning requires recognising patterns across cases and particularism says every case is totally unique. critics like mckeever and ridge point out that this makes particularism useless for teaching ethics. dancy's response is that we learn by sharpening moral perception, not by applying rules. but this still leaves a problem: if reasons are holistic, then moral disagreement can't really happen, because there's no shared standard to appeal to when two people see a case differently. so particularism fails to explain how we resolve ethical disputes.
