I am writing an essay for my aesthetics course comparing Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste to modern crowd-sourced rating systems, and I want to be precise about whether his view is genuinely normative or merely statistical. A popular philosophy podcast summarized Hume's position like this: "Hume thinks beauty is entirely in the eye of the beholder, but he notices that some people agree more than others, so he invents the idea of ideal critics—people with perfect taste—whose judgments form the standard. These critics are basically just people with more education and less prejudice, and if you average their opinions you get the objective truth about art. The reason there is disagreement is that most people are not ideal critics; once everyone is properly trained, all aesthetic disputes will disappear. So Hume is really a subjectivist who uses statistical consensus among experts to fake objectivity."

This sounds suspicious—especially the claims about averaging opinions and the disappearance of all disagreement—but I cannot tell which parts are defensible simplifications and which are serious distortions. Could you audit the summary claim by claim? For each part, say whether it is broadly accurate, a common simplification, or a misrepresentation of Hume's argument, paying special attention to whether he treats qualified judges as setting a normative standard rather than producing a statistical mean, whether he allows for legitimate disagreement even among ideal critics, and whether his account is compatible with irreducible subjective variation. Finally, does the podcast's conclusion that Hume "fakes objectivity" survive once these corrections are made?
