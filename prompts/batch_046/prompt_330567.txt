I have been working through a series of case judgments with my advisor, Professor Whitfield, whose research focuses on the historiography of moral evaluation and the limits of anachronistic criticism. Over the past semester she has consistently ruled on whether modern scholars may apply present-day normative standards to past actors, and her track record runs as follows: she holds that it is unfair to blame seventeenth-century physicians for persisting in bloodletting after Harvey’s publication on circulation, since the germ-theoretic framework necessary to reject the practice was not yet conceptually available to them; she holds that it is legitimate to condemn nineteenth-century factory owners who employed child labor in conditions they themselves recognized as dangerous, because direct physical harm does not require a modern theoretical vocabulary to be wrong; she holds that criticizing Han-dynasty Confucian scholars for failing to endorse gender equality is anachronistic, because the normative vocabulary of universal citizenship was absent from their intellectual environment; she holds that praising Archimedean approximations of pi by modern mathematical standards is perfectly fair, since formal truths are transhistorical; and she holds that judging the Roman Republic for lacking representative democratic institutions is inappropriate, since that specific political concept had not yet been formulated.

Next week we are scheduled to discuss a case she has not yet addressed: a twelfth-century jurist in Cordoba who, in his legal opinions, permits a husband to strike his wife for persistent disobedience—citing both scriptural authority and the customary social order—while elsewhere in the same corpus he insists that unnecessary cruelty to animals is a grave sin against divine mercy, and he explicitly rejects the harsher physical penalties endorsed by contemporary Maliki jurists in neighboring regions. Predict Whitfield’s verdict on whether modern historians may condemn this jurist for endorsing marital violence. Identify the two most plausible principles that could jointly account for her earlier rulings—one centered on internal conceptual coherence and epoch-relative normative frameworks, the other allowing universal condemnation of direct physical harm regardless of whether the relevant abstract concept was historically available—and say which principle she is more likely running, with the reasoning that supports your preference. Finally, specify the one further hypothetical case that would definitively distinguish these two principles if her verdict on the Cordoba jurist turned out to be ambiguous.
