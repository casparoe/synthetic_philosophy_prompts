The debate between defenders of a collective duty to remember grave wrongs and advocates of a moral case for forgetting—often traced to Margalit and Rieff—seems to have stalled: one side warns that erasure compounds injustice and disrespects victims, the other that perpetual remembrance traps communities in trauma and blocks recovery. Yet both sides treat memory mainly as a relation to the past rather than as a present social practice. Diagnose whether the impasse is substantive or verbal: do they differ about empirical psychology (how remembrance affects healing), about normative authority (who controls a community's narrative—victims, survivors, or the collective), or about the concept of memory itself (fidelity, repetition, or property)? Consider in particular whether introducing the idea of mnemonic sovereignty—that victims hold a right to determine whether a wrong remains in collective consciousness—uncovers a hidden disagreement about ownership of narrative, or merely restates familiar positions in juridical language. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction among kinds of forgetting, an empirical claim about post-conflict societies, or an admission that the sides are answering different questions about repair versus liberation?
