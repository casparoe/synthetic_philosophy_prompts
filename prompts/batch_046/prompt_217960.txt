You are a professional philosopher of applied ethics with particular expertise in risk theory and distributive justice. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm the core commitments that separate a threshold-reserve lifeboat ethic from a stochastic-burden-sharing ethic: list the normative premises each view needs, the objections each is likely to raise about nonlinear risk and moral arbitrariness, and the point at which each side must concede that judgment rather than calculation decides the case.

Then write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Elena Vasquez, defending the threshold-reserve position, and Dr. Marcus Chen, defending the stochastic-burden-sharing position—set in a concrete but conceptually focused scenario. An orbital habitat is calibrated for 80 occupants and currently shelters 72; a damaged neighboring module sends 18 survivors requesting admission. The habitat’s life-support can be expanded, but each occupant beyond 80 increases the probability of catastrophic system failure exponentially rather than linearly, so the risk curve is convex.

Vasquez should argue that this nonlinear structure justifies a strict admission cap—admitting exactly eight newcomers and deterministically excluding the remaining ten—to preserve a reserve margin and protect system survival. She should address Chen’s claim that deterministic exclusion is morally arbitrary by appealing to dependency, prior occupancy, and the asymmetry of imposing risk versus bearing it. Chen should argue that because the risk is collective and probabilistic, fairness requires distributing admission chances via a weighted lottery among all candidates, including—if the numbers require it—some existing residents who volunteer to risk their spots, rather than shielding the 72 incumbents. He should respond directly to Vasquez’s survival argument by questioning why prior presence should function as a moral shield when all parties face the same convex risk function.

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should answer the other’s actual point rather than change the subject. Neither should be a strawman. Do not resolve the dispute with a declared winner, but ensure each interlocutor has articulated at least one direct reply to the opponent’s strongest objection. Keep the language precise but accessible, avoid any bibliography, reading list, or citation request, and do not invoke obscure technical literature that a reader without web access could not reasonably assess. End with both positions intact but sharpened by the exchange.
