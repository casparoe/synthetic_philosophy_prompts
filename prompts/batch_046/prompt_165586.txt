At a seminar dinner, two doctoral students — Lena and Marcus — got into a sustained disagreement about whether it makes sense to claim that the world is becoming less violent.

Lena opened by observing that long-run records of homicide and battlefield mortality show the rate of violent death per capita has fallen sharply over centuries. "That is the metric that matters," she said, "because it measures the risk an average person actually faces. Moral assessment should track individual exposure to harm, not just divide total suffering across an ever-larger population."

Marcus pushed back: "But absolute numbers are not morally irrelevant. If global population has grown exponentially, a falling per-capita rate is fully compatible with a stable or rising total quantum of suffering. You cannot dismiss aggregate harm just because there are more containers for it."

"That leads to an absurdity," Lena replied. "By your logic, a world of ten billion people with near-zero violence would be worse than a world of one million with a single murder, solely because the absolute count is larger. Per-capita risk is the more fundamental measure."

Marcus then raised a conceptual objection. "Violence is not a natural kind with sharp boundaries. If you restrict it to direct physical harm, you miss structural violence — systemic deprivation, institutional coercion — and you compare incomparable historical regimes that lacked the institutions that produce modern structural harms. But if you broaden the concept to include all systemic harm, you lose the data consistency needed for any historical comparison at all."

Lena answered that this skeptical move proves too much. "If conceptual vagueness dissolved every empirical claim, we could never speak of progress or decline. Direct intentional harm is the core case. Expanding the term without measurable indicators is not refinement; it is normative overreach that makes the historical question untestable."

Marcus added a final point. "Both of your metrics assume 'the world' is a single coherent unit for aggregation, but violence is geographically and socially clustered; averaging globally commits an ecological fallacy. Moreover, the organizational capacity for large-scale violence — state apparatus, technological coordination — has grown enormously even as its exercise has become less frequent. The underlying nature of global violence may have transformed rather than diminished, and neither your rate nor your absolute count captures that."

Please go through their exchange step by step. For each round, rate the strength of the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say whether there genuinely is a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, or whether the disagreement ultimately rests on normative commitments — about what counts as violence, how to weight aggregate versus individual risk, and whether organizational capacity should be included — that empirical data alone cannot settle. Finally, identify what both Lena and Marcus are missing, especially regarding how demographic transition affects the interpretation of both metrics and whether global aggregation obscures distributional realities that should matter for moral judgment.
