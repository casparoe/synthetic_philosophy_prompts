Consider the following widespread but rarely examined pattern. In many countries, temporary visitors are socially expected to observe local conventions that are not universal: leaving gratuities in U.S. restaurants, queuing in orderly single-file lines in British public spaces, and obeying pedestrian signals in German cities. Failure to do so typically draws social blame, practical exclusion, or service-worker resentment, not legal punishment. At the same time, legal systems sometimes excuse visitors who violate obscure local statutes on grounds of unavoidable ignorance, yet social norms almost never extend a parallel excuse—no one accepts I come from a non-tipping culture as a justification for withholding a gratuity. Meanwhile, the mechanisms of enforcement differ sharply: tipping relies on economic custom and implicit worker expectation, queuing on peer shaming and spatial design, and jaywalking on direct state penalty. Yet all three are treated by locals as binding on the visitor in roughly the same moral register.

What implicit philosophical theory, if any, does this triad of practices presuppose about the source and scope of moral obligation? Reconstruct the view that best explains why temporary presence generates these duties: is it a tacit consent theory in which the visitor implicitly accepts local terms by entering the territory; a harm-based account in which violation causes predictable social or economic damage to local participants; a role-based view in which occupying the role of guest carries specific obligations; or something less familiar—perhaps an unacknowledged theory of moral jurisdiction that treats local normative systems as having territorial authority over behavior regardless of citizenship or prior agreement? Distinguish carefully what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates: could the same patterns exist if obligations came purely from universal practical reason, or purely from an explicit contract entered knowingly? Identify the central internal tension—especially the asymmetry between legal excuses for ignorance and social refusal of them—and say whether it reveals a genuine inconsistency in the implicit theory or can be resolved by distinguishing legal jurisdiction from moral jurisdiction. Finally, describe what a practice built on an alternative foundation—say, a universalist harm principle with no deference to local custom, or a pure consent-based view requiring explicit opt-in—would have to change in each of the three domains, and whether any part of the current pattern could survive that replacement intact.
