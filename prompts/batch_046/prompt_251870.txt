As a novelist long preoccupied with the peculiar authority that invented characters wield over a reader's heart, please construct a rigorous philosophical argument for the following position: the classic paradox of fiction—that we feel genuine pity, fear, or grief for beings we know to be non-existent—is not a paradox at all once we reject the premise that such emotions must be object-directed in the ordinary doxastic sense. Instead, argue that these responses are structurally calibrated affective registrations directed at what I shall call the narrative's moral topology—the real, abstract relational properties of a plot, such as the irreconcilable tension between individual autonomy and institutional form, that exist independently of any belief in the characters' actual existence. You should defend the claim that when a reader weeps for Anna Karenina, the emotional object is not the fictional woman but the instantiated tragic structure she embodies; the particularity of the attachment is explained not by belief in a person but by the unique phenomenological profile of that structural instantiation. Address at least two serious objections: first, that this account confuses feeling about a pattern with feeling for an individual, and second, that it cannot explain why readers develop sustained, character-specific loyalties rather than merely appreciating abstract plots. In answering, distinguish your view sharply from the standard make-believe account, and show why treating fiction as a form of abstract emotional engineering avoids both the paradox and the ontological commitments that make-believe theories require.
