In recent debates over whether to retain colonial-era street names and public statues, one often encounters the analogy that urban commemorative space functions like a history textbook: altering a name or removing a monument is likened to tearing out an unwelcome page, leaving future citizens with a sanitised, incomplete record of the past. A local councillor recently deployed this comparison, describing an existing street name as merely a footnote in the urban archive and warning that renaming it amounts to editorialising history rather than recording it. I would like you to audit this analogy thoroughly. First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the source domain onto the target: what plays the role of the author, the reader, the footnote, the library, and the act of tearing; and what, in turn, corresponds to the municipal authority, the pedestrian, the named figure, the city fabric, and the act of renaming or removal?

Second, identify precisely where the mapping fractures—particularly regarding whether a street name operates primarily as an archival record of fact or as a present-tense act of honour and attribution, and whether monuments and textbooks share the same pedagogical or mnemonic purpose. Examine which features of textbooks the analogy quietly imports into commemorative infrastructure—claims to narrative completeness, authorial neutrality, or implied reader consent—and note which of these imported features the target domain actually lacks. Third, assess whether the normative conclusion that preservation is required to avoid sanitising the past survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along. Finally, if the textbook model collapses under scrutiny, sketch a more precise alternative analogy—perhaps drawn from curatorial practice, urban semiotics, or the logic of honorific naming—that better captures what renaming or removal actually does to public space, and say whether that replacement analogy supports a different ethical verdict.
