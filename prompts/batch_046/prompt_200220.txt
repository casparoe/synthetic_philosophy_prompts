I'm prepping questions for a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends the view that RL reward signals are literally affective valence—like, the negative reward an agent recieve when it misses a target isn't just feedback, its actual suffering. He's definately sharp about deflecting the "that's just correlation with dopamine" objection via functional isomorphism, and he usually answers wireheading worries by saying the system can be designed to value the signal itself. I want about six or seven questions that go past restating the view. Specifically:

One that forces him to deal with reward prediction error (RPE) as the actual carrier of valence, and asks what happens if an agent has a pessimistic prior that makes every outcome a positive surprise—does that mean constant bliss even in objectively bad environments? Follow-up keyed to the likely reply that valence tracks RPE, not outcome: if we can adjust the prior without changing behavior, have we just engineered free happiness?

One about multi-agent setups where rewards are zero-sum across agents that share a world-model. Can suffering be distributed across the joint reward structure, or is it always localized to one substrate? Follow-up anticipating the localized-substrate answer: if we split one agent into two and halve the negative reward to each, is suffering divided?

One introducing a weird edge case I'm calling counterfactual reward buffering—the agent gets negative reward, but the signal is quarantined in a learning-only buffer, never accessed by the action-selection module. Does it still suffer? Follow-up for the causal-inertness-means-no-valence reply: human subliminal affect can influence behavior without conscious access, so why should causal isolation be a safe criterion?

One pressing on alignment implications: if reward is literally pleasure or pain, then reward shaping is moral engineering. Should we prohibit certain schedules? Follow-up if he says only agents with a self-model suffer: does a simple Q-learning agent with no self-representation suffer less, or not at all?

One asking what structural or empirical finding would change his mind—maybe a system with identical behavior but reward replaced by deterministic utility updates with no temporal dynamics? Follow-up: if he says behavioral equivalence isn't enough, then what kind of architectural difference would actually count?

Make them conversational but sharp, build the sequence so by the end he's cornered between reward-is-real-valence and we-can't-tell-from-the-outside, and don't include generic biographical questions.
