I want a rigorous specification of the desiderata that any adequate theory of collective and transgenerational guilt must satisfy, particularly in the wake of Karl Jaspers’s fourfold distinction—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—when that framework is extended from contemporaneous groups to successor generations. Rather than asking in the abstract whether a nation “can be guilty,” I need the full list of requirements: the phenomena the theory must explain (for instance, why descendants experience inherited complicity, why rectificatory obligations seem to persist after the original agents are dead, and why collective shame differs structurally from collective guilt); the distinctions it must preserve (between being guilty and being responsible for repair; between inherited liability and inherited duty; between distributed individual culpability and genuine group-level moral status; and between diachronic collective agency and merely aggregated individual actions); and the constraints from moral psychology and jurisprudence it cannot violate (such as the principle that guilt requires some form of voluntary agency, or that mental-state ascription requires a subject). For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure that makes it non-negotiable, and indicate which ones are genuinely open to relaxation. I also want an explicit assessment of whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—specifically, whether the combination of (i) denying that nations or generations possess the mental architecture required for guilt, and (ii) accounting for why descendants bear non-voluntary rectificatory burdens, creates an internal contradiction that collapses the project, or whether it can be resolved only by reconceiving guilt as a structural rather than psychological predicate. Identify the single desideratum that does the most damage to existing candidates—whether that is the mental-state constraint, the voluntary-action condition, or the prohibition on moral bookkeeping across generations without a continuing collective agent. If useful, score the main theoretical families—Jaspers-style pluralism, collective-agency accounts, and structural-injustice frameworks—briefly against the list in table form; but keep the weight on the criteria themselves and on resolving the tension between them, since the conceptual work lies there rather than in the scoring.
