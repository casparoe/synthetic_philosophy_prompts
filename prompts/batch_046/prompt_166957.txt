I'm working through epiphenomenalism in my philosophy of mind seminar, and I want to test an extension of the paradox of phenomenal judgment. I introduce a hypothetical "phenomenal mirror" — a system that reports its qualitative states using only structural-functional descriptions, with no causal path from the qualitative state itself to the report. Here is the argument I built, but it seems to prove too much: (1) If epiphenomenalism is true, qualitative states have no causal effects. (2) Every judgment that attributes a qualitative state is a physical event. (3) So no such judgment is caused by the qualitative state it attributes. (4) If a judgment about X is not caused by X, then it is not epistemically justified in attributing X. (5) Therefore no judgment attributing qualitative experience — including introspective reports and reports about other minds — is justified. (6) This holds even for the phenomenal mirror, since its structural descriptions are still physical events lacking qualitative causes. (7) But the conclusion that all phenomenal attribution is unjustified is absurd, since we routinely treat introspection and other-minds reasoning as at least somewhat reliable. (8) So one of (1)–(6) must be wrong. I would prefer to keep (1) for the sake of the exercise. Is (4) the right premise to reject? If so, why does it seem plausible, and what does giving it up cost — does it force us to allow justification without causal connection, or is there a more subtle equivocation between causal isolation and epistemic failure hiding in the move from (3) to (4)?
