Im in a course on global economic history and we're stuck on whether the 'why Britain and not China' framing of the Great Divergence is actually well-posed or if its just a parlor trick with counterfactuals. I got to wondering: if Adam Smith were somehow around now and read the modern literature — not the pop-history version, but the stuff that uses these 'scale-normalized fiscal capacity' metrics to compare Qing and British state extraction, plus the newer claim that China's problem wasn't weak institutions but an institutional equilibrium that was too stable — what do you think he'd actually say? I don't mean 'he'd like capitalism.' I mean, given his actual concerns about how the division of labor depends on market size and about how mercantile interests capture the state, would he think comparing a continental empire to a small island trading nation is conceptually fair? Or would he argue that once you control for geographic scale and resource endowment the institutional comparison collapses into tautology? There's also this weird methodological move I keep noticing where historians adjust the comparison metric to account for climate risk or population density, but then use the adjusted metric to prove that institutions, not geography, drove divergence — which feels circular, like baking the answer into the normalization. Is there an established name for that kind of circularity in comparative historical explanation, or am I describing something that doesn't have a label yet? I'd like to know both what Smith's reasoning would probably look like and whether my worry about the metrics is a real problem or just my confusion.
