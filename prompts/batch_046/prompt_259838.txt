I run a 60-minute graduate philosophy seminar with about 12 students. Design an in-class simulation—using only symbolic stakes such as points, temporary speaking privileges, or tokens—that makes participants experience the rational paradox of nuclear brinkmanship: that actors can be driven toward mutual catastrophe precisely through rational attempts to make threats credible, especially when credibility requires leaving outcomes partly to chance. The rules and payoff structure should generate this pull before anyone names it; include a hidden mechanical “automatic escalation” trigger (unknown to players at the start) that can force disaster regardless of either side’s current preference, so participants must choose whether to disable it—thereby undermining credibility—or leave it active and risk accidental escalation. Specify roles and materials, the payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute 60-minute flow. Provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto credibility, commitment, information asymmetry, and accidental-risk dynamics. Also tell me what will predictably fail the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of real-world brinkmanship—say, true irreversibility of launch or complete opacity of adversary resolve—the activity necessarily simplifies and that you must supply in discussion. The activity should require no prior knowledge of international relations and should not involve any research, citations, or real-world case study during the session.
