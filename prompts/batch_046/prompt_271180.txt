Read Spielberg's Schindler's List through the lens of the paradox of tragedy — specifically, through what philosophers sometimes call the conversion problem, the claim that negative emotions elicited by tragic art are transmuted into a positive aesthetic response. The film is an obvious candidate: it depicts the Holocaust with unflinching brutality, offers no conventional redemption arc (Schindler's breakdown in the final scene explicitly refuses heroic closure), and employs black-and-white cinematography that withholds the sensory pleasure typical of spectacle cinema. Yet audiences seek it out, discuss it, and return to it. The standard solutions — Aristotelian catharsis, Hume's transformation of sympathy into pleasure, Nietzsche's affirmation of suffering — all presuppose some mechanism by which horror becomes satisfying. Schindler's List seems to deny that mechanism: the horror remains horror, the girl in red remains an image of lost innocence rather than a symbol resolved by narrative, and the liquidation scene witnessed from the hill offers aesthetic distance without emotional relief. So does the film dissolve the paradox by showing that we can value representations of suffering without any hedonic payoff, perhaps because they serve epistemic or moral ends? Or does it deepen the paradox by showing that we are drawn to precisely those representations that refuse to console us? I'd like a reading that takes both the film's formal strategies and the philosophical debate seriously — that identifies which scenes illuminate which theories, where the film resists being read as an illustration, and whether Schindler's List ultimately supports the view that the paradox is a pseudo-problem generated by assuming aesthetic value must be hedonically positive.
