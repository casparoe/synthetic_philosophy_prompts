Consider the widespread institutional practice of prepending content warnings—brief notices about potentially distressing material—to university syllabi, assigned texts, and public broadcasts. In the framework that distinguishes frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages (the layered-message analysis associated with Gödel, Escher, Bach), any communicated artifact carries a frame message (the contextual setup instructing the recipient how to interpret what follows), an outer message (the explicit content being delivered), and an inner message (a self-referential signal about the message’s own status, origin, or authority). A content warning appears to function as a frame message: it directs the reader to adopt a particular interpretive posture before encountering the outer message (the novel, the lecture, the film). Yet the warning also frequently carries an inner message—an implicit claim about the institution’s values, its recognition of trauma, or its authority to classify certain experiences as harmful—which can alter the reader’s relationship to the outer message independently of any explicit framing instruction.
