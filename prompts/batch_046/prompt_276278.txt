I've been arguing with my sister about our grandma's advance directive—she signed one refusing feeding tubes if she ever had severe dementia, but now she's in that state and seems genuinely content, even happy in her own way. My sister thinks honoring the paper is just cruel, but I feel like there's something disrespectful about ignoring a choice she made with full capacity. I'm writing this for a philosophy course on medical ethics and I don't want a both-sides essay; I want to take a real position and defend it.

The usual framing is Ulysses-contract style: the competent self binds the incompetent future self. But I think that's too simple, because the issue might not just be changed preferences—it's that the criteria for a good life might be totally incommensurable across these stages. The earlier self valued narrative coherence and bodily autonomy; the present self lives in pure present-tense contentment with no sense of narrative loss. So is enforcing the directive actually a kind of cross-temporal imperialism? Or is ignoring it a failure to respect diachronic agency?

Write an essay that takes a definite stance on whether advance directives should remain binding when there's what you could call "normative framework collapse" between the earlier and later self—not just value drift, but a breakdown in shared criteria for well-being. Don't lay out every option and leave me hanging; defend a verdict. Say exactly where you draw the line—maybe you think bindingness should depend on the type of intervention, or on whether the present self can still articulate any preferences, or on something else entirely—and explain why that line holds up. Be committed: if you think the answer is mostly yes with narrow exceptions, say so and argue for it; if you think it's mostly no, same deal. I want to see your reasoning, not a literature review, so please don't include bibliographies or citation lists.
