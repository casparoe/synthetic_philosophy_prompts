After a long argument with a friend about whether philosophical thought experiments actually reason or merely stage-manage our judgments, I keep returning to her specific charge: that an intuition pump does not merely suppress context but hides its own editing, presenting a constructed scenario as if it were a bare possibility—a “director’s cut” that makes the outcome feel inevitable rather than arranged. I want to close that conversation with a formal artifact that enacts the problem rather than summarizing it. Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains and only two rhymes—spoken in the voice of someone who has just run an intuition pump on themselves about agency and now suspects the frame. The first refrain should announce that the choice is visible and clean; the second should insist that something has been cut from the shot. As the form forces the refrains to alternate and finally converge in the closing quatrain, let their relationship shift: they should pass each other without resolving, ending side by side so that the reader feels the suppression rather than being told about it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Frankfurt-style manipulation arguments recognizes the hidden background conditions—narrative continuity, the agent’s own history being bracketed—without naming philosophers or citing texts. Take a definite position: the pump misleads precisely by concealing its directorial editing, not by being false. Once the poem is finished, append a brief, self-critical note—no more than a short paragraph—stating where the verse takes liberties with the philosophical literature, for example by collapsing distinct objections to Frankfurt cases into a single image of the cut frame, or by leaning on a contested view that all intuition pumps work through suppressed narrative context rather than through isolated conceptual analysis.
