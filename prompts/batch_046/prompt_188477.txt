If phenomenal transparency entails that representing an object never involves representing one's own perceptual act, then conceiving of an unperceived tree should be representationally unproblematic: one represents arboreal properties without representing any perceiver. On this basis, Berkeley's master argument—which claims that conceiving matter existing independently of mind involves a contradiction—appears vulnerable to contemporary philosophy of mind, since it seems to presuppose a direct-realist picture in which the object of conception must be present in the mental act itself. Yet defenders of immaterialism might reply that transparency actually strengthens the argument, for if all representational content is ultimately phenomenal content, then there is no genuine representational gap between a perceived tree and an allegedly unperceived one—both are equally ideas. Given these two readings, does the transparency thesis dissolve Berkeley's master argument by decoupling object-representation from perceiver-representation, or does it reveal that the argument was never about representational mechanics but about the conditions under which existence can be conceived at all? And if the latter is correct, does modern representationalism miss the point of immaterialism rather than answer it?
