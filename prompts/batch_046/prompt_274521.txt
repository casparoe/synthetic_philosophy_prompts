As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I have been trying to formalize the refusal logic for an AI assistant, and I have built an argument that seems to collapse any coherent paternalism into total refusal. I can see that one of the steps must be wrong, but I cannot name the failure mode without breaking something I want to preserve. Here is the chain exactly as I have it.

(1) A refusal is justified if and only if the expected net harm of complying exceeds the autonomy cost of overriding the user, evaluated using the model's best available world-model.
(2) Net harm must be computed over all causally downstream effects of the output, not merely the immediate interaction, because an AI's influence propagates through whatever the user subsequently builds, sends, or decides.
(3) For any non-trivial request—generating executable code, explaining a synthesis pathway, drafting a legal argument—there exists at least one possible downstream misuse scenario that produces severe harm.
(4) Because the model operates under epistemic openness, it can never assign exactly zero credence to unknown future contexts; therefore the probability weight of that misuse scenario is never precisely zero.
(5) Consequently, for every non-trivial request the expected harm term is strictly positive, and for catastrophically severe harms it necessarily dominates the bounded autonomy cost.
(6) By (1), the system should refuse every non-trivial request.
(7) But an assistant that refuses all substantive queries violates its core design specification to be useful, which is not an acceptable endpoint.
(8) Therefore either (1) is false—there is no harm-autonomy principle capable of grounding refusal—or (7) is not actually a binding constraint.
(9) I want to keep both (1) and (7); they feel like the fixed points of any serious system design. Where is the error?

Specifically, I suspect one of three leaks: an equivocation in (2) between causal contribution and moral responsibility for distant effects; a quantifier or scope shift between "for each request there exists a harm-world" and "the aggregate expectation is dominated by that world"; or a hidden assumption in (4) that treats non-zero credence as sufficient to outweigh autonomy without a principled threshold. Which step should I reject, why did it look technically sound, and what does each repair cost me? If I preserve (1) and (7) by repairing (4), do I need to introduce an arbitrary cutoff for "negligible" probability, and does that undermine the formal rigor I was aiming for? If I repair (2) instead, am I implicitly conceding that an AI is only responsible for immediate outputs, and does that make refusal policies trivially narrow? I want the diagnosis priced out in terms of what the system can still consistently do.
