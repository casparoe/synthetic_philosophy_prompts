Read Robert Louis Stevenson's Jekyll and Hyde through the lens of the metaphysics of dispositions—specifically the taxonomy of finks, masks, and partner-sensitive manifestations. The plot turns on a chemical serum that suppresses or activates Hyde, which invites asking whether Jekyll's violent or transgressive tendencies are an intrinsic disposition that is merely masked or finked by his social persona and the antidote, or whether they are partner-dependent dispositions that exist only in the presence of the chemical trigger, with no stable underlying property in Jekyll alone. Examine the initial experiment, the progressive failure of the serum to restore Jekyll, and the final emergence of Hyde without ingestion: does the narrative treat these as the unmasking of a true self, or as evidence that the conditions for finking and masking are themselves fragile and context-bound? Consider also whether the novella resists this metaphysical framing—particularly in its treatment of moral responsibility—by insisting that Jekyll remains accountable even when his dispositions are chemically altered. I am trying to see whether the horror of the story derives from the idea that our dispositions are reliably hidden and can be accidentally revealed, or from the more unsettling possibility that they are not intrinsic at all but constructed by unstable conditions. What does Stevenson's narrative contribute to this philosophical debate that abstract examples of finked vases or masked fragility do not capture?
