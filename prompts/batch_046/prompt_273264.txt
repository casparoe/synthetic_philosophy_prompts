I'm a high school student preparing a podcast interview for my independent study in philosophy of science. My guest is a philosopher I'll call Dr. Voss, who defends a position she names institutional reflection. She accepts the core idea behind Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle for individual agents: roughly, that a rational agent's current credence in a claim ought to align with her expected future credence in that same claim, given the reasoning process she anticipates undergoing. But Dr. Voss extends this to collective epistemic bodies—peer-review panels, funding boards, and interdisciplinary research committees. She argues that such an institution should calibrate its present collective judgments and resource allocations to match the expected outcome of its own future deliberative procedure, even when individual members currently hold divergent private credences. Critics object that this either collapses into tautology or licenses dangerous epistemic centralization.

Please adopt the role of an expert philosopher of science with deep familiarity with Bayesian epistemology, social epistemology, and van Fraassen's writings. Before drafting the questions, think carefully and provide a brief brainstorming outline of the main pressure points on institutional reflection, including standard individual-level objections to reflection—such as diachronic Dutch-book worries, problems with self-defeating predictions, and known reasoning errors—and how they might scale up to groups. Then, write a polished, formal sequence of six to eight interview questions that press Dr. Voss on the actual mechanics of her view. Each question should anticipate a standard defense she might offer and include the immediate follow-up that makes that deflection harder. Specifically, include questions that ask: how she handles cases where the institution knows its future deliberation will likely be corrupted by funding bias or disciplinary prestige; whether her view requires members to suppress their private credences in favor of the institutional expectation; and what conceptual or empirical considerations could count against institutional reflection. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write entirely in American English, maintain a formal tone appropriate for a student research project, and avoid generic biographical questions.
