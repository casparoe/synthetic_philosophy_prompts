Student answer: Ayer's verification principle, articulated in his major early work, holds that a statement is meaningful only if it is either analytic or empirically verifiable. Assertions about life after death fail this test because there is no possible observation—whether direct or indirect—that could confirm the continued existence of a personal consciousness detached from bodily function. After his 1988 near-death experience, Ayer reported seeing a red light and sensing an impersonal cosmic order. He insisted that this was a neurological episode occurring during severe physiological crisis, not perception from a post-mortem state. This is fully consistent with verificationism: brain-state descriptions are in principle reproducible and intersubjectively checkable, whereas survival claims are not.

The objection that this reasoning is circular misses the point. Ayer does not need verificationism itself to license the gap between phenomenal reports and metaphysical commitments; he relies on the ordinary conceptual distinction between describing what one experienced and asserting what exists independently of that experience. His report describes neural events; survival asserts a disembodied subject. The critic conflates these levels. Furthermore, Ayer's remark that the experience felt profoundly real does not entail any ontological commitment—it merely registers subjective quality. Hence there is no real tension in his position. The only residual difficulty is that Ayer never explained why a single-subject report should carry epistemic weight if verification is strictly intersubjective, but that problem belongs to philosophy of mind generally, not to his specific argument about the afterlife.
