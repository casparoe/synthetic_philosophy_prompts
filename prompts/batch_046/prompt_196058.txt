I'm a doctoral student in comparative sociology and gender studies, and I've been tracking a particular argumentative loop that keeps surfacing in cross-national work on occupational segregation. The basic empirical pattern is familiar—countries with stronger institutional gender equality often show larger, not smaller, gender differences in career and field choices, especially in STEM and care sectors. But I'm interested in a specific conceptual configuration that accompanies this finding, rather than the finding itself.

Here is the pattern as I've observed it in the literature. First interpretation: once legal and economic barriers are lowered, remaining divergence must reflect freely expressed, possibly innate preferences; therefore high equality plus high divergence is evidence that gender differences are real and should be accepted rather than corrected. Second interpretation: structural equality is superficial because deep cultural norms remain intact, so the divergence under equality conditions actually proves that gender socialization is more powerful and pervasive than previously thought; therefore the same data call for deeper cultural intervention. What strikes me is that both readings take the identical empirical outcome—persistent or widened segregation in egalitarian contexts—and treat it as confirmation of their opposing causal story. Moreover, neither side specifies in advance what distribution of occupational choices would count as falsifying their account. The preference account can absorb near-parity as suppressed preference and large divergence as liberated preference; the culture account can absorb parity as successful de-socialization and divergence as resilient norms. So the paradox doesn't just generate disagreement; it seems to generate a situation where the very conditions meant to resolve the nature-versus-culture question instead make both answers compatible with every observable outcome.

I've seen this referred to informally as the Nordic paradox or the gender-equality paradox, but those labels name the empirical correlation, not this specific epistemic structure. Is there an established term—perhaps in philosophy of social science, comparative methodology, or feminist theory—for this kind of double interpretation of residual variance, where two mutually exclusive causal frameworks are both rendered unfalsifiable by the same institutional conditions? Or is it better understood as an instance of a more general phenomenon that happens to appear here? I care about the carving, not the label: what exactly distinguishes this configuration from ordinary confirmation bias, where its edges lie, and whether there are parallel cases in other comparative domains, for instance in debates about multicultural policy and integration outcomes.

One further point: I'm framing this as a failure of the debate to specify counterfactual signatures. If that framing already mischaracterizes how the relevant scholars actually argue—if, for example, one or both camps do offer clear falsification conditions that I'm overlooking—then please say so plainly rather than accommodating my premise. I want the analysis to stand or fall on whether the configuration is real, not on whether it can be politely mapped onto existing terminology.
