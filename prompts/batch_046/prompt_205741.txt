I'm a PhD student in democratic theory, and in two weeks I will deliver the opening address at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Beyond the Ballot: Institutional Design in an Age of Distrust." The room will be split evenly between political philosophers who have published on sortition and local government officials who have implemented participatory budgeting but have never encountered the term lottocracy. The organizers want the address to do genuine conceptual work rather than serve as decoration: specifically, they want me to frame the day's central problem—whether democratic authorization can be transmitted through chains of selection—without resolving it, and to show that treating delegation as mere administration implicitly commits one to a particular philosophical position about the nature of representative legitimacy.

To that end, I want to ground the speech in a concrete but somewhat contrived scenario that I have devised for the occasion. Imagine a city that selects its climate-adaptation board by pure lot from the adult population; this board must then constitute a technical sub-panel to evaluate proprietary flood-risk models. The board faces three options for constituting that sub-panel: direct appointment of credentialed experts, a second random lottery restricted to citizens with relevant technical backgrounds, or nomination by the mayor subject to board confirmation. I need you to write a ten-minute address—formal in register but accessible to non-philosophers—that uses this tri-partite choice to structure what can and cannot be said about legitimacy in multi-level sortitive bodies. The speech should make clear to the theorists that the three options correspond to distinct answers to whether authorization is fungible across selection mechanisms, while remaining intelligible to the officials without resorting to unexplained jargon. Please do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations; the argument must stand on its conceptual architecture alone. Crucially, the address should not resolve whether nested delegation undermines lottocratic legitimacy, but it should demonstrate that whichever mechanism is chosen, something philosophically substantive is being assumed about the homogeneity of citizen competence and the transmission of democratic mandate.
