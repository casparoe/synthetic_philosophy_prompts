Write a polished dialogue between two interlocutors, Mara and Theo, who disagree about whether Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the formation of "the public" in Two Ages reveals a permanent structural feature of mediated discourse or only a contingent feature of nineteenth-century newspapers. Mara should defend the stronger structural claim: that whenever communication is addressed to an abstract, physically absent, numerically indefinite audience, it necessarily produces "the public" as Kierkegaard describes it—a phantom collective that absorbs individual ethical responsibility into reflective distance, making decisive commitment structurally impossible. She should emphasize Kierkegaard's distinction between "the crowd," which requires physical co-presence and therefore dissipates, and "the public," which exists only in reflection and is therefore permanent and leveling. Theo should resist by arguing that Kierkegaard illicitly conflates the formal properties of mass address with the psychological effect of anonymity, and that mediated collectives can sustain genuine ethical commitment provided members recognize one another as concrete others despite physical absence. To test their disagreement, Mara should introduce a thought experiment: imagine a society in which every citizen receives a daily, personalized statistical aggregation of every other citizen's moral choices—complete transparency without any face-to-face encounter—asking whether this still produces leveling. Theo should respond that the scenario actually supports his view, because the problem is not abstraction or distance per se but the substitution of quantitative comparison for qualitative judgment. Ensure that each speaker directly addresses the other's previous point, that neither is a strawman, and that the dialogue moves through at least three substantive exchanges.
