A colleague in my retirement study circle, formerly in cognitive psychology, offered the following summary of Daniel Dennett’s account of the self as a center of narrative gravity. The self, she claimed, is not an internal observer or Cartesian substance but an abstract theoretical posit strictly analogous to a physical center of gravity: useful for predicting behavior, yet possessing no localized neural substrate. Because this narrative center is purely fictional—no more real than the center of mass of a chair—neurological damage cannot affect the self itself, only the brain’s storytelling apparatus. It therefore exerts no downward causal influence; it functions solely as a post-hoc rationalization, like a spokesperson explaining decisions already executed by other organizational units. From this she inferred that Dennett is essentially a Cartesian dualist in reverse: he retains an immaterial, causally inert ego but denies it any bodily location or functional role.

Having spent my career analyzing non-linear dynamical systems in condensed-matter physics, I find this characterization suspicious on physicalist grounds. If the self genuinely parallels a center of gravity, then like any dynamical invariant it ought to shift continuously with alterations in the underlying distribution—brain injury should change its trajectory in empirically predictable ways, rather than leaving it intact. Furthermore, contemporary predictive-processing models treat autobiographical coherence not as passive commentary but as a hierarchical prior that actively constrains sensory inference and action selection. Would treating the narrative self as a strange attractor in autobiographical state-space—rather than a static, causally inert abstraction—better capture Dennett’s physicalism, or does that framing import commitments he explicitly rejects?
