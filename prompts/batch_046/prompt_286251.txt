I'm preparing a unit for a philosophy of art seminar on the paradox of fiction—specifically, the puzzle of why we experience what seem to be genuine emotional responses to entities and events we know to be invented, and whether those responses lack the action-motivating force that comparable real-world emotions possess. Rather than treating the paradox as an abstract puzzle about propositional attitudes, I want to examine how a canonical work might dramatize its own conditions of affective engagement. Read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through this lens, with particular attention to the novel's nested narrative architecture: Walton's letters framing Victor's account, which in turn frames the creature's autobiography. How does each successive layer of mediation alter the reader's emotional access? At which points does the text seem to invite emotional contagion—an immediate, visceral reaction to the creature's physical appearance or suffering—versus sympathetic imagination, a cognitively mediated grasp of his motives and grievances?

Consider especially the creature's request that Victor build him a companion, and Victor's subsequent destruction of the half-finished female creature. Does our pity for the creature at this moment carry the same moral weight it would in a real-world case of abandonment, or does the framing remind us that we are engaged in a kind of affective rehearsal? Conversely, does the novel ever resist being read as merely an illustration of the paradox—perhaps by suggesting that the emotions it elicits are not deficient but differently structured, or by using the framing device to implicate the reader in a chain of failed responsibility that mirrors Walton's and Victor's? I am especially interested in whether the distinction between contagion and imagination can help explain why readers often report stronger sympathy for the creature in his own narrated chapters than in Victor's summary judgments of him. Please identify the scenes that best support and best complicate this reading, and conclude by saying what, if anything, Shelley's narrative strategy adds to the philosophical debate that a purely theoretical analysis of fictional emotions misses.
