I have been trying to work out how Scanlon's contractualism handles the aggregation problem in cases of distributed statistical risk. The standard presentation says that contractualism avoids aggregating small harms across many people to justify a large harm to one, because each person's reason not to suffer that large harm stands independently and cannot be cancelled by others' smaller benefits. But I am unsure whether this logic applies symmetrically to risk-imposing collective action. Suppose a public policy—such as a nationwide carbon-capture infrastructure programme—saves thousands of lives but imposes on every citizen a minuscule, imperceptible increase in personal risk, say a 0.001 per cent rise in mortality. Since contractualism requires us to ask whether each affected individual could reasonably reject the principle permitting this risk, and since we are not allowed to aggregate the millions of tiny complaints into a single collective burden that is then weighed against the aggregate lives saved, does it not follow that every citizen has an unrebutted, individual veto? If so, hasn't Scanlon's framework inadvertently made all large-scale preventive policies that distribute microscopic risks morally impermissible, not because the risks are large, but precisely because they are too small to be aggregated away?

In answering, please distinguish carefully between what is settled in Scanlon's own texts and what remains genuinely contested among philosophers working on contractualism and moral risk, and avoid presenting any particular reading of 'reasonable rejection' as the consensus position. Please reply in British English.
