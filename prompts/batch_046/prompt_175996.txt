I have been working through Frege's puzzle about identity statements on my own—the idea that stating something is identical to itself can be trivial, while stating that two different names pick out the same thing can actually teach you something, provided those names present the object in genuinely different ways. I understand the familiar astronomical case with the morning and evening star, but I want to see how the reasoning holds up in a situation that could not have existed in Frege's time and that involves no physical observation at all.

Consider a digital identity system in which every user possesses two independent identifiers. One is visible to other users for messaging and social interaction; the other is used only by internal matching algorithms that coordinate records across separate servers. For any given account, these two strings are permanently linked to the exact same underlying profile, yet they are assigned through different registration procedures, formatted according to different rules, and updated through different administrative pathways—altering the visible name leaves the internal key untouched, and modifying the internal key requires an entirely separate verification process. A user eventually receives an automated notification revealing that her public display handle and her hidden database key resolve to one and the same account, even though she had always treated them as belonging to separate domains of her digital presence.

If Frege were alive today and analyzing this discovery, would he treat the realization that Handle-Alpha is Key-Beta as genuinely informative in the same manner as the realization that Hesperus is Phosphorus? Or would he argue that, because both identifiers are purely conventional instruments created by engineers rather than by natural perceptual encounter—there is no analog to seeing Venus at dawn versus seeing it at dusk—this is merely a trivial identity disguised by institutional complexity? I am especially interested in whether he would require that two distinct senses depend on some difference in how the object is cognitively or perceptually given to the thinker, or whether he would allow that purely functional divergence—different rules of use, different contexts of access, different behaviors under update—can be sufficient to establish two genuinely different modes of presentation.

There is a further variation I want to understand. Suppose the system is designed so that the internal key not only remains constant when the visible name changes, but also behaves differently in predictive algorithms: the matching engine treats Key-Beta as a persistent entity that retains properties across time, while treating Handle-Alpha as a replaceable label with no inherent continuity. Would Frege regard this behavioral asymmetry as establishing two distinct senses, even though the reference never actually splits? And if he were forced to choose between preserving his commitment that sense determines the pathway to reference and accommodating institutional designs that create multiple functional routes to one object, which principle do you think he would prioritize?

Please reconstruct the reasoning from the conceptual structure of the puzzle itself rather than from modern scholarly commentary I have not read; I am less interested in what commentators say about Frege's texts and more interested in how the logic of sense and reference applies to this kind of engineered case.
