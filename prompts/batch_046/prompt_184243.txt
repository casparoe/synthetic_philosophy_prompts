I'm preparing a seminar paper on authenticity and value in art, and in recent discussions with classmates I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I don't have a vocabulary for. The pattern involves how people defend the special value of severely damaged or reconstructed originals while simultaneously denying that value to perfect copies or to long-mistaken forgeries, but the defense doesn't seem to rest consistently on either material continuity or aesthetic quality.

Consider two situations. First, a Renaissance panel painting that art historians agree has been overpainted so extensively that perhaps only fifteen percent of the visible surface is by the master's hand; it remains in a national collection, insured and displayed as an original, and defenders of its status point not to the artist's direct touch but to an unbroken paper trail of ownership, exhibition, and legal attribution. Second, a marble statue shattered in the eighteenth century, then reconstructed over decades with substantial modern filler and replacement fragments; it is still treated as the authentic work, whereas a modern sculptor who carved an identical figure from the same quarry using the original design is dismissed as producing a mere replica. Now consider the asymmetry: if a perfect forgery had hung in that same museum for a century, acquired the same paper trail of display and cataloguing, and was then exposed, people say its value collapses because it never "really" belonged to that history; yet if the genuine statue had been lost in storage for the same century, hidden and unknown, and was then rediscovered, people say its value was always intact and merely unrecognized. The criterion seems to shift — material origin is invoked when it protects the original, the continuity of social and curatorial history is invoked when material origin is weak, but that same continuity is denied to non-originals that have acquired exactly it.

Is there an established name for this specific double-standard — the selective deployment of curatorial and legal continuity as a fallback criterion for authenticity, coupled with the refusal to grant that continuity independent value-conferring power? How does it differ from the Ship of Theseus, from institutional theories of art, and from plain equivocation on "original"? If there is no precise term, coin one with a rigorous definition and boundary cases that distinguish it from cases where people simply change their minds about value. Give me a committed answer: take a definite position on whether this asymmetry is a coherent philosophical distinction or a motivated rationalization, defend that position with reasons, and present it with your bottom line in the first sentence followed by reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
