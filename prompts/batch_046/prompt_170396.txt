I'm wondering about the philosophy behind binding your future self in law. In mental-health policy, a lot of jurisdictions let people sign advance psychiatric directives—basically Ulysses contracts—saying "if I go manic, treat me even if I refuse then." Meanwhile constitutional design uses eternity clauses or crazy-high amendment thresholds that bind future generations who never consented. Both are precommitment devices, but liberal theory usually celebrates the first as autonomy-preserving and attacks the second as undemocratic. What philosphical commitments about identity and authority do these practices share? Do they presuppose a thick diachronic self that stays the same across mental states or generations, or can they survive on a thin time-slice welfare view? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether individual and collective precommitment actually rest on the same view or just look alike, and tell me what a legal system that adopted a Parfit-style successive-selves model—treating future voters or future mental states as more like different people—would have to change about psychiatric directives and constitutional amendment rules. Is there a hidden tension: if future democratic generations are "not us" and therefore can't be bound, why isn't the manic future self also "not me"?
