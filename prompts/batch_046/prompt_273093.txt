In my advanced seminar on political philosophy, we have spent several weeks with Berlin's taxonomy and Pettit's republican alternative, yet my students persist in treating negative, positive, and republican liberty as parallel sorting bins for policies rather than as frameworks that can generate genuine conceptual friction. I want to present them with a single institutional arrangement—drawn from welfare design, workplace governance, or digital infrastructure rather than pure abstraction—where one structural feature produces exactly that friction. Specifically, construct a case in which the same mechanism (1) eliminates an agent's exposure to arbitrary interference of the kind Pettit associates with republican unfreedom, (2) substantively expands the agent's positive liberty by building a real capacity they previously lacked, and yet (3) institutes a diffuse, systemic dependency that is not an actual interference and therefore remains entirely invisible to a negative-liberty analysis. The dependency should be constitutive of the mechanism itself, not a separable cost or side effect: the very feature that removes domination and builds capacity should also embed the agent in a relation of structural vulnerability that negative liberty lacks the vocabulary to register, and that republican liberty might mischaracterize as mere non-domination. Describe the institutional mechanics in concrete terms, then explain precisely how each of the three concepts evaluates the feature differently—paying special attention to why negative liberty sees no problem, why republican liberty might celebrate the gain, and how the residual dependency escapes both frameworks without collapsing into a simple trade-off. I was thinking of something like algorithmic eligibility systems in public services or mandatory credentialing pathways in labor markets, but I am open to whatever arrangement best sharpens the tension.
