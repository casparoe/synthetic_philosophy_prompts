I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on intergenerational ethics, and I'm helping design a reusable evaluation framework for a philanthropic review board that must compare near-term welfare proposals against long-term existential-risk projects with no direct present beneficiaries. I want a decision procedure grounded in Samuel Scheffler's idea that human extinction is bad not only because of the deaths it entails, but because it eliminates the "afterlife" of future generations—the continued human community whose existence gives present projects and relationships their meaning and point. To prevent the framework from collapsing into a generic risk-benefit checklist, it must operationalize a specific conceptual distinction I am developing: it should classify each proposal according to whether the present generation's stake is primarily project-structured (value depends on continuing a specific endeavor, such as scientific or cultural work) or relationship-structured (value depends on the expectation that particular others—descendants, colleagues, audiences—will exist to receive and carry forward what we do). The board needs a step-by-step protocol they can run in about thirty minutes, requiring no philosophical training, with clear stopping rules: where a factual answer suffices, where the procedure issues a provisional recommendation, and where it must halt because the question is irreducibly normative. Please construct the checklist in sequence, specifying what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage and flagging the points where judgment is unavoidable. Then apply it to three cases: (1) funding a two-century biosecurity program to prevent engineered pandemics; (2) a malaria eradication campaign whose benefits are confined to the present generation; and (3) a digital-archive preservation initiative that assumes human continuation but includes no mechanism to secure it. Include at least one case—likely the biosecurity program—where the procedure recommends supporting the existential-risk proposal even though the board's intuitive welfare calculus favors the near-term option, and explicitly state what the procedure cannot settle, such as whether the afterlife value is agent-relative or extends to strangers' projects.
