I recently came across an essay in a popular history-of-science magazine that makes the case for treating non-human forces—specifically epidemic disease, long-term climate patterns, and major volcanic events—as "historical actors" deserving the same explanatory weight as statesmen or social movements. The piece offers this summary of the position: "Traditional historiography grants agency only to beings with intentions, but that is an arbitrary human bias. When cholera bacteria forced the redesign of urban water systems, when the Tambora eruption triggered widespread famine and redirected global migration flows, and when multi-decadal climate shifts undermined agricultural empires, these forces did not merely influence events—they caused turning points. If historians take this seriously, they must concede that human culture and political choice are largely epiphenomenal; deeper biological and geological drivers do the real work. Critics who invoke 'the perils of environmental determinism' are mostly defending human exceptionalism, and their standard rebuttal—that correlation between climate and conflict does not prove causation—actually misses the point, since determinism has not been empirically refuted but only fallen out of fashion."

The framing strikes me as more ambitious than the usual "environment matters too" argument, but I am unsure how much of it fairly represents the scholars who actually study disease, climate, and geology as historical factors, and how much is a popularizer's overreach. In particular, please clarify whether the dismissal of the determinism objection is fair: do critics of environmental determinism typically deny correlation, or is their concern something more subtle about monocausality, contingency, or reductive explanation?
