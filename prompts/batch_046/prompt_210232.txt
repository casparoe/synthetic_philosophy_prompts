I am preparing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of cognitive capacities, and I would like your analysis of a tension embedded in a widespread institutional practice. In psychometric assessment, there is a long-standing design commitment to so-called culture-fair testing—exemplified by non-verbal matrix-reasoning instruments that deliberately exclude linguistic and culturally specific content—coupled with the practice of norming these instruments against reference populations whose educational and socioeconomic backgrounds vary widely. The resulting scores are then treated as measuring a single, domain-general capacity—often labeled g—that is simultaneously culturally neutral and causally predictive of academic and occupational outcomes. At the same time, the field’s own factor-analytic tradition represents intelligence as hierarchically differentiated, with distinct broad abilities (fluid reasoning, crystallized knowledge, processing speed, working memory, etc.) that do not reduce without remainder to a unitary factor.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of intelligence does this institutional practice presuppose? Specifically: does the design of culture-fair instruments presuppose that intelligence is an intrinsic, individual-level property rather than a relational or context-dependent achievement? Can the practice of using predictive validity as the primary justification for treating these scores as intelligence measures be reconciled with a conceptual account that requires intelligence to be manifest in adaptive problem-solving within specific environments? Is there an implicit commitment to a form of psychological essentialism—that there is one “true” intelligence that can be isolated from its manifestations—or is the practice philosophically underdetermined, compatible with pluralist, contextualist, or even eliminationist conceptions? Furthermore, examine whether the practice is internally consistent: does treating intelligence as both unitary (for the purpose of generating a single composite score) and differentiated (for the purpose of clinical and research factor models) depend on a sustainable distinction between measurement convenience and ontological commitment, or does it collapse under scrutiny?

Please provide a rigorous analysis, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, identifying where rival philosophical accounts of intelligence would require altering the practice itself, and noting any apparent inconsistencies that dissolve on closer inspection.
