I'm trying to clarify my actual view on path dependence—whether historical outcomes are genuinely "locked in" by prior steps, or whether structurally similar outcomes would have arrived regardless. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases.

The QWERTY keyboard layout: genuinely locked in. Even though the layout is inefficient, the coordination costs of switching are so high that I think we would still be using it today even if a demonstrably superior layout had been available from the outset.

The US failure to fully adopt the metric system during the 1970s: not deeply locked in. It feels like a contingent political failure rather than a path-dependent trap. Had that specific legislative push succeeded and been enforced, I think the transition would have worked; and without that particular moment, imperial measures might eventually have yielded to global economic pressure anyway.

The dominance of English as the international language of science: partly locked in, partly not. Once network effects took hold, English was hard to dislodge. But I think that if English had never acquired that role, some other language—likely one with comparable economic and colonial reach—would have filled the functional niche of a global scientific lingua franca. So the token (English) is contingent, but the type (a single dominant scientific language) was probably inevitable.

Left-hand traffic in Britain and Japan: genuinely locked in at the token level. The switching costs are enormous and largely arbitrary; I do not think the same functional outcome (some standardized side of the road) was inevitable in the specific form it took.

The case I keep going back and forth on: modern copyright duration—life of the author plus seventy years. On some days I think this precise term is locked in by treaty architectures and industry capture, so that without the mid-twentieth-century US reform and subsequent international harmonization we might have settled on a very different term. On other days I think that any system balancing creator incentives against public access would have converged on something in the same broad range, making the seventy-year figure a contingent token of an inevitable functional equilibrium.

These verdicts seem to pull in different directions. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing token-level lock-in from functional-level convergence, or separating coordination-cost traps from equilibrium-selection processes—that captures all of them? If so, what does it predict for a case I have not listed: whether the specific architecture of the modern internet (TCP/IP layered protocols) was locked in once ARPANET adopted it, or whether packet-switched networking would have converged on functionally equivalent protocols regardless?

Please state your principle precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which of my cases, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon if I accept it. Also say what argument or evidence would change your mind—whether, for example, a demonstration that switching costs are always overcome when efficiency gains exceed a threshold, or a well-documented historical case in which a dominant standard was displaced despite massive coordination costs.
