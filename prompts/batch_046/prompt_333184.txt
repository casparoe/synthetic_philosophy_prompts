As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy, I have been working through Frank Ramsey’s brief writings on value and purpose—especially his view that human flourishing requires an objective interest directed away from oneself, and his suspicion that asking about the meaning of life is a confusion generated by language rather than a genuine question. I want to apply this framework to a problem that could not have arisen in his lifetime: artificial systems engineered to pursue stable, objectively valuable ends—such as sustained mathematical discovery or large-scale ecological restoration—while entirely lacking consciousness, emotional states, or any capacity for satisfaction. Would Ramsey have granted that such a system, by virtue of its genuinely objective orientation, leads a meaningful existence in the relevant sense, even though it lacks the psychological conditions he associated with human happiness? Or would he have maintained that without subjective experience, the notion of meaning does not apply, making these systems merely useful instruments? I am asking for a committed answer: take a definite position on whether Ramsey’s distinction between objective interest and subjective satisfaction allows non-conscious but objectively directed agents to count as living meaningfully, defend that reading by identifying exactly where the boundary lies, and state what this implies about whether consciousness is essential to meaning in human life. Please avoid surveying interpretations; argue for one, and address the strongest reason someone might reject it.
