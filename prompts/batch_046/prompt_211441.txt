I'm taking a course on urban ethics and me and my study group got stuck in a loop about zoning rights. A friend cooked up this argument that NIMBYism is actually morally required, but the conclusion feels insane and I can't find where it leaks.

(1) The right to the city isn't just about access; it's about continuity. If you've structured your whole life around a particular urban fabric—certain shops, noise levels, sightlines, walking routes—then radical alterations to that fabric wrong you in something like the way destroying your home would, even if you don't technically own the land being changed.
(2) Zoning is the institutional mechanism that protects this continuity-intrest by giving current residents democratic control over alterations to their neighbourhood's built enviroment.
(3) So the moral force of democratic zoning comes from protecting continuity-interests, not from property rights or simple majoritarian preference.
(4) But continuity-interests don't only cover physical density and architecture; they also cover the social composition that sustains the neighbourhood's character—local institutions, informal networks, the kind of households that keep things running.
(5) Therefore zoning authority legitimately extends to controlling who can move in, what household types are permitted, and which social uses are allowed, as long as these defend continuity rather than express naked prejudice.
(6) This means residents can democratically block affordable housing, multi-family units, or populations they deem 'incompatible' without it being unjust discrimination, since they're defending continuity.
(7) But if that's right, then exclusionary zoning—one of the main drivers of segregation and inequality—is morally justified whenever long-term residents invoke continuity.
(8) Nobody accepts that segregation is justified just because existing residents feel disrupted.

My friend is attached to (1) and (3), and I actually want to keep (2) if I can. Where does the argument go wrong? Is (4) smuggling in something that (1) doesn't licence, or is (5) conflating physical continuity with social homogeneity? I want to know which step to reject and what each rejection costs—especially whether giving up (1) forces us to abandon any special moral standing for long-term residents. Please answer in British English.
