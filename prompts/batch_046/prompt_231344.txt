I'm preparing a podcast interview with an unnamed defender of a metaphysical interpretation of Ubuntu: she holds that personhood is not an intrinsic property of individuals subsequently embedded in relations, but is ontologically constituted by the density and reciprocity of one's relational networks, so that personal boundaries are drawn by those ties rather than by bodily or psychological continuity. To avoid collapsing into standard social-constructionism, she distinguishes her view by claiming that relations do not merely shape a pre-given self but literally compose it; she further proposes a model of "distributed agency" in which moral and causal responsibility tracks the structural properties of the network rather than being localized in individual nodes. I want a sequence of sharp, content-driven questions for this interview—no biographical warm-up—that forces her to defend the coherence of boundary-drawing without retreating to metaphor. Begin by asking how her ontological claim differs in kind from familiar narrative or social accounts of identity. Then press the persistence problem: if my closest relational ties are gradually replaced through estrangement and new attachment, do I survive as the same person, and can she answer without smuggling back in the psychological or bodily continuity criteria she rejects? Next, anticipate her "distributed agency" reply to the liberal autonomy objection and ask whether it can do genuine work in institutional contexts—say, criminal law—where we seem required to punish or exculpate discrete agents rather than networks. End with one question about what conceptual or empirical finding could genuinely compel her to concede that some substrate of individual substance is necessary after all. Order the questions by increasing damage, and for each indicate the standard reply she is likely to give and the precise follow-up that blocks it.
