I am a retired physical chemist who, in recent years, has turned my attention to the philosophy of biology. After a career studying self-organizing chemical systems far from equilibrium, I find that our ordinary concept of life frays quickly when we press beyond familiar organisms. I should like you to write a formal essay addressing the following controversy: is life fundamentally a matter of organizational closure—the continuous regeneration of a system's own boundary conditions and metabolic network—or is it fundamentally a matter of evolutionary openness—the capacity for heritable variation that is subject to natural selection?

Your essay should weigh both positions carefully, but it must reach and justify a definite conclusion. To do so, examine four cases in parallel. First, synthetic minimal bacterial cells engineered with genomes of only a few hundred genes: they maintain metabolism, homeostasis, and division, yet they were designed in a laboratory and possess no evolutionary lineage of their own. Second, viroids—small circular RNA molecules that replicate, mutate, and evolve within host cells, yet have no metabolism, no membrane, and no ribosomes. Third, yeast prions that propagate conformational information across generations without nucleic acids and without contributing any metabolic function. Fourth, consider a hypothetical informational crystal: a self-replicating lattice in which errors in the growth pattern are copied to daughter lattices, yielding heritable structural variation, but in which there is no chemical metabolism, no boundary membrane, and no Darwinian selection for functional advantage—only structural drift.

Your task is to determine whether organizational closure, evolutionary openness, or some integrated criterion best captures the concept of life across these cases, without either excluding entities that should count as alive or including entities that should not. Specifically, address the objection that your preferred criterion is either too parochial—restricted to Earth-like cellular metabolism—or too promiscuous, extending to computer viruses and mineral lattices. Defend your verdict with conceptual reasoning rather than empirical cataloguing. I do not want a bibliography, reading list, or citation of particular authors; I am interested solely in the coherence and implications of the position you defend.
