I'm preparing a decision-tree handout for a philosophy of mathematics seminar on philosophical reactions to Skolem's paradox. The audience is graduate students who understand the basic result—that first-order ZFC proves there are uncountable sets and that the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem yields a countable model in which some interpreted set is internally uncountable yet externally countable—but who need to locate their own commitments rather than memorize labels.

Please construct a decision tree that starts from this shared premise and sorts respondents into positions through exactly six branch nodes. Each node must be a concrete conceptual question with two or three answer options that a student could choose without already knowing which philosophical interpretation it leads to.

The tree must include these leaves: (1) the standard resolution that treats cardinal properties as model-relative, with no further ontological cost; (2) a position that takes the mismatch to motivate adopting second-order logic with full semantics; (3) a structuralist position that embraces model-variability as showing there is no unique absolute universe of sets; (4) a skeptical position holding that the paradox undermines determinacy in attributing specific cardinalities; and (5) one unoccupied combination of answers, which you should diagnose briefly—whether incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored.

For the branch structure, begin by asking whether the student sees the internal/external mismatch as a dissolved pseudo-problem or as a genuine tension requiring resolution. From there, include nodes that ask: whether the absence of an internal bijection is by itself sufficient for uncountability or whether it requires an absolute background of all sets; whether in the countable model the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth-value relative only to that model's internal resources; whether accepting model-relativity commits one to denying that set-theoretic statements have model-independent truth conditions; and whether the practical success and completeness of first-order logic outweigh its failure to uniquely determine cardinality.

At each leaf, provide either a named position or mark it 'unnamed,' and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment—for instance, that it must treat first-order logic as permanently inadequate despite its ubiquity, or that it must deny that questions about the size of the continuum have a single correct answer. Also flag explicitly any pair of leaves that the tree genuinely cannot separate—if two positions answer every node identically, do not invent a spurious distinction; simply name the ambiguity.

Please conclude by stating how confident you are in the tree's separation of positions, and single out the specific branch pairing or leaf about which you are least sure.
