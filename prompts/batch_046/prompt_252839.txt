I'm drafting this scene where two diplomats argue about whether its worse to lie politely or be brutally honest, and I got stuck on my own position. I think so-called white lies are actually always wrong—not just usually—because they violate what I call the reciprocity of face: the idea that respecting someone means treating them as capable of bearing the same truths you'd want borne to you, without cushioning. I also think Dutch directness is morally preferable to Japanese indirectness not because bluntness is nicer, but because indirectness often hides the speaker's true stance behind a performance of care, which feels like condescension. So politeness that requires deception is worse than rudeness that dont.

What would actually count against this? I dont want the usual culture-is-relative shrug, or some bibliography on pragmatics. I want the arguments, intuitions, or possible cases that should move someone who holds this view—sorted by how fatal they'd be. For instance, if there's evidence that indirectness actually preserves autonomy better than directness does, or if there's a case where withholding truth clearly respects reciprocity rather than violating it, that'd matter. Would defenders of my view have to concede anything? Is there a risk this position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—like saying real respect is directness so any counterexample gets redefined as fake respect?
