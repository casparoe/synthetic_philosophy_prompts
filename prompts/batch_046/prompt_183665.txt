I'm building a decision-tree handout for an advanced epistemology workshop on inside-view versus outside-view reasoning and the limits of epistemic modesty. I want the tree to begin from a concrete conflict case—specifically, you have a strong first-person conviction that your present reasoning about the moral status of synthetic consciousness is sound, yet the historical base rate of philosophers reversing analogous judgments within a decade is very high—and then branch through the logical space of responses rather than through named philosophers.

Please construct a taxonomy in branching-tree form with these properties:

- The root is that conflict case, but the first split should ask whether the tension is best explained as (A) two different kinds of evidence operating on the same question, (B) a cognitive bias affecting one side, or (C) the inside and outside views actually answering different questions that have been conflated.
- Each subsequent node should pose a concrete diagnostic claim on which positions genuinely diverge: for example, whether first-person access to one's own reasoning process counts as a distinct epistemic kind; whether statistical base rates about expert disagreement apply to first-person rational evaluation; whether there is a domain boundary (e.g., consciousness, aesthetic taste, moral intuition) beyond which outside-view modesty is inappropriate; and whether coherence with one's other present beliefs should be weighed more heavily than convergence with long-run expert opinion.
- The leaves should carry standard position names only where they exist (for instance, radical modesty, inside-view exceptionalism, domain-restricted dualism, deflationary unified view) and should be marked unnamed otherwise. Every leaf must include one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—something it is forced to deny or accept that feels counterintuitive.
- Include at least one logically possible combination of answers that is currently unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.
- Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate—positions that yield identical answers to all your diagnostic questions.
- Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure literature that requires verification. You may, however, inject one niche conceptual distinction not standard in textbook treatments—perhaps involving pre-emptive coherence or temporal discounting of one's own past reasoning—to prevent the tree from being a pure recitation of familiar syllabi.

The audience is graduate students who already grasp the basic inside/outside-view distinction but need to locate their own commitments with precision.
