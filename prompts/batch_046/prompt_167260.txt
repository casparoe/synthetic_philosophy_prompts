I am drafting a satirical interlude for a novel set in an administrative near-future where jurisprudence has been fully operationalized, and I need a deadpan parody of an ISO-style internal compliance audit—approximately six hundred words—evaluating the legal system of the fictional Republic of Nova Lex. The document should be formatted as a formal bureaucratic memo, complete with a subject line, an executive summary, and numbered sections on System Validation, User Acceptance, and Deprecation Notices. The voice must remain technically precise and cheerfully neutral throughout, never winking at the reader, so that the conceptual critique emerges entirely from what the audit treats as unremarkable.

The satire should push H.L.A. Hart’s legal positivism to its literal bureaucratic extreme: the separation thesis rendered as a firewall protocol between the Law Module and the Ethics Module; the rule of recognition treated as a purely social-conventional checksum with no moral input; and the internal point of view described as a user-acceptance metric that can be satisfied even when citizens find the law repugnant. Against this, the audit should quietly map Lon Fuller’s objections onto “legacy compatibility issues” and “deprecated feature requests”—particularly the eight principles of legality and the claim that law possesses an inner morality—without ever framing them as moral arguments. For example, include a section in which the system correctly rejects an urgently needed statute because it fails the recognition checksum, logging citizen outrage as “external noise” that the architecture is designed to filter. To keep the piece from being mere summary, inject one genuinely novel twist: perhaps an algorithmic Recognition Engine that weights official acceptance by social-credit metrics, or a proposed “Fuller Patch” that was rejected after testing showed it introduced non-positivist validation criteria. The target is the theoretical position and its practical logic, not any living figure. By the end, a reader familiar with jurisprudence should feel the chill that Fuller’s critics identified—the normalization of legality without morality—while every sentence in the memo remains defensible on its own bureaucratic terms.
