I am a retired population biologist who spent three decades studying selection dynamics, and since leaving the lab I have been reading moral philosophy in a fairly unsystematic way—mostly metaethics, moral epistemology, and some evolutionary ethics. I have no formal philosophical training, so I often find myself noticing argumentative patterns that I cannot map onto the vocabulary I have picked up, and I rely on a small reading group—mostly other retired scientists and one patient graduate student—to help me orient myself.

In our recent discussions about evolutionary debunking arguments and about whether reflective equilibrium is trustworthy, I keep encountering a specific asymmetry that does not seem to match the standard failure modes I know. One interlocutor will dismiss another person’s moral conviction—say, that unnecessary suffering is bad—by labeling it a “bias” on the grounds that the conviction tracks a non-moral feature, such as evolutionary kin-selection or cultural transmission; the causal origin is treated as debunking. Yet when the same interlocutor defends her own conviction—say, that suffering ought to be minimized impartially—she will point out that it, too, tracks a non-moral feature, such as cognitive coherence, aesthetic harmony, or a particular pattern of neural activation, but she will classify that tracking as a “genuine preference” or a “rational insight” rather than as a bias. The structural situation is identical: a normative belief correlates with something outside the domain of moral reasons. What changes is only whether the correlation supports the speaker’s prior conclusion. I have seen this in debates over effective altruism, in arguments about whether our fairness intuitions are trustworthy, and in casual disagreements about the status of evolutionary explanations of morality. It is not exactly special pleading, because no general principle is being endorsed and then excepted for oneself; rather, the classificatory rule itself shifts with the case at hand. It is not the motte-and-bailey, because there are not two versions of a single claim being swapped for defensive and offensive purposes. And while it resembles motivated reasoning or confirmation bias, those terms usually describe belief-formation, not the meta-level act of labeling one correlation a bias and an otherwise parallel correlation a legitimate preference.

If there is not, can you name and define it rigorously—specifying exactly what distinguishes it from special pleading, from motivated reasoning, and from plain inconsistency—and can you provide boundary cases that show where its edges lie? I am not looking for a bare label; I want the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and the conditions under which it arises.
