In the lounge after our determinism seminar, I overheard Maya and Jonas arguing about the Death in Damascus parable—the servant who flees Samarra for Damascus to escape Death, only to find Death waiting there, surprised to see him because their appointment had been in Samarra all along. I said nothing at the time, but the exchange was sharp enough that I want an outside verdict rather than picking sides myself.

Maya began: (1) The story is not mere folklore but a compressed argument that evasion is structurally self-defeating; the servant’s flight to Damascus is precisely the causal path that delivers him to the appointment, so fatalism explains the mechanism of arrival rather than just predicting the endpoint. Jonas replied: (2) That is a retrospective narrative illusion produced by omniscient storytelling; from the servant’s first-person perspective, Damascus is a rational escape route, and if he had stayed in Samarra, Death would not have “known” to look there unless one smuggles in backward causation or divine foreknowledge that collapses contingency into necessity. Maya countered: (3) You are treating Death as a causal agent, but the point is structural—the meeting is fixed, while the location is contingent on the servant’s choice, so he determines where, not whether; this is a modal fixed point, not backward causation. Jonas pushed back: (4) A fixed point still requires that future truths constrain present actions without being caused by them, which is either ontologically mysterious or just logical fatalism in costume; if it is logical—simply that the proposition “he dies in Damascus” is now true—then you have abandoned the story’s causal imagery and replaced it with a tautology about bivalence, meaning the parable either cheats with agency or proves nothing beyond the law of excluded middle. Maya answered: (5) The imagery is pedagogical, not ontological; the “appointment” symbolizes a non-causal explanatory dependence of present action on an already-true future—he goes to Damascus because it is true he will be there, and he cannot make that false—thereby preserving freedom in every causal sense while showing modal correlation. Jonas concluded: (6) If explanation is genuinely non-causal, then the servant’s choice remains free in every operative sense, and you have demonstrated only descriptive consistency, not that alternatives are impossible; the parable therefore shows coherence, not fatalism, and you are using narrative closure to disguise a semantic point about tense logic.
