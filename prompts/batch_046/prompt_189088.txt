As a novelist who studies how people narrate their own inaction, I keep noticing a pattern in modern Stoic revival discourse that I cannot place within existing terminology. A speaker invokes the dichotomy of control—what lies within one's power versus what lies outside it—not to cultivate personal equanimity, but to reframe another person's distress over structural or collective harm as an irrational attachment to externals. The speaker does not deny the harm; rather, they relocate it into the category of the uncontrollable and therefore dismiss the emotional response as philosophically illegitimate, even when the speaker benefits from the conditions causing the distress. This is not mere toxic positivity, since it deploys a genuine philosophical distinction with apparent rigor, nor is it classical Stoic political quietism, since the move is typically individual and therapeutic rather than civic. Does this specific distortion have an established name in the literature on Stoicism's modern reception? If not, can you propose a precise term and delineate its boundaries—distinguishing it from healthy acceptance, from apathy, and from the original Epictetan framework?
