I am currently enrolled in an advanced philosophy course at my high school, and during a recent discussion with classmates about whether evolutionary theory can fully account for ethical behavior, I encountered a difficult passage from Charles Darwin's The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871). I am hoping someone can help me understand the specific connection Darwin draws between the origins of moral sympathy and the origins of religious belief, because the argument seems more subtle than a simple reduction of religion to superstition.

In the passage, Darwin proposes that the mental faculty responsible for extending social sympathy beyond one's immediate kin—allowing a person to feel obligation toward strangers—is closely related to the faculty that causes human beings to see purposeful agency behind natural events they do not understand. He appears to suggest that both morality and religious belief emerge from the same evolutionary source: an instinct for social cooperation that becomes over-extended. On one side, this over-extension produces altruistic concern for distant others; on the other, it produces the attribution of invisible intentions to storms, dreams, or cosmic order. Darwin therefore treats advanced moral feeling and primitive religious sentiment not as enemies, but as parallel misfirings of a single adaptive mechanism—each pushing the human mind past the boundaries for which natural selection originally shaped it.

My difficulty is in determining exactly what Darwin means by this parallel. Does he intend to say that religious belief is essentially a byproduct of the same cognitive excess that makes genuine morality possible, so that we should value the excess despite its erroneous manifestations? Or does he mean that both phenomena are equally illusory extensions of an instinct that should properly remain confined to the tribe, leaving us with no special reason to privilege moral altruism over religious fantasy? I would be grateful for an explanation of how to read this relationship in Darwin's text, particularly whether he sees the two domains as mutually supporting errors or as competing explanations for the same evolutionary surplus.
