Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for a graduate philosophy-of-mind seminar on whether the posit of intrinsic, non-representational qualia is conceptually coherent or collapses into incoherence—the problem Dennett frames in “Quining Qualia” and that has been pressed more recently in incoherence arguments such as those by Chase and Sanborn. The tree should take a student from an initial commitment about phenomenal/functional separation through successive diagnostic questions to a named position, an unnamed combination, or a flagged ambiguity.

Use exactly this root question: “Is there a property of experience—‘what it is like’—that is genuinely distinct from all functional, representational, and relational properties of the mental state?”

From there, build branches using only claims that can be evaluated without prior knowledge of the taxonomy’s labels:

- First split: Does that putative phenomenal property have to be intrinsically private (epistemically inaccessible in principle to any third party)?
- Second split (for those who accept separation): Is phenomenal character constituted entirely by representational content (the transparency thesis), or does it survive as a non-representational residue?
- Third split (for those who deny pure representational constitution): Is the residue best understood as a relation to an external environment (phenomenal relationism) or as an intrinsic monadic quality?
- Parallel split (for those who deny separation altogether): Is the denial driven by a positive reduction of first-person reports to functional/dispositional descriptions, or by a rejection of any unified bearer of properties (e.g., a bundle-theoretic dissolution of the subject)?

Each internal node should be phrased as a concrete case or yes/no claim, not as a theory-name. Every leaf must carry: (i) the position name (e.g., “Dennett-style eliminativist/incoherence charge,” “Block-style phenomenal realist,” “Strong representationalist,” “Phenomenal relationist,” “Analytic functionalist-reductionist,” “Bundle-theoretic eliminativist”) or “unnamed”; (ii) one sentence stating its most awkward commitment; and (iii) for unnamed leaves, a brief diagnosis—whether the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Require that the tree include at least one unnamed leaf and explicitly flag any pair of named leaves it cannot cleanly separate (for example, whether strong representationalism and phenomenal relationism diverge on your branch questions, and if so, what further case would be needed to split them). Keep the tree to no more than eight leaves. The audience knows Dennett’s argument but has not memorized every variant; do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to texts they would need to look up. Instead, inject one niche distinction into the tree—such as whether the same qualitative state can be “modally robust” across inverted-spectrum scenarios without behavioral change—to ensure the classification is not answerable by lifting a standard encyclopedia entry. Finish by asking what the tree predicts about one unlisted case: a subject who sincerely reports a vivid “what-it’s-like” quality for a state that plays no causal role in behavior and carries no representational content about the world.
