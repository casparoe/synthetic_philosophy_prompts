As a novelist whose current manuscript interrogates the boundaries between engineered and evolved cognition, I find myself in the somewhat unlikely position of preparing a single seminar for a small reading group of fellow fiction writers—intelligent, verbally adept, and entirely without scientific training—who have asked me to clarify John von Neumann's actual views on the brain rather than the pop-science caricature they keep encountering. They arrive believing, more or less, that von Neumann endorsed a straightforward digital-computer analogy for neural function; my purpose is to dislodge that assumption and replace it with his more nuanced, and for a novelist far more interesting, position: that the brain operates as an analog, probabilistic, massively redundant system capable of reliable computation despite unreliable individual components, a mode of organization that differs not merely in degree but in kind from the deterministic architectures von Neumann himself designed. Plan the explanatory arc for this ninety-minute session. I need to know the precise order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced—what must be secured first so that the analog-digital distinction does not collapse into vague mysticism—what single concrete scenario should carry the weight of the explanation (and why the familiar computer-brain metaphor must be actively withheld until the end, if at all), the specific misconceptions these writers are likely to generate—perhaps that analog implies emotional, or that redundancy signals waste rather than robustness—and the exact point in the sequence where each should be preemptively addressed. I also want one rigorous check for understanding, posed as a question at the close, that would reveal whether they have genuinely grasped von Neumann's statistical-reliability thesis or have merely swapped one slogan for another. Finally, be explicit about what I should refuse to treat—whether the formal mathematics of self-replicating automata, the neurophysiology of synaptic transmission, or both—and state that boundary clearly, so the group understands the limits of what a novelist can responsibly teach in an evening without scientific apparatus.
