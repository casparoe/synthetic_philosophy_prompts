I hold a specific version of the view that every genuinely self-referential hierarchy—logical, artistic, or cognitive—contains an inviolate level that escapes representation by the levels beneath it. The standard reading of Escher’s Drawing Hands, shaped by Hofstadter’s analysis of strange loops, treats the absent artist’s hand as a single, singular ground outside the frame. I want to defend something more precise and, I think, more vulnerable: that the inviolate stratum is necessarily plural and distributed. In the drawing, the blank paper’s grain, the inconsistent shadow logic, and the viewer’s own perceptual position each function as separate, irreducible exit points; together they prevent the loop from closing, not because one master hand is missing, but because no single internal level can represent all of these distributed outsides at once. Without such multiplicity, I claim, a hierarchy risks not paradox but a false representational completeness that erases the figure-ground distinction.

What would count against this distributed-plural version? I am not asking for the standard Gödelian recursion objection; I want defeaters targeted specifically at the claim that there must be several unrepresentable exit points rather than one. Would a formal model of a fully self-contained strange loop—one with no residual representational gap—refute the view, or could it be absorbed by redescribing hidden exits? Could an art-historical analysis of Escher’s actual drafting technique show that the inconsistencies are accidental rather than structurally necessary, and would that matter? I want you to rank potential counter-evidence from fatal to merely awkward, to say which objections a committed defender of this view would concede and which they would dismiss, and to assess honestly whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability—whether every apparent counterexample can be reinterpreted as revealing yet another hidden distributed exit, and if so whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
