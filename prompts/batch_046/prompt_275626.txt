The policy operates in two layers. First, it applies lexical matching to flag certain tokens as high-toxicity slurs regardless of syntactic context: the same string is flagged in an academic quotation, a hostile reply, or a user biography. Second, it includes a reclamation exception based solely on speaker metadata: if the poster's verified profile indicates membership in the group targeted by the term, the flag is downgraded and the content remains visible. There is no parsing of audience, conversational intent, or power dynamics; the decision hinges entirely on the word plus the speaker's group identity.

This architecture puzzles me. If the harm is intrinsic to the word's semantic content, then speaker identity should not neutralize it. If the harm is instead pragmatic—a matter of who can say what to whom, with what social authority—then lexical matching is the wrong primary filter, and speaker identity is only a crude proxy for a much richer contextual picture. So I want to ask: what implicit theory of slurs, meaning, or harm does this practice presuppose? Does it treat slurs as having an essential toxic meaning that can be authorized away by group membership, or does it smuggle in a pragmatic account of speech acts while using a semantic tool to enforce it? Reconstruct the philosophy of language that would make this two-layer system coherent, and distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it. Is the policy philosophically consistent, or does it combine commitments—about semantics, pragmatics, and harm—that pull in opposite directions?
