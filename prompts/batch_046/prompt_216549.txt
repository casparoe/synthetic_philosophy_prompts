I have been trying to reconcile David Lewis's account of convention with evolutionary explanations of social norms. On Lewis's view, a convention is a regularity in behavior that solves a coordination problem, is arbitrary in the sense that there is another regularity that could have served the same purpose, and is sustained by mutual expectations and common knowledge among the agents involved. Some evolutionary game-theoretic treatments of norm emergence suggest that once a strategy profile becomes evolutionarily stable, the selection dynamics themselves generate a kind of population-level regularity that functions like mutual expectation: agents reproduce the behavior not because they represent alternatives, but because deviation is selected against. This has led me to wonder whether evolutionary stability can serve as a substitute for the belief-based conditions in Lewis's analysis—so that any evolutionarily stable social regularity automatically qualifies as a Lewisian convention, with arbitrariness satisfied by the existence of other biologically possible strategies and common knowledge replaced by the shared selective environment. If that substitution works, then the distinction between a convention and an evolved norm seems to collapse into a single category, and philosophers who treat Lewisian conventions and evolutionary norms as competing explanations are making a mistake. Is this line of reasoning sound? Or does it rest on a misunderstanding of what Lewis requires—perhaps that the agents must be rational and must actually represent the regularity and its alternatives—such that evolutionary dynamics, however stable, can never constitute a convention in his sense?
