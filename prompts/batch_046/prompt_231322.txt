I'm on the university's Committee on Student Welfare and Institutional Data Ethics, and we've got a real mess of a proposal sitting in front of us. The administration want's to pilot something called "Anticipatory Care Analytcs" — basically they want to pool dorm access-card logs, library entry times, dining hall swipe patterns, and LMS clickstream data into a single predictive model that assigns every undergrad a "flourishing-risk score" that resident advisors and academic deans can see in real time. The idea is to catch mental-health crises and potential conduct violations before they happen, and they want a decision by next month's board meeting with a rollout timeline if we approve.

The philosophical problem here isn't just privacy — it's about what kind of power this is. I was talking with a collegue in sociology and kept coming back to Foucault's analysis of discipline, the panopticon, and how knowledge and power get co-produced, but this system feels like it's doing something weirder than classic disciplinary normalization. In the panopticon you internalize the gaze because you might be watched; here students are assigned algorithmic risk-profiles based on correlations they can't see or contest, and then they start self-regulating around these opaque predictive categories — not "am I breaking a rule?" but "am I fitting the pattern of someone who will break a rule?" It seems like a kind of pre-emptive subjectivation, or maybe disciplinary normalization that works backward: the norm gets applied before any deviation occurs, and the student is formed by a statistical identity they never consciously performed. I don't know if there's already a term for that specific twist, but that's the concept I'm trying to hold onto.

Could you draft the formal memo I'd circulate before the meeting? It should recommend a concrete course — approve with strict conditions, reject outright, or table for a defined six-month review period — with an actual time frame attached. The reasoning needs to take seriously the Foucauldian analysis of power/knowledge and this idea of backward normalization, but it also has to engage honestly with the strongest argument on the other side, which is that the university has a genuine duty of care and that preventing even one serious harm justifies a limited, well-governed pilot. Don't just restate the legal privacy rules; I want the memo to treat the philosophical objection as substantive, and I want the recommendation to be something the board can actually vote on. You can assume I lean toward rejecting or severely constraining the pilot, but the memo shouldn't sound like it's already made up its mind.
