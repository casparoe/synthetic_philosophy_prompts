I am in my final year of a politics and history degree, studying how nations are narrated into being. For a coursework exercise in conceptual writing, please produce a deadpan satirical internal policy memo of about five hundred words, written entirely in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though sent by a senior official in the fictional Ministry of Cultural Continuity to regional heritage administrators. The memo should treat Hobsbawm’s idea of invented tradition and Anderson’s concept of imagined communities not as critical historiographical tools but as neutral administrative manuals, outlining mandatory procedures for districts that have failed their quarterly Imagined Cohesion Audit—specifically, accelerated tradition-invention protocols including retroactive founding-myth calibration, synthetic folklore production, and compulsory collective remembrance exercises. The satire should land by exaggerating a real feature of the constructivist argument: that if national identity is genuinely manufactured rather than discovered, then state bureaucracy ought to engineer it with the same efficiency it applies to infrastructure. Include, in passing, a warning from an invented in-house historian about the risks of mnemonic inflation if rituals are fabricated faster than populations can internalise them. The tone must remain bureaucratically earnest throughout, with no winking at the reader, and the piece should be composed in British English without any citations, bibliographies, or references to specific publication years and page numbers—only general allusion to the thinkers and their arguments.
