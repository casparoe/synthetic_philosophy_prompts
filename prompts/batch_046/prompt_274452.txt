I serve on our university medical center's Clinical AI Governance Committee, and next Thursday we must vote on piloting a diagnostic decision-support tool for ambiguous early-stage neurodegeneration. Unlike conventional systems that update beliefs only upon discrete confirmed findings, this tool applies Jeffrey conditionalization: it ingests radiology reports that distribute partial credibility across a partition of possible pathologies—never fully confirming or denying any single diagnosis—and redistributes prior probabilities across that entire partition. Our biostatistician warns that sequential Jeffrey updates with ambiguous reports can violate the principle of total evidence and produce non-commuting belief revisions, leaving clinicians with unstable reasoning; the vendor counters that this accurately models real-world uncertain evidence, since clinicians rarely encounter crisp binary results. Draft a short written opinion—circulate-ready, with a clear recommendation and a concrete six-month review or sunset timeline—stating whether we should approve, reject, or approve with mandatory modifications (for example, restricting use to single-update contexts or requiring explicit likelihood-ratio reporting). Treat the vendor's argument as a serious claim in the philosophy of science, not marketing gloss, and if you reject the system, still present the strongest consideration in its favor with intellectual honesty. One further request: if my framing mischaracterizes Jeffrey's rule—say, by implying it is necessarily incompatible with standard Bayesian conditioning in a way it is not—say so plainly and correct the premise rather than constructing an opinion around the error.
