I serve as chair of a university faculty ethics review board with oversight of both empirical and normative research protocols, and I am required to sign off on every final determination. We have been asked to design a recurring institutional self-assessment—something the full board can execute in a single ninety-minute session each quarter, conducted by members who have philosophical training but no administrative certification, and constrained so that any recommendation still requires my explicit approval. Rather than adopting a generic compliance audit, I want the procedure derived from Benjamin Franklin's actual project of moral perfection: his sequential cultivation of thirteen virtues, his claim that certain virtues operate as prerequisites for others—Temperance producing the coolness of head necessary for Silence and Order, Silence enabling the acquisition of knowledge that feeds Resolution, and so on—his cyclical tracking of failures in tabular form rather than any binary measure of success, and his explicit concession that perfect attainment is impossible though incremental improvement is obligatory.

Please construct an operational decision procedure that my board can actually run. I need the following in order: first, the translated institutional virtues—mapped from Franklin's personal list onto collective deliberative capacities—together with the prerequisite logic that determines the sequence in which they are tested; second, for each virtue, the specific question or test the board applies to itself or to a protocol under review, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether failure stops the procedure with a negative determination or triggers a remedial cycle; third, clear demarcation of the points where the question is factual and the procedure can decide, versus where it is a question of institutional values and must be handed to me for judgment; fourth, an honest accounting of which distinctions from Franklin's theory must survive this institutional translation—especially the cyclical, non-binary structure of assessment—and which can be dropped without distorting the underlying principle; fifth, explicit flagging of misfire risks, including false positives where procedural compliance masks substantive moral failure, and false negatives where a technically imperfect session is wrongly halted.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. Case one: a session in which members rapidly converge on rejecting a controversial study, displaying what Franklin would recognize as a collective failure of Temperance and Silence—demonstrate how the procedure detects this and what it mandates. Case two: an urgent request for expedited review of a study documenting imminent public-health harm, where strict adherence to procedural Order and Resolution conflicts with the need for immediate action—here the procedure should yield a recommendation that I, as chair, will likely find unwelcome, and you must show exactly where and why; case three: a member's undisclosed financial attachment to an applicant, requiring simultaneous assessment of institutional Chastity—understood as freedom from corrupting attachments—and Humility—understood as accurate recognition of one's own compromised judgment—explain whether this produces an automatic disqualification or remains a judgment call, and identify the precise point in the procedure where that determination is made.

Finally, specify what this Franklin-derived procedure is structurally incapable of doing. Which pathologies of institutional deliberation will it miss? Does its cyclical, improvement-oriented framing create perverse incentives—such as tolerating repeated failures in the same virtue? And is there any deliberative virtue that, by its nature, resists proceduralization altogether?
