I am a retired research scientist—my career was in atmospheric physics—who has recently begun reading seriously in the philosophy of history and the philosophy of science. I am trying to work out a principled boundary for when non-human forces (pathogens, climate oscillations, volcanic aerosols) can legitimately appear in historical explanations without collapsing into the kind of environmental determinism that treats human societies as passive substrates. I have formed the following tentative distinction, but I am unsure whether it holds: a non-human factor may be incorporated only when it functions as a constraint-modifier or boundary-condition shift within which human agents still exercise deliberative and institutional agency; it becomes determinism when it is assigned proxy-agency or used to substitute for missing social mechanisms in the causal chain. Below are my intuitive verdicts on concrete cases. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle—perhaps this one, perhaps another—that captures all or most of them; what it predicts for the case I keep revising; and which of my verdicts must be sacrificed for the sake of simplicity.

Case 1: A historian writes that the Black Death was the primary agent that dissolved European feudalism by depopulating the labor force. Verdict: this is determinism. It replaces peasant resistance, legal innovation, and political negotiation with a microbial proxy-agent.

Case 2: In explaining the 1816 migration waves and food crises, a scholar notes that the 1815 Tambora eruption altered global temperature and precipitation patterns, and lists this alongside local governance failures. Verdict: acceptable. The eruption modifies the constraint set; it does not substitute for explanations of how governments and communities responded.

Case 3: A narrative of the seventh-century Islamic expansions emphasizes that the Justinian Plague and subsequent epidemics had weakened Byzantine and Sassanid military and fiscal capacity, thereby altering the strategic landscape that Arab armies encountered. Verdict: I think this is acceptable, though I worry it sounds structurally identical to Case 1.

Case 4: A synthetic history lists the 536–540 volcanic winter, alongside human rulers and religious movements, as one of the principal agents of late-antique transformation. Verdict: not acceptable. The framing assigns intentional or quasi-intentional standing to an atmospheric aerosol event.

Case 5: An analysis of the 1876–1879 famine in British India pairs the El Niño–Southern Oscillation disruption of monsoon patterns with colonial grain-export policies and local market failures. Verdict: fully acceptable; the climate oscillation is integrated with institutional mechanisms rather than replacing them.

Case 6: A study argues that the transition from the Medieval Warm Period to the Little Ice Age caused the early-modern European witch-hunts by increasing agrarian stress and social scapegoating. Verdict: not acceptable. The climate shift is used as a monocausal substitute for the legal, religious, and gendered dynamics that actually drove the prosecutions.

Case 7 (the one I keep revisiting): Suppose a novel pathogen emerges from thawing permafrost, and the thaw is partly driven by anthropogenic climate forcing. Should we treat the climate system or the permafrost as a historical actor in the subsequent pandemic and its social aftermath, given that the forcing loop is itself partly human in origin? I oscillate: on some days I think the hybrid human-natural causation blocks proxy-agency; on other days I think the distinction collapses and we are forced to treat coupled socio-climatic systems as the relevant unit of explanation.

Is there one account—perhaps built on constraint-modification, asymmetric causal integration, or explanatory substitution—that delivers verdicts on Cases 1 through 6, predicts a clear answer for Case 7, and states honestly which of my verdicts (especially the tension between Case 1 and Case 3, or between Case 4 and Case 5) it must overturn in order to remain coherent?
