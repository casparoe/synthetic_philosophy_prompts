I have been reading on my own about historical transformations that are usually called collapses—the disruptions at the end of the Bronze Age, the abandonment of major Maya lowland cities, and the ecological and social changes on Easter Island—and I keep feeling that the word "collapse" hides more than it reveals. I am not a historian or anthropologist, but I want to try framing what actually happens in these cases without assuming that a society dies whenever its central institutions stop working. Below are the notes for an essay I am trying to shape; I would be grateful for comments on whether the argument holds together, whether I have mischaracterized any of these cases, and whether my central distinction is genuinely useful or just a semantic rearrangement.

My core idea is to replace the binary of collapse versus survival with what I am calling a "continuity gradient," measured along three dimensions I have been trying to separate out. First, institutional decoupling: in the Late Bronze Age crisis, palace economies, writing-based administration, and long-distance trade networks dissolved, yet local agriculture, kinship structures, and regional pottery traditions continued with surprisingly little interruption. Second, ecological recalibration: on Easter Island, the loss of palm forests and soil degradation altered resource flows severely, but the population did not vanish; instead, people adapted through new agricultural practices and social reorganization, which suggests that ecological stress produced restructuring rather than termination. Third, narrative rupture: in the Maya case, the urban political centers with their dynastic kingship and monumental construction programs collapsed, yet the language, religious practices, and settlement patterns persisted in altered highland and northern forms; what broke was a specific centralized model of governance, not Maya culture as such.

From these observations, I want to argue that we should distinguish "structural failure"—the breakdown of particular organizational or technological forms—from "societal termination," which almost never occurs in the archaeological record. Most events labeled collapses are better understood as rapid transitions along a gradient of institutional simplification and ecological adaptation. The risk of the standard "collapse" narrative, I think, is that it treats large-scale centralized complexity as the only healthy form of society, so any move toward smaller-scale organization is read as death rather than change. At the same time, I worry that my framework might sanitize real suffering: the Bronze Age disruptions involved widespread violence, displacement, and population decline, and the Maya transition included warfare and the loss of elite cultural production that may have mattered deeply to the people living through it. So I want to ask specifically: does the continuity gradient capture something real about these three cases, or does it obscure the genuine trauma and loss that accompanied them? Am I overstating continuity—particularly for Easter Island, where population levels may have fallen sharply—or am I right that the people who remained were still recognizably Rapa Nui rather than survivors of an extinct society? And does this distinction between structural failure and societal termination apply well beyond these examples, or does it depend on selecting cases where populations clearly persisted?

Please comment on the coherence and fairness of the essay as sketched: whether the three-dimensional framework is clear and defensible, whether any of my historical characterizations strike you as inaccurate or overly smooth, and whether there is a hidden bias—perhaps a modern preference for decentralized resilience—that I have not examined. I am especially interested in whether the idea of "narrative rupture" as distinct from institutional decoupling makes sense for the Maya case, or whether I am artificially separating things that were actually intertwined. I do not need references or reading suggestions; I am trying to think this through from first principles, and I want to know whether the reasoning itself holds up.
