In philosophical and neuropsychological discussion of split-brain cases, a common analogy treats the severed corpus callosum like a cut telephone cable between two offices: each hemisphere becomes an independent agent that can still answer its own line, but the two can no longer confer. This analogy is often used to license the conclusion that a split-brain patient literally houses two conscious subjects, or at least two streams of awareness that fail to unify. There is a rival framing that treats the hemispheres not as separate offices but as two branches of a single bank that have lost their inter-branch server: they still draw on the same capital—the body, the brainstem, shared subcortical structures—and report upward to the same headquarters. Audit both analogies systematically. Provide a correspondence table for each—what plays the role of the cable, the offices, the capital, the headquarters, the customers—then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion. Specifically, address which features of agency, ownership, and information access each analogy quietly imports; whether the shared-capital problem undermines the two-subject conclusion more severely than the cable-cut supports it; and whether the conclusion that there are two persons survives once the analogies are on the table, or whether the situation is better captured by a different model altogether, such as a single cognitive architecture with a severe inter-hemispheric bottleneck. Say also what a more adequate analogy would be, if either of these fails.
