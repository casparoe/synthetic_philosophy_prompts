In our philosophy of science seminar we have spent months applying Imre Lakatos’s methodology of scientific research programmes to whether the deep-learning scaling paradigm is progressive or degenerating. Both sides accept the framework: a hard core of commitments about neural architectures and gradient-based optimization; a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses about data curation, regularization, and post-training; and a positive heuristic directing researchers to increase scale and compute. One side argues the programme is progressive because scaling laws yielded genuinely surprising, previously unanticipated capabilities—robust in-context learning, emergent multimodal reasoning—before they were observed. The other side replies that these are not risky predictions but post-hoc curve descriptions, and that every serious failure mode—systematic bias, brittle reasoning, hallucination—is handled not by revising the hard core but by thickening the protective belt: larger corpora, reinforcement learning from human feedback, mixture-of-experts routing, and longer training runs. We all agree on Lakatos’s historical cases—Copernican astronomy versus Ptolemaic adjustments, the phlogiston programme—and on his formal distinctions. Yet after a year of discussion, no one has moved.

My hypothesis is that we are not disagreeing about history or about the formal structure of research programmes, but about what should count as an independently risky prediction in a domain where the hard core is essentially a family of function approximators rather than a set of law-like statements. Is that right? Or is the stall caused by something else: a hidden normative disagreement about whether philosophy of science should guide present research or merely classify past success; a verbal ambiguity in “ad hoc” that lets every protective-belt adjustment look either necessary or desperate depending on prior sympathies; or are we asking different questions—one about empirical novelty, one about explanatory depth—under the single heading of Lakatosian appraisal?

Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says about scaling, anomalies, and heuristics. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress in resolving it: a conceptual distinction separating predictive risk from curve-fitting; an empirical result that one side could not absorb into the protective belt; a formal argument about the limits of scaling; or a reason to think the question is ill-posed for programmes lacking law-like hard cores. I want the structure of the impasse, not a verdict on which programme is correct.
