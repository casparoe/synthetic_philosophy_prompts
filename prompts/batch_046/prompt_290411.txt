hey, im a philosophy phd student working on value theory and hard choices, and ive been chewing on this idea that doesnt quite fit ruth chang's framework. you know how chang argues that in hard choices between incommensurable options - say artistic vocation vs parenthood, or two incompatible justice principles in policy - the options are on a par, and once you commit thats rational and done? i think theres a further layer. after you choose, the unchosen value doesnt just disappear; it leaves what i want to call a structural haunting. not guilt, not ordinary regret, but a persistent normative pressure to keep the unchosen value represented in your practices or institutions, so that your commitment isnt misread as a judgment that it was inferior.

so please argue for the view that hard choices generate these haunting obligations: obligations to sustain practices that keep the unchosen value visible, distinct from compensation, from rational regret, and from chang's own idea of commitment. you should tackle the obvious objection - that if i owe something to the unchosen option, that smuggles comparability back in. defend the claim that the obligation is structural, not transactional, and that it actually protects incommensurability rather than collapsing it. and maybe say where it breaks: does it apply to purely private one-off choises with no instutional footprint? i dont need citations, just a tight conceptual argument with clear boundaries. thanks!
