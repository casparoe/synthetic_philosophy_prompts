In debates over cross-cultural experimental philosophy, defenders of traditional philosophical method often invoke an analogy: philosophical intuition is like visual perception—cultural background is analogous to lighting conditions that alter how an object appears, but the object itself (the philosophical fact) and the perceiver's basic faculty remain constant. The analogy is used to argue that reported variation in judgments about knowledge attribution or moral responsibility across populations should be treated as noise around a universal core, leaving the reflective judgments of any single tradition largely authoritative for theory-building. Audit this analogy systematically: provide the correspondence table (lighting conditions, the object, the perceiver, the perceptual mechanism, and the distortion), then identify the precise points of breakdown—especially whether cultural framing operates like external illumination or like part of the perceptual apparatus itself, and whether there is a culture-neutral "object" available for comparison independent of framing. Assess whether the reliability conclusion survives once these disanalogies are admitted, and say which structural feature of the case does the work if the analogy collapses. Conclude by proposing a more apt analogy—if one exists—that captures the relationship between culture and intuition without importing the metaphysics the original needs, and state whether that replacement undermines or supports granting special authority to any single cultural group's judgments in constructing philosophical theories.
