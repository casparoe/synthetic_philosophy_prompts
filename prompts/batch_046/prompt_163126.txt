As a novelist tracing the implicit moral jurisprudence of Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, I have been recording his verdicts across several imagined and historical arrangements of freedom and happiness. In the desert temptations, he judges that Christ failed humanity by refusing to turn stones into bread, since people will invariably choose security over autonomy; verdict, a catastrophic misreading of human need. In the medieval Church's governance, he judges that removing political freedom and substituting it with miracle, mystery, and authority was an act of necessary compassion; verdict, fully justified. In the revolutionary era that followed, he judges that granting formal liberty without providing authoritative structure produced only terror and despair; verdict, proof that freedom unaccompanied by authority destroys happiness. In a modern liberal society where citizens possess extensive personal choice yet suffer epidemic anxiety and isolation, he judges that freedom itself has become the source of suffering; verdict, confirmed. In a hypothetical benevolent panopticon where citizens are unaware that an invisible system engineers their preferences, yet report perfect subjective well-being, he judges the arrangement illegitimate—not because the people suffer, but because they never performed a conscious, ritual act of laying their freedom at the feet of a human authority; verdict, false and unworthy.

Consider now a new society I am inventing for my novel: the Synarchists, who through an annual public ceremony knowingly and collectively delegate all economic and personal decisions to a transparent, non-human algorithm designed solely to maximize aggregate well-being. There is no deception, no hidden control, and no suffering from choice; happiness is high. But there is also no miracle, no mystery, and no priesthood—only rational, computable certainty. Predict the Grand Inquisitor's verdict on this arrangement. Is he implicitly running the view that happiness requires merely the absence of freedom regardless of mechanism (A); that legitimate happiness requires a conscious, ritualized surrender specifically to a human or sacred authority (B); that true contentment must be mediated by mystery and miracle rather than transparent reason (C); or that the decisive criterion is simply whether people are relieved of the burden of choice and its attendant anxiety (D)? Identify which of the earlier five cases is the closest analog, state with reasons which rule you take to govern his judgment, and specify the single further case—perhaps a society that retains complete freedom yet eliminates anxiety through an entirely different means—that would definitively distinguish your chosen rule from its nearest rival.
