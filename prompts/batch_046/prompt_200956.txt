I've been discussing the socialist calculation debate with a friend who works in machine learning, and our conversation left me with a nagging confusion. Reading Mises and Hayek, I understand their claim that rational economic calculation requires actual market prices for capital goods, and that without them a central planner is essentially flying blind. But when I study Hayek's essays on dispersed knowledge, it strikes me that what he's describing is, at bottom, an information-aggregation problem: no single mind can hold all the local, tacit knowledge scattered across millions of economic agents, and in the 1930s no machine could either. Given that, I started wondering whether the debate hasn't been quietly resolved by technological change. Specifically, if we imagine a planning authority equipped with modern distributed computing and large neural networks trained on real-time transactional data, couldn't such a system encode dispersed tacit patterns in its weights and generate approximate shadow prices without relying on actual markets? In other words, wouldn't this be a kind of algorithmic realization of Lange's iterative adjustment method, but scaled to a level that overcomes Hayek's epistemic objection?

I'm trying to figure out whether there's a residual argument—perhaps about entrepreneurial discovery, the institutional generation of knowledge, or the normative function of prices—that survives even if the computational aggregation problem is solved. Or have I mischaracterized the Mises-Hayek critique from the start, treating an institutional and epistemic argument as if it were merely a complaint about processing speed and data storage? If the latter, what exactly is the non-computational core of their objection, and how does it apply to a planning system that can approximate market information synthetically?
