I am preparing a decision-tree taxonomy for my graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and I want it built so that students are sorted by what they actually believe about boundary cases rather than by which label they have memorized. The tree should have seven or eight terminal leaves, each corresponding to a distinct ontological position on the species problem—specifically including the species-as-individuals thesis, a strict phylogenetic concept, the biological species concept with an unqualified interbreeding clause, a pluralist or ecological hybrid view, and a deflationary nominalist option. Rather than asking students to identify themselves with names, the tree should proceed through four concrete diagnostic commitments, each presented as a yes/no or either/or judgment that requires no prior knowledge of technical terminology.

The first branch should ask whether reproductive isolation is necessary and sufficient for species membership, using a ring-species case in which adjacent populations interbreed but terminal populations do not. The second should present a cryptic pair that is morphologically identical but reproductively isolated by genetic mechanisms, testing whether morphology can ever override reproductive dynamics. The third should involve an asexual bacterial lineage that engages in horizontal gene transfer almost exclusively with distant relatives rather than close ones, probing whether shared evolutionary history can substitute for direct reproductive continuity. The fourth should present a fossil sequence showing morphological continuity across a temporal gap in which reproductive behavior is entirely unknown, forcing a judgment about whether historical continuity without present interbreeding can sustain a species identity.

For each combination of answers, map the resulting position onto one of the required leaves, and then identify any logically possible answer patterns that remain unoccupied—briefly diagnosing whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect in the literature. The tree should also explicitly flag any pairs of the required positions that collapse onto the same branch, explaining precisely which diagnostic question fails to separate them and why. Frame the entire instrument for an audience that has encountered the standard debates but should not be required to produce bibliographies or author citations; the point is conceptual sorting, not scholarly attribution. Write in a formal register suitable for a syllabus appendix, and ensure that the branching logic is displayed clearly, with the questions preceding the named positions rather than the reverse.
