I've been trying to work out whether there's any consistency in how I judge dead people, and I keep landing on different answers depending on the case. Maybe you can tell me if there's a hidden principle, or if I'm just cherry-picking.

Columbus — I think he's genuinely culpable for what happened to the Taino, and the man-of-his-time defence doesn't wash because Las Casas was there at the time making the same criticisms. So if a contemporary could see the wrong, he should have too.

Jefferson — owning slaves feels different in kind but not in excuse. He literally wrote that slavery was a moral wrong, yet kept people in bondage for economic convenience. Here the defence fails even harder, because he had internal access to the argument, not just external critics. But I still don't think we should tear his statues down in the same way; there's a distinction between condemning the man and condemning the contribution.

Churchill — his racism and imperialism are disgusting, but I find myself saying well, that was the air he breathed more readily than with Columbus or Jefferson. Maybe because he didn't enact a specific policy of racial violence in the same direct way? Or because his moral failures were more diffuse? I'm not sure, but I do think we should criticise the views without letting them swallow the whole legacy of opposing Hitler.

Then there's this hypothetical: an 18th-century physician who experiments on animals, genuinely convinced they lack moral standing, in a world where no one has articulated animal rights. I'd be inclined to excuse him, even if the suffering was real. But if he had read someone arguing that animals suffer like humans and dismissed it, then he's culpable.

So my pattern might be: culpability depends on whether the moral truth was epistmically availble to the agent, plus whether they had the practical freedom to act on it. But then why do I feel differently about Churchill? His critics existed — people in Britain were already questioning empire. Is it that structural prejudice gets a different weight than direct personal violence? Or am I just inconsistent?

Can you state a principle, or a small family of principles, that captures my verdicts? If you have to overturn one, which is it? And what does your principle say about a factory owner in 1840 who employed child labour but also funded local schools — was he culpable for the labour, or partly excused because the economic structure made it unavoidable?
