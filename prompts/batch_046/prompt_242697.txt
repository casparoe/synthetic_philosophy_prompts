Design a 75-minute seminar activity for about 18 philosophy undergraduates (mixed background, no assigned reading) that makes them feel the specific force of Dennett’s brain-transplant puzzle: not just that identity becomes ambiguous after copying, but that first-person authority fractures when two narrative centers share an identical past and must jointly authorize one future. The insight should emerge from negotiation and failure, not from any opening lecture; do not mention Dennett’s name until the debrief.

Split the class into three parallel “Continuity Boards.” Each board has one Original (brain in a failing body), two Copies (identical brain-scans instantiated in new bodies, with full shared memories up to the split point), and one Trustee. Provide each board with a “Continuity Contract”—a single-page document that opens “I, [name], hereby direct . . .” and takes legal effect only if signed by exactly one party. Give each subject ten “autonomy tokens” (small cards representing future decision-rights). The rules: if the board fails to produce exactly one valid contract within 20 minutes, all three subjects forfeit half their tokens to the Trustee, who then allocates them unilaterally. Each subject writes a first-person justification (maximum 100 words) for being the sole signatory. The two Copies must negotiate a joint statement; if they submit separate contracts, both are void. The Trustee applies three evaluation criteria—biological continuity, psychological continuity, and narrative coherence—but must announce which one was used only after reading the contracts, not before.

Inject a “narrative overlap zone” to keep the scenario from being a standard replay: each Copy receives a private card describing one shared memory (e.g., the day they first encountered philosophy of mind) but with a subtly different emotional interpretation that has developed post-split. They may not show the card’s text; they may only argue from it. This forces the Copies to discover that psychological continuity is not mere memory retention but active narrative construction, and that two centers constructing competing interpretations from the same data have no neutral internal arbiter.

After 20 minutes of negotiation, each board reports how many contracts were produced, which criterion the Trustee applied, and whether the Copies experienced themselves as “the same person arguing with myself” or as “two different people with a stolen past.” Then run a 30-minute debrief: map the negotiation deadlock onto Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theater by asking where the “real me” was supposed to be seated during the debate; show that the Trustee’s criterion-shifting reveals identity is not discovered but practically decided; identify precisely what the activity cannot reproduce—the phenomenological horror of waking up in one body without knowing whether a duplicate exists elsewhere—and supply that verbally; and predict the failures that will occur on the first run (Copies will coalition against the Original; Trustees will conflate psychological continuity with emotional similarity; groups will “resolve” the tension too quickly by having the Original surrender, which misses the fracture entirely). Give specific facilitation moves to block each failure.

Specify materials (role cards, contract templates, token slips), the minute-by-minute flow within the 75 minutes, and exactly what students should leave seeing: that “where am I?” is not a hidden-fact question but a practical crisis that forces an institutional choice among equally legitimate claimants. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; do not ask participants to research Dennett; keep everything self-contained so an instructor can run it without web access.
