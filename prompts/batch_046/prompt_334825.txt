I'm giving the opening remarks tomorrow morning at the workshop Material Traces, Institutional Silences, hosted at the museum's research center. The audience is deliberately mixed: field archaeologists who have spent decades interpreting settlement patterns and storage assemblages; philosophers of science concerned with underdetermination and inference; and curators who will later have to decide how reconstructed institutions are displayed. I want these remarks—about fifteen minutes—to do more than welcome participants. They should establish the conceptual tension that will govern the entire weekend.

The idea I want to structure everything is an asymmetry in how institutions leave material traces. The institutions we most want to reconstruct—redistribution obligations, ritual hierarchies, legal categories—tend to leave the faintest archaeological signatures precisely when they are functioning well and are fully internalized by participants. A stable system does not need specially marked spaces or standardized vessels; it needs them mainly when it is being imported, contested, or breaking down. Consequently, our material record is structurally biased toward institutional crisis and novelty, not toward the ordinary, stable operation we typically seek to understand. I want you to build the remarks around this asymmetry, treating it not as a decorative metaphor but as a constraint on what can be claimed. The speech should explain why this means an archaeological inference can never be treated as a simple discovery of a past belief or institution; why negative evidence—the absence of expected objects—is far more ambiguous than standard method admits; and why the workshop's real task is negotiating the gap between object and institution rather than pretending to close it.

Please write formally but not with the stiffness of a journal article; this is an occasion, not a submission. Weave in one concrete hypothetical image to ground the abstraction: imagine an excavation layer in which standardized weights suddenly appear at the same moment that communal storage pits vanish, and use that single shift to show how the same material pattern can be read either as institutional consolidation or as institutional collapse, depending on which side of the asymmetry one begins from. The archaeologists in the room should recognize the technical stakes—depositional practices, assemblage change, settlement layout—without feeling they are sitting through a methods lecture. The philosophers should hear a genuine epistemological claim about inference and absence, not a vague observation that objects are silent. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the reasoning should stand on its own.
