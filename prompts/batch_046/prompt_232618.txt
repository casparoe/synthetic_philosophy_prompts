I've been thinking on my own about Kavka's toxin puzzle—the case where someone is offered a large reward merely for forming the intention, at a specific future time, to drink a mildly harmful toxin, even though once the reward is secured there is no longer any reason to actually drink it. I am not an academic, but I want to lead a single ninety-minute session for a small group of friends who are intellectually curious, have no formal philosophy background, and have heard of the prisoner's dilemma but have never studied decision theory. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself: the exact dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so the paradox feels structurally inevitable rather than like a trick, the misconceptions my audience will predictably bring or form and the precise point in the arc where each should be intercepted, and a clear check that they have grasped the underlying split rather than just remembering the story.

To carry the idea, I want to build around a concrete scenario I devised rather than the original billion-dollar toxin: a novelist deposits ten thousand dollars with a lawyer, to be donated to a cause she despises, if she ever misses her self-imposed daily writing session. She genuinely intends to write every day, and she sets up the contract precisely to strengthen that intention, yet once the contract is signed and the money secured, she could miss a session, suffer the financial loss, and still have satisfied the condition of having intended to write at the moment of signing. For this example to actually carry Kavka's structure and not collapse into a simple willpower or self-discipline anecdote, what must it include and what must it explicitly lack?

Please lay out the plan as follows:

- The sequence of ideas that must be in place before the paradox can be presented, with each step's dependency on the prior one—particularly how to establish the distinction between having a reason to form an intention and having a reason to carry it out, without assuming any background in formal rational-choice theory.
- Where in that sequence to introduce my novelist-escrow example, what structural features it needs to preserve the separation between the reward for intending and the absence of reason to act, and what features—such as physical harm, social pressure, or ongoing incentives—must be absent so it does not drift into a different problem.
- The predictable misconceptions—such as that this is just about willpower, that it is structurally identical to the prisoner's dilemma, that rationality requires going through with harmful actions once promised, or that the puzzle dissolves if we simply assume people always do what they intend—and for each one, the specific moment in the explanatory arc where it should be headed off.
- A check for understanding I can administer near the end that demonstrates whether the group has seen the structural gap between reasons-to-intend and reasons-to-act, rather than merely feeling puzzled or remembering the plot.
- What elements of the broader literature—causal versus evidential decision theory, Newcomb-style problems, utility calculus, or formal game-theoretic notation—should be named as beyond the scope of this session and deliberately left out.

Treat the audience as capable of following a tight, disciplined argument, but not as people who have read the philosophical literature.
