I am working through the Hart–Fuller debate for an essay on legal philosophy, and a friend offered the following summary of Hart's position, which I am unsure how far to trust. They said that Hart essentially conceded Fuller's eight canons of legality—generality, promulgation, prospectivity, and so on—but reinterpreted them as conditions of technical efficacy rather than as moral demands, analogous to the craft rules a carpenter must follow to build a stable table. They went on to claim that, in The Concept of Law, Hart admitted every functioning legal system necessarily contains a minimum content of natural law, by which he meant that rules prohibiting murder, theft, and deception are built directly into the criteria of legal validity, not merely into the substantive laws societies happen to enact. From this, my friend concluded that Hart's separability thesis is much narrower than usually taught: it applies only to the outer form of legal rules, whereas Fuller's inner morality of law and these necessary moral prohibitions show that positivism cannot truly separate law from morality at the level of validity conditions.

This sounds partially right—Hart certainly discusses efficacy and the minimum content—but I worry it conflates two different arguments and misplaces where Hart locates the moral-neutral character of law. Could you audit this summary? I would like you to separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification that goes too far, and what misrepresents Hart's actual view, particularly regarding whether the minimum content operates at the level of the rule of recognition or elsewhere. I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of secondary sources; I want to know whether the reasoning holds together and which parts a defender of Hart would reject most sharply.
