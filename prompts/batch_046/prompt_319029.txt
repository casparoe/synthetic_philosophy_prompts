I have just finished a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend who teaches clinical ethics, and it has left me convinced that our hospital system needs a tool we currently lack. We were debating whether collective decisions in high-stakes care settings—family conferences to withdraw life support, interdisciplinary crisis teams redirecting treatment, or board votes on experimental protocols—ever amount to genuinely joint intentional actions in the philosophical sense, or whether they are always reducible to an aggregation of individual mental states that happen to converge on the same outcome. My friend insisted that phrases like “the family decided” or “the team intended” are mostly convenient fictions, and that attributing collective responsibility on that basis is conceptually sloppy. I disagreed, but I also realized I have no practical, reusable way to tell the difference in real time. I work as a clinical ethics mediator in a large academic hospital, and I am called into family conferences and rapid-response team deliberations several times a week. I need a decision procedure—essentially an ordered checklist or flowchart in words—that translates the core distinctions in collective intentionality and joint action into something a mediator with no philosophy background can run in about thirty minutes, with clear questions in sequence, criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, explicit stop-points where the classification is “not joint action,” and marked hand-off points where the remaining issue is one of values rather than conceptual fact. The procedure should preserve the distinctions that actually matter for moral responsibility—such as mutual responsiveness versus parallel planning, shared cooperative activity versus strategic alignment, and joint commitment versus individually coincident commitment—while honestly discarding distinctions that cannot be reliably detected in practice, such as explicit verbal articulation in every case. It must be robust enough that a senior administrator can sign off on its institutional use, and it must say what it cannot do.

Please construct this procedure, grounding it in the central theoretical ideas—Bratman-style shared cooperative activity, Searle-style we-intentions, Gilbert-style plural subjects and joint commitment—without requiring me to master the literature first. Then apply it to three cases: first, a four-person family that spends an hour in mutual discussion, expresses reassurance, and jointly requests withdrawal of ventilation; second, a seven-member ethics board that votes 4-3 to approve a protocol without any cross-deliberation between the two factions; and third, an emergency nursing team that coordinates rapidly through non-verbal cues to redirect a crashing patient’s care, where my own intuition strongly favors treating this as joint action but I want the procedure to render its verdict honestly even if that verdict is unwelcome. For each case, state the classification, what the mediator should record for accountability, and whether the decision should be treated as binding future members. Clearly flag the cases the procedure should refuse to classify and identify where judgment is unavoidable—particularly when assessing whether a silent or remotely consulting member participates in a shared plan. Finally, please state explicitly what argument or observation would change your mind about the hard case—specifically whether rapid non-verbal coordination can constitute shared cooperative activity without explicit mutual belief—and identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail or misfire.
