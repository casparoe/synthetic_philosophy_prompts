I am preparing a paper for a seminar on collective moral agency, and I keep returning to a tension I see in how historians and philosophers talk past each other regarding the First World War. The Fischer controversy—centered on the argument that German leadership actively pursued expansionist war aims and therefore bore primary responsibility—seems to slide between two very different claims: first, that a specific set of state actors causally engineered the conflict through deliberate policy choices; and second, that the German nation as a collective entity incurred a moral debt or liability that justifies speaking of national blame. Meanwhile, the structuralist counter-narrative insists that alliance rigidities, bureaucratic fragmentation, and militarized social structures dispersed decision-making so thoroughly that attributing the war to a unified national will is historically naive.

Write an essay that disentangles these two dimensions—causal origin versus collective moral responsibility—and applies them rigorously to the Fischer debate. Your task is not merely to summarize the historiography, but to reach and defend a substantive conclusion about whether holding a nation responsible for a war requires demonstrating a coherent collective intention, or whether dispersed structural causation can still ground legitimate moral blame at the national level. In constructing your argument, treat the Fischer case as a concrete testing ground for your conceptual claim, but avoid offering bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations.
