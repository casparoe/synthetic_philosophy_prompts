In my philosophy seminar, a classmate recently shut down a debate about whether feeling guilty for a sibling's mistake can ever be unjustified by saying, "You can't be wrong about how you feel." She seemed to treat the line as proof that emotional states are self-validating and therefore immune to any normative criticism. Before I respond in class, I want to understand exactly what that slogan could be claiming. Could you lay out the precise theses it might express—ranging from the trivial (first-person authority over whether a feeling is present), through the substantive (emotions lack intentional content and so cannot misrepresent their objects), to the radical (there are no fittingness conditions for affect whatsoever)—and for each say whether it is defensible, whether it actually supports her dismissal, and which version she most likely meant? In particular, please include a middle reading that distinguishes being wrong about the presence of a feeling from being wrong about whether that feeling is appropriate to its object, since that is precisely where I think her argument collapses.
