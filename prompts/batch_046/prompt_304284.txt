I just finished a long argument with a friend about hypocrisy and standing to blame. She summarized the philosophical view this way: blame is best understood as a form of moral address that requires a precise fault-matching condition — the blamer must not be guilty of the very same wrong they are condemning, though they need not be morally perfect in other respects. This is not a general credibility requirement but a relational license: if you commit the same fault, you dissolve your entitlement to address others about it, regardless of whether the addressee is a stranger or intimate, and regardless of whether your own version of the fault is more or less severe. She added that philosophers distinguish horizontal hypocrisy (the same fault at the same severity) from vertical hypocrisy (a more severe version of the same fault), and on this account only vertical hypocrisy removes standing entirely, while horizontal hypocrisy merely weakens the force of the address without dissolving it. She concluded that this makes the view surprisingly permissive — since most people avoid the exact faults they condemn, broad blame is allowed — and that the familiar objection 'everyone is a hypocrite, so no one can blame anyone' simply misunderstands the precision of the matching condition.

Something in this feels like a sleight of hand, especially the part about strangers and the vertical/horizontal distinction saving the view from overbreadth, but I can't pinpoint the error. Please separate what is accurate about this summary, what are subtle misrepresentations or conflations of distinct claims, and whether her two conclusions — that the view is broadly permissive and that the 'everyone is a hypocrite' objection misses the point — actually survive correction.
