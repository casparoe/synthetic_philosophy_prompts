I serve as the student representative on my school district's Emerging Biotechnology and Ethics Advisory Panel. At next week's session we must vote on whether to issue an institutional endorsement for a university research partnership that has requested access to our anonymized health-data registry and a formal letter of cooperation. The proposal describes a Phase I clinical trial of a dual-modality intervention: germline editing of nociceptive ion-channel genes (SCN9A and TRPV1) combined with an implanted subcortical microstimulator that sustains tonic release of endogenous cannabinoid analogues in the anterior cingulate cortex. The explicit goal is to render adult volunteers permanently incapable of negative affect, beginning with a cohort of patients suffering from intractable chronic-pain syndromes.

The principal investigators defend the study under a framework they term the hedonistic imperative—the thesis that suffering is an unqualified moral evil and that biotechnology ought to pursue its abolition rather than mere palliation. They further argue that successful elimination of negative valence produces what they call a "valence-neutral moral agent," a being for whom conventional harm-based informed-consent frameworks may be inapplicable because affective harm is definitionally impossible. Half the panel regards this as a logical extension of compassionate medicine; the other half contends that erasing the capacity for suffering collapses the distinction between harm and preference-frustration, and that the "valence-neutral agent" concept either is incoherent or functions as a pretext for weakening consent protections.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions and a defined time frame—supported by reasoning that takes the strongest objection on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it. The memo should address whether abolishing suffering through biotechnology alters the conditions under which we recognize autonomy and harm, and it should be formulated as an actionable institutional opinion that both researchers and dissenting panel members could respond to. I ask that you not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since this is intended for circulation within our panel and must stand on its reasoning alone.
