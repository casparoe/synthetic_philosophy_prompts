I'm trying to work through what P.F. Strawson—who died in 2006, before the current wave of deployed algorithmic decision-making—would say about the moral crumple zone dynamics that have become visible in human-automation interaction. By that I mean the structural pattern in which human operators serve as the visible targets of reactive attitudes like resentment or blame for system failures, even though their actual discretion is severely constrained by predictions they cannot fully interpret or override. Given Strawson's distinction between participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude, and his view that reactive attitudes depend on attributing a particular quality of will, how do you think he would analyze whether directing resentment at the human-in-the-loop is ever fitting? Specifically: would he hold that being the visible face of the system is sufficient to make one a proper target, or would the absence of genuinely attributable ill will—because the operator's choices are structurally predetermined—push us toward adopting an objective attitude not just toward the algorithm but toward the operator as well? And might he draw an internal line between blaming an operator for negligent monitoring, where some quality of will might still be present, versus blaming them for the system's direct output, where it is absent? I'm not asking for a literature review or citations—just a careful conceptual reconstruction of how his framework applies to a situation he never addressed.
