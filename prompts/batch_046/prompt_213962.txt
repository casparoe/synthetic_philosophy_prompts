I chair a government commission tasked with recommending whether our present democratic republic should formally acknowledge inherited collective guilt for mass displacement policies enacted by the authoritarian regime that preceded it, despite the fact that our current constitution explicitly repudiates that regime's legal continuity. We are considering Karl Jaspers's distinction among political, criminal, moral, and metaphysical guilt, but the central difficulty is whether this framework applies when the collective agent has undergone what I will call constitutional discontinuity—an explicit legal rupture—while maintaining institutional purpose continuity through the same bureaucracy, territory, and citizenry. I want a memo, addressed to the commission, recommending whether we should classify this inherited wrong as generating only political guilt for the current citizenry, extend it to moral guilt, or conclude that constitutional discontinuity dissolves collective agency altogether and therefore eliminates inherited guilt. The memo should treat seriously the strongest counter-argument, namely that guilt is inherently non-transferable across generations and legal regimes, and it should engage with the concept of diachronic collective agency—the view that responsibility travels not through legal identity but through sustained institutional purpose and benefit. Please present your reasoning in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one writes a formal letter, and conclude with an explicit bottom-line recommendation that the commission can vote on.
