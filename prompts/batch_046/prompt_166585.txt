I'm taking this course on practical rationality and me and my roommate got into it about whether weakness of will and hyperbolic discouting are actually the same kind of failure or if they split in weird ways. I have a bunch of verdicts on concrete cases but I can't tell if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

First, the classic smoker: she decides to quit, throws the pack away, then an hour later buys another and lights up. That feels like real akrasia — she judged quitting was best and then acted right against that judgement. Second, a guy takes $50 now over $100 in a year but doesn't feel torn at all, he just says future me can deal with it. To me that's hyperbolic discouting, but not weakness of will, since there's no live conflict in the moment. Third, my roommate pulled an all-nighter before her final she really wanted to ace, not because she planned to, but because she kept saying five more minutes. She knew she was sabotaging tomorrow but kept drifting. That feels like akrasia, but it's weird because she never made a firm resolution — it's more like sliding. Fourth, a friend gives his roommate his gaming console for exam week and succeeds. He says it's not that he overcame weakness, it's that he changed who he'd be during exams, like the future self isn't fully him. I think that's overdramatic, but I also feel commitment devices solve a different problem than akrasia. Fifth, someone saves for retirement at 25, then at 50 spends it on a boat because she now genuinely thinks enjoyment now matters more. At 25 she might call her future self weak-willed, but from inside at 50 she's just living by her current lights. So my verdicts are: 1 and 3 are akrasia; 2 is irrational but not akrasia; 4 is a coordination problem between temporal selves, not overcoming weakness; 5 isn't akrasia because there's no single authoritative judgement being violated. But there's tension between 3 and 5 — both involve drift without a prior firm plan, but I treat them differently.

So is there a coherent principle, maybe something like "akrasia requires a present normative judgement that is actively overridden by a competing desire, where the agent identifies with the judgement"? Does that save all five, or do I have to drop one? And what does it say about hyperbolic discouting — is it just a mechanism for case 2, or can it also generate case 1? Also please predict for a case I haven't listed: someone takes a pill that eliminates her craving for cigarettes and then quits easily. Is that overcoming weakness, or just removing the conditions for it? I don't need citations or a reading list, just the conceptual analysis, and tell me honestly which verdicts the best principle can't accommodate.
