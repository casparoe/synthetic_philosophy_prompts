I was arguing with my roommate about that old Hofstadter Letter Spirit project — you know, the one where they tried to build a program that could design letterforms and understand what makes every 'a' an 'a' despite wild stylistic varation. I'm taking a cognitive science course and this came up in a discussion about categorization, and he summed the whole thing up like this: the core insight is that human letter recognition works through detecting an abstract relational skeleton (like "loop on right, tail extending left") rather than matching specific geometric features, and the brain treats all the stylistic variation as noise around that skeleton. He said this explains why people instantly recognize an 'a' in thirty different fonts without explicit rule-learning, and he went further: modern vision networks fail on adversarial letter perturbations precisely because they lack this skeleton-layer, relying instead on memorized pixel correlations. From that he concluded that neural nets don't have real representations — just statistical pattern-matching — whereas human cognition has genuine categorical understanding anchored to these relational essences.

Something feels off about the jump from letterforms to AI representations, but I'm not deep enough in the original papers to tell if he's also misrepresenting the Letter Spirit view itself. Is this summary fair? Specifically, does Hofstadter's account actually rely on a single fixed relational skeleton for each letter, or is the "spirit" more like a distributed cluster in style-space? And is the claim that CNN adversarial vulnerability proves the absence of representations a legitimate extension of the theory, or is he conflating two different arguments? I'd like an audit: which parts match what Letter Spirit actually proposed, which are standard oversimplifications, and whether the anti-representation conclusion actually follows once the letter-part is corrected.
