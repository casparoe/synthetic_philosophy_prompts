I was arguing with my friend last night about whether it's rational to fear death, and we kept circling around this one thing. She said death can't be bad for you because once it happens, you're not there to feel it — like, there's no 'you' left to suffer the loss. Okay, I get that. But then she said that if you save someone's life, you've done them this huge favor, because now they get to keep living. So on one hand, being dead isn't bad for the dead guy, but on the other hand, not being dead is really good for the living guy. I pointed out that this seems like your treating the same state — being alive vs not being alive — with totally different rules depending on which direction you come from.

Like, if I never existed at all, nobody says that's bad for me, which fits her logic. But if I exist and then stop, suddenly that's... also not bad, but preventing it is great? We tried to apply it to other things, like being in a coma forever vs being dead. In both cases there's no experiencing subject, but people seem way more okay with permanent coma being 'not bad for the patient' than they are with death being 'not bad', even though the outcome is basically the same absence.

So I'm wondering if there's an established name for this specific pattern — not just the general Epicurean idea that death isn't an evil, and not exactly that symmetry argument about prenatal and posthumous nonexistence, but this particular bookkeeping glitch where we deny harm to a missing subject in one framing but treat preserving that subject as a massive benefit in the mirror framing, and then apply it inconsistently across coma and death. If there is a term, I'd want to know exactly how my version differs from it, because I might be mixing in something about permanent unconsciousness that doesn't belong. And if there isn't one, maybe there's a reason philosophers haven't carved out this exact corner?
