A colleague summarized the debate between expressive and strategic voting as follows: "Expressive theorists treat voting as a form of public speech — you should support the candidate who best represents your values, regardless of electability, because the ballot expresses your commitments to the polity. Strategic theorists treat voting as a causal instrument, so rational agents must back the least-bad viable candidate to prevent the worst outcome. These positions are usually cast as rivals, but they actually converge: since voting for an unelectable candidate is causally inert, an expressive voter can only justify her choice if she expects it to influence others, which transforms her expression into strategic reasoning about influence. Consequently, pure expression is coherent only in safe districts or landslide elections; elsewhere it is either self-deception or strategic reasoning in disguise."

Is the claim that expressive justification necessarily collapses into strategic reasoning about influence a genuine difficulty for the view, or does it overlook a distinction — perhaps between expressing values to oneself or to one's community versus attempting to alter the electoral outcome — that allows expressive voting to remain coherent even when it is causally inert? And does the summary obscure the specific moral problem raised by voting for the "lesser evil," namely that democratic authorization may involve a kind of moral complicity that strategic reasoning alone does not resolve?
