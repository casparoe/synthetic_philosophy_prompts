Sphexishness comes from the wasp Sphex ichneumoneus, studied by entomologists and used by philosophers like Dennett to show intelligent-looking behavior that's really just a fixed loop. The wasp checks her burrow, drags prey in, checks again—if a researcher moves the prey while she's inside she pulls it out and restarts the sequence. That actually proves she has interruptibility: she can adapt to the change by restarting with modified conditions, so she's not totally rigid.

People now talk about computational sphexishness in LLMs: they generate the next token in a local window and can't globally rewrite the plan mid-generation, which is like the wasp loop. Human habits—like driving home on autopilot—work the same way. But Frankfurt's hierarchical compatibilism says free will requires that our second-order volitions align with first-order actions, and that alignment depends on having alternative possibilities—without them we couldn't have formed the volition differently. So a sphexish human habit lacks those alternatives and isn't free.

Still, I think the analogy fails because humans can break the loop and restructure the goal, while both wasps and models just restart from the nearest check-point. So sphexishness only undermines free will for actions without a meta-cognitive exit point.
