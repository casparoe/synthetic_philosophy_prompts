For a feature I am drafting on the hidden architecture of consent in medicine and research ethics, I have sketched the position I intend to defend, but before filing it I need an internal audit of whether these commitments actually hang together. Here is where I stand.

(1) Valid consent requires actual understanding, not mere disclosure or a signed form. The agent must genuinely grasp the nature of the act and its material risks; if that grasp is absent, consent is void, regardless of what was communicated.

(2) Deception vitiates consent only when it bears on the essential nature of the act or its direct risks. If an agent is misled about peripheral or contextual features—the prestige of the institution running a trial, the identity of a secondary observer, the aesthetic framing of a procedure—consent remains intact, even if disclosure of the truth would have altered the decision.

(3) Coercion should be defined narrowly: a proposal is coercive only if it threatens to make the agent worse off relative to a baseline of legitimate entitlements. Offering desperately needed benefits in exchange for consent is not coercive, however dire the agent’s vulnerability.

(4) Competence is binary and uniform in threshold. If an agent meets the cognitive and volitional conditions for competence in one structurally analogous decision-context, they meet them in all such contexts, regardless of stakes.

(5) Despite (1), clinical and research institutions should treat properly executed disclosure and signature as sufficient authorization to proceed. Requiring verified actual understanding in every case would paralyze necessary care and legitimate research.

(6) Consent also requires what I call deliberative continuity: the decision must be traceable to a stable preference structure of the agent, not to temporary emotional disturbance. Consent given in acute crisis should therefore be treated as provisionally invalid, pending reaffirmation after emotional equilibrium is restored.

(7) Yet emotional disturbance does not diminish competence. Because competence is binary (4), crisis-given consent remains legally binding unless explicitly withdrawn—even though it lacks the deliberative continuity demanded by (6).

For each genuine tension, specify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what that revision costs the overall view. Then identify the single revision that would clean up the most conflicts with the least damage to the package. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or literature survey; I want an audit of this particular set of commitments.
