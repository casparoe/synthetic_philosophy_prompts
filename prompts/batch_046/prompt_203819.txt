Hey — I'm putting together a little assignment for my grad seminar on philosophy of history and I keep circling back to this annoying question that doesn't have a clean answer. When exactly does a legend harden into history? Not in some vague metaphorical sense, but actually — at what point do we stop treating a narrative as myth and start treating it as a record?

Take three cases we keep arguing about in class: the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus story. For Troy we've got Hittite references that seem to nod at a place like Wilusa and some destruction layers that match the rough era, but the Homeric version is still soaked in divine machinery. Arthur is even weirder — there's a 9th-century mention of a battle leader, then centuries of literary embroidery until he becomes the king we recognize, with basically no material trace matching the Round Table version. The Exodus is different again: no direct archaeological trail for a massive Sinai migration, yet it's anchored in a textual tradition that functions as collective historical memory and gets treated differently by scholars precisely because of that institutional weight.

Here's the niche framework I'm forcing my students to deal with. I think people conflate two things — "material corroboration" (digging up stuff that matches) and "narrative autonomy" (the story standing without continuous mythic scaffolding). Some philosophers say corroboration is necessary and sufficient; others say autonomy is what matters, meaning a legend becomes history when it stops needing miracles or archtypes to hold together, regardless of pottery. A few colleagues think both miss something and that we actually need a third criterion: institutional transmission independance, meaning the story survives in archives that don't depend on a single living tradition to stay credible.

Write me an essay that takes these three cases and applies that three-way framework. Don't just list them — actually weigh whether one criterion does the real work, or whether they interact in ways that change the verdict for each case. Reach a real conclusion about whether there's a single threshold or if we need different standards for different kinds of narratives. Don't end on "it depends" unless you can specify exactly what it depends on and defend that specification.

Please end with a line that reads exactly "Verdict: [your conclusion in under 12 words]" and then right after that a one-sentence summary of your main reasoning.
