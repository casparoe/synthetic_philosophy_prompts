I've been reading a bit about whether life can have meaning if the universe doesn't care—Camus's idea of the absurd, I guess—and also about the newer philosophers who say meaning comes from doing objectively valuable things like art or caring for people, even without any cosmic purpose. I tried to put them together and ended up with an argument that seems to destroy the newer view. I must have slipped somewhere, but I can't see where.

(1) If something in my life really matters, there has to be a reason for it that doesn't just loop back to me deciding it matters. Otherwise I'm just cheering for my own team.

(2) The absurdist point is that the universe gives us no such outside reason—it's silent.

(3) So if the absurdists are right, nothing actually matters; we just act like it does.

(4) The newer accounts say my life is meaningful if I'm deeply engaged in projects that are genuinely worthy—creating something beautiful, loving someone, pursuing truth.

(5) But the reason those projects count as worthy is usually that they enrich human life or contribute to flourishing.

(6) So when these accounts say "do worthy projects to get meaning," they're using "meaningful human life" as part of the explanation for why the projects are worthy.

(7) That's circular: you can't explain why life is meaningful by pointing to activities that are only valuable because they make life meaningful.

(8) To break the circle you'd need to say art or love is valuable for some reason that has nothing to do with human lives—maybe God intended it, or it serves some cosmic function.

(9) But that's just giving up on absurdism.

(10) So it looks like either the universe does care (absurdism is wrong), or these newer philosophers are going in circles.

That can't be right, because I want to say both that the universe is silent and that some people clearly live meaningful lives. Which step should I reject? I especially don't want to give up (2) or (4), but I want to know what it costs to keep them and which premise I'm actually paying for.
