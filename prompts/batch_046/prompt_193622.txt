Maya studies economic history and Leo writes philosophy of science, and they were sitting by the fire arguing about whether culture can actually cause things, using Weber's thesis about the Protestant ethic as their test case. I want you to go through their exchange step by step and say who's right at each point, not just who wins overall. Also please separate what's pretty settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually study this, and don't present a contested view like its the consensus.

Here's how it went, more or less. Maya said look, Weber's whole thing collapses if you take causation seriously. The Protestant ethic might have coincided with capitalism, but values don't push people around like physical forces do. If you can't point to a mechanism where a belief literally makes someone work harder, then calling culture a cause is just poetry. Leo said that's a strawman—Weber never claimed values are efficient causes like billiard balls. He talked about elective affinity, where religious values and economic conditions select for each other. The values don't do the pushing alone; they shape which economic behaviors feel legitimate, and without that selection filter you wouldn't get the specific capitalist spirit Weber describes.

Maya came back with okay, but if the economic conditions are doing all the heavy lifting, then the values are just along for the ride. You've renamed correlation as causation. If values never operate independently of material conditions, they aren't explaining anything extra; they're decorative. Leo said that's where narrative causation comes in—something I threw in because I'm a novelist and it seemed relevant. In a novel, a character's belief explains why she boards the ship without being a physical push. Weber's adequate causation works the same way: it's not reducible to material conditions, but the historical sequence wouldn't make sense if you stripped out the meaning people assigned to profit and salvation. Without that layer, you'd misread why capitalism took the form it did in those specific regions.

Maya said that's a neat analogy, but analogies aren't arguments. You're using fiction to smuggle mental causation back into history. Leo said he wasn't smuggling—Weber's methodology explicitly allows meaning-adequate explanations that can't be collapsed into material ones, otherwise you can't distinguish modern capitalism from earlier profit-seeking societies that lacked the ascetic framing. The values don't cause capitalism out of nothing, but they determine its specific form, and that determinative work is real even if it's not efficient causation.
