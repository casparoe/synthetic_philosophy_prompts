A colleague proposed the following argument concerning ontology identification under epistemic inaccessibility, and I am trying to locate the fault without abandoning the motivating intuition. (1) Ontological identity conditions should depend only on properties that are in principle accessible to some observer; differences that are permanently hidden do not ground distinctions in being. (2) Artifact X and artifact Y share every accessible property—causal history, functional behavior, and macroscopic structure—while differing solely in the configuration of entities beneath the threshold of any physically possible measurement. (3) Therefore X and Y are the same object, differently described. (4) If X is destroyed and rebuilt using the materials and hidden configuration of Y, the result has every accessible property of X restored. (5) By (3), this rebuilt entity just is X, since the hidden configuration was never part of X's identity. (6) But then Y, prior to any rebuilding, already shared X's identity conditions, so destroying X and leaving Y intact means X was never destroyed at all. (7) Consequently, no artifact can ever be genuinely destroyed if any other entity shares its accessible properties, regardless of material continuity. (8) This is clearly false: we can burn a manuscript and leave only ashes. (9) Hence (1) is false. I am inclined to preserve (1) as a constraint against metaphysical excess, yet I also want to preserve (8). Where does the reasoning break? Is there a scope ambiguity in "accessible," a modal slide between epistemic and metaphysical possibility, or a confusion between identity conditions and persistence conditions?
