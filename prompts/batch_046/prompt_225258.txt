I've noticed a recurring defense pattern in long-range predictive theories—particularly demographic and resource-limit models—that I can't place precisely within philosophy of science, and I'm wondering whether it has an established name. The pattern works like this. A theory predicts that a hard constraint will bind within a specific window, say fifty years, leading to a crisis. The window closes without the crisis, partly because of an exogenous buffer: synthetic fertilizers, an unexpected fertility transition, or temporary energy substitution. Rather than treating the miss as disconfirmation, defenders argue that the buffer was a one-time reprieve that allowed the underlying pressure to accumulate, so the mechanism is now closer and more severe—say, within thirty years, and with greater intensity. The failed deadline is reinterpreted not as a strike against the model but as a "spent loan" that makes the next milestone steeper.

A second instance shows the same temporal accounting. A projection forecasts that an ecosystem threshold will be crossed by mid-century; when the date arrives with the threshold intact, advocates explain that carbon sinks or technological adaptation absorbed more stress than anticipated, but they insist this merely postponed the inevitable crossing to an earlier date with amplified consequences. In both cases, each exogenous factor is treated individually as reasonable, but cumulatively they form a ledger of spent buffers. The net result is that no human-scale observation period can genuinely falsify the theory, because every failure is converted into proof that the system is "loading" the crisis more heavily.

This is not quite motte-and-bailey, since the core mechanism is held constant rather than retreated from, and it is not exactly a Lakatosian degenerating problemshift, because the adjustments are not arbitrary theoretical modifications but specific rules about temporal accounting—treating missed deadlines as debits that intensify future predictions. Is there an established concept that captures this specific move? If not, can you define it precisely, distinguish it from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses, from simple date-shifting, and from unfalsifiable historicism, and indicate whether it should be read as a rational update about hidden mechanisms or as a distinctive methodological failure mode in long-run forecasting?
