We both know the usual stuff — Hobsbawm's invention of tradition, Anderson's imagined communities and print-capitalism — and we both agree that medieval kingdoms weren't nations in the modern bureaucratic sense, and that 19th-century state-building did a lot of heavy lifting. But then we hit this wall: I say that's proof of modern construction, she says it's just the administrative shell being added to an older ethnic core that was always there in some latent form. We've circled this for weeks without anyone budging.

My working theory is that the stall isn't about missing archives but about something I keep calling mnemonic sovereignty — basically, who gets to decide which bits of the past count as continuous "us" and which bits get treated as foreign or deliberately forgotten. The modernists seem to treat continuity as an institutional claim that has to be actively maintained through schools, censuses and monuments, while the perennialists treat it as a cultural substrate that survives even when formal institutions collapse. So is this actually a factual disagreement about what existed in, say, 1200, or is it a hidden normative disagreement about whether collective identity needs explicit political recognition to be real? Could you diagnose what's really going on here? Is it a hidden empirical question about transmission mechanisms, a clash over what counts as evidence for continuity, or just bedrock intuitions about belonging that neither side can argue for?
