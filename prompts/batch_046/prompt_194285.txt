Here is roughly my reconstructed philosophical profile on Kepler’s astrology, and I want a consistency audit before I present it in a history-of-science seminar. I hold: (1) that Kepler accepted a physically causal mechanism for celestial influence—specifically, that geometric aspects (angular relations among planets) act on sublunary matter via forces analogous to light or magnetism, rather than through occult sympathies; (2) that he simultaneously held Copernican heliocentrism to be physically true, and therefore that the geocentric house-system used in practical horoscopy is a false physical description of the cosmos; (3) that despite (2), the geocentric frame remains instrumentally justified for casting charts because the relevant angular relations (except those involving Earth) are coordinate-invariant, and because human experience is terrestrially situated; (4) that natal astrology—specific predictions about individual temperament and fate—is empirically unreliable and largely superstitious, and should be rejected except for broad medical and meteorological correlations; (5) that Kepler was nonetheless intellectually honest in casting horoscopes for patrons such as Wallenstein, because he presented them as probabilistic, weather-like forecasts rather than deterministic prophecies, and because the practice was socially embedded and financially necessary; and (6) that astrology ought to be evaluated as an empirical, subordinate science to astronomy, not as a theological or symbolic system, so that its failures count as scientific failures rather than spiritual ones.

Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to conflict. For each real tension, rank them by severity, specify what each commitment costs the other, and identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency without abandoning the core physicalist-causal interpretation in (1). Also tell me whether (5) can survive once I take (4) seriously, or whether the social-justification in (5) collapses once predictive value is largely denied. Finally, check whether (3)’s coordinate-invariance claim actually saves the geocentric practice, or whether it smuggles in a conventionalist view about physical causation that conflicts with (1). I am not asking for a lecture on Kepler biography; I want an audit of this specific set.
