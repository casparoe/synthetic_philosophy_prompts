I’m working on a feature about rational commitment and decision theory, and I’ve sketched an argument that seems to prove far more than it should: specifically, that rational agents can never honor a binding agreement whenever an unopened alternative has the classic two-envelope payoff structure. The conclusion strikes me as absurd—contracts and precommitment devices like Ulysses contracts are plainly coherent—but I can’t pinpoint exactly which step smuggles in the error. Here is the reasoning, numbered as I have it:

(1) You hold envelope A, known to contain $100. Envelope B contains either $50 or $200, with equal prior probability.
(2) The expected value of B is $125, so rationality requires taking B over A.
(3) This reasoning depends only on the structural relation—B is either half or double A—not on the specific dollar amount inside A.
(4) Therefore the argument applies identically for any value in A: if A held $200, B’s expectation would be $250, and so on.
(5) Now suppose you have signed a binding contract to retain A and not open B. After signing, you still hold A ($100) and B remains unopened.
(6) By (3), the structural relation still holds, so rationality requires breaking the contract and taking B.
(7) Once you take B and observe, say, $200, the other envelope is either $100 or $400 with expectation $250, so you should switch back; the loop never terminates.
(8) Hence rational decision theory cannot endorse any stable choice in this setting, and specifically cannot endorse keeping a contract.
(9) But rational agents clearly can enter and honor contracts; a theory that eliminates all commitment proves too much.
(10) So one of premises (1) through (7) must be wrong.

If forced to choose, I’d rather preserve (2) and (3)—the core intuition about maximizing expected value in the simple case—than accept (8). Where exactly does the argument collapse? Is there an equivocation between information states, a hidden scope shift over the prior distribution, or a modal slide about what “rationality requires” means across different decision points? Please diagnose the faulty step, explain why it looked plausible, and tell me what giving it up costs. Answer in American English.
