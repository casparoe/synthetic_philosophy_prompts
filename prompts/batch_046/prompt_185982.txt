A junior quantitative researcher holds that requiring pre-registered analysis plans and public replication archives improves the reliability of published findings, relative to the conventional practice of selecting only statistically significant results for high-impact journals. She observes that her department's senior faculty have publicly endorsed open-science values, yet the tenure committee's evaluation rubric assigns no formal weight to data-sharing practices and continues to rank candidates primarily by journal prestige and citation velocity. Among her peer cohort, several colleagues—who have privately told her they share her methodological reservations—have begun abandoning pre-registration in favor of optimizing exclusively for traditional publication metrics. She is now deciding whether to maintain her current practices, which she believes are epistemically superior but professionally risky, or to align her behavior with the observable pattern around her. She asks three interrelated questions: first, whether her peers can be rational in suppressing their private judgments and following the collective behavior they observe; second, whether she herself possesses a coherent rational basis for conforming even while she believes the collective choice is suboptimal; and third, what conditions would need to obtain for her resistance to be justified rather than an instance of overestimating her own immunity to error.
