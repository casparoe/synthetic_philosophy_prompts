Analyze the Brown Dog affair of 1903–1910 — the controversy that followed experiments on a dog at University College London, the 1906 erection of a public statue commemorating the animal, the sustained riots and protests by medical students, the statue’s removal in 1910, and the involvement of anti-vivisection activists (including feminists who linked the cause to women’s rights) — through the lens of Kant’s indirect-duty account of obligations regarding animals. First, supply the disciplined mapping: identify the rational agents in the episode, specify what in the dog’s treatment counts as the absence of rational nature, and locate precisely where Kant’s framework says the moral harm of cruelty resides — in the animal’s suffering or in the effect on human moral character. Then apply the theory: would Kant’s view that we have no direct duties to animals but only indirect duties to humanity explain why the activists insisted on framing the dog as a wronged party in its own right, rather than as a mere symbol of human corruption? Examine at least one feature that standard readings of Kant miss — for instance, the gendered dynamics of the protest crowds, the destruction of the statue as symbolic violence directed at a non-rational being’s memorial, or the activists’ explicit comparison between the dog’s legal vulnerability and that of disenfranchised women — and say whether Kant’s indirect-duty framework illuminates it unexpectedly or collapses entirely. Finally, state what the affair reveals about the limits of grounding animal ethics solely in the cultivation or degradation of human moral sensibility, and whether any element of the case forces a revision of that grounding rather than merely an extension of it.
