I am preparing to defend a chapter of my dissertation—tentatively titled Calibration and Drift: Hinge Commitments as Dynamic Normative Structures—before our department’s epistemology and philosophy of mind joint seminar next Thursday. The topic has taken on unexpected personal weight since my father’s recent diagnosis with early-onset dementia, which has made me acutely aware of how fragile the basic certainties that ground everyday reasoning can become when the practices that maintain them begin to erode. In the chapter I argue that Wittgenstein’s hinge propositions in On Certainty are best understood neither as static background beliefs immune to evidence nor as purely pragmatic presuppositions, but as dynamic calibration points sustained through what I call epistemic anchoring practices—normative structures that determine whether a challenge to a hinge registers as rational disagreement or as a breakdown in shared epistemic conditions. I further contend that these hinges are susceptible to normative drift when anchoring practices decay across a community, a possibility I think Wittgenstein understates because he treats hinges as essentially inherited rather than actively maintained.

The audience is precisely divided: three formal epistemologists who model belief revision through probabilistic frameworks and will want to know why hinge stability cannot be captured as extreme credence; four social epistemologists concerned that treating hinges as non-evidential licenses conservatism and silences those whose basic certainties—particularly around embodied identity or historical testimony—are systematically destabilized; and two Wittgenstein specialists who know On Certainty intimately and will test whether my reading respects the text’s resistance to systematic theorizing. Crucially, include the objection I am most hoping no one raises—namely, that normative drift is merely evidentialism about hinge revision disguised in normative language—and advise me how to preempt or defuse it without appearing defensive.
