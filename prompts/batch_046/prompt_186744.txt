The precise question is this: do both frameworks presuppose that cultural membership is synchronously practiced and territorially anchored, and if so, does that shared premise collapse when applied to diasporic communities whose cultural practices are maintained asynchronously through digital mediation rather than local institutions? I need the proposal to state exactly why the standard comparative literature—treating laïcité as assimilationist universalism and multiculturalism as group recognition—leaves this open, specifically by assuming a stable subject of culture that can either be dissolved into citizenship or protected as a minority right. The approach should be philosophical and conceptual rather than empirical sociology: map the logical structure of each framework, identify where the territorial/synchronous premise operates, and test whether either can be reconstructed for non-territorial, asynchronous membership without losing its normative core. A useful deliverable should be a clear diagnostic—either a reconstructed conceptual framework or a rigorous argument that both traditions necessarily fail for this case—written for scholars of political theory and policy analysts working on integration, not a literature review. Include what a result would look like, what would count as failure (e.g., showing only that both frameworks are "incomplete" without specifying the conceptual cost of extending them), and a realistic scope for six months of work.
