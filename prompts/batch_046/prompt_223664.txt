Here is an argument about Madhyamaka that seems to prove too much and I cannot see where it slips. (1) Nāgārjuna's tetralemma rejects all four ontological positions—being, non-being, both, neither—because each presupposes svabhāva, an inherent, self-standing nature. (2) The rejection is meant to be universal: it applies not only to ordinary objects but also to distinctions, relations, and meta-level claims, including the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth and the claim that all things are empty. (3) Apply the tetralemma to the universal claim "all things lack svabhāva." If that claim itself lacks svabhāva, then it is not ultimately true in any of the four permitted modes; but if it is not ultimately true, it cannot serve as the ground for denying svabhāva everywhere else. (4) So either the claim undermines itself, or it is excepted from its own scope. (5) If excepted, then at least one thing—the universal claim, or the boundary that marks the exception—possesses svabhāva after all. (6) Therefore Madhyamaka either collapses into self-refutation or abandons universal emptiness for an arbitrary restriction. I want to keep (1) and (2), since selective skepticism seems to miss the radical point of the view. Which step should I reject—likely (3), (4), or (5)—and what does each rejection cost? In particular, if I reject (3) by treating the meta-claim as merely conventional, does that relocate rather than solve the problem?
