My study group constructed an argument about state data collection that now strikes me as suspicious because it concludes citizens should withhold nearly all personal information from government, yet each premise seemed defensible on its own. Here is the chain:

(1) A state's moral right to collect personal data depends on an enforceable purpose limitation: the data may be used only for the specific legitimate end that justified its collection, and bureaucratic repurposing violates that right.

(2) The German jurisprudence of informational self-determination—crystallized in the census ruling of 1983—treats centralization itself as the danger, because administrative interoperability makes purpose limitation technically unenforceable: tax records become accessible to immigration enforcement, health data to law enforcement, census data to social planners.

(3) American openness norms and Chinese social credit both violate purpose limitation, but in opposite directions. The U.S. makes state holdings visible to citizens (FOIA, public records) without blocking secondary use; China actively integrates data across domains for behavioral scoring. Both confirm that, once centralized, data flows are uncontrollable.

(4) If enforceable purpose limitation is impossible in any centralized apparatus, then condition (1) is never actually satisfied in practice, regardless of the state's stated intentions.

(5) Therefore citizens have no moral obligation to disclose data to the state—whether for taxation, census, public health, or democratic representation—because the precondition for legitimate collection always fails.

(6) But progressive taxation, emergency coordination, and democratic representation all require broad disclosure; without it the state collapses into either anarchy or pure coercion, which is unacceptable.

(7) So (1) must be false: the state's right to data does not depend on enforceable purpose limitation, and citizens do owe disclosure even when repurposing is likely.
