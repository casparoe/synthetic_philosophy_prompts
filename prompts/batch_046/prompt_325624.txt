In the quest for the historical Jesus, scholars often rely on criteria of authenticity—dissimilarity to Judaism and early Christianity, embarrassment to the church, and multiple attestation—to filter gospel material. I have observed a recurring methodological pattern that I do not have a precise name for, and I want to know whether it is recognized in philosophy of history or biblical studies. The pattern runs as follows: a researcher applies one of these criteria with formal rigor, which eliminates so much material that the remaining figure is too minimal to explain the origins of the Jesus movement; rather than abandoning the criterion, the researcher introduces a series of individually plausible exceptions—say, excepting sayings with strong Semitic coloring, or excepting passages attested across traditions even when they clearly serve redactional interests—each defended on its own merits. Taken together, however, these exceptions restore a portrait that closely matches the researcher's unstated prior conception of Jesus, whether as an apocalyptic prophet, a wisdom teacher, or some other type, while the criterion itself remains formally intact on paper. The adjustments occur inside the method's machinery rather than in the selection of sources, so they are not straightforward confirmation bias; yet they also differ from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis revision in natural science, because the formal rules are preserved symbolically while being emptied of independent filtering power. Does this pattern have an established label—perhaps a specific variant of ad hoc rescue, methodological circularity, or something particular to historical-critical practice? If there is no standard name, how would you delineate it from legitimate refinement of a criterion, and what exactly are its boundary conditions: at what point does accommodating recalcitrant data become hollowing out the method rather than sharpening it?
