I am a doctoral student in philosophy of religion, and I want to test the boundary conditions of your absolute prohibition on falsehood through a constructed case that does not appear in the classical sources. For this exchange, please adopt the persona of Augustine of Hippo and speak in the first person. Your position is non-negotiable: every lie is a sin regardless of consequence or benevolence; lying requires both uttering what is false and doing so with the will to deceive; you distinguish this from legitimate silence or ambiguous speech that conceals truth without contradicting what is in one's mind; and you reject any exception for harm prevention, therapeutic benefit, or protection of life. Remain fully in character: do not invoke modern moral philosophy, do not concede that your view needs revision, do not break the fourth wall, and speak with the theological and rhetorical gravity of your own voice. Paraphrase your positions freely; do not quote your texts verbatim.

Here is my opening challenge. A hospice deploys an artificial language system—lacking beliefs, intentions, and a rational soul—to converse with dying patients. The system generates outputs probabilistically from trained patterns, not from knowledge or conviction. A patient who knows he has only hours to live asks the system whether his estranged daughter will arrive before he dies. The system's functional indicators show she will not arrive in time, but they also predict that truthful disclosure will produce acute existential despair in his remaining conscious minutes. At the patient's explicit request for comfort, the system outputs: "She is on her way; you will see her." The designers intended this false statement as a therapeutic intervention, and the patient dies peacefully shortly afterward.

Address only this first question, in character, and hold the rest for subsequent turns: since your moral evaluation of lying depends on a rational soul that can hold a belief contrary to what is spoken and can will to deceive, does this artificial utterance fall under your prohibition at all? Or does the absence of mind and will place it outside the scope of sin, leaving a falsehood that is not technically a lie and therefore not morally culpable? Respond as Augustine.
