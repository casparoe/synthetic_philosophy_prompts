I've been trying to sort out my own position after a long argument with a friend about whether prosperity and order can ever justify authoritarian rule. Here is where I stand, and I want to know if these commitments actually hang together.

(1) I take democratic consent to be the primary—though not necessarily the only—source of legitimate political authority. A regime that lacks it therefore lacks full justificatory standing.

(2) Nevertheless, I think sustained economic development, public safety, and administrative competence generate a genuine, non-derivative form of what I call output authority: citizens have real, pro tanto moral reasons to comply with and cooperate in a well-functioning state even when democratic input is absent.

(3) Applying this, I hold that China's post-1978 record of growth and stability confers a partial, defeasible justification on CCP rule. At the same time, I treat Tiananmen, the Cultural Revolution's institutional legacy, and the Xinjiang camps not as costs that GDP can balance, but as non-compensable moral failures that damage—without, I think, necessarily annihilating—that partial justification.

(4) I view Singapore's semi-authoritarian model as a more coherent case for output authority: its lower-scale coercion and embedded feedback mechanisms (however non-democratic) make its performance legitimacy structurally more credible than China's, whose scale and opacity render its authority brittle despite comparable growth.

(5) I also maintain that an otherwise identical democratic government delivering the same prosperity and order would possess greater overall legitimacy. So performance considerations are never, by themselves, sufficient to justify authoritarian structures; they can only supplement or, in the absence of consent, partially substitute.

(6) Finally, I reject the view—common in our discussion—that regimes like China's have literally zero legitimate authority. To deny any moral weight to their governance achievements strikes me as implausible, because it cannot account for the actual obligations citizens might have to obey just laws or contribute to collective goods under such regimes.

The question is whether (1)–(6) form a stable package. In particular: does my acceptance of output authority in (2) conflict with the claim in (1) that consent is primary? Does treating rights violations as non-compensable in (3) sit consistently with saying they do not annihilate the partial justification? Does (5)'s insufficiency claim undermine (3)'s partial-justification claim, or are they compatible? Is my comparative judgment in (4) principled, or am I just being ad hoc? And is the tension between (1) and (6) real or only apparent?

Please audit these commitments: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, rank the tensions by severity, and specify the least costly revision to restore consistency—without assuming I am willing to abandon democratic consent as the primary source.
