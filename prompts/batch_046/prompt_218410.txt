For a seminar I am co-running on strategic commitment and rational failure, I have asked two long-arguing participants to produce an adversarial-collaboration document that neither can dismiss as a straw-man. One is committed to the view that Shubik-style dollar-auction escalation is driven by a structurally irrational sunk-cost mechanism: accumulated expenditure causally alters the subjective valuation of winning in a way that violates the independence axiom of rational choice, best explained by prospect-theoretic loss framing or by a specific failure of Bayesian belief revision about exit costs, and she treats real-world analogues—military quagmires, corporate bidding wars, regulatory brinkmanship—as manifestations of the same cognitive bias. The other is committed to the view that escalation is fully rational once the payoff matrix is correctly specified to include status-contingent utility, reputation preservation, and incomplete-information beliefs about the opponent's private exit threshold; he treats those same real-world analogues as strategic interactions mis-described by normative economists who omit face-loss and audience-cost parameters from the utility function, and he holds that a rational agent with common knowledge of rationality should anticipate the trap and avoid entry unless pre-commitment devices are available.

Write the joint statement they should both be able to sign. I want four parts in formal, signed-off prose, with no bibliography, citation requests, or appeals to authority. First, a numbered section of claims both accept: about the formal rules of the dollar auction; about what exactly requires explanation—entry, escalation past the prize value, and the conditions of exit; and about which real-world escalation phenomena are structurally analogous (shared two-party, sequential-bid, both-pay-cost structure) versus disanalogous (asymmetric information, third-party enforcement, or irreversible physical commitment). Second, the precise disagreements, each phrased in wording that its own camp would accept without distortion—especially whether accumulated expenditure non-rationally inflates the utility of winning, and whether entry by a fully rational agent is ever justified in a one-shot game with common knowledge. Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—including whether there exists an experimentally isolable parameter of "exit-threshold elasticity" that behaves differently under pure loss-framing versus strategic-belief conditions, and whether observed escalation frequencies in laboratory auctions collapse when subjects are given explicit pre-commitment contracts. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete experimental design, hypothetical case, or documented real-world episode that both sides agree is relevant, and record each camp's explicit predictive bet about what the evidence will show. Conclude with a disagreements table summarizing the residual disputes in neutral language, and state clearly whether any portion of their conflict is verbal rather than substantive—specifically, whether disagreement over labeling behavior "irrational" dissolves once both sides distinguish "violates expected-utility axioms given a fixed preference ordering" from "fails to maximize given the agent's full preference ordering including status costs." The document should read as a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with every residual disagreement made exact rather than rhetorical.
