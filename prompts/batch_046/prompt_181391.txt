I am a high school junior preparing a research presentation for my school's philosophy symposium, and I came across the following summary in an unnamed online encyclopedia entry about the ontological argument. The summary reads: Anselm claimed that God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived, and since existing in reality is greater than existing merely in the understanding, God must exist in reality. Gödel updated this by using modal logic: he defined a property as positive if it is necessarily good, showed that being God-like means possessing all positive properties, and then proved that if a God-like being is possible then it necessarily exists, because necessary existence itself is a positive property. According to the summary, this version avoids Kant's famous objection that existence is not a real predicate, since Gödel does not treat existence as a property added to a concept but rather derives it from modal consistency conditions. The summary concludes that the only serious remaining problem is whether the argument illegitimately defines God into existence, and that Gödel's axioms are ultimately too abstract and technically remote to carry persuasive force.

My philosophy teacher, however, raised a concern that the summary never mentions: she said that if positive properties are necessarily positive and closed under logical entailment, then Gödel's system might entail modal collapse—that is, that every true proposition becomes necessarily true and all possible worlds collapse into one—which would be a far more damaging structural flaw than the "defining into existence" worry. In particular, address whether the summary correctly captures how Gödel's modal derivation relates to Kant's objection, whether its claim that "the only serious problem" is definitional omission is misleading given the modal-collapse threat, and whether that threat actually undermines Gödel's proof or can be blocked by restricting the domain of properties without abandoning the argument.
