In a recent philosophy of medicine seminar, a discussant invoked the phrase “one thought too many” to object to a proposed emergency-triage algorithm that would require physicians to compute explicit survival-probability weights—ranking family members against strangers—before initiating rescue. She seemed to mean that inserting such a calculation corrupts the moral character of partiality, yet another participant took the same phrase to imply that any reflective justification of saving one’s child over a stranger is psychologically impossible or conceptually incoherent. I am trying to prepare a response for the next session that disentangles these uses rather than picking sides. Can you lay out, as a precise menu, the distinct claims that “one thought too many” might be expressing in this context? For each reading, specify whether it is (i) a trivial point about cognitive load or decision speed, (ii) a substantive empirical thesis about the psychology of moral motivation, (iii) a normative claim about the legitimate structure of moral justification, or (iv) a false conflation of reasoning with affect; and indicate which of these readings actually supports the objection to algorithmic triage rules, which supports the stronger claim that partiality can never be defended, and which are plainly mistaken. Present the disambiguation without collapsing it into a single “correct” interpretation, and for each entry note whether its force depends on a conceptual point about moral reasons or on an empirical claim about human psychology that might be contested.
