Construct a thought experiment for democratic boundary theory that meets the following specifications. Invent a realistic, non-science-fictional institutional setting—no exotic physics or technology, with stakes a normal person could grasp—centered on a transboundary water-and-flood governance body (you may name it, but keep it generic) that exercises coercive authority over a defined territory shared by multiple sovereign states. Within its jurisdiction lives Group G: long-term seasonal laborers who hold citizenship in none of the member states, reside and work inside the territory for eight months annually, are directly subjected to the body’s regulations and sanctions, and face tangible ecological risk from its dam-release and allocation decisions, yet are formally designated as temporary non-resident workers without political standing.

Engineer the case so that the all-affected-interests principle and the all-subjected principle both deliver the verdict that Group G belongs to the demos, while a principle of democratic closure—holding that only the existing demos may authoritatively determine its own membership—delivers the opposite verdict, excluding Group G.

Present the scenario in one paragraph. Then demonstrate, for each of the three principles, the reasoning that produces its verdict. Identify the precise structural feature of the case—specifically, the self-constituting authority claimed by the current demos—that allows democratic closure to peel away despite the agreement of the other two. Answer honestly whether removing that feature collapses the disagreement back into the standard boundary-problem tension between affectedness and subjection, or whether it yields a different kind of dispute altogether. Finally, show how a defender of democratic closure would attack the case: do they deny that Group G is genuinely subjected to coercive power, deny that ecological affectedness generates membership claims, or instead concede the premises and defend the exclusion as a legitimate exercise of collective self-determination?
