I was talking with a friend last week about whether there is really a continuous inner director behind my choices, or whether feeling like a single person is more like a story that keeps rewriting itself. She mentioned a comparison I had not heard before: that the self might be like a center of narrative gravity, not an actual object but the point where all the little stories about what I did, felt, and wanted seem to pull together, the way physicists talk about a center of mass that is not made of any particular atom. I keep returning to how strange that is — something can feel absolutely central and yet be empty of substance, just a convergence. I am not a student of philosophy, only someone who thinks about these things late at night, and I would like a poem that holds that tension open rather than resolving it.

Could you write a sestina for me? I want exactly thirty-nine lines — six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi — using these six end-words in rotation: center, story, pull, empty, weight, voice. Let the repetition of those words mirror how the self is reconstructed over and over from the same narrative fragments, never finally settling into one true version. In the envoi, bring all six words together in the final three lines so that the reader sees the center is only where story, pull, and empty happen to meet — not a thing possessing its own weight, but a voice that sounds singular only because the lines of narrative keep intersecting there. Use blank verse or loose iambic pentameter, with a serious and quiet tone. Keep the idea accurate: the self is not false in the sense of being a lie, but real only in the way a calculated point is real — stable, useful, and without material substance. Please do not resolve the paradox in the closing lines; instead, let the end-words sit side by side, unresolved, so that the form itself demonstrates what the concept means.
