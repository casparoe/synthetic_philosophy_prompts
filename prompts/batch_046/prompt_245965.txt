After a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about whether pain is bad in virtue of its intrinsic feel, its motivational force, or its representational content, I find myself holding a set of case-judgments that seem difficult to unify, and I want to know whether any precise principle can accommodate them all or whether I must sacrifice one. Here is where I stand. In ordinary acute pain—say, pressing a hand against a hot surface—I think the experience is genuinely bad, and the badness seems tied both to an unpleasant sensory quality and to the urgent, protective behavior it produces. In patients with pain asymbolia, however, I am inclined to say that whatever they report is not genuinely bad in the same way: they can localize the stimulus precisely, sincerely say “this hurts intensely,” and yet show no emotional distress, no facial expression of suffering, and no tendency to withdraw or protect the affected area; my intuition is that the affective-motivational dimension is missing and with it the real badness, even though the sensory-discriminative report remains intact.

The other cases complicate this picture. Phantom limb pain strikes me as clearly and profoundly bad—often agonizing—despite the absence of peripheral tissue damage and despite the fact that no withdrawal behavior is physically possible, which suggests that actual injury and successful motor response are not necessary for pain’s badness. By contrast, individuals with congenital insensitivity to pain lack the experience entirely, suffer repeated serious injuries, and I regard their condition as a major welfare deficit, but primarily because they are deprived of a protective signal rather than because they miss out on some intrinsic phenomenal good. Finally, my friend pressed me on a more abstract case: imagine a purely subpersonal nociceptive system—perhaps in an artificial agent or a decorticate preparation—that detects tissue damage and initiates reflexive withdrawal with no subjective experience whatsoever; I am strongly inclined to say that nothing bad is happening to it, which implies that bare functional role or representational content is insufficient. Is there one account—perhaps requiring a specific conjunction of sensory representation and negative affect, or a particular subject-level relation to bodily representation—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn, and what does the best available principle predict for a further dissociation I have not considered, namely a subject who experiences intense negative affect localized in a limb but with no corresponding sensory discrimination of damage?
