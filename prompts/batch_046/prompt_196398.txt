I'm taking an upper-division philosophy of science seminar on demarcation and falsificationism, and I'm trying to articulate my own intuitive judgments about which research programs actually count as scientific rather than pseudoscientific or merely speculative. Here are my verdicts on six concrete cases, ordered from clear to murky.

First, Einstein's 1919 gravitational deflection prediction: the theory risked a precise quantitative value that contradicted Newtonian mechanics, and the measurement confirmed it. This strikes me as the paradigm of scientific practice—high risk, high payoff, no retrospective tinkering.

Second, a psychoanalytic framework that explains a patient's aggression as repressed Oedipal conflict and the same patient's passivity as successful repression of that very conflict, with no independent conditions specified in advance for which outcome should occur. This feels like pseudoscience precisely because it never exposes itself to failure.

Third, a climate model that fails to predict a specific decadal warming pause, is then adjusted by incorporating previously unmodeled aerosol forcing—a physically independent mechanism—and subsequently predicts an unrelated extreme-weather frequency that is then observed. I still count this as science, though the initial failure weakens my confidence; the repair seems legitimate because it draws on external physics, not on the anomaly itself.

Fourth, a string-theoretic framework that makes no observable predictions below the Planck scale and offers no intermediate signatures reachable by foreseeable technology. Despite its mathematical rigor, I want to call this non-scientific in practice, even if it is not pseudoscientific.

Fifth, a genetic study that posits gene G influences trait T, finds no correlation in the full sample, then defines a protective environment post-hoc—using only the non-correlating subjects—to exclude them, and reports a significant effect in the remaining subgroup. This feels like case two in scientific dress: retrospective immunization.

Sixth, a clinical trial that predicts drug D benefits all patients with condition C, fails in the full cohort, but then reveals—using a biomarker B that was measured pre-trial but never previously theorized to interact with D—that D works strongly in B-positive patients and not in B-negative ones. Despite the post-hoc subgroup split, this feels like legitimate science to me, unlike case five.

The tension is between cases three, five, and six: all involve failure followed by revision or partitioning. I want a principle—perhaps something like independent predictive exposure, where a theory is scientific only if its repairs or subdivisions risk predictions in domains not used to construct them—that distinguishes three and six from five. But I'm not sure it handles case four (unobservable in principle) or case two (no risk at all). There's also a further case I keep going back and forth on: a machine-learning forecasting system that is automatically retrained on every missed prediction and then correctly predicts the next out-of-sample event, with no theoretical mechanism linking the updates to the world. Is that like case three or case five?

Can you state the most coherent principle, or a small family of competing ones, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible? For each clause, indicate which case motivates it and what verdict it delivers on the ML forecasting case and on case four. Be honest about which of my six verdicts the principle must overturn if simplicity is to be preserved, and whether the distinction between case five and case six collapses under pressure.
