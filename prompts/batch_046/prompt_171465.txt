I spent much of last evening in a heated but good-faith argument with a friend about zoning reform and whether long-time residents have a moral claim to block new construction. To defend his position, he summarized a view he said he had encountered in an urban-theory essay on Henri Lefebvre’s idea of a “right to the city,” and I want to check whether his rendering is fair before I reply in writing, because some parts sounded accurate while others seemed to smuggle in conclusions the original idea does not actually support. Here is his summary, reconstructed as faithfully as I can: The right to the city is fundamentally a collective property entitlement held by the existing inhabitants of a neighborhood, not merely a claim to democratic participation. Because a neighborhood is not an aggregate of private lots but a single social object that its residents collectively compose, they possess a pre-political moral veto over any development that would alter its physical or demographic makeup. Lefebvre therefore rejected both state-led redevelopment and private gentrification as violations of this integrated right. On this reading, zoning boards do not restrict individual liberty but instead institutionalize the residents’ collective authority over their shared object; when neighbors organize to oppose upzoning, they are not being selfish but are defending legitimate ontological boundaries. Consequently, pro-housing arguments that appeal to individual property rights or aggregate welfare miss the point entirely, since they wrongly treat the city as a collection of separable parcels rather than as a unified whole with rights of its own.

I am inclined to believe that he has turned a participatory-democratic thesis into a metaphysical doctrine about collective ownership of a composed object, but I am not well read enough in the literature to specify exactly where the distortions lie. Could you audit this summary in detail? For each major claim, tell me whether a careful defender of Lefebvre’s view would accept it, whether it is a subtle misrepresentation or an overstatement, and whether the conclusion that NIMBY zoning is morally justified survives once the accurate parts are separated from the errors. I want a committed assessment: take a definite position on whether the summary is fair, take a definite position on whether the anti-development conclusion follows from anything defensible in the original idea, and give your reasons directly rather than laying out a menu of interpretations and leaving me to choose.
