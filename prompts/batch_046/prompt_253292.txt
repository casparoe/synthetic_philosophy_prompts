I am working through the case-judgments of a contemporary philosopher, Dr. Lin, who applies a reconstructed Mingjia (School of Names) taxonomy to modern categorization. She treats compound names as subject to what she calls co-present property isolation, drawn from her reading of the Jian Bai (Hard and White) material: once a compound specifies one property, the unspecified properties that normally travel with the simple term are isolated rather than inherited. Her verdicts on actual cases have been consistent but leave the underlying rule ambiguous.

Her record so far:
1. A yellow horse is correctly called a horse, and not correctly called a white horse.
2. A white horse is correctly called a white horse, and not correctly called a horse.
3. A black horse is correctly called a horse, and not correctly called a white horse.
4. A hard, white block of granite is correctly called hard white granite, correctly called granite, but not correctly called hard granite and not correctly called hard stone.
5. A soft, black block of granite is not hard white granite, not hard granite, but is correctly called granite.

The new case she must rule on is a white marble statue carved in the precise anatomical form of a horse, displayed in a gallery with no living tissue present.

Predict her verdict: is it a horse, a white horse, marble, a stone, none of these, or some combination? Identify which of her earlier cases is the closest analog. Then lay out at least two distinct rules that could explain her entire track record, say what each predicts about the statue, and give your best-guess verdict with reasons. State explicitly what principle she would be adopting if she rules one way rather than the other, and name one further case that would best tell the competing rules apart. Please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point about which you are least sure.
