As a retired biologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy systematically, I attend a small discussion group with no assigned leader. At our last meeting, two other members—Elena, a historian of science, and Marcus, a logician—debated whether philosophy makes progress in anything comparable to natural science. I am relaying their exchange as precisely as I can, because I genuinely could not decide between them and want a point-by-point assessment.

Elena began by observing that philosophy’s central questions—what justice requires, whether consciousness is physical, whether free will is compatible with determinism—have remained live for millennia without convergence. In biology, she noted, we no longer seriously defend spontaneous generation or the inheritance of acquired characteristics; those views were abandoned because evidence accumulated against them. Philosophy, she argued, exhibits what she called conceptual sedimentation rather than progress: old answers rarely disappear; they sink beneath notice and become invisible framing assumptions. The Cartesian division of mental and physical substance, for instance, is rarely defended today, yet it still structures how we pose problems about consciousness, so we mistake an inherited scaffold for neutral ground. Without an empirical filter, philosophical change is not falsification but burial, and burial is not epistemic advancement.

Marcus replied that this misdescribes the dynamics. Philosophy does not require laboratory falsification to progress; it requires the refinement of distinctions, the elimination of pseudo-problems, and greater coherence among commitments. He granted Elena’s sedimentation point but reinterpreted it through an analogy to taxonomy. Linnaean categories were not disproved in any single experiment; they were superseded by phylogenetic classification, yet we rightly call that progress. The older taxonomy is not buried garbage but soil—the conceptual ground from which sharper questions grow. Likewise, the shift from substance dualism to functionalist and embodied frameworks is progress precisely because the older framing is now visible as one option among others, disciplined by argument rather than by predictive failure.

Elena countered that the analogy collapses on closer inspection. Taxonomic revision is guided by explanatory and predictive success—phylogenetic trees anticipate genetic similarities and fossil distributions—whereas philosophical sedimentation lacks any comparable external check. Functionalism gained prominence not because it resolved an internal contradiction that dualism could not survive, but because it aligned with the institutional rise of cognitive science and computer modeling; its victory was sociological, not rationally compelled. If philosophical shifts are driven by disciplinary fashion rather than by argument-driven failure modes—explicit contradiction, infinite regress, or the inability to preserve distinctions that matter for practice—then labeling them progress is merely dignifying historical drift with an epistemic title.

Marcus conceded that sociology influences which frameworks receive attention, but insisted that arguments possess their own failure modes independent of fashion. A framework that systematically collapses distinctions required for normative judgment, or that generates internal contradictions under pressure, is not merely unfashionable; it is defective. Philosophy progresses when a framework avoids these rational failures with greater scope and coherence than its rivals, even when both survive in the literature. The absence of a decisive empirical experiment does not entail the absence of rational constraint; it only changes the currency of evaluation from predictive accuracy to structural soundness and explanatory breadth.

They then turned to me and asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their dispute point by point: on whether the comparison with scientific progress is apt or misleading; on whether conceptual sedimentation constitutes genuine progress, mere burial, or something else; on whether the taxonomy analogy holds and where it breaks; and on whether the lack of empirical filters renders philosophical change primarily sociological rather than rational. State who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing that someone with a background in mature natural science might notice about how conceptual change actually operates when a field lacks a single decisive testing protocol. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I want only the adjudication itself.
