I am assisting with an advanced course on Buddhist metaphysics, and after discussing with colleagues how often Madhyamaka emptiness is taught as a purely logical exercise, I want to stage something more visceral. I am convening a 90-minute postgraduate workshop for about 14 students who have already read Nāgārjuna's root verses in translation and are comfortable with the distinction between conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya) and ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya). The aim is not to rehearse the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) as a list of four positions, but to make the group feel why it applies reflexively: the very framework that distinguishes conventional designation from ultimate emptiness must itself be seen as dependently originated rather than intrinsically real, without this insight collapsing into mere relativism or scepticism.

Please design a structured simulation centred on a concrete institutional case study — for instance, a university faculty that, over fifty years, has replaced every staff member, document, building, and curriculum, yet retains legal continuity, a name, and obligations from the past. Divide the participants into four groups, each inhabiting one corner of the tetralemma with respect to this institution's identity: intrinsic persistence; total absence of identity; simultaneous possession and absence; and the radical exclusion of both possession and absence. Give each group genuine decision-making authority — for example, the collective allocation of a symbolic budget or the assignment of responsibility for a historical debt — and construct rules and incentives that make them act consistently within their assigned stance rather than treating it as theatrical play. Then introduce a hidden meta-phase in which the same fourfold analysis must be applied to the rules of the game itself and to the distinction between the two truths, so that participants discover that their own judging framework is also conventionally designated.

Specify the physical layout or spatial movement between phases, a precise timeline in minutes, the payoff structure that creates real social investment, and a debrief that maps each round onto its corresponding logical position, explaining exactly where the practical breakdown occurs and why conventional practice remains necessary. Be explicit about the feature this activity cannot reproduce — namely, the non-conceptual awareness that Madhyamaka texts point toward beyond all four corners — and state precisely how you will supply that absence in the closing discussion. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I anticipate that at least one group will refuse to inhabit the "both" or "neither" positions with sincerity — and describe how to recover without breaking the simulation's integrity.
