Having spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics and only recently begun serious study of philosophy of science, I find the long-standing dispute between spacetime substantivalists and relationists—classically dramatized by Newton's rotating bucket—peculiarly resistant to resolution. On the surface, the positions are sharply defined: the substantivalist holds that the concavity of water in a rotating bucket reveals motion with respect to an independent spatiotemporal substance, whereas the relationist maintains that inertia arises solely from relations among material bodies, requiring either a Machian explanation in terms of distant masses or an admission that the phenomenon remains unexplained without them. General relativity subsequently showed spacetime to be dynamical, yet philosophers continue to ask whether the metric field is itself a substance or merely the codification of relational structures. Despite this, the debate has advanced little in half a century.

My hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but methodological: both camps share the same formal apparatus—Einstein's field equations, the geodesic principle, diffeomorphism invariance—yet they apply incompatible criteria for ontological commitment. The substantivalist treats gauge redundancy as an epistemic limitation that hides real structure; the relationist treats it as proof that only invariant relations exist. If this is right, they are not disagreeing about physics but about what counts as an explanation, with each side interpreting the success of background-independent dynamics as vindication of its own standard.

But I wonder whether there is also a hidden formal disagreement that neither side has articulated precisely—specifically, whether discrete symmetries at a Planck-scale cutoff might break continuous diffeomorphism invariance in a way that makes rotational inertia either granular or relationally irreducible, and whether such a result would adjudicate the dispute or merely displace it onto quantum-gravitational ontology.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is this a verbal dispute about the term space, a substantive ontological conflict masked by shared mathematics, or two distinct projects—one metaphysical, one explanatory—conducted under a single heading? Engage concretely with what each side says about the bucket argument in the context of modern gauge-theoretic dynamics, not merely with the labels substantivalism and relationism. And, given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would constitute progress? Would it require a formal result about the physical significance of gauge redundancy, a conceptual distinction between dynamical and kinematical structure, an empirical scenario involving discrete rotational spectra, or should we conclude that no resolution is possible because the questions asked are not ones that physical theory is designed to answer?
