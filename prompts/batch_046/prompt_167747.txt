I am taking an advanced ethics elective at my high school, and our seminar recently worked through David Benatar's argument for antinatalism based on his well-known asymmetry between suffering and pleasure. My study group then constructed what we call a genealogical cascade argument that begins with the asymmetry but seems to drive straight past antinatalism into something far more extreme, and I cannot locate the exact misstep, though I want to preserve the core intuition if I can. Here is our reasoning, laid out in numbered steps as we presented it in class. (1) Benatar's asymmetry holds that the absence of suffering is genuinely good even when there is no subject who benefits from that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there actually exists someone who is thereby deprived. (2) If this is correct, then bringing a person into existence can be justified only by avoiding a greater harm, never merely by conferring a benefit, since benefits to people who do not exist do not register positively on any moral ledger. (3) Now apply the cascade: any person brought into existence will, with nonzero probability, eventually produce descendants, and each of those descendants, should they come to exist, will be subject to suffering that counts as genuinely bad, while if they never come to exist their absence of pleasure is not bad according to the asymmetry. (4) Therefore preventing the conception of any single person not only spares that individual from suffering but also prevents an indefinite tree of future suffering, all without any corresponding moral loss, since none of the merely potential people in that tree are deprived of pleasure by remaining non-existent. (5) Applying this retroactively, every death that cuts short a reproductive line is, on this view, a net moral gain, because it terminates a cascade of potential harms without generating any deprivation. (6) But then the asymmetry seems to entail not merely that we should stop reproducing, but that we have an obligation to hasten extinction, and it further implies that saving a life is morally equivalent to perpetuating harm, which is clearly absurd. (7) I am inclined to keep (1) and (2) because they match Benatar's explicit commitments, yet I cannot tell whether the error lies in (3)'s probabilistic reasoning about descendants, in (4)'s aggregation over merely possible people, in (5)'s retroactive application to actual deaths, or whether the absurdity in (6) simply demonstrates that (1) must be abandoned altogether. Could you diagnose which step fails, explain precisely why it looked plausible on its face, and price the alternatives for someone who wishes to preserve the core asymmetry without endorsing extinction?
