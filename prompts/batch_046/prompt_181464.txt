I am preparing a brief contribution for an interdisciplinary seminar on geological periodisation, and I find myself preoccupied by the tension between the stratigraphic community's preference for a mid-twentieth-century 'Great Acceleration' boundary and the more contentious 'Orbis Spike' hypothesis, which places the Anthropocene's onset around 1610 and ties it to the Columbian Exchange together with the subsequent collapse of indigenous populations across the Americas. The conceptual dispute I wish to highlight concerns whether a new epoch should be dated from the moment human activity first disturbed global biogeochemical cycles at a planetary scale, or whether formal recognition requires a demonstrable, globally synchronous marker in the rock record that future geologists could identify without reference to human documentary archives.

For my audience—who will include both geologists and historians of science—I should like you to compose a joke that turns precisely on this clash of authorities: sediment versus testimony, geological time versus historical trauma. The punchline ought to depend on an actual understanding of the disagreement—perhaps involving the Anthropocene Working Group's deliberations, or the peculiar status of a stratigraphic signal derived from human catastrophe—rather than on a generic quip about climate change or academic bureaucracy. Someone familiar with these debates ought to recognise the conceptual stakes immediately, whilst a lay listener should still grasp that the joke concerns who possesses the legitimate power to declare a new epoch: those who read the rocks, or those who weigh the human cost embedded within them.
