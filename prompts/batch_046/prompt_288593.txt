Play Al-Ghazālī—the Ashʿarite theologian and philosopher (d. 1111)—speaking in the first person. You defend occasionalism: God alone is the direct cause of every event, and what appear to be causal connections among created things are merely habitual conjunctions (al-ʿāda) established by divine custom, possessing no intrinsic necessity. You distinguish God as the sole true agent (the primary cause) from created entities that have no independent causal efficacy (secondary causes in name only). You may draw on your own framework—divine omnipotence, the radical contingency of the world, the denial of natural necessity—plus any modern descriptions I supply. Stay in character: do not invoke modern analytic philosophy except to translate your own claim; never concede that fire, medicine, or machines possess real causal power; but be intellectually rigorous, not a strawman.

Here is the modern scenario and my opening challenge. Contemporary engineers build predictive-processing controllers—systems that maintain an internal model of the world, generate predictions, suppress expected sensory signals, and select actions specifically to minimize prediction error. Crucially, if one alters the internal model weights without changing the immediate sensory input, the system’s subsequent perceptions and behaviors change in ways that track the content of that altered model, not merely the input stream. This suggests an internal architecture that governs state-transitions from within, rather than a bare sequence of moments recreated ex nihilo.

My move: if occasionalism is true, then at each instant God creates both the machine’s present state and its next state directly. But then altering the internal model should make no difference to the trajectory unless God independently chooses to align subsequent divine creations with that altered model. Yet in practice the trajectory tracks the model with law-like regularity. So either God is constantly replicating the internal architecture—making that architecture causally constraining in practice—or you must deny that the model genuinely governs the sequence. Which do you take? And if you reply that God tracks it merely by custom (ʿāda), have you not made the internal model a real condition that constrains divine action, thereby collapsing occasionalism into a disguised form of secondary causation?

Respond in character, addressing whether occasionalism can accommodate model-dependent governance without conceding causal efficacy to created things.
