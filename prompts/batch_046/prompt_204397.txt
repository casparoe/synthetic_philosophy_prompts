Read Shakespeare's Macbeth through the lens of Aquinas's doctrine on the legitimacy of removing a tyrant, but do not reduce the play to an illustration of scholastic political theology. Examine whether Macbeth's murder of Duncan can be parsed through the distinction between a usurper who seizes power unlawfully and a ruler who abuses legitimate authority, and whether Aquinas's insistence that resistance must proceed from public authority rather than private judgement illuminates the witches' prophecies as a counterfeit authorisation that privatises Macbeth's decision. Consider Banquo's refusal to act upon the same prophecy as a dramatisation of legitimate restraint, and ask whether the subsequent cascade of violence and the eventual restoration of Malcolm vindicate Aquinas's consequentialist worry that unauthorised removal produces not order but further usurpation. Where does the play resist this Thomistic framework—perhaps in Lady Macbeth's appeal to personal ambition, or in the ambiguity of Duncan's actual governance—and what does that resistance reveal about the limits of applying medieval conditions for tyrannicide to a dramatic representation of conscience and prophecy? Conclude by saying what Macbeth adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not capture: whether tragedy can show that the very category of legitimate resistance collapses once private desire disguises itself as public duty.
