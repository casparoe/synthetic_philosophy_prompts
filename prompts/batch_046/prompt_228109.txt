I'm trying to clarify a distinction that keeps dissolving whenever I press on it in moral psychology: the boundary between a genuine moral judgment and a culturally acquired preference that has been mistaken for one. To ground the problem concretely, imagine a moral agent raised in a society where restrained, silent meals are treated not merely as etiquette but as an expression of respect for others' interiority; when she encounters a culture where loud, overlapping conversation during meals is the norm, she judges the practice as not just different but morally deficient—an affront to dignity rather than a harmless social variation. She insists her reaction tracks a real moral feature, yet an outside observer might see nothing more than an aversion to unfamiliar social noise shaped by her upbringing.

Write an essay that investigates whether any criterion can reliably separate preferences from biases in cases structured like this. You should weigh the skeptical view—that all such judgments are ultimately expressions of enculturated taste dressed in moral language—against the position that some norms genuinely carry moral weight independent of familiarity. In particular, examine whether appeals to harm, autonomy, or universalizability can do the distinguishing work without smuggling in the very biases they claim to filter out. Do not end on a non-committal note: defend either that the distinction collapses and these conflicts should be reconceived as negotiations among incommensurable preferences, or that a specific boundary condition survives—identify it precisely and justify why it is not merely another artifact of comfort or habit.
