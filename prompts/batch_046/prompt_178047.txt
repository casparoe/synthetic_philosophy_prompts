I have recently become the primary caregiver for my mother, who was diagnosed with early-onset dementia, and this shift has made me acutely aware of how quickly private family obligations can consume every available hour. At the same time, I belong to a small mutual-aid cooperative in which members exchange eldercare labor through a closed-loop system—no money changes hands, only accumulated hours of care that can be drawn upon later. One fellow member, whose own parent just entered hospice care, has asked to suspend all her cooperative duties indefinitely so she can focus exclusively on her family. Several others argue that allowing selective withdrawal whenever personal crisis strikes dissolves the reciprocity that sustains the pool, and that she remains bound by the caring relationships she has built through prior exchanges. I feel the urgency of her situation, yet I also fear that if every member retreats to isolated family duty at the first crisis, the collective collapses and we are all left with unsustainable private burdens. Without naming any particular ethical tradition, can you help me determine whether insisting she continue her cooperative obligations is a failure of compassion, or whether excusing her entirely risks reducing care to an atomized, privatized duty that ultimately harms everyone? Please answer in American English.
