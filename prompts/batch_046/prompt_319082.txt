I'm grading take-home essays for a philosphy of mind course I'm TAing for, and I'm second-guesing one response about Buddhist anatta and promises. The exam question was: 'Buddhist doctrine denies a permanent, essential self (anatta), yet people clearly make commitments and feel bound by past promises. Can a defender of anatta account for this binding force without either (a) sneaking in a metaphysically substantive self, or (b) reducing promises to mere causal sequences with no normative weight? Discuss with reference to the conventional/ultimate truth distinction and at least one modern theory of personal identity.'

The student writes well overall. They say anatta is basically impermanence—everything is always changing—so there's no problem with promises because the self that made the promise and the self that keeps it are just causally linked streams. They correctly invoke sammuti-sacca versus paramattha-sacca, noting that conventionally we talk about people but ultimately there's only dharmas. Then they bring in Derek Parfit's psychological continuity view, arguing that since memories and intentions carry over, the 'person' is preserved in the only way that matters, which lets anatta keep promises without a soul. They conclude that the binding force comes from the continuity of the psychological stream itself, so no substantive self is needed.

I'm wondering if they collapsed anicca into anatta—anatta is specifically about lacking essence or ownership, not just changing over time. I'm also unsure whether they misread Parfit as endorsing the Buddhist two-truths framework directly, when he might just be drawing an analogy. And there's a step that feels shaky: they claim psychological continuity itself supplies normative binding, but continuity seems descriptive rather than normative—why should a stream feel obligated just because it's continuous? What's genuinely right, what's subtly wrong, and does the reasoning hold?
