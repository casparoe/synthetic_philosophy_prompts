As a novelist working in long-form serial fiction, I am trying to navigate the philosophical terrain of the paradox of fiction not for scholarly completeness but for practical clarity: I need to understand why readers feel genuine grief or moral outrage for invented people, and whether that emotional contract changes when a narrative unfolds across years and multiple volumes rather than in a single sitting. Among the live debates—the status of quasi-emotions versus full-blooded affective responses, the hedonic puzzle of why audiences seek out fictional suffering, the function of imagination in suspending ontological judgment, and the emerging question of whether sustained parasocial attachment in extended narratives operates by different psychological mechanisms than momentary immersion—which should command my attention first? Give me a defended agenda rather than a survey: rank the open questions or lines of work by which are upstream of the others, which are tractable for someone without a neuroscience background, and which, if settled, would actually alter how I think about the ethics of manipulating reader empathy or about the moral weight I can ask an audience to assign to characters they know do not exist. I want explicit criteria—dependency, tractability, and practical consequence for narrative craft—and I want to know which debates you would deprioritize because resolving them would change almost nothing downstream.
