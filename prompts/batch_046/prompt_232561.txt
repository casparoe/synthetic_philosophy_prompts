Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophical questions about probability and rational belief, I have been working through the classic problem of the principle of indifference—particularly in the geometric form usually associated with Bertrand's paradox. Rather than ask for a survey, I want to test whether my own concrete judgments about particular cases form a coherent pattern. Below are my verdicts on how probabilities should be assigned in various chord-selection scenarios, together with one case about which I remain genuinely uncertain. I am looking for a precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that delivers these judgments, explains which case motivates each clause, applies to the unlisted case I describe at the end, and honestly identifies which of my verdicts must be sacrificed if simplicity is to be preserved.

Case 1. A point is selected inside a homogeneous circular disk of radius R with no further specification. By rotational and translational symmetry, I judge the correct probability that it lies within distance r of the center to be proportional to the area πr², hence (r/R)². A parameterization that selects the radial coordinate uniformly on [0,R] and the angular coordinate uniformly on [0,2π) yields a linear dependence on r; I reject that assignment as spurious, because it violates the physical homogeneity of the space.

Case 2. A chord is formed by selecting two points independently and uniformly on the circumference with respect to arc length. I accept that, under this precise operational definition, the probability that the chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle is 1/3.

Case 3. A chord is formed by selecting a point uniformly over the area of the disk and drawing the unique chord perpendicular to the radius through that point. I accept that, under this operational definition, the corresponding probability is 1/4.

Case 4. A chord is formed by selecting a distance p uniformly from [0,R] along a randomly oriented radius and drawing the perpendicular chord at that distance. I accept that, under this specific physical interpretation—akin to dropping a straight stick whose midpoint is uniformly distributed along a line through the center—the corresponding probability is 1/2. I deny, however, that this answer applies to the unqualified question “what is the probability that a random chord is longer than the side of the triangle”; it applies only when the generating mechanism is exactly this one.

Case 5. Now consider an ellipse with semi-major axis 2 and semi-minor axis 1. A chord is specified by selecting two points independently and uniformly on the boundary with respect to arc length. Direct computation using the arc-length parameter s gives one numerical answer for the probability that the chord exceeds the minor axis in length. But if one begins with the uniform endpoint distribution on the unit circle (which yields 1/3 for the analogous question) and applies the affine transformation that produces this ellipse, one obtains a different value. I am genuinely torn: my intuition about physical homogeneity pulls me toward the direct arc-length parameterization, while my intuition about structural invariance pulls me toward the transformed-circle answer. I keep revising my judgment about which, if either, is correct.

Case 6, which I have not listed and which your principle should address. Consider a chord inside the unit circle generated by selecting a point uniformly from the area of the annulus with inner radius 1/2 and outer radius 1, then selecting an orientation uniformly in [0,π), and drawing the line through the point in that orientation clipped to the circle. Is there a unique probability—independent of further specification—that the resulting chord exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, or does the answer depend on an unstated feature of the selection mechanism?

My questions are these. First, is there a coherent principle—perhaps requiring an explicit reference to the physical or operational mechanism that defines the sample space—that unifies my judgments in Cases 1 through 4, and what does it imply for the tension in Case 5? Second, apply that principle precisely to Case 6 and state its verdict. Third, if preserving simplicity requires overturning one or more of my judgments, say explicitly which ones they are and why the simpler principle is worth the cost. I do not want bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations; I want the principle itself, its motivating clauses, its predictions for the cases I have not considered, and an honest assessment of the price it exacts.
