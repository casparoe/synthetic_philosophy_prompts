I work as a senior analyst in a regional legislative review office, and my team must evaluate proposed ordinances that reshape how citizens interact with public authority; we face these reviews repeatedly, often under tight deadlines, and our assessments go directly to elected officials who must sign off. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the conceptual distinctions among negative liberty, positive liberty, and republican liberty as non-domination, that we can actually run in about thirty minutes per proposal without consulting outside texts. The procedure should proceed through a fixed sequence of analytical steps: first, determine whether the ordinance removes an obstacle to action, creates an obstacle, or restructures the conditions under which action is possible; second, examine whether any power left intact by the ordinance—bureaucratic, private, or algorithmic—is arbitrary in the specifically republican sense, meaning it can be exercised without tracking the affected party's interests and without meaningful avenues for contestation, regardless of whether it is ever actually exercised; third, assess whether the ordinance expands effective self-direction or merely shifts dependency from one source to another, paying particular attention to cases where formal freedom remains intact but the practical range of choice collapses. At each step, the procedure must explicitly flag where classification depends on a normative judgment that cannot be eliminated by further factual inquiry—especially the boundary between mere dependency and domination, and the question of whether formal procedural constraints on discretion eliminate arbitrariness or only mask it. Include a small set of internal test questions, phrased so an analyst can apply them directly to the text of a proposal, that distinguish actual interference from its absence, and domination from benign dependency. Then apply the full procedure to three specific proposals: first, an ordinance requiring all applications for housing assistance to be submitted through a single state-managed digital portal that automatically rejects any submission lacking a particular encrypted signature, with no provision for human review of rejections and no alternative submission pathway; second, an ordinance that prohibits all private neighborhood security associations and assigns exclusive policing and surveillance authority within designated districts to a municipal force whose operational priorities are set by an appointed board serving fixed terms without direct electoral accountability; third, an ordinance that imposes a substantial recurring fee on private vehicle registration, using the revenue to fund universal free public transit and to eliminate fares entirely. Walk through each case in continuous detail, showing exactly where the procedure yields a clear classification among the three conceptions and where it halts and demands a judgment call from the analyst. Crucially, include at least one case where the procedure produces a result that an advocate of expanded public provision and reduced private dependency would find unwelcome, and identify precisely which step in the sequence forces that conclusion. Present the entire response—procedure, tests, and applications—as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, numbered lists, or tables, as if writing a detailed internal memo or letter. Do not include citations, bibliographies, references to specific authors or texts, or requests for further reading; work solely from the conceptual distinctions themselves.
