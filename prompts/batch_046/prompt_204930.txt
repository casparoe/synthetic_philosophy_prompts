I'm planning a fifty-minute seminar for advanced philosophy undergraduates who have encountered the classic experience-machine scenario but have not systematically studied well-being theories. Rather than treating it as a stock objection to hedonism, I want to frame it around a specific condition I call "reality-contact": the claim that well-being requires a causal relation to actual states of affairs that is not reducible to phenomenology, even when that relation is never introspectively available. Plan the session's explanatory arc. First, the dependency order: what conceptual distinction must be secured before the reality-contact condition can be introduced, and why must it come first? Second, the carrying example: design or select a modified machine scenario—for instance, one with an intermittent, user-controlled portal to reality—and specify exactly what features it must possess and what it must lack to isolate the reality-contact intuition from confounding factors like risk, ignorance, or social comparison. Third, the misconception map: identify the predictable misunderstandings (such as conflating the intuition with mere risk-aversion, or taking it to vindicate an objective-list theory wholesale), and state precisely where in the sequence each should be anticipated and neutralized. Fourth, the understanding check: propose one targeted question or task that would show students have grasped the reality-contact condition itself, rather than merely reproducing standard objections to the machine. Fifth, state explicitly which standard taxonomic material—for example, the full typology of hedonist, desire-satisfaction, and objective-list accounts—should be left out of this session, with a brief justification for each omission. Do not ask for readings, citations, or a bibliography in your plan.
