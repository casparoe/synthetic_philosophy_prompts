Compare karma, understood as a diachronic theory of moral desert that distributes outcomes across successive lives, with a strictly synchronic theory holding that an agent can deserve outcomes only for actions within her present psychological continuity. Focus on the problem of "desert without memory": if a being suffers for past misdeeds she does not recall and with which she shares no psychological connection, the synchronic view seems to deny that the suffering is deserved, whereas karma affirms it through some non-psychological substrate of identity. Analyze whether karma genuinely resolves the arbitrariness this raises, or whether it merely relocates the problem to the metaphysics of personal persistence. Distinguish what each view must presuppose about the conditions for self-identity over time, and assess whether a hybrid account—retaining diachronic desert but replacing rebirth with causal-trace persistence—could escape the objections facing both.
