As a doctoral student in philosophy serving on my university's Strategic Research Allocation Panel, I need help drafting our decision memo on a proposal that forces our own reasoning into an uncomfortable symmetry. A behavioral-research group wants approval for a study in which adult participants choose between (a) a near-certain, mildly unpleasant sensory task with no further consequence, and (b) the same task paired with a 0.0000001% chance of an outcome the protocol describes as preventing the death of ten billion people. The researchers defend the design by explicit expected-value reasoning: the discomfort is trivial, but if the study improves long-term risk-governance decisions even slightly, the expected social value is astronomical, so the study is not merely permissible but obligatory. Our charter requires risk-benefit proportionality and prohibits justifying near-certain subject-level harms through speculative, unquantified downstream effects. Our precedents: we rejected deception about physical risk even with debriefing; we approved temporary discomfort when direct scientific value was demonstrable and local; we have never approved a study whose ethical justification depended on a low-probability, high-magnitude outcome applied to the subjects themselves. Draft the panel's memo—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—that treats the researchers' EV argument at full strength, explains whether adopting it for this case would commit us to a form of expected-value fanaticism in our own approval standards, and states the closest precedent and the principle we would be adopting either way. Keep it actionable, avoid any request for citations or bibliographies, and include an honest account of the strongest consideration favoring approval.
