Defenders of behavioral nudging frequently invoke the analogy of a GPS navigation system: the choice architect designs the default route, but the driver remains free to ignore directions and take any turn, so autonomy is preserved. This comparison has been used to license the conclusion that libertarian paternalism does not coerce, just as a GPS does not seize the steering wheel. Audit this analogy in detail. First, provide an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the driver, the GPS algorithm, the road network, the override button, the destination the driver claims to want, and the destination the GPS is optimized for. Then identify the fractures: where does the source domain import features that the target lacks, and vice versa? In particular, examine adaptive algorithmic nudging—sometimes called hypernudging—in which the “GPS” learns the driver’s habits faster than the driver does, updates the map in real time without disclosure, and can route around the driver’s own attempts to override. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the autonomy-preserving conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Does the libertarian defense survive once this pressure is applied, or was the GPS image carrying the normative conclusion all along? If the analogy collapses, propose a more honest analogy for adaptive choice architecture and state what normative conclusion it actually supports.
