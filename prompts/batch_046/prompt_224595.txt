At a discussion group I belong to, two members have been arguing over whether we genuinely know anything about the distant past, or whether source criticism reduces ancient history to well-constructed fiction.

Maya said that source criticism shows we cannot trust distant accounts: every manuscript is a copy of a copy made centuries later; authors like Herodotus and Thucydides relied on hearsay, partisan witnesses, and selective memory; and modern methods—such as identifying interpolations or applying the criterion of embarrassment—only compare unreliable layers against one another. She added that archaeology is epistemically circular: we identify artifacts and sites using textual descriptions, then use those artifacts to confirm the texts, so material evidence never breaks out of the textual loop.

David replied that this skepticism is self-undermining. Material evidence—coins, inscriptions, stratigraphic layers—provides checks that do not depend on manuscript transmission. Even within texts, Thucydides deliberately rejected mythic narrative in favor of eyewitness rigor, and source-critical techniques like detecting contradictory traditions or embarrassing details can recover that rigor without circularity. He also cited cross-cultural attestation: when separate traditions with no shared transmission chain record similar events or figures, shared corruption is less plausible than a shared cause.

Maya countered that cross-cultural comparison merely relocates the problem. Each tradition has its own fragile transmission history, and judging them independent requires assuming that corruption patterns are uncorrelated—a probabilistic premise, not a proof. She concluded that distant history is best understood as a coherent explanatory model, not as direct knowledge, and that treating it as factual knowledge mistakes explanatory usefulness for epistemic certainty.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of Maya's and David's central claims, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. What does each side miss? In particular, is Maya's circularity charge a genuine epistemic problem specific to historiography, or just an instance of how all indirect knowledge works? Is there a defensible middle position that captures what is right in both views?
