For a graduate seminar on Indian philosophy, construct a decision tree that sorts positions on whether Jain anekāntavāda (the doctrine of many-sidedness) permits assertoric moral judgment despite its commitment to epistemic humility. Build the tree from logical space, not from a list of scholars: begin with whether the seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī) applies to ethical claims at all; then branch on whether syādvāda (conditional predication using perhaps) operates as a modal hedge on the speaker's certainty or as an ontological thesis about partial truth; then branch on whether kevalajñāna (omniscient cognition) functions as a conceptually available regulative ideal that licenses non-omniscient agents to assert. At each leaf place either a named position if a recognized Jain tradition holds it, or mark it unoccupied with a brief diagnosis (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored), and for each occupied leaf state in one sentence its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: items 1-3 should state the three successive branch questions; item 4 should enumerate the occupied and unoccupied leaves with their commitments; item 5 should flag inseparable pairs and state what argument or counterexample would change your mind—specifically, which branch distinction is most likely to collapse and what consideration would lead you to merge two leaves or open a previously unoccupied branch.
