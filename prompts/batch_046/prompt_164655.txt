In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence we have been trying to disentangle what exactly is being asked when researchers invoke “the alignment problem.” Some treat it as a single technical challenge—getting a system to pursue the objectives its designers intended—while others insist it fragments into conceptually distinct issues: specification gaming under reward functions, value drift across successive self-modifications, the preservation of corrigibility, and what we might call normative deference, where the system must not merely match stated preferences but remain genuinely open to revision in light of them. Before I settle on any framework, I want a rigorous specification of what an adequate taxonomy of alignment must deliver.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate conceptual account of the alignment problem must be able to do to count as adequate—which phenomena it must explain (with the concrete case behind each), which distinctions it must respect (for instance, between misalignment arising from faulty reward design versus misalignment arising from instrumental convergence or from what we could call “preference lock-in”), which puzzles it must dissolve or explain, and which results or constraints from decision theory, philosophy of mind, or social epistemology it must remain consistent with. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or consideration; flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—for example, whether the demand for a unified formal specification is compatible with the demand that the account accommodate open-ended normative revision. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on extensive scoring of particular theories, though you may briefly indicate how the “value-loading” picture, the “structural coherence” picture, and the “corrigibility-first” picture fare against your list. Finally, reach a reasoned verdict: is the alignment problem best understood as one problem with multiple symptoms, or as several conceptually distinct problems that happen to share a name? State your verdict in a fixed, machine-readable form at the very end, beginning with “Verdict:” followed by a concise label (e.g., “unitary,” “plural,” or “conditionally plural”).
