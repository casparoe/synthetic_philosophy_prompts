I've been arguing with a fellow student in my normative ethics seminar about whether indirect normativity—roughly, the view that what an actual agent ought to do is fixed by what she would endorse after sufficiently idealized deliberation—can avoid collapsing either into direct act-evaluation or into an alien authority problem. Rather than continue talking past each other, we want to produce a joint adversarial-collaboration document that both camps could sign. The two parties are the Continuity defenders, who hold that idealized deliberation preserves enough psychological continuity to generate genuine, action-guiding reasons for the actual agent, and the Discontinuity critics, who hold that the informational and motivational transformations required for idealization break the identity conditions needed for normative authority, leaving indirect normativity either empty or externally imposed.

Please draft the document in the following structure. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—covering, for instance, that actual agents operate with limited and sometimes false information; that coherence and expanded information are at least pro tanto normatively relevant; that there exists at least one concrete case in which direct evaluation of an act and the verdict of an idealized deliberator come apart; and that framing effects in practical reasoning are a real psychological phenomenon. Second, a disagreements table with precise neutral formulations of each residual dispute, phrased so that each side recognizes its own view: for example, whether psychological continuity survives counterfactual knowledge-expansion; whether there exists a non-arbitrary framing of the deliberative problem that does not smuggle in the very norms being justified; and whether indirect normativity requires only "thin" idealization (stripping false beliefs) or also "thick" idealization (restructuring desires and framing commitments), and if the latter, whether that undermines continuity. Third, identify the cruxes—especially the framing-dependency problem (whether counterfactual endorsement remains stable across decision-frame variations, such as framing a choice as an immediate act-selection versus a long-term identity-maintenance plan) and the modal-proximity constraint (how epistemically distant the idealized world may be before its verdicts cease to bind the actual agent). For each crux, specify a concrete test case or argument both sides agree bears on it—say, a scenario in which an agent's hidden preference reverses only when the problem is reframed as life-plan maintenance rather than discrete choice—and record in advance what each camp predicts the outcome or implication should be.

Finally, separate clearly what is broadly settled in discussions of ideal-observer theories and indirect normativity from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on the topic, and do not present any contested claim—such as the assertion that idealization necessarily preserves motivational identity, or that framing-dependency always undermines normative authority—as though it were the consensus view.
