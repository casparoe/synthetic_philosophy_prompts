Consider the institutional practice, common in European research ethics review, of applying a graduated framework to animal protocol approval: experiments on vertebrates trigger full individual-level harm-benefit justification; experiments on cephalopods and decapod crustaceans, following recent legislative expansions of sentience protections, require intermediate review; while experiments on insects typically pass with only population-level ecological justification and no individual welfare assessment. At the same time, these same boards routinely approve conservation biology field protocols—mass trapping, specimen collection, or habitat disruption—that kill large numbers of insects for purely taxonomic or survey purposes, again without welfare review. What philosophical commitments about the gradation of moral status does this two-track pattern presuppose? Must it assume that moral status comes in discrete tiers rather than continuous degrees, or that ecological or scientific context cancels minimal individual status? Could a consistently gradualist view, on which insects possess a small but non-zero moral weight, explain both tracks without contradiction, or does the practice implicitly depend on an unstated threshold theory? And if the underlying view were replaced by a genuinely continuous scale of consideration weighted directly by sentience capacity, what specific procedural changes would have to occur in both laboratory and field review?
