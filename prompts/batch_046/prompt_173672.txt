I've come to accept a stricter replacement for the "man of his time" defence: when evaluating figures like Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus, we should distinguish structural inevitability (no alternative institutional path existed) from epistemic closure (the counter-argument was genuinely inaccessible rather than actively suppressed). I hold that for these three, the relevant moral dissent was not only present—abolitionist arguments within Jefferson's own intellectual milieu, anti-imperial critiques available to Churchill, and contemporaneous indictments of colonial violence reaching Columbus's circle—but was epistemically open to them, and that their failure was therefore personal epistemic negligence rather than historical determinism. The standard defence, on my view, confuses descriptive explanation with normative excuse and collapses whenever we can show that a figure had access to live counter-positions they chose not to engage. What would count against this? I want the arguments and evidence that should actually move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward: historical findings showing that dissent was present but genuinely unhearable given institutional position; conceptual work demonstrating that "epistemic closure" inevitably collapses back into presentism; results in moral psychology about motivated reasoning that apply symmetrically to modern critics; and whether my framework has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability, such that any counter-evidence can be dismissed as "just more closure." I also want an honest assessment of whether the view is well-insulated and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a thesis of this kind. Please distinguish what is settled about these episodes from what remains genuinely contested among serious historians and philosophers, and do not present a contested historiographical reading as if it were the consensus.
