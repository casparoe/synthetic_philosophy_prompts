I'm putting together a feature on Halden prison in Norway — probaly you've heard of it, the place where guards don't carry guns, inmates cook in proper kitchens together, and the cells are more like student flats than cages. The guiding idea is 'normalisation': life inside should mirror life outside as closely as possible, so people don't come out broken. Meanwhile the US model runs on mandatory minimums, supermax isolation, and private contractors running the show.

But I'm trying to work out what philosophical commitments are baked into that contrast — not just the politics, but the underlying concepts. Halden's architecture seems to presuppose that punishment is mainly about restoring someone's place in the social contract, and that dignity isn't forfeited by a crime. That sounds like a particular theory of moral responsibility, maybe one where agency is relational rather than atomic. But does the practice actually need that? Could a pure consequentialist justify the same layout just on recidivism stats, without buying into any fancy view of personhood? And does the American system presuppose that offenders are essentially defined by their worst act, or is it just a pragmatic risk-management strategy?

There's also a weird tension: Halden treats prisoners as future citizens, yet Norwegian law still excludes them from voting and certain jobs. So the institution seems to presuppose one view of civic identity while the legal code presupposes another. Is that incoherent, or can the two sit together?

I don't need a bibliography — just reconstruct the implicit philosphy the buildings and rules are running on, say whether it's consistent, and tell me what would have to change in the physical layout and daily routine if we replaced that view with a pure deterrence model, or with a restorative-justice framework that doesn't assume people have a 'true self' separate from their crimes.
