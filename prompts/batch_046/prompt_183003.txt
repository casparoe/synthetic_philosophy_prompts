The course covered induction, underdetermination, and formal learning theory. The exam question, reproduced below exactly as it was given to the students, asked them to evaluate what we might call the Inversion Thesis: the claim that Wolpert's no-free-lunch results do not merely parallel Hume's problem of induction but restructure it, by showing that the burden of justification shifts from the inductive inference itself to the prior distribution over problem instances that any learning rule must carry.

The exam prompt read:

Consider the argument that Wolpert's no-free-lunch theorems invert the structure of Hume's problem of induction. On this view, Hume asks how we can rationally justify preferring induction over counter-induction in the absence of prior knowledge about nature's uniformity; the NFL results, by establishing that all learning algorithms achieve identical average performance when summed uniformly over all possible target functions (in supervised learning) and over all possible cost landscapes (in optimization), show that any preference for one rule over another is necessarily grounded in a prior that cannot be justified within the system. The problem is therefore not the inductive step but the choice of prior.

Answer the following:
(a) State precisely what the NFL theorems establish about average algorithmic performance, and carefully distinguish the supervised-learning version from the optimization version.
(b) Assess whether the theorems apply only under a uniform distribution over all logically possible worlds, or whether an analogous result holds for any prior distribution when performance is averaged with respect to that same prior.
(c) Evaluate whether the Inversion Thesis correctly captures the relationship between NFL and Hume's problem. In particular, address whether adopting a non-uniform prior—say, one weighted toward smooth or local functions—resolves Hume's worry, or whether it presupposes the very inductive knowledge Hume questioned.

Below is the anonymized student answer, reproduced in full.

Student answer:
The NFL theorems, first established by Wolpert in the 1990s for both supervised learning and discrete optimization, prove that if you average error uniformly over every possible target function or cost landscape, then every algorithm—including random guessing—ends up with exactly the same expected performance. This means that without a prior bias, induction has no rational advantage, which is precisely Hume's point.

The Inversion Thesis captures this well but gets the direction slightly wrong. Hume thinks the hard part is the move from past to future; NFL shows that the hard part is actually picking the prior that makes induction work in the first place. So the burden does shift, but it shifts downward, not upward: we still need induction to choose our prior, which means Hume's problem is deepened rather than relocated.

That said, the theorems apply specifically to optimization procedures searching over discrete spaces, and the same uniform-average result holds for generalization error in supervised learning, so the two versions reinforce each other. A critic might object that in reality we do not face a uniform distribution—we know the world is smooth and continuous—so we can justify a non-uniform prior empirically, breaking the circularity. Once science has observed continuity and locality, we have independent grounds for weighting those worlds more heavily, which answers Hume for practical purposes. The only remaining philosophical puzzle is whether we can justify the prior without any empirical input at all, but that is a separate issue from induction itself. Therefore, while NFL highlights the importance of priors, it does not truly invert Hume; it confirms that induction works fine in our world and that the skeptical problem only arises in an unrealistic uniform distribution over all possible worlds.
