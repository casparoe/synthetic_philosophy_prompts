I've been trying to work out where the line falls between what morality demands and what goes beyond it — the actions where you'd be a saint to do them, but not a bad person for walking away. I don't have a philosophical vocabulary, just a set of gut reactions that feel partly consistent and partly tangled.

Here is how I call them:

- Jumping into a fast river to save a stranger when you are a strong swimmer and risk only a cold and exhaustion: this is required, not heroic.
- Doing the same rescue when you are a weak swimmer and will very likely drown: clearly beyond duty, admirable precisely because you could refuse without blame.
- Donating one kidney to a complete stranger: beyond duty; refusing is not a moral failure.
- Giving away ten percent of a moderate income every year to famine relief: good, but I don't think you violate an obligation if you keep it.
- Caring every weekend for a decade for a neighbor with dementia who has no family, at serious cost to your own health and relationships: I keep flipping. At first it seems like pure surplus goodness, but over time it feels as if you've built an obligation, yet demanding that anyone do this still seems wrong. This is the case I can't settle.
- Forgiving someone who gravely wronged you and then actively helping them rebuild their life: beyond duty, but I also feel it is "purer" supererogation than the kidney case, because the cost is emotional and self-chosen rather than physical.

There is also a pair that pulls against each other:

- Spending ten years in isolation writing a novel that might inspire strangers, knowing it will cost you your career and savings: I think this is beyond duty — no one can demand art.
- Working months of unpaid overtime to finish a colleague's critical project after they fall seriously ill, when you have no formal contract to do so: I think this is closer to required loyalty, not to heroism, even though the personal cost is large.

Is there one principle — perhaps about risk, prior relationship, or the kind of good produced — that sorts all of these? If the principle is simple, it will probably have to overturn one of my verdicts; if it saves them all, I want to see the complexity made explicit.
