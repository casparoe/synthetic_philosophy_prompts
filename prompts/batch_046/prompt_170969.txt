I'm a PhD candidate in history of philosophy, and I've been working through an essay for a seminar on early modern metaphysics with a somewhat unorthodox take on Descartes' cogito. A colleague and I were arguing last week about whether the Second Meditation establishes anything stronger than the bare occurrence of a thinking event, and I want to push a particular version of the thin reading. On my account, the cogito does not immediately license the claim that there exists a persisting mental substance; rather, it demonstrates only that any act of doubting requires an irreducible indexical position—a minimal subject-slot—without which the thought could not be owned or entertained. The move from this minimal position to the conclusion that the thinker is a res cogitans, I suggest, depends on premises about substance and real distinction that are introduced later, particularly in the Sixth Meditation, rather than being contained in the initial proof itself. I find this attractive because it separates the epistemic security of self-awareness from the ontological commitment to a Cartesian mind, but I worry I may be importing distinctions that Descartes would not have recognized.

I'd be grateful for your comments on whether this interpretation holds up textually and philosophically. Specifically, does the structure of the Meditations allow us to disentangle the proof of existence from the identification of that existing thing as a thinking substance, or does Descartes treat these as inseparable? And does framing the cogito in terms of indexical self-reference—rather than substantial existence—make it a more interesting contribution to contemporary debates about de se thought, or does it simply drain the argument of its metaphysical significance? Please feel free to point out inaccuracies, internal tensions, or passages that seem to contradict this reading; I am not asking for a reading list, just substantive critical feedback on the coherence and interest of the idea.
