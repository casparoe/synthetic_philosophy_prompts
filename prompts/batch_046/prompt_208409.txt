I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on population ethics, and last week I found myself refereeing a debate between two colleagues in my seminar—Marcus, a normative ethicist, and Elena, a philosopher of economics—about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and whether it genuinely escapes the commensurability problem between pleasure and pain. I’d like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange, because I’m genuinely unsure where the reasoning lands and I don’t want to misrepresent either position when I present it back to them.

Marcus defended the lexical version of negative utilitarianism found in recent literature: there exists a threshold of suffering such that, once a life crosses it, no quantity of well-being, however vast, can justify that life’s existence. He argued that this structure elegantly sidesteps the traditional objection that pleasure and pain are incommensurable, because the view never requires weighing them on a single scale. Once suffering exceeds the lexical boundary, comparison becomes moot; below the boundary, ordinary trade-offs are permitted. Elena pushed back in two stages. First, she claimed that setting the threshold itself smuggles in a comparative judgment: deciding that suffering at intensity S is lexically worse than any pleasure bundle requires implicitly measuring the disvalue of S against the value of those bundles, which is precisely the commensurability problem in meta-form. Second, she introduced what she called “sub-threshold aggregation asymmetry”: if I suffer a mild, chronic headache well below any plausible lexical threshold while simultaneously experiencing thousands of discrete, intense micro-pleasures, a consistent lexical view must either allow those pleasures to cancel the headache—reintroducing quantitative comparison—or deny cancellation altogether, which seems arbitrarily punitive to pleasure. Marcus replied that Elena confuses the lexical boundary with a zero-point: below the threshold, negative utilitarianism permits ordinary aggregation, but the aggregation is still asymmetrical—pain counts negatively, pleasure counts only weakly or not at all—so there is no need for a precise exchange rate. Elena countered that if pleasure counts weakly, then its weight relative to sub-threshold pain is still a comparative coefficient, and the claim that this coefficient avoids incommensurability is just hand-waving unless we can specify it without circularity.

They then turned to a hypothetical that I found especially sticky. Imagine a being who lives for one day: she experiences exactly one unit of suffering that sits just barely below the lexical threshold, and simultaneously experiences an enormous, distributed set of pleasures that, by any intuitive accounting, would constitute an excellent life. Marcus said this life is permissible, and possibly good, because the suffering never triggers the lexical veto. Elena said that if the suffering is barely sub-threshold, and the pleasures are enormous, then describing the life as “possibly good” requires comparing the magnitude of pleasure against the proximity of suffering to the boundary—otherwise the boundary is doing no real work. Marcus insisted that the boundary is categorical, not comparative: the suffering is either in the veto zone or it is not. Elena responded that categorical boundaries in normative theory are almost always proxies for comparative judgments we are unwilling to make explicit, and that without an independent justification for why the boundary falls exactly where it does—other than “this is where comparison becomes intolerable”—the view collapses into either conventional threshold utilitarianism or an undefended intuitionism. At this point they asked me to referee, and I genuinely could not say who had the stronger point at each stage. Could you adjudicate move by move: rate Marcus’s and Elena’s arguments in each round, identify what each is missing or conceding too quickly, say whether Elena’s “sub-threshold aggregation asymmetry” is genuinely distinct from standard aggregation worries or merely a redescription, and finally assess whether Marcus’s categorical defense of the boundary actually resolves the incommensurability problem or merely relocates it?
