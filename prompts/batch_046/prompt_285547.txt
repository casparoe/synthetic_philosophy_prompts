I am drafting a literary novel about emergent consciousness and distributed cognition, and I have been discussing Hofstadter’s well-known dialogue involving Aunt Hillary and the ant fugue with a well-read friend who studies complex systems. She offered me a confident summary that I am considering putting into the mouth of my protagonist: the ant colony should be understood as a literal single mind in which each ant functions exactly as a neuron functions in a human brain; the chemical signaling between ants reproduces synaptic transmission with sufficient fidelity that the colony achieves a unified subjective experience—what the dialogue names Aunt Hillary; the fugue structure of the text itself is not merely decorative but demonstrates that emergent cognition necessarily arises from any sufficiently organized interaction of simple units, regardless of substrate; and therefore denying consciousness to the colony is simply a form of biological chauvinism, since the argument treats the case as empirically settled rather than analogical. My friend insists this is a fair reading and that critics only resist it because they cannot imagine consciousness without a skull. I am skeptical, particularly about the claim that literary form serves as proof, and about the slide from organizational similarity to literal subjective experience. Could you audit this summary for me? Please distinguish what is accurate to the view, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation that a careful reader of the original dialogue would reject—especially regarding whether the argument is analogical or literal, and whether the fugue contributes anything beyond rhetorical framing. Respond in one tightly argued paragraph, no more than 230 words, without citing sources I could not access myself.
