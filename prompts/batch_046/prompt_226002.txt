After my partner received a diagnosis that will eventually erode decision-making capacity, I have been examining how advance directives actually function in clinical settings, and one institutional asymmetry puzzles me. A competent person can execute a document that binds future physicians to withhold ventilation or artificial nutrition even after capacity is lost and even if family members object; yet the same document cannot compel physicians to provide an intervention they deem futile or contrary to standard care—requests for aggressive treatment are treated as non-binding preferences. Furthermore, in many legal frameworks, an apparently content patient with advanced dementia who reaches for food is not automatically taken to have revoked a prior refusal of tube feeding; revocation requires a formal legal process or an explicit capacitated statement, not mere behavioral change.

What philosophical commitments about the authority of an earlier self does this institutional architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat negative autonomy—the right to refuse—as extending across temporal stages of the self in a way that positive claims—the right to demand—do not? Does it presuppose a specific view of diachronic personal identity, namely that the pre-incapacity agent retains veto authority but not directive authority over the later patient? Or can the asymmetry be explained without any deep metaphysics of the self, perhaps through a pragmatic division of labor between patient sovereignty and medical judgment?

Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, assess whether its parts are philosophically consistent with one another, and describe what clinical and legal practice would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with one on which the earlier self either has full symmetrical authority over both refusal and demand, or has no special authority at all and is replaced entirely by present-best-interests reasoning.
