I'm taking a seminar on aesthetics and social philosophy, and we've been working through W. E. B. Du Bois's argument that all art is propaganda—not in the narrow sense of carrying explicit slogans, but in the deeper sense that every formal choice, every use of color or rhythm or silence, necessarily takes a position within a social world. Du Bois insisted that the only meaningful distinction is not between propaganda and pure form, but between truthful propaganda and false propaganda; beauty itself, on his view, is a mode of telling the truth about human experience. I'm struck by a contradiction in my university's public-arts funding office. They require grant applicants to file impact statements specifying how the work will advance racial equity or community welfare, evaluated partly by social-science reviewers; yet the same office rejects proposals that are too didactic, insisting that funded work must preserve aesthetic autonomy and avoid preaching. Meanwhile, the university museum acquires works by Black artists almost exclusively under curatorial frames that present them as interventions in structural racism, even when the artists describe their own practice in formal, spiritual, or metaphysical terms.

What philosophical commitments about art, propaganda, and value does that institutional pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat social value and aesthetic value as separate domains that must be balanced against each other, or as the same reality expressed in different registers? Then tell me what would have to change in the funding criteria, review panels, and acquisition protocols if the institution genuinely adopted Du Bois's position that there is no art without propaganda and that the task is not to minimize propaganda but to ensure it is truthful.
