Next Thursday I present the central argument of my dissertation chapter to our philosophy of science colloquium. I defend a restricted form of the Self-Indication Assumption (SSA) against Bostrom’s presumptuous-philosopher problem. Rather than accepting Bostrom’s reference-class restriction—which I argue collapses SSA into a disguised self-sampling principle—I propose that observer-moments be weighted not by bare metaphysical possibility but by epistemic accessibility: the degree to which a cosmological parameter is in principle observable from that observer’s epistemic position. This preserves SSA’s predictive success in multiverse cosmology (it explains why we observe parameters compatible with conscious life without invoking a selection effect over all physically possible observers) while blocking the absurd prior that we should overwhelmingly favor theories predicting vast populations of observers, since most such observers lack any cognitive access to the parameters that distinguish competing cosmological models.

The audience is calibrated to be hostile in specific ways: three philosophers of science who regard anthropic reasoning as empirically unfalsifiable philosophical bookkeeping, two cosmologists who routinely deploy anthropic arguments in string-landscape work and know Bostrom’s Anthropic Bias inside out, and a Bayesian statistician who treats any non-updating manipulation of priors as methodological cheating. They will not be moved by intuition pumps about being “surprised to exist.” They care about empirical calibration, the measure problem, and whether my move distinguishes prediction from bookkeeping.
