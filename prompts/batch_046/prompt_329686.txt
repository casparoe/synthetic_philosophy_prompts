I'm delivering the opening remarks for a two-day workshop called "Lives in Parallel: Biography as Moral Technology," hosted at a university center with about thirty people in the room. The audience is deliberately mixed: classicists and ancient historians who know Plutarch's paired biographies intimately; moral philosophers working on virtue and exemplarity who rarely open ancient texts; and a small group of secondary-school teachers looking for practical ways to use biography in the classroom. I need a ten-minute address written in American English. The philosophical idea must do structural work, not serve as ornament: I want to frame Plutarch's pairing method not as a comparison that produces a winner, but as a technique that forces the reader to hold two incompatible moral exemplars in simultaneous suspension, generating what we might call calibrated dissonance rather than imitation. This framing should shape the welcome itself — it should explain why the workshop is organized around disagreement rather than consensus, set expectations for the sessions, and close with an invitation to participate that only makes sense if one accepts that moral learning from lives works through tension, not replication.

Please avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific editions; the specialists do not need them and the teachers will not use them. Do not present biography as a simple source of role models to copy — that misrepresents the structural point and will alienate the classicists. The speech should be accessible to the teachers and philosophers who do not work on Plutarch, yet precise enough that the experts recognize the claim about paired suspension as a real interpretive position rather than a generic platitude. Keep the tone formal and welcoming, not lecturing, and make sure the idea shapes what I can and cannot say in the room: for instance, I should be able to welcome disagreement without undermining the purpose of the gathering, and I should end by proposing that our task over the next two days is not to extract rules from lives but to practice holding exemplars in productive tension.
