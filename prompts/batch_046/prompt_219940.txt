Since my father's stroke three months ago, I have been living with the distinction between lost and masked capacities in a way that makes the philosophy feel urgent rather than academic. His neurologists maintain that his recognition of family members is not destroyed but masked by inflammatory interference with retrieval pathways; his disposition to recognize me, they insist, remains intact though systematically blocked. This has led me to construct a general argument about dispositions, finks, and masks that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to undermine the very clinical language I depend on.

(1) If a disposition can be genuinely possessed while every actual and nearby counterfactual manifestation is blocked by an interfering factor—whether an internal fink or an external mask—then possession of that disposition is logically independent of any behavioral, neural, or physiological pattern we could observe.
(2) If possession is independent of all observable patterns, then for any object and any proposed disposition, one can defend the ascription by postulating an undetectable interfering factor whenever manifestation fails.
(3) Therefore, admitting genuinely masked or finked dispositions renders every dispositional claim empirically unfalsifiable and explanatorily vacuous.
(4) But dispositional talk—whether about the solubility of salt, the fragility of glass, or the cognitive capacities of a brain-injured patient—derives its scientific and clinical value precisely from its vulnerability to empirical correction and its constraint by categorical bases.
(5) So we face an intolerable choice: either deny that capacities can be genuinely masked (and thus tell the neurologists that my father has genuinely lost, not merely masked, his recognition capacity) or accept that all dispositional ascriptions are compatible with any evidence.
(6) The second horn renders medicine and materials science equally empty; the first seems both cruel and inconsistent with the structural preservation of his retrieval pathways.

Where exactly does the reasoning fail? I am particularly reluctant to surrender (1) or (4), since both seem essential to preserving the neurologists' claim and the explanatory discipline of dispositions in science. Is the error in the slide from (1) to (2)—perhaps because postulating an interfering factor is not ad hoc but constrained by independent knowledge of the categorical basis and the specific mechanism of interference? Or does the distinction between systemic masks internal to the disposition-bearer and environmental finks external to it block the generalization? I want to know which premise is least costly to reject, whether repairing the argument requires distinguishing pure masks from degenerate finks, and what the repaired account predicts for cases where the categorical basis itself is temporarily altered but expected to recover.
