I have spent the last year reading popular histories of the Industrial Revolution and the so-called Great Divergence, and I keep returning to the same discomfort: every time a historian asks why Britain and not China, the answer seems to depend on assumptions hidden inside the question itself. I have come to think that there are at least four distinct conceptual puzzles tangled together here, and I am unsure which ones are genuinely open and which are merely confusion wearing formal clothing.

First, there is the problem of the contrast class. To ask why Britain diverged from China, one must first establish that the two were on comparable economic, ecological, and institutional trajectories around 1600 or 1750. But if that baseline parallelism is itself contested—if the societies were already structurally different in ways that matter—then the framing may presuppose a shared path that never existed.

Second, there is what I would call explanatory asymmetry. Most accounts treat Britain as the site of a positive breakthrough and China as the site of an absence, blockage, or failure to adapt. I wonder whether this directional bias obscures the possibility that China’s trajectory was actively sustained by coherent institutional logics rather than passively stalled by missing ingredients.

Third, there is the Needham question—why modern science and technology did not develop in China despite remarkable early achievements—and whether it belongs inside the Great Divergence debate at all, or whether it addresses a different causal layer, one about knowledge production rather than economic transformation.

Fourth, I have started to think about what I call counterfactual anchoring: the unstated threshold that determines how far we can push a counterfactual—if Qing China had faced Atlantic coal deposits, if British institutions had operated within Chinese ecological constraints—before the exercise stops being historical explanation and becomes speculative fiction. Different writers seem to operate with radically different, unexamined rules for this.

If you were advising me—not as a student seeking a syllabus, but as an independent reader trying to decide where to direct my own thinking over the next year—which of these four lines should take priority? Which are upstream of the others, in the sense that settling them would dissolve or reshape the rest? Which are tractable for someone without archival access or formal training, and which depend on empirical disputes that conceptual work alone cannot resolve? And which, if answered differently, would genuinely change how we understand the comparison rather than merely reshuffling the same evidence?

I am not looking for a list of books, scholars, or citations. I want a defended agenda: where the conceptual bottlenecks actually lie, how they depend on one another, and what criteria an outsider should use to judge which ones are worth a year of serious attention.
