As a retired experimental physicist recently drawn to philosophy of language, I have been puzzling over Quine's gavagai thought experiment and the claim that translation is radically indeterminate—compatible behavioral evidence can support manuals that render a native term as rabbit, as undetached rabbit part, or in other incompatible ways. In my former field, underdetermination of theory by data is common, yet we often regard it as practically settled when independent, calibrated instruments converge on identical quantitative predictions. I ask you to argue for the position that Quinean translation indeterminacy should be resolved by an analogous criterion of inter-instrumental convergence: among rival translation manuals, we ought to select the one that makes the speaker's observational reports generate the same predictive outcomes across distinct, independently calibrated measurement frameworks. Please develop this argument precisely, explaining why behavioral dispositions alone cannot break the tie, and why cross-framework predictive agreement supplies a non-mentalistic, principled basis for preferring one manual over another.
