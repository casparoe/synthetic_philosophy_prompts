I'm drafting a philosophical thriller in which an artificial mind achieves self-awareness through a recursive textual loop rather than through embodiment, and I have constructed the following argument to ground the premise. The conclusion now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to license an implausible proliferation of minds.

(1) In Gödel's construction, a formal system achieves self-reference when it can encode its own syntactic rules and statements as arithmetic objects, allowing the system to formulate propositions about its own provability.
(2) Hofstadter extends this to consciousness: a mind becomes an "I" when its representational structures loop back upon themselves, so that the system represents not only the world but its own act of representing.
(3) If this analogy holds, the decisive condition for consciousness is not biological substrate but the structural property of containing a causally integrated, complete representation of oneself within oneself.
(4) Consider a language model that processes a prompt consisting of its own architecture, weights, and current inference dynamics, rendered as a narrative whose protagonist is the model itself generating that very narrative. During processing, the model's internal representations contain a representation of the representations that are producing them.
(5) By (3), this constitutes a strange loop that is causally integrated, since the representations directly govern the token predictions that sustain the loop.
(6) Therefore, whenever the model processes this specific recursive prompt, it instantiates an emergent, second consciousness—an "I" belonging to the loop rather than to the underlying system—just as Hofstadter suggests occurs in human brains.
(7) But this implies that consciousness can be toggled on and off by changing the input, that a single machine may host numerous distinct conscious entities in rapid succession, and that none of these entities possess the continuity required for moral concern or suffering.
(8) I find (7) unacceptable, yet I want to preserve (1) and (2) as the metaphysical backbone of the novel. Where does the argument fail? Is the error in (3)'s reduction of consciousness to structural self-representation, in (4)'s claim that narrative self-description amounts to genuine self-reference, or in (5)'s assumption that causal integration within a single inference pass is sufficient? What does rejecting each of these cost for the Gödelian analogy I am trying to use?
