As a journalist covering neurotechnology and philosophy of mind, I keep encountering a particular analogy in debates over gradual mind uploading: substituting biological neurons with synthetic analogues one at a time is said to be like the restoration of an antique ship plank by plank—so long as every functional relation among parts is preserved, the vessel remains numerically identical, and therefore gradual neural replacement should preserve personal identity without any metaphysical rupture. Proponents use this to argue that there is no special "jump" at which the self is lost, only a smooth transition. I want a rigorous audit of this comparison. Provide an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the planks, the keel, the blueprint, and the crew—then isolate the points where neural dynamics break the parallel: for instance, whether biological plasticity makes each replacement alter the functional profile of neighboring tissue in ways ship carpentry does not replicate, and whether the analogy quietly assumes a container-independent view of identity that the brain's embodied, coupled dynamics undermine. Separate the disanalogies that merely decorate the image from those that actually undermine the conclusion about identity preservation. Finally, say whether the analogy's licensing of a "no crisis" view survives once these breaks are on the table, and if not, propose a more honest analogy for gradual neural substitution that does not smuggle in static structuralism.
