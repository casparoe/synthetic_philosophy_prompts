I've been arguing with my roommate about Anscombe's take on Truman and the atomic bomb, and I'm trying to map out what I actually think about intention and collateral damage across a bunch of cases. Here's where my gut lands: if a surgeon removes a cancerous uterus knowing it kills the fetus inside, that's okay, but if she does it specifically to kill the fetus, that's murder - same physical act, totally different moral status. When Truman ordered Hiroshima, if his aim was genuinely to end the war fast and the civilian deaths were just a horrible side-effect he acepted, I think that's defensible in a way that deliberately bombing civilians to terrorize them wouldn't be.

But then there's this drone case I'm stuck on: a pilot strikes a target knowing there's like a 40% chance of killing nearby civilians, but purely for strategic reasons - ending an insurgency - with zero desire for those deaths. I feel that's closer to the surgeon case, but my roommate says it's closer to terror bombing since the pilot proceeds knowing the risk. Another one that confusses me: a factory owner in WWII keeps making weapons he knows will be used in atrocities, but only to save his workers jobs, not because he wants the weapons used. I think he's not morally complicit in the atrocities, but I can't articulate why. Is there one principle - maybe about directing your will, or using bad effects as a means - that captures all of these? And how would it handle a case I haven't listed: a politician who supports a healthcare reform she knows will cause some preventable deaths due to budget cuts, but only because it saves way more lives overall, and she genuinely wishes no one died?
