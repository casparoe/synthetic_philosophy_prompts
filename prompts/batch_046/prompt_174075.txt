Write a dialogue between two people—Mara and Lee—sitting over coffe arguing about whether weakness of will is basically hyperbolic discounting wearing a philosophical trenchcoat. Mara buys the intertemporal-bargaining story (Ainslie-style picoeconomics): your present and future selves are like separate bargainers, and akrasia is just the present self defecting from a deal because the discount curve is hyperbolic, not exponetial. Lee thinks that misses the point entirely: weakness of will isn't just a preference reversal, it's failing to carry out a plan you've already rationally locked in, and hyperbolic curves only redescribe the symptom without explaining why the agent feels specifically betrayed by their own hand rather than just outvoted by another self.

Have them work through one shared case—someone who sets a strict morning writing schedule, installs an app that locks the internet for two hours, but still manually disables the lock to scroll news, then sits there feeling not relieved but weirdly ashamed, like they violated their own prior filter on options. Mara should argue that the app is just a commitment device that raises the cost of defection but can't flatten the discount rate, so disabling it is predictable. Lee should push back that if it were only discounting, the agent wouldn't experience that particular shame of breaking their own settled intention; they'd just feel like the "present self" won fair and square. Mara can counter that shame is post-hoc rationalization to protect the narrative of diachronic unity... and Lee should have a real reply to that, not just change the subject. Keep it in the weeds—no Aristotle-Davidson 101 recap, and definitely no bibliography or reading list, I'm using this for a philosophy-of-action readin group that's bored of the standard summary. Make sure neither sounds like a stramwan and let the disagreeement stay genuinely open at the end.
