Having spent three decades as an experimental physicist studying phase transitions and collective phenomena, I have recently turned to philosophy of science and find the debate between Humeanism and anti-Humeanism about laws of nature more puzzling than the textbooks suggest. The standard formulations—laws as the axioms of the best descriptive system versus laws as necessary relations among universals—seem to miss something I encountered constantly in the laboratory: macroscopic order parameters do not merely summarize microscopic behavior but actively constrain which microstates are physically accessible, a form of downward explanatory dependence. I want to press this observation into metaphysical service.

Please argue for the anti-Humean view that this downward constraint is not an artifact of description but reveals lawhood as an asymmetric relation of nomic governance, in which higher-level organizational properties genuinely determine the modal boundaries of their constituents. In particular, explain why the Humean best-system analysis cannot accommodate this directional dependence without either circular privileging of one descriptive scale or collapsing governance into mere correlation. Deliver your argument as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion outside the list.
