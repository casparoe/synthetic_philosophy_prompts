I'm working through some notes for an informal reading group on reflective rationality, and I constructed the following argument to show that any agent capable of self-reflection is ultimately paralyzed from updating on its own reasoning. I can feel the tension building step by step, but I cannot locate the exact misstep, and I'm worried the conclusion is genuinely forced rather than absurd.

(1) A rational agent should accept a soundness schema for its own belief-formation: for any proposition p, if the agent comes to believe p through its reasoning procedures, then p is true. (2) Call this the Global Self-Trust Schema (GSTS). A rational agent that rejects GSTS seems to license itself to ignore its own best reasoning without cause. (3) Now consider an agent A that can formalize its own reasoning within a system S that includes basic arithmetic. If A can prove within S that GSTS holds—specifically, that for every sentence φ, Prov_S(φ) → φ—then by Löb's theorem S proves every sentence φ. (4) So if A can fully reflectively endorse GSTS inside its own formal apparatus, A becomes either logically omniscient or inconsistent. (5) Therefore, A can never fully formalize its endorsement of GSTS without logical catastrophe. (6) But if A cannot formalize GSTS, then A cannot apply it to any particular case: to trust a specific proof that q, A must invoke the schema on that proof, which requires having the schema available within the system that evaluates the proof. (7) Hence A has no rational basis for trusting any particular instance of its own reasoning. (8) If A cannot trust any particular instance of its reasoning, then A can never rationally update its beliefs based on its own thinking. (9) But rational improvement clearly requires such updates; therefore, rational self-improvement is impossible.

The conclusion feels wrong—agents do learn from reasoning—but every step seems defensible. Is the error in the move from (3) to (5), in treating informal self-trust as requiring full formalization? Or is it in (6), where I conflate having a global schema with applying it to particular local cases? Or is the argument actually sound, and the real cost is giving up (1) or (2)? I'd especially like to know which premise I'd have to abandon to preserve the idea that rational agents can maintain graded, local self-trust—trusting specific reasoning steps without triggering the Löbian obstacle globally—and what abandoning that premise commits me to about the nature of rational confidence.
