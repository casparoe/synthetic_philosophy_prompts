Student answer:

Pain asymbolia happens when someone feels a burn or cut but does not mind it and does not pull away. Philosophers use this to show that pain is not intrinsically bad. The idea is that the patient still receives all the sensory information about tissue damage—the same nerve signals that travel in anyone else—but the unpleasant feeling is completely gone. This proves that the sensory part of pain is just a neutral bodily signal, like a temperature reading, and that the badness is added later by a separate emotional reaction. So pain itself is never bad; it only becomes bad when that emotional layer is present.

One major objection is that people with pain asymbolia actually have a much higher pain threshold, so they do feel the pain but their brains suppress the negative response. Critics say this means the badness is still built into the experience, it is just being hidden by a malfunctioning suppression system. Therefore, the case does not show that pain lacks intrinsic badness; it only shows that some people's brains block the feeling of that badness.
