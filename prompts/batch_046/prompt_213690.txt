I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken to auditing courses in philosophy of science and epistemology at a nearby university. A colleague who teaches an upper-level undergraduate seminar on knowledge and context has asked me to assist in marking a batch of examination essays, as my background in scientific measurement is thought to be relevant. I should be most grateful if you could provide a rigorous, point-by-point assessment of the following student response, expressed in the usual British undergraduate classification (First, Upper Second, Lower Second, Third, or Fail) together with the specific comments that justify the mark; I am particularly keen to catch any subtle conflations between metaphysical and semantic claims, or any inferential steps that do not quite follow, which a hurried examiner might allow to pass.

The examination question was: In contemporary epistemology, pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism are often discussed together, yet they concern different dimensions of the knowledge relation. Consider a case in which a research scientist, Dr. Chen, uses a spectrometer to determine the concentration of a contaminant. In a routine quality-assurance report (low stakes), she checks the manufacturer’s calibration sheet and asserts that she knows the reading is accurate. In a subsequent regulatory hearing that may determine whether a factory is shut down (high stakes), she insists on repeating the measurement with an independent instrument and refuses to claim knowledge until the results converge. (a) Distinguish pragmatic encroachment from epistemic contextualism, explaining precisely what each thesis claims about the role of practical stakes. (b) Apply both theses to Dr. Chen’s behaviour. Does the fact that she seeks further verification in the high-stakes case provide decisive support for pragmatic encroachment over contextualism? (c) Identify one conceptual difficulty that arises if we treat scientific measurement protocols themselves as being shaped by pragmatic encroachment rather than by purely methodological norms.

The student’s answer reads as follows. (a) Pragmatic encroachment is the thesis that whether a subject knows a proposition depends partly on the practical stakes facing that subject: the higher the cost of error, the stronger the evidence required. Epistemic contextualism, in contrast, is the view that the semantic content of the verb “to know” varies with the attributor’s conversational context, so the same subject with the same evidence can correctly be said to know in one context but not in another. (b) Applying this to Dr. Chen, pragmatic encroachment tells us that in the regulatory hearing she genuinely lacks knowledge because her evidence, though unchanged in kind, is now insufficient given the elevated stakes; she therefore undertakes further measurements to reach the requisite standard. Contextualism tells us only that an observer in the hearing context would be correct to deny that she knows, but it does not explain her motivation to collect more data, since on that view her epistemic position has not actually altered. Consequently, her behaviour provides stronger support for pragmatic encroachment. (c) One difficulty is that if scientific calibration norms are themselves governed by pragmatic encroachment, then the acceptable margin of error for an instrument would vary with the practical importance of the measurement rather than with the intrinsic properties of the instrument or the phenomenon being measured. This seems to undermine the objectivity required for inter-laboratory replication.
