I'm on the oversight panel for our city's pilot autonomous delivery program — those little sidewalk drones that drop off meds and groceries. Last Tuesday one hit a cyclist at an intersection, not fatal but enough to break a collarbone, and now my panel has to issue a memo by Friday with a concrete reccomendation: suspend the pilot, restructure oversight, or keep running with new conditions. What's making this hard is that nobody can point to exactly who messed up. The navigation model updated its weights that morning based on crowd-sourced traffic data, the human monitor was watching six screens and clicked 'approve' 0.4 seconds too late — after the drone had already committed to the turn — and the manufacturer claims their contract only covers hardware failure, not 'adaptive decision-making under uncertainty.' I want the memo to take seriously the idea that this kind of distributed system creates a real responsibility gap — not just a legal loophole, but a situation where harm happens and there's no clear agent who could have prevented it with the information they had. At the same time I want it to address the 'moral crumple zone' problem: the human operator is the easiest target for blame even though they never really had meaningful authority to override the model in real time. I'm personally invested in this because my own pharmacy started using these drones last month, and I need our recommendation to be something we could actually act on, not just philosophy. Draft the memo with a clear bottom line and an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the opposite call. Please don't just restate the legal definition of liability — I want us to decide what responsability actually means when control is split between code, data, and a distracted human.
