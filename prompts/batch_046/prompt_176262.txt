I am in the middle of drafting a literary novel whose narrator works as a mid-level administrator at an institution that treats normative ethics as an internal compliance framework, and I need to embed within the manuscript a satirical institutional document—something between an employee handbook and a wellness protocol—that the protagonist discovers in a forgotten filing cabinet. Over dinner with my writing group, one friend who had recently sat through a graduate survey on moral philosophy observed that the sharpest satire of demandingness objections arises not from mocking them outright but from adopting their bureaucratic logic with absolute, deadpan sincerity. I would like you to compose this document, approximately six hundred words and formatted as numbered clauses or sections, in the voice of a compassionate but relentless human-resources office that has taken extreme consequentialist demandingness as its organizing principle rather than as a criticism of it.

The target should be the real conceptual tension at the heart of the demandingness debate: the idea that ordinary moral life is already a form of failure if it does not maximize aggregate welfare, that the boundary between obligation and supererogation collapses into a mere scheduling conflict, and that personal attachments and aesthetic pursuits are permissible only on a provisional, revocable basis subject to optimization audits. The document should parody the genre of corporate wellness guidelines—using language about productivity, residual capacity, and emotional hygiene—while quietly exaggerating genuine philosophical moves, such as the appeal that ought implies can being treated as a staffing issue, or the framing of family obligations as negative duties that must be continuously justified. It must remain readable to someone unfamiliar with the literature, yet rewarding to a specialist who recognizes that the exaggeration is fair and conceptually precise.

To keep the piece philosophically anchored without relying on named theorists, bibliographies, or citations to specific texts, please invent and deploy a fictional but rigorously coherent framework—perhaps a metric called the Residual Guilt Coefficient or a policy known as the Threshold of Permissible Attachment—that captures the pressure of insatiable moral demand on ordinary human life. Do not include quotations from actual philosophical works, reading lists, or references to living scholars; the humor should come entirely from the document’s internal consistency and its gentle, bureaucratic insistence that relaxation itself is a form of negligence. Keep the tone deadpan, institutionally warm, and utterly unyielding.
