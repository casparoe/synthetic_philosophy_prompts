Okay, so I retired from field biology a few years back—mostly macaque social dynamics—and now I'm trying to read philosphy for fun. I just got through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments and I think I mostly get it: sympathy is basically emotional contagion, right? Like when one monkey sees another hurt and starts screaming too. So the 'imparial spectator' must be Smith's evolutionary workaround for modern cities, where you're surrounded by strangers and can't actually feel their pain directly. You imagine this neutral guy inside your head to do the contagion for you.

But here's what's bugging me. If sympathy is really just contagious affect, then your spectator is only going to be as good as the emotional climate you grew up in. I studied troops where aggression was basically the norm, and the monkeys all 'sympathized' with the alpha's cruelty. So if Smith's spectator works the same way, wouldn't he just rubber-stamp whatever bad norms your local culture feeds him? Does Smith think there's some universal biological brake—like an innate fairness instinct—that stops the spectator from approving cruelty? Or is he secretly assuming every society eventually corrects itself, which seems like a pretty big leap for a guy who never ran a primate study?
