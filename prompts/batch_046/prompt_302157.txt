I recently decided to begin trying for a child, which has forced me to take Derek Parfit’s nonidentity problem seriously as it applies to climate change and reproductive ethics. I have settled on a specific view: because climate policies determine which particular people come to exist, no future individual can be harmed by our current emissions in the person-affecting sense—those people would not exist in the cleaner world—so person-affecting moral frameworks do not apply across climate scenarios. From this I conclude that our climate obligations must be entirely impersonal, and that this also dissolves any special climate-related reason not to reproduce now, provided any child born can be expected to have a life worth living. I want an honest stress-test of this position. What would actually count against it? I am not asking for the standard “future people can still be wronged” reply alone; I want the defeaters that defenders of this impersonal, nonidentity-aware framework themselves concede are serious—whether formal results in population ethics, intuitions about harm and identity across worlds, findings about persistence or welfare aggregation in other domains, or practical predictions about reproductive behavior and climate policy that rival views do not share. Please rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by retreating to adjustable “life worth living” thresholds? If it is well-insulated, is that insulation a philosophical virtue or a sign that it has stopped explaining anything? I would personally be most moved by evidence that the framework yields wrong predictions about actual moral judgments in non-climate reproductive cases, or by a reconstruction of person-affecting harm that does not depend on identity across possible worlds.
