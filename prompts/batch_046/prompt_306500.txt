As a doctoral student working at the intersection of political philosophy and EU constitutional law, I have been struck by an institutional tension that seems to carry hidden philosophical baggage. The European Union maintains treaty-level commitments to an ever closer union among its peoples, yet it simultaneously institutionalizes differentiated integration—opt-outs for the Euro and Schengen, enhanced cooperation mechanisms, and multi-speed arrangements—while addressing concerns about democratic legitimacy through dispersed tools such as national parliamentary subsidiarity checks and the European Citizens' Initiative, rather than through a unified European demos. At the same time, the European Commission retains an exclusive monopoly on legislative initiative, suggesting a technocratic, expertise-driven conception of governance, whereas major sovereignty-sensitive decisions still require intergovernmental unanimity in the Council.

This architecture appears to treat sovereignty neither as fully transferred to a federal center nor as strictly retained by member states, but as pooled in domain-specific ways, with democratic accountability distributed across national and supranational levels rather than anchored in a single representative body.

My question is this: what view of sovereignty, democratic legitimacy, and collective agency does this practice presuppose? More specifically, does the coexistence of an ever-closer-union aspiration with institutionalized differentiation presuppose an indicative understanding of union—where closer union simply means whatever cooperative arrangements currently exist—or a counterfactual one, where the present configuration is always measured against an unrealized ideal? Does the design presuppose a singular European people, a plurality of demoi, or something else altogether? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify where the architecture is philosophically underdetermined—such that rival theories of sovereignty or democracy could license the same institutional form. Finally, tell me what would have to change in practice if we replaced the underlying commitments with a straightforwardly monist alternative: either pure intergovernmentalism with no supranational initiative, or a full federal democracy with a single sovereign people and no opt-outs.

Please push back plainly if the premise is confused—if, for instance, the tension between differentiation and closer union dissolves once the correct legal doctrine is applied, or if the democratic deficit should not be analyzed through the lens of sovereignty at all. I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I want a conceptual reconstruction that does not depend on looking up specific treaty articles or case law.
