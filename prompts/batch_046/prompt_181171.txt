I am trying to determine whether the following interpretive and normative commitments about John Stuart Mill's political philosophy, especially regarding colonial India, form a jointly tenable package.

(1) Mill's justification for British administration in India was not primarily economic or civilizational in a static sense, but a form of developmental trusteeship: imperial rule is legitimate only as a temporary, pedagogical apparatus designed to cultivate the civic capacities necessary for eventual self-government, and it becomes illegitimate the moment it ceases to aim at its own abolition.

(2) This trusteeship logic applies symmetrically to domestic politics: representative institutions are inappropriate for any population—whether in India, Ireland, or Britain—that has not achieved the requisite intellectual and moral development, which is why Mill's domestic writings support qualified, weighted voting and express deep skepticism about universal suffrage unaccompanied by widespread education.

(3) Mill's advocacy for direct Crown rule after the 1857 uprising should be read not as a step toward Indian autonomy, but as an effort to render the trusteeship explicitly political and accountable to Parliament rather than to corporate shareholders; the change in administrative form left the developmental timeline untouched.

(4) I reject the 'exceptionalist' interpretation that treats Mill's India writings as a betrayal of his liberal universalism. Instead, I take him to be applying a single, staged theory of political legitimacy in which sovereignty is not a natural right but an earned status, contingent on demonstrated civic capacity, with the standard applied uniformly across colonized and metropolitan populations.

(5) At the same time, I maintain that this staged theory contains an internal instability: because the trustee—the imperial or domestic state—retains the authority to define and measure 'civic capacity,' the framework structurally permits the indefinite postponement of self-government for any group whose deficiency is defined by the very power that withholds sovereignty. I deny, however, that Mill himself racialized these criteria; I read him as treating them as culturally and educationally contingent rather than biologically fixed.

(6) Finally, I hold that if commitments (2) and (4) are taken seriously, Mill should have supported temporary, class-based disenfranchisement within Britain analogous to colonial trusteeship—restricting full political participation to those who had demonstrably achieved the relevant capacities—yet he never explicitly endorsed such domestic disenfranchisement, which suggests an asymmetry between his colonial and domestic applications of the same principle.

My questions are these: Do these six commitments hang together, or is there a hidden contradiction? Specifically, does (2) genuinely support (1), or does the domestic analogy in (2) actually undermine the colonial application by revealing that Mill never intended the capacity standard to be satisfiable? Is the tension between (4) and (5) merely apparent—because (4) concerns Mill's intended universalism while (5) concerns a structural feature he may not have recognized—or is it a genuine contradiction in his position? And does (6) identify a real asymmetry that forces me to abandon either (2) or (4), or can it be dissolved by arguing that domestic education made the capacity gap temporary and observable in ways that colonial conditions did not?

Please audit the package: order the tensions by severity, specify exactly what each tension commits me to giving up, identify which apparent conflict dissolves on closer inspection, and indicate the minimal revision—dropping or weakening one commitment—that restores consistency without destroying Mill's core liberal-utilitarian framework.
