I have been working through a short essay for a philosophy of aesthetics seminar on why audiences actively seek out artworks that elicit pronounced negative affect, and I would appreciate critical comments on the line of argument I am developing. The standard explanatory landscape—cathartic release, the safety of fictional framing, and compensatory pleasure—strikes me as incomplete because each treats the sadness as a cost that must be outweighed or neutralized, rather than as an integral part of the aesthetic payoff itself. In response, I want to defend what I am calling a coherence-calibration view: the enjoyment of tragedy derives not from the cancellation of sorrow but from experiencing one's own emotional response as structurally proportionate to the narrative arc, yielding a distinct meta-affective reward that I term affective coherence. On this account, tragedy functions like a rigorous emotional exercise in which the intensity of negative feeling is precisely matched to the formal weight of the events depicted, and the pleasure lies in recognizing that one's capacity for grief has been engaged with appropriate precision rather than being either numbed or overwhelmed.

To make this more concrete, I distinguish between vicarious grief, which is the direct negative response to the characters' misfortunes, and aesthetic sorrow, which is the self-aware recognition that one's vicarious grief is ordered correctly by the work's design. The coherence-calibration view holds that aesthetic sorrow carries a positive valence not because it feels pleasant in itself, but because it confirms a kind of emotional calibration: we discover that we can suffer in a manner that is proportionate, responsive, and formally integrated. This is why, on my view, the correlation between the intensity of negative affect and overall aesthetic valuation is not paradoxical but expected—the more precisely the sorrow is calibrated to the structure, the stronger the coherence reward. I also want to resist the common analogy between tragic pleasure and the pleasure of resolving musical dissonance, because in tragedy the emotional dissonance often remains unresolved at the level of content; the resolution occurs instead at the meta-cognitive level, where the audience recognizes that unresolved suffering can still be experienced as fitting.

Given this framing, I am particularly interested in feedback on three points. First, does the coherence-calibration view collapse into a variant of the control theory, since both seem to locate pleasure in the audience's awareness of their own emotional management? Second, is the distinction between vicarious grief and aesthetic sorrow phenomenologically robust, or does it risk being a theoretical artifact that splits what is actually a unified experience? Third, can the view explain the selectivity of tragic consumption—why we seek out artistic representations of suffering but typically avoid analogous real-world situations—without simply appealing to the absence of real-world risk, given that real-world suffering could in principle also provide opportunities for emotional calibration? Any suggestions for sharpening the argument, identifying hidden premises, or pointing out cases where the calibration analogy breaks down would be very welcome.
