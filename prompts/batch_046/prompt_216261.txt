Popular expositions of Benjamin Libet's experiments on the readiness potential often rely on an analogy: consciousness is like a referee in an athletic contest, the unconscious neural processes are like the player who initiates a play, and the conscious "veto" is like the referee's whistle blown after the play has already begun. The analogy is typically used to salvage a restricted form of free will—not "free will" to initiate action, but "free won't" to suppress it. Audit this analogy explicitly. First, provide the correspondence table: what in the neuroscience plays the role of the player, the play, the whistle, the rules, and the audience? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended normative conclusion: does the veto itself have an unconscious precursor (a readiness potential for inhibition)? Does the referee model implicitly treat consciousness as an external observer rather than an integrated part of the neural system? How well does the temporal gap observed in Libet's paradigm—the readiness potential preceding reported conscious awareness by several hundred milliseconds—generalize to genuine deliberative choice rather than arbitrary wrist-flexion? Third, determine whether the analogy quietly imports an agent-causal picture of the conscious self that the underlying neuroscience undermines. Finally, render a verdict: once the mapping failures are on the table, does the "free won't" conclusion survive on its own merits, or was the referee analogy carrying the essential argumentative weight?
