I'm trying to work out whether Plato's claim that intoxication can function as a kind of mirror for the soul actually yields a consistent principle, or whether it collapses into contradiction once you apply it to real cases. His basic idea seems to be that wine lowers the guard of shame and lets the true ordering of reason, spirit, and appetite become visible; but I'm not sure whether that means drunken speech always reveals hidden judgments, never reveals them, or reveals them only under specific conditions. Here are my own verdicts on concrete situations, and I keep finding pairs that look structurally identical yet pull in opposite directions.

Case one: A student who is normally reserved and polite gets drunk at a party and confesses, with precise detail, that he has been secretly envious of his best friend's achievements for years. The next day he is embarrassed but does not deny it was true. Verdict: this reveals a real judgment he suppresses for social reasons; the alcohol stripped away the mask.

Case two: The same student, on a different occasion, drinks heavily and declares that he hates philosophy, wants to drop out immediately, and intends to become a professional gambler. Sober, he is a committed philosophy major with no interest in gambling, and he laughs the statement off entirely. Verdict: this reveals nothing real about his commitments; it is merely appetite and inhibition running loose without the ordering of reason.

Case three: A public official who always soberly defends strict environmental regulations gets drunk among close friends and admits she thinks the regulations are economically destructive, though politically necessary. She never repeats this sober, and her public advocacy remains unchanged. Verdict: here the drunken statement reveals her true rational assessment, hidden behind political loyalty and social expectation.

Case four: A person widely known for gentle kindness gets drunk and becomes vicious, insulting strangers and voicing cruel opinions he never expresses otherwise. The next morning he is genuinely horrified, apologizes sincerely, and returns to his usual conduct. Verdict: this does not reveal his true character; rather, it is a temporary disorder that masks, rather than unmasks, the actual ordering of his soul.

Case five: This is the pair that troubles me most, because the two situations seem parallel yet I assign them opposite verdicts. In the first, a friend who always praises my artwork sober tells me, when drunk, that she has actually found it mediocre all along and was only being kind. I take the drunken statement as the true one, and her sober praise as social pretense. In the second, a different friend who has always been critically honest about my work tells me, when drunk, that she secretly believes I am a genius and has always thought so. Here I take the drunk praise as false—merely alcohol-induced sentimentality—and her sober criticism as the real judgment. Both involve a hidden belief opposite to the public one; both are revealed only under intoxication. Yet I treat one as disclosure and the other as distortion.

Given all of this, is there one coherent principle that saves cases one through four and resolves the tension in case five, or must I drop one of my verdicts? I am especially interested in whether the principle should turn on whether the revealed content aligns with long-term behavioral patterns, whether the speaker retains or reaffirms the belief after sobering, whether the content belongs to reason, spirit, or appetite in the Platonic framework, or on some further distinction I have not seen. Whatever principle you propose, please state it precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate.

Finally, apply the principle to a case I have not listed: a normally timid person who, only when drinking, consistently argues for a specific political position she never mentions in sober conversation. Over the course of several months, she gradually comes to defend that same position in sober debates as well. Does your principle say the position was always genuinely hers and alcohol merely removed inhibition, or does it allow that repeated intoxication gradually reshaped the ordering of her soul so that a once-foreign belief became truly her own?
