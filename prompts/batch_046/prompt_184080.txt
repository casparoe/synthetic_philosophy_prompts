I'm noticing a recurring explanatory architecture in debates about perpetrators of mass violence—particularly in the literature that contrasts situational explanations (authority pressure, bureaucratic routine, gradual escalation) with dispositional ones (ideological hatred, character pathology, cultural conditioning). The pattern runs as follows. A theorist assigns the baseline rate of participation—say, that most members of a given unit did carry out killings—to situational mechanisms alone. Then, to account for those who exceeded orders with gratuitous or inventive cruelty, the theorist invokes disposition or culture as the source of the "excess." But when accounting for those in the identical situation who refused or resisted, the theorist invokes the very same dispositional or cultural factors—moral independence, different upbringing, absence of prejudice—to explain the "deficit." The result is that situation explains the central tendency, while character and culture explain both directions of deviation. This renders the situational claim insulated: conforming behavior confirms it, and non-conforming behavior is explained away by the opposing category, without any independent criterion specifying when the situational mechanism should be expected to break down. Is there an established concept—perhaps in the philosophy of explanation, in historiography, or in social psychology—that names this specific asymmetry, in which one explanatory family governs the mean and the rival family governs both tails? If not, can you coin and delimit a term for it, carefully distinguishing the pattern from genuine interactionism (where situation and disposition jointly determine outcomes with specified weights), from general unfalsifiability, and from the fundamental attribution error? I am not asking for a bibliography; I want to know whether the carving itself exists, and exactly where its edges lie—what counts as an instance, what counts as a near-miss, and what theoretical cost is incurred by recognizing it.
