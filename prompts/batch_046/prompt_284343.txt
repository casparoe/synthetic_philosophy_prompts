The debate over whether transformative experiences—those that are simultaneously epistemically transformative, in that one cannot know what they are like without undergoing them, and personally transformative, in that they alter one’s core preferences and values—can be objects of rational choice has reached a well-documented impasse. Proponents of rational choosability argue that autonomy and discovery value can justify selecting an unknown outcome, maintaining that a chooser can rationally seek self-change via a second-order preference for transformation even without ex ante first-person phenomenal knowledge. Skeptics reply that rational choice requires ranking outcomes according to the agent’s present preference structure; since a genuinely transformative experience replaces the very preferences that generate the ranking, the chooser lacks normative standing to evaluate the option, reducing the decision to an unreasoned leap rather than a justified selection. Both positions are now thoroughly rehearsed, and recent contributions largely vary only in the choice of examples rather than in argumentative structure.

My hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about decision theory but about a concealed disagreement in the philosophy of mind: specifically, whether the phenomenal concepts required to represent the transformed state are strictly non-transferable—available only through direct instantiation—or whether they can be approximated through high-fidelity testimony, simulation, and conceptual decomposition. The proponents implicitly treat some approximate conceptual access as available, which allows them to maintain that the chooser possesses sufficient information for rational evaluation; the skeptics implicitly treat phenomenal concepts as indexically locked to the subject who instantiates them, which renders the knowledge gap absolute and therefore undermines any ex ante preference ordering. Yet I am unsure whether this diagnosis holds, or whether the disagreement is instead verbal—turning on whether “rational choice” means instrumentally rational relative to current preferences or all-things-considered rational across temporal stages—or whether the sides are answering different questions altogether, one framed within decision-theoretic models of agency and the other within epistemic models of self-knowledge.

Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually commits to regarding preference stability, phenomenal knowledge, and the conditions for rational agency. Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work—for instance, whether intertemporal preference consistency is a necessary rational constraint, or whether phenomenal concept acquisition admits a non-first-person pathway? Are the sides talking past one another because one addresses the norms of choice and the other addresses the epistemology of first-person access? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction between transformation-types (those altering evaluative frameworks versus those altering only phenomenal inventories), an empirical result about concept acquisition, a reformulation of the rational-choice framework that internalizes preference change as a parameter, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the dispute ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about personal identity?
