I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether Kierkegaard’s attack on “the public” and the press in his 1846 writings on the present age and in the related discourses on the crowd and the individual is still philosophically alive, or whether it collapses into a generic elitism once you stop treating 1840s Danish newspapers as a special case. We kept circling one question: is “the public” for Kierkegaard essentially a quantitative idea—more people equals less responsibility—or is it a qualitative structure of abstraction that could exist even with few participants? I want to settle this for a small graduate philosophy seminar I’m running, so I need a diagnostic instrument rather than a lecture.

Please construct a decision tree that sorts interpretive positions on Kierkegaard’s diagnosis of the press and the public. Start with the claim that modern public discourse involves some form of anonymity or abstraction. The first branch should ask whether this anonymity is primarily a function of scale (numerical magnitude) or of structural mediation (the press, or any intermediary mechanism, as an intermediary that dissolves direct relation). From there, build branches that ask: does the press create the public ex nihilo, or does it merely accelerate a tendency already present in any mediated relation? Is the core harm the dilution of passionate commitment, or the substitution of reflective observation for decisive action? Is withdrawal from the public a necessary condition for authentic selfhood, or can one engage publicly without being leveled?

Each leaf should be either a named interpretive stance—such as the “leveling” reading, the “phantom-public” reading, the “existential-withdrawal” reading—or an unnamed combination, with a one-sentence note on why it might be unoccupied (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). Include, specifically, an unnamed branch for an “algorithmic-leveling” thesis that applies Kierkegaard’s leveling mechanism to recommendation-based aggregation without altering his ontology of the self, and flag whether your tree can actually separate this from the standard leveling reading. Where your tree cannot separate two positions—say, a reading that treats the press as purely instrumental and one that treats it as constitutive but still quantitative—flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than forcing a split.

The audience is graduate students in philosophy or intellectual history; keep the tree rigorous but do not require specialist knowledge of nineteenth-century Danish publishing history to answer a node. Finally, separate what is broadly settled in the scholarship—e.g., that Kierkegaard distinguishes the crowd from the individual, that he sees the press as facilitating abstraction—from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether his critique applies to non-print media, whether it presupposes an impossible ideal of face-to-face community, or whether the “phantom-public” reading overstates the press’s creative power. Do not present any contested interpretive claim as the consensus view.
