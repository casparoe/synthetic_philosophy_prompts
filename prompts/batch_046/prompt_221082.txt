Read E.B. White's Charlotte's Web through the lens of contemporary animal ethics, specifically the comparative analysis of meat consumption, wild animal suffering caused by crop cultivation, and the differential impact of beef versus chicken production. The narrative centers on Fern's refusal to allow her father to kill the runt piglet Wilbur, Charlotte's subsequent efforts to save him from slaughter by writing messages in her web, and the farm's ultimate commodification of Wilbur at the county fair. Take these plot elements seriously alongside the philosophical aggregation problem: sustaining Wilbur to adulthood requires grain and crop acreage that indirectly kills wild rodents, birds, and insects during planting and harvest, and some ethical analyses suggest that this indirect wild suffering may exceed the direct suffering of pasture-raised beef per unit of nutrition, whereas chicken production involves more individual animal deaths but lower feed-conversion losses. Does White's intense literary investment in Wilbur's irreplaceable individuality—Charlotte's framing of him as an exceptional creature worthy of salvation—implicitly resist the kind of replaceability and aggregation reasoning that makes beef or chicken appear comparatively worse? Or does Fern's initial rescue, followed by Zuckerman's decision to fatten Wilbur for market, dramatize a tension between viewing animals as particular subjects of moral concern and treating them as outputs within an agricultural system? I want a reading that neither reduces the novel to an illustration of animal-ethics statistics nor distorts the philosophical comparison to fit the plot. Instead, show where the literary form—Charlotte's web as public rhetoric, the fair's spectacle, the spider's own mortality—illuminates something about wild suffering, crop-death externalities, or the ethics of replacement that philosophical argument alone tends to miss, and be honest about where the novel resists or complicates the comparison.
