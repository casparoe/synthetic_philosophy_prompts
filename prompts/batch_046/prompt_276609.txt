i'm putting together questions for a podcast interview with a historian who defends this somewhat niche framing: macro-level historical change is best understood as deterministic chaos, not branching probabilities, so the old proverb about the lost nail is real but hidden inside our epistemic limits — we cant predict which tiny perturbation gets amplified, but the amplification itself is fully determined. She usually gets hit with three standard objections: that this just collapses into vanilla determinism, that its unfalsifiable since we never have perfect initial conditions, and that it kills counterfactual reasoning. I want questions that bypass those deflections and actually test what her view predicts differently from a probabilistic branching model. Could you write a sequence? Start with asking how she tells apart a strange-attractor path in, say, the decline of the Roman Republic from a regular probabilistic cascade — and follow up with what kind of archival evidence she thinks could, even in principle, seperate the two dynamics. Then ask about the nail proverb directly: if the lost horseshoe was just a small perturbation inside an existing basin of attraction, doesnt that mean the kingdom was never actually hanging on that nail, which undermines the contingency intuition she wants to preserve? Next, instead of asking "isnt this untestable", ask what specific historical pattern would falsify her model — like long eras of stability despite huge micro-shocks, or massive shifts with no detectable small trigger. Finish with a question about whether deterministic chaos leaves room for genuinely open futures or just moves the openness into our ignorance, and press her on what practical difference that makes for policy decisions that might be the nail for some later outcome.
