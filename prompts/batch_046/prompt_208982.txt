I keep encountering a particular stall-point in discussions of valid consent—particularly in biomedical ethics and contract law—that I cannot name precisely. The situation is this: an agent gives consent to a specific intervention or agreement, with full understanding of what that intervention is, what it directly entails, and its immediate risks; there is no deception about the nature of the act, no coercive pressure, and the agent is fully competent. However, the agent is unaware of a systemic feature—say, a structural dependency or an institutional feedback loop—that lies causally downstream, affects the agent’s welfare only through the aggregated choices of other actors, and is not part of the act being consented to. Critics then argue that the consent is invalid because this downstream feature was not disclosed or understood. Defenders respond that consent concerns the act itself, not the environment into which it is inserted. The critics then tend to broaden the disclosure requirement without specifying its boundary, treating any significant downstream vulnerability as if it were a hidden feature of the act; defenders then retreat to an overly narrow conception of consent as purely episodic. What I am trying to isolate is the specific conceptual error—if it is one—of classifying systemic, environmentally mediated vulnerability as an internal defect of consent (akin to deception about the act), rather than as an external risk that makes consent dangerous but not necessarily invalid. Does this misclassification have an established name in the literature on consent? And if not, can you define the distinction sharply—distinguishing it from ordinary failure of disclosure, from deception by omission about the act’s nature, and from coercion—together with boundary conditions that would tell us when such downstream opacity genuinely vitiates consent rather than merely rendering it imprudent?
