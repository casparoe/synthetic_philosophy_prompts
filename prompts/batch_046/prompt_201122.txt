I'm moderating a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a refined account of superrationality in one-shot prisoner's dilemmas. On her view, cooperation is not guaranteed by identical rationality alone, as in the classic Hofstadter-style mirror argument; instead, it requires two conditions to hold jointly: strong common knowledge of the payoff structure, and what she terms reasoning-transparency—the capacity of each agent to access the structure of the other's deliberation without strategic opacity. She claims this blocks the standard asymmetry objection and explains why superrational cooperation remains confined to idealized settings.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that avoid having her restate the view. Include at least one question pressing whether reasoning-transparency collapses into a form of shared agency or team reasoning she wants to reject; at least one on how her account treats mixed-strategy reasoning when agents hold probabilistic beliefs about each other's rationality; and at least one on whether the strong common-knowledge requirement renders the view empirically empty. For each, provide a follow-up keyed to her most likely reply: for the shared-agency question, anticipate her insistence that transparency is epistemic rather than agential, and ask what distinguishes her position from standard team-reasoning frameworks that rely on shared deliberative frames; for the probabilistic-belief question, anticipate her appeal to a confidence threshold, and ask why that cutoff is not arbitrary; for the empirical-emptiness question, anticipate her response that the conditions are normative ideals, and ask what explanatory work the concept then performs for actual cooperative behavior. Keep the questions in the weeds—press on the mechanics of the argument, not on general philosophy of game theory.
