Ex-lab biologist here, recently got bitten by philosophy of science and now I see Goodman's grue problem hiding in every replication failure I used to deal with. Heres the pattern: someone maps a metabolic pathway in E. coli, then it fails in yeast, and instead of treating that as a falsification people invent a new predicate - "this pathway is only active in prokaryotic contexts with high NADH flux" - and frame it like they discovered a hidden feature of the pathway, not like they added a fence after the fact. Then comes "except under oxidative stress," then "except in aged cultures," each clause individually reasonable, but together they shrink the original claim to a tiny island nobody specified in advance. I see the same move in climate models and nutrition studies. Is there an established name for this specific trick - treating post-hoc boundary conditions as if they were pre-existing natural kinds that the law always implicitly referred to? Its not quite grue, since the predicates are functional not bizarre colors, and its not exactly Duhem-Quine because the auxilliaries are being reclassified as part of the phenomenon itself rather than separate assumptions. What exactly is it, how does it differ from a normal protective belt, and if theres no label, what should I call it and where are its edges?
