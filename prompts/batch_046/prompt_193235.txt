I've been working through some constitutional-design literature for a seminar, and I've noticed a recurring institutional pattern that doesn't sit comfortably inside the standard Ulysses-contract framework. Consider an amendment that does not flatly prohibit a future legislature from reinstating a particular policy—say, a progressive wealth tax or the dissolution of an independent agency—but instead embeds that option inside a multi-layered decision procedure. To exercise the option, lawmakers must first secure a three-fourths vote, then survive a mandatory ten-year cooling-off period, and finally win approval in a referendum conducted under a ranked-choice rule with a high quorum that systematically fragments the coalition needed for change. The policy remains legally available, yet the pathway to it has been redesigned so that assembling the necessary support becomes a coordination problem the procedure itself prevents from resolving. This feels distinct from the classic ship-of-Ulysses image, where the present self binds the future by removing the sirens' reach; here the sirens are still audible, but the ship's rudder has been rewired so that steering toward them requires a coalition structure that the decision architecture makes unstable. Is there an established term for this kind of "architectural" or "procedural-nested" precommitment, as opposed to flat entrenchment clauses, path-dependent lock-in, or ordinary supermajoritarian rigidity? And if the concept hasn't been named, how should one draw its boundaries—particularly against cases where procedural complexity is an unintended byproduct rather than a deliberately designed binding mechanism? I want to understand whether constitutional theory treats this as a distinct mode of democratic self-binding, or whether it is generally collapsed into broader discussions of constitutional rigidity without attention to the specific mechanism by which the pathway, rather than the destination, is obstructed.
