I'm running an upper-level ethics seminar with about fourteen graduate students, meeting for a single ninety-minute session, and I've been struggling to move our conversation about hypocrisy and standing to blame past abstract agreement. Everyone quickly concedes that hypocrites lack standing, yet the group never feels the friction between the desire to condemn and the risk of self-incrimination. I want you to design a structured in-class simulation—call it the Domain-Locked Blame Market—that makes participants experience that friction through rules and hidden information rather than through lecture.

In this activity, each student receives a private "moral ledger" card listing two assigned failures in two specific domains—say, environmental negligence and intellectual dishonesty—where one failure is marked public (known to the group from the start) and one is hidden (known only to the holder). The facilitator then presents three vignettes describing agents who commit failures in those same domains. Before anyone may formally cast blame in a round, they must "stake" one of their own failures by revealing it to the table; the staked failure must be in the same domain as the target failure and must match or exceed it in severity according to a simple ordinal scale printed on the cards. Casting blame earns "standing tokens," but the payoff is structured as a domain-specific reputation score: tokens accumulate separately by domain, and at the final revelation stage all hidden cards are exposed. Any participant who cast blame in a domain where they held an unstaked hidden failure of equal or greater severity suffers a "standing collapse" in that domain—their accumulated tokens there are annulled, and they are marked as having blamed without adequate comparative standing. The rules should include a strategic option to "pass" rather than blame, allowing participants to protect hidden failures, but passing too often should leave them with low authority scores and trigger a group-level coordination problem in the final round.

Please specify the exact card distribution (how many students get which combinations), the timeline across the ninety minutes including the hidden-card revelation moment, and the scoring mechanics that create real tension without using real money. The debrief should map each phase onto the philosophical idea: the staking rule should correspond to the requirement that standing demands non-hypocrisy in the relevant respect rather than general moral purity; the hidden-card revelation should correspond to the exposure that undermines prior blame; and the domain-lock should illustrate that hypocrisy is localized, not global. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—likely the first-person phenomenology of genuine guilt rather than strategic fear of exposure—and warn me what will predictably go wrong in the first run, such as students deliberately selecting minor failures to stake in order to game the token economy. Finally, include an honest note on which feature of the philosophical view the activity can only suggest structurally and must be supplied by the facilitator verbally.
