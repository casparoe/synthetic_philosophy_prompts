I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of technology and AI governance. In a recent seminar, a speaker summarized the "low-impact" constraint on artificial agents as follows: impact should be measured as the divergence between the actual world-state and the counterfactual world-state in which the agent was never deployed, computed across an exhaustive, fixed vector of physical and social variables. Because this is a purely descriptive distance metric between two possible worlds, it requires no prior normative specification of which outcomes are valuable; once you set a technical threshold epsilon on that divergence, you have a complete, value-neutral operational constraint. The speaker added that selecting epsilon is analogous to choosing a hyperparameter—purely a matter of engineering tolerance, not moral commitment—and that objections about hidden values confuse the baseline-selection problem with conceptual incoherence, leaving only computational tractability as an open issue.

I suspect this gloss collapses important distinctions—perhaps between causal attribution of the agent's effects and the dimensionality of the state-space, or between technical parameterization and normative framing—but I cannot locate the precise misstep. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what accurately captures the counterfactual-divergence proposal, what subtly misrepresents how its defenders treat baseline identification and variable selection, and whether the conclusion—that "avoid side effects" can be fully defined without an axiology—actually survives once those corrections are made. In particular, address whether fixing epsilon and defining the comparison class are genuinely value-free in the way the summary claims.
