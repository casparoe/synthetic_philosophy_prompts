Here is an argument I built for a seminar on practical rationality, and I now find it suspicious because it dissolves weakness of will entirely and, as a side effect, makes diachronic personal identity depend on precommitment technology. (1) Akrasia occurs only if, at the very moment of action, an agent holds an all-things-considered judgment that they ought to do A and yet intentionally do B. (2) A hyperbolic discounter applies a steep present-biased discount factor: as the temporal distance to a reward collapses, its present value jumps discontinuously relative to an exponential curve. (3) So the agent who at t=0 judges that abstaining (A) is best applies one weight set, while at t=1—when B is now immediate—the same hyperbolic function is applied and B is genuinely judged best; no single judgment persists across the interval to be violated. (4) Hence there is no judgment-against-action at the moment of choice, and therefore no akrasia—only intertemporal conflict between stage-preferences analogous to disagreement between two people. (5) The only way to preserve both a unified agent and the possibility of akrasia is to treat the earlier judgment as authoritative and the later shift as a deviation, which requires either adopting an exponential norm or binding oneself via external commitment devices that suppress the hyperbolic revision of preferences. (6) Thus either we eliminate akrasia altogether, or we accept that personal identity over time is not given but constructed—held together solely by mechanisms that prevent preference revision. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? In particular, does step (3) illegitimately conflate a change in preference weights with a change in practical judgment, and if I block that move, must I treat hyperbolic discounting as irrational rather than merely present-biased? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists in action theory and decision theory, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
