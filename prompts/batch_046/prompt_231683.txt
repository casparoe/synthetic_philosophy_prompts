I am drafting a literary novel whose protagonist is a cognitive neuroscientist, and I am constructing her interior monologue around a cluster of philosophical commitments I want her to hold about consciousness and temporal experience. Before I finalize the narrative voice, I need an audit of whether these commitments can genuinely cohabit, since the plot turns on whether her self-model is trustworthy. The commitments are these: (a) conscious awareness is assembled in discrete presentational frames rather than as a continuous stream, with the brain constructing intermediate contents—such as the illusory trajectory and intermediate hue in the color phi phenomenon—before they ever reach awareness; (b) there is no central archive or master record of experience, which means an Orwellian revision, in the sense of retroactively altering a previously stored conscious event, has no structural target and is therefore impossible; (c) Stalinesque revisions nevertheless occur, in that the brain can fabricate intermediate experiential contents and present them as if they were genuinely perceived in real time, without any retrospective rewriting of a prior record; (d) this fabrication entails a localized epistemic betrayal—the subject is genuinely deceived about the immediate past, not merely prone to later misremembering; (e) the temporal ordering of these fabricated contents is governed by narrative coherence rather than by strict sensory chronology, so that when two discrete stimuli fall within the same binding window the brain invents a smooth transition that never actually occurred; and (f) despite all of this, first-person authority about one's current experiential state remains largely intact outside specifically engineered psychophysical setups.

My worry is that several of these pull against each other in ways I have not fully mapped. Does the absence of a central archive in (b) genuinely block Orwellian revision, or does it actually make Stalinesque fabrication more pervasive and undetectable because there is no original record against which to compare? Is there a hidden tension between (d)—the claim that the subject suffers a localized deception about the immediate past—and (f)—the preservation of first-person authority—such that if deception is real it must erode authority more broadly than I allow? Furthermore, does (e)'s appeal to narrative coherence as the governing principle for temporal binding conflict with (a)'s discrete-frame ontology, or can coherence operate as a cross-frame mechanism without collapsing into a continuous-stream theory? There also seems an apparent tension between (a) and (b): if experience comes in discrete frames, one might think those frames function like individual records that could be rewritten, yet I deny that any rewriting is possible; I suspect this tension dissolves on inspection, but I want confirmation. Which of these conflicts are severe and genuine, which are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision—dropping or softening one commitment—to restore consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each exactly what must give and what that costs the protagonist's worldview, and indicate whether the set can be made coherent without rendering her self-trust either implausible or narratively inert.
