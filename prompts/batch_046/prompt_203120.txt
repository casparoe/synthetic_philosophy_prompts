I'm preparing a forty-minute session for my school's philosophy discussion group—mostly juniors and seniors who have read some Plato but no analytic philosophy—on Wittgenstein's private language argument. Rather than walking through the standard beetle-in-a-box or pain-diary illustrations, I want to build the explanation around a constructed case: a lone researcher who invents a private notation system to record the precise qualitative feel of synthetic textures that no other human has ever handled, and who insists she can apply these symbols correctly over months without any public check or correction. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself: the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the failure of this notation becomes clear rather than assumed; why this synthetic-material example carries the argument more sharply than the familiar introspective-pain case, and what features it must have and lack to do that work; the misconceptions the group will predictably form—perhaps that Wittgenstein is merely skeptical about memory, or that the problem is practical isolation rather than logical privacy—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a single question I can ask at the end to distinguish understanding the principle from mere discomfort with solipsism; and which part of the broader debate I should explicitly set aside at this level and admit I am not addressing.
