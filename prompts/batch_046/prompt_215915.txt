I'm giving a talk next Thursday at my local science-and-philosophy meetup about this idea I've been chewing on for months: consciousness isn't some magical inner glow or an unsolved hard problem, but basically a user-illusion like the desktop on your laptop — a slick interface that hides all the actual machinery. The brain doesn't show you neurons firing; it builds this colourful, sound-filled display and sticks a little character called "me" right in the centre, as if there's someone sitting behind the eyes watching the show. I think that "me" is just part of the interface too, like a cursor that genuinely believes it's driving the computer. There's nothing it's actually like to be the brain underneath, but this practical fiction lets us navigate social situations without having to compute every synapse. It's not eliminativism — the interface is a real pattern in the behaviour of the system — and it's not dualism, because it's all physical processes wearing a mask. The twist I'm adding is that this self-model isn't just for navigation; it's a debugging tool the brain uses to predict what other people will do, which means the "illusion" is actually doing serious work.

The crowd's gonna be split: about half are neuroscience undergrads who favour global workspace or integrated-information theories, and they get annoyed when people call consciousness an "illusion" because to them that's either trivial or means it doesn't exist at all. The other half are philosophy hobbyists who are convinced qualia are irreducible and that calling it an interface misses the point entirely — they'll say if I'm being fooled, there still needs to be a real "me" to recieve the fooling, otherwise who is the audience?
