I'd like you to adjudicate a disagreement I witnessed between two colleagues, Mara and Leo, following a seminar on the ontology of digital artifacts. They were debating whether permanently deleting a unique, procedurally generated virtual sculpture from a persistent shared virtual world can constitute a direct harm to the object itself, or whether any harm is necessarily indirect—falling instead on the creator, the audience, or the community that valued it.

Mara began by arguing that virtual objects are ontologically dependent on servers and code in a way physical objects are not; since there is no substrate-independent entity, destroying the underlying data is analogous to erasing a chalk drawing—there is no object that suffers harm, only a display that is interrupted. Leo replied that this applies an inappropriate physicalist mereology: virtual artifacts have functional persistence conditions defined by interactive roles and social recognition rather than by material substrate, so damaging that functional structure harms the object in its only possible mode of being. Mara countered that even if one grants functional persistence, any harm remains derivative—damage to a user's emotional investment, labor, or social status—so claiming the object itself is harmed commits a category error, comparable to saying a canceled meeting was injured. Leo then introduced the idea of counterfactual robustness: if an artifact's continued existence depends on sustained social and technical support networks, then disrupting those networks harms the entity itself, not merely its users, just as dissolving a corporation harms the corporation. Mara responded that counterfactual robustness actually undermines Leo's position, because perfect recreability from backups demonstrates that the specific token lacks historical indexicality—the instance is fungible with its copies—so destroying it harms neither the type nor any non-fungible token. Leo's final move was that users relate to tokens with embedded causal histories—who created it, when, within which relational context—so the harm is precisely to that non-fungible relational token; treating virtual objects as mere types misses their mode of existence, which is constituted by those very relations.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether virtual harm requires a subject with interests, or whether structural damage alone can constitute harm within a non-biological ontology. Finally, address whether injecting a criterion of modal robustness—the degree to which an object maintains its identity across possible disruptions to its substrate or instantiation—would change your verdict. Please answer in American English.
