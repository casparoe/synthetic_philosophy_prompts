Design a classroom simulation for an upper-level philosophy seminar (14 students, 80 minutes, one meeting) on the theme of competence without comprehension—specifically the strange parallel inversions of reasoning in Darwin and Turing. The core idea to stage is a “double inversion”: Darwin shows that adaptive functional competence (the appearance of design) can emerge from a selective process that possesses no comprehension of ends, overturning the natural-theological inference from order to intelligent reason; Turing shows that reasoning-competence (problem-solving, pattern-tracking) can be realized by formal computation that possesses no cognition or understanding, overturning the Cartesian assumption that reasoning requires a comprehending mind. Together they suggest that reasoning is not necessarily the source of ordered competence—rather, competence can arise without reasoning, and reasoning itself can be mechanically reproduced without comprehension.

The activity must make this felt, not explained. Do not lecture on the “double inversion” beforehand. Structure the session in two consecutive phases, both using only paper, cards, colored tokens, and a hidden rule-sheet for the facilitator.

Phase One (the Darwinian inversion, about 30 minutes): Divide students into small “lineages” of 3–4. Give each lineage a hidden selection environment—e.g., a deck of cards with hidden payoff rules that reward certain combinations of traits (color, shape, number) without revealing why. Each round, lineages submit choices; the facilitator applies the hidden rule and returns only binary survival/elimination feedback. Over four or five generations, lineages adapt their strategies and improve survival rates dramatically, yet no student should be able to articulate the actual selection criterion. The rules must be arranged so that students naturally invent “hidden designer” explanations—someone must know the rule—to account for their growing competence.

Phase Two (the Turing inversion, about 30 minutes): Reconfigure the group into a distributed “network” of 14 nodes, each student holding a simple local rule card (e.g., “If you receive red, pass blue left; if blue, stay silent”). Feed an input problem through the network—something like computing parity or sorting a sequence—that the network collectively solves with high accuracy, even though no individual node understands the global computation, the input meaning, or the output significance. Students should experience the frustration of local obedience combined with global ignorance: they execute correctly but cannot explain what the system just did.

The debrief (about 20 minutes) must map specific moments onto the philosophy: in Phase One, point to the moment when a lineage achieved high fitness while remaining ignorant of the selection logic, linking it to Darwin’s inversion of design-based reasoning; in Phase Two, point to the moment when the network produced correct output while every node lacked comprehension, linking it to Turing’s inversion of cognition-based reasoning. The facilitator should then name the pattern—competence without comprehension—and ask students to reflect on where they themselves inserted “hidden minds” into the system.

Make sure the prompt does not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and does not depend on students having prior knowledge of specific texts. The students should come away seeing that reasoning can be an effect or product of non-reasoning competence, not only its cause.
