I spent the better part of last evening in a prolonged and somewhat circular argument with a friend who works in legal philosophy, trying to settle whether hypocrisy undermines one's standing to blame in a normative sense or merely deflates the blame's persuasive authority without touching its validity. We kept returning to a distinction that the standard examples don't seem to isolate cleanly: on one side, cases where the blamer's own fault is structurally parallel to the target's—same kind of wrong, different magnitude or context—and on the other, cases where the blamer's fault is categorically different but symbolically resonant, such as a carbon-tax advocate who takes a single unavoidable flight versus one who holds a passive investment in fossil-fuel extraction. By the end of the evening we had accumulated a list of intuitive judgments but no agreement about which underlying conceptual question, if resolved, would actually reorganize the rest of the terrain.

Rather than asking for a survey or a bibliography—please do not include reading lists, citations, or references to specific papers, since I want to test whether the conceptual structure holds without bibliographic scaffolding—I want a defended prioritization of the open questions in the standing-to-blame and hypocrisy literature. Specifically, I want you to consider three clusters: whether hypocrisy operates as a strict defeater of standing, a scalar mitigator, or merely a pragmatic disqualifier in public discourse; whether collective or institutional agents can possess standing to blame in ways that make hypocrisy assessments structurally asymmetrical with individual cases; and whether the 'no hypocrisy' condition applies symmetrically to the blameworthy act itself and to the blamer's relationship to that kind of act. Which two or three of these are genuinely upstream—meaning that settling them would shift our evaluation of the others rather than running in parallel?

Please defend your ordering by tractability for pure conceptual analysis (as opposed to empirical moral psychology), by how much practical moral life—such as the design of institutional accountability mechanisms or the norms of public moral discourse—would actually change if each were resolved, and by specifying what kind of philosophical result (not an empirical study) would cause you to reorder them. I am especially interested in whether the collective-agent question turns out to depend entirely on the individual-defeater question, or whether they are independent branches that only appear connected because philosophers tend to use the same stock examples for both.
