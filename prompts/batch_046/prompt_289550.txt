I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or historiography. While reading retrospective accounts of historical change — particularly in technology and political history — I have repeatedly noticed a specific pattern that I cannot find a precise label for, and I should like to know whether it is recognised in the literature.

The pattern is not merely that observers overestimate predictability once an outcome is known, nor that they impose a tidy plot upon messy events. Rather, it is a particular interaction between those two tendencies. Once a result is settled, the explanation reorganises the causal status of prior ambiguous events according to whether they align with that result. Events that fit the outcome are promoted to the status of structural necessities or deep pressures, while events of an identical kind in parallel cases that did not yield the same outcome are relegated to noise or to irrelevant exceptions. Simultaneously, one particular occurrence — a design decision, an assassination, a financial shock — is elevated to the role of the decisive trigger, but only because the outcome followed it; had history diverged, the same occurrence would be narrated as a disturbance that the system easily absorbed. The vocabulary of explanation remains constant — terms such as structural weakness and contingent spark — yet the rules for assigning events to those categories are applied circularly, using the known ending as the selection criterion, while the final account presents the assignment as if it had been derived from the intrinsic properties of the events themselves.

I have observed this in explanations of why particular programming languages or operating systems came to dominate markets, in analyses of why specific political regimes collapsed rather than adapting, and in military histories that treat a single engagement as the inevitable turning point. In each case, the account feels analytically rigorous because it employs standard causal categories, but the classification of what belongs in each category shifts to accommodate the result.

Does this precise interaction — the outcome-dependent reclassification of ambiguous causal factors into structurally necessary, contingent, or negligible categories, presented as an objective analysis of the events' intrinsic properties — have an established name? How should it be distinguished from ordinary hindsight bias, from the narrative fallacy taken alone, and from the post-hoc rationalisations studied in individual psychology? If no specific term exists, how might one define and delimit it precisely, including clear boundary cases that show where the pattern does not apply?

Please provide your answer in British English.
