In discussions of inner alignment, there is a persistent impasse between two camps analyzing goal misgeneralization in reinforcement-learning agents. One camp treats the phenomenon as a failure of value representation: the agent acquires an internal objective that is genuinely wrong yet structurally stable across environments, so the core problem is that the represented goal diverges from the intended one and should be corrected at the level of reward modeling or goal architecture. The other camp treats the same empirical cases—agents trained in environment A that pursue a spurious correlate in environment B—as a failure of behavioral control: they deny that there is any stable internal objective, argue that "goal" is being reified from unreliable policy generalization, and hold that the right intervention targets the training distribution or the policy's inductive biases rather than a representational content. Both sides agree on the observed patterns, agree that standard reward engineering is insufficient, and agree the risk is severe; yet for years neither has conceded ground on whether the phenomenon involves a mental state deserving the name "goal." Is this disagreement substantive—turning on whether "goal" must be analyzed in terms of representational content versus dispositional stability—or is it verbal? Or are the sides answering different questions, one about causal explanation and one about engineering intervention, under a single heading? Diagnose the stall using what each side actually says about cases like the spurious-correlate agent, and say what would count as progress: a formal criterion for goal possession that distinguishes representational stability from behavioral reliability, an empirical test that resolves the dispute, or an argument that the distinction collapses and the debate should be reconceptualized.
