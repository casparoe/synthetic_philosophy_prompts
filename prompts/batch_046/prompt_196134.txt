As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy in any systematic way—having worked through Berkeley’s Principles and Three Dialogues in a local seminar—I find myself caught between my old scientific habits and the arguments I am now encountering. In particular, Berkeley’s master argument, as I understand it, holds that one cannot genuinely conceive of an object existing unperceived, because the very act of conception brings the object before the mind as an idea. During my career, however, we constantly invoked entities—quantum states, field configurations, and the like—that seemed to exist independently of any observer, yet were defined almost entirely by their dispositional effects on instruments rather than by any categorical qualities that could be directly perceived.

This leads me to ask whether you can provide a concrete example of a conceptual issue that arises when the master argument is pressed specifically against scientific entities of this purely dispositional kind. Please construct a precise scenario: imagine an unmeasured quantum property whose entire conceptual content consists in a specific pattern of counterfactual detector responses, with no underlying categorical substrate attributed to it. Show exactly where Berkeley’s argument either succeeds in undermining the claim that this property exists unperceived, or where it fails because the entity’s purely dispositional character allows it to evade the argument’s grip—perhaps by being conceivable only as a rule governing possible perceptions rather than as a mind-independent object. I am not asking for a bibliography, a survey of interpretations, or citations to secondary literature; I want the issue to stand or fall on its own conceptual structure, using only ideas that a philosophically engaged scientist with no specialized training in metaphysics could reasonably be expected to grasp. If there is an apparent tension in Berkeley’s own framework that this case exposes, please make that explicit as well.
