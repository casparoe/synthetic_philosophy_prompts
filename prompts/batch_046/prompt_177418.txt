As a doctoral student in philosophy of technology and philosophy of mind, I am working through a conceptual problem for my dissertation on distributed moral agency. Please describe the most important implications of the thesis of multiple realizability—specifically the strong version that denies law-like mappings between psychological kinds and physical substrates, as developed by Putnam and defended within the autonomy of the special sciences by Fodor—for evaluating whether hybrid socio-technical systems can qualify as moral agents. By hybrid socio-technical systems I mean decision architectures in which cognitive labor is partitioned across human operators and adaptive algorithmic subsystems that continuously reconfigure functional roles through feedback loops, rather than static artificial agents with fixed architectures. I want a conceptual analysis, not an empirical review of current AI: does substrate-independent functional equivalence to human moral cognition remain sufficient for ascribing moral status once the functional architecture itself is dynamically mutable? Address whether this reveals a tension, previously unrecognized in the standard literature, between the liberal interpretation of multiple realizability and the stability conditions required for moral responsibility—specifically whether responsibility attribution requires a fixed functional profile that multiple realizability explicitly denies—and whether we must therefore either restrict functionalist criteria to systems with invariant architectures or abandon substrate-independent functionalism altogether in normative contexts.
