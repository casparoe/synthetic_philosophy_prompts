me and my friend were arguing about epiphenominalism late into the night and she kept falling back on that train analogy — consciousness is just the steam whistle on a locomotive, it makes a lot of noise but the pistons and gears do all the real work. she used it to argue that the view is totally harmless: we can still talk about our inner lives, report pain, all that, because the whistle at least signals what's going on inside even if it doesn't push the train. but then i brought up the paradox of phenominal judgment — if my belief that i'm in pain is caused purely by the physical brain machinery and not by the pain itself, then i'm basically like a zombie giving a weather report about a ghost.

so how far does the whistle analouge actually hold? i want a correspondance table: what plays the role of the engineer, the coal, the tracks, the sound waves, and the person listening? then seperate out the places where the mapping breaks, especially around the part where i form judgements about my own consciousness. does the analogy smuggle in a hidden listener who hears the whistle, and if so who is that listener in the brain? and does her relaxed conclusion — that epiphenominalism is harmless because we can still have these reports — survive once you notice that the whistle doesn't cause the engineer to announce there's steam? or was the analouge carrying the whole argument all along? also if there's a better analouge that captures the causal inertness without importing that listener, what would it be?
