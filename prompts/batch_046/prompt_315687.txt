As a retired experimental biologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy in any systematic way, I keep encountering a particular analogy: animals—especially mammals with complex nervous systems—are essentially biological automata or behavioral robots, mechanistically processing inputs into outputs without anything resembling subjective awareness. The image is usually traced back to Descartes' comparison of animal bodies to clockwork, though it is now often updated with talk of brains as predictive-processing engines or closed-loop control systems. In both the historical and contemporary versions, the analogy is deployed to license a specific moral conclusion: if animal behavior can be fully accounted for by mechanical causation, then positing inner experience is superfluous, and treating animals as instruments raises no special ethical problem.

Before I accept that inference, I want an explicit audit of how far the analogy actually holds. Please provide a correspondence table mapping the source domain—first a Cartesian clockwork automaton, then a modern robotic or predictive-processing control system—onto the target domain of a living mammal with plastic neural circuitry, homeostatic regulation, and an evolutionary history. For each mapping, identify what plays the role of the gears or algorithms, the designer or clockmaker, the system's purpose or teleology, the boundary between system and environment, and the external observer who interprets the output.

Then lay out the disanalogies that matter specifically for the intended conclusion. Address whether the analogy quietly imports features that animals lack—such as being constructed for an externally assigned purpose, lacking self-repair, or having behavior decomposable into independent reflex arcs—while suppressing features that automata lack, such as evolutionary adaptation, integrated sensory-motor loops that reshape the system's own parameters, and homeostatic maintenance of internal conditions. I am particularly interested in whether replacing "clockwork" with "predictive processing machine" rescues the analogy or instead introduces representational capacities that undermine the denial of inner states.

Finally, evaluate whether the conclusion—that animals lack consciousness and therefore merit no distinct moral consideration—survives once these disanalogies are fully visible, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. If the comparison collapses at a critical point, suggest a more apt framework for thinking about animal minds, drawn preferably from biological organization rather than from mechanics. Please conclude with a one-line summary beginning "Verdict:" that states whether the automaton analogy, in either its Cartesian or modern form, genuinely supports the denial of animal consciousness, or whether it fails under its own disanalogies.
