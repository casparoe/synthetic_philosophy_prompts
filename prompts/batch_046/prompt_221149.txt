I have come to hold a view I will call asymmetric co-obligation: in genuine joint action, exactly one agent or designated subset undertakes a commitment structurally meant to bind the collective, while the remaining participants need not share that mental state but must recognize the commitment as operative for them without reciprocally adopting it. The collective proceeds not through fused shared intentions, which seem psychologically implausible for large or transient groups, nor through purely interlocking individual intentions, but through this one-sided normative structure that the others treat as authoritative for coordination. It is meant to explain why we hold groups responsible for outcomes that no single member intended, without requiring universal mental duplication. Tell me what would count against this position. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which intuitions about shared mental states, which conceptual results in social ontology, and which boundary cases — spontaneous crowd behavior versus structured institutional roles — it stands or falls with. Which of these would a committed defender of asymmetric co-obligation concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be dismissed by refining the conditions for recognition? In particular, assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, by allowing that recognition can be so tacit as to be empirically undetectable — and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice.
