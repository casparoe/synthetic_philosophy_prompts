A colleague summarizes the “interoceptive constructivist” account of emotion—associated with predictive-processing models of brain function—like this: “Emotions are not judgments about value in the world, nor perceptions of value analogous to color vision. Rather, each emotion is the brain’s interoceptive prediction: a best-guess about internal bodily change (heart rate, hormonal state, and so on) generated to explain an external event. The conscious feeling is just the minimized prediction error. Because the representational content is entirely self-directed—about the body’s predicted state—emotions are never literally about injustice, danger, or loss; they are only about anticipated bodily change. So appraisal theories are wrong to treat emotion as world-directed cognition, and feeling theories are wrong to treat it as passive registration of bodily states—emotion is active construction, but construction solely of the self, with no representational link to the world.” Something about this sounds both sophisticated and too neat. Is it a fair summary of the constructivist position? Please separate: (1) what is accurate to the view, (2) what is a defensible simplification a popularizer might make, and (3) what misrepresents the view or overstates its conclusions—especially regarding whether predictive models really deny all world-directed content, and whether the argument against appraisal and feeling theories succeeds as presented. Finally, does the claim that emotions “are never literally about injustice” actually follow from the premises given?
