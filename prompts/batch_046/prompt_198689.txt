As a journalist covering philosophy of science and ethics, I am drafting a feature on whether Kant's moral system can accommodate anything like direct obligations to animals, and I have arranged an adversarial collaboration between two scholars who have debated this for months without convergence. The first is a traditionalist who holds that Kant's ethics is irreducibly anthropocentric: rational nature alone confers moral status, animals are mere means in a strict sense, and our duties regarding them—found in the Metaphysics of Morals and the Lectures on Ethics—are entirely indirect, serving only to preserve human sympathy and moral character. The second is a revisionist who argues that Kant's teleological judgments about organic life in the Critique of Judgment, combined with his analogical reasoning about the subjective purposiveness of living beings, generate a non-derivative moral constraint against cruelty—one that is neither a rights-based direct duty nor a purely instrumental concern for human virtue, but rather a recognition that practical reason must respect the internal teleology of sentient creatures.

Please write the joint statement both scholars could sign. Deliver it as exactly five numbered parts, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: first, the shared textual and doctrinal claims both accept about Kant's corpus; second, the precise disagreement formulations, each worded so its own side would endorse it; third, the cruxes—those specific interpretive or normative sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one party; fourth, for each crux the concrete textual passage or conceptual test both agree bears on it, paired with each side's advance prediction; and fifth, a joint assessment of whether part of their conflict is merely verbal—about what qualifies as direct moral standing—rather than a substantive divergence over animals' ethical status.
