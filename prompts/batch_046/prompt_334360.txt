I've been working through a formal argument about collective remembrance that seems to destroy the very duty it starts from, and I want to know where the collapse happens. The background is the debate over whether communities owe specific remembrance to victims of grave wrong—something like Margalit's claim that thick moral relations generate non-substitutable mnemonic obligations—set against the pressure, in Rieff's therapeutic framing, to treat memory as a burden to be managed rather than a duty to be fulfilled. Here is the chain:

(1) If a community is implicated in grave injustices, it owes a specific duty of remembrance to each identifiable victim: not merely to record that wrong occurred, but to preserve that victim's particular status as wronged within the collective self-understanding.
(2) This duty is non-substitutable and non-aggregative. Remembering victim A with full specificity does not discharge the duty owed to victim B, and merging B into an undifferentiated statistical or symbolic category violates the specificity that (1) requires.
(3) Actual commemorative practices—public rituals, curricula, archives, monuments—operate with finite symbolic bandwidth. A community can actively maintain only a bounded number of specific victim-identities in its living narrative.
(4) So any large-scale injustice forces the community into mnemonic triage: it remembers some victims specifically and relegates the rest to symbolic compression (for example, "the disappeared" or "the six million" as an aggregate figure).
(5) But symbolic compression, by its nature, erases the specific identification that (2) demands; therefore for every victim who is compressed, the community breaches the specific duty owed to her.
(6) Hence every feasible commemorative practice necessarily violates the principle of specific remembrance for some non-empty subset of victims.
(7) A moral principle that necessarily generates its own breach in every feasible application is self-defeating and should be abandoned.
(8) Therefore there is no coherent collective duty to remember specific victims—only, at best, a generalized duty to acknowledge that wrong happened, which is precisely the therapeutic reduction that treats memory as manageable information rather than as moral relation.

The conclusion is intolerable to me: I want to defend a robust, non-therapeutic duty of remembrance, not collapse into forgetting. I suspect the error lies somewhere between (3) and (7), but I cannot tell whether it is an equivocation between institutional capacity and moral obligation, a hidden modal slide from "cannot do all" to "must do none," or an unjustified assumption that symbolic compression violates specificity rather than encoding it collectively. Which step should be rejected? Explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives—especially whether giving it up forces us into Rieff's therapeutic position, or whether we can salvage a scalable but still non-aggregative duty. I would strongly prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact.
