In a discussion about whether the French Revolution is best understood through class struggle, ideological conviction, or historical contingency, a colleague dismissed the first two frameworks by invoking the proverb that revolutions devour their children, treating it as proof that the Terror had no structural or ideological logic—only an accidental, self-consuming violence. I want to understand exactly what this phrase could be asserting. Please distinguish at least four precise readings: a structural-functional claim that revolutionary regimes necessarily generate violence against their own agents; an empirical-generalization claim that this pattern recurs across revolutions; a moral-paradox claim that the pursuit of radical virtue produces radical vice; and a contingency claim that the devouring was not determined by class dynamics or ideas but by unpredictable interaction effects. For each reading, state whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false; and indicate whether that reading actually supports my colleague's move—using the slogan to exclude both Marxist and idealist explanations—or whether it undermines that exclusion. Finally, map which of these readings are available to, or incompatible with, the class-struggle, ideology-centered, and contingency interpretations of 1789–1794, and say whether there is any coherent reading on which the proverb does genuine argumentative work without collapsing into one of the three frameworks it was meant to defeat.
