I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion and serving as the student representative on our university's Interdisciplinary Program Review Board. The board is evaluating a proposal for a new certificate program—tentatively titled "Reference-Class Forecasting in Religious Communities"—that would apply base-rate and outside-view predictive methods, drawn largely from historical studies of institutional decay and empire lifecycles, to living religious traditions. The proposal treats doctrinal commitments as implicit predictive frameworks whose empirical failures or successes can be tracked across reference classes, and it argues that this approach is methodologically neutral with respect to the truth of the beliefs being studied.

Several board members, including a sociologist of religion and a theologian, see this as innovative interdisciplinary work. However, I am concerned that the proposal commits a philosophical category error of precisely the kind Wittgenstein identified: it treats religious belief as a falsifiable empirical hypothesis rather than as a framework or form of life within which questions of evidence and prediction themselves take on meaning. At the same time, I recognize that forecasting methods might be used descriptively without reducing belief to a testable claim, and I want our recommendation to reflect that ambiguity rather than dismiss it.

Please draft a formal memo addressed to the board recommending whether we should approve the certificate, require substantial revision, or reject it outright. The memo should present a clear, actionable recommendation supported by reasoning accessible to non-philosophers; it should explicitly address the strongest counterargument—that predictive modeling need not collapse the distinction between framework and hypothesis, and that Wittgenstein's boundary is more porous than a simple prohibition suggests; and it should avoid relying on bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to obscure texts the board cannot evaluate. The recommendation should treat the case as a concrete institutional decision that turns on a philosophical issue, not as an abstract exercise.
