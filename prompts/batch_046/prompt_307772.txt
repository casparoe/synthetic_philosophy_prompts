I'm puzzling over a recurring move in debates about whether well-known traditional narratives count as history—think of the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus narrative. In each case, scholars confront details that are implausible by modern standards: divine intervention, magical artifacts, mass events that leave no material trace. The usual response is to distinguish a "historical kernel" from later embellishment. But I've noticed a further regularity: the rules for carving out that kernel are not applied uniformly. For a narrative with deep cultural or religious significance, even strong negative evidence—say, a complete absence of archaeological support for a large population movement—typically leads scholars to shrink the kernel to a smaller, unfalsifiable version (a "small group," a "local chief") rather than to eliminate it. By contrast, for a narrative of comparable age and form but less cultural weight, the same absence of trace usually yields the verdict "pure invention" or "etiological myth." The result is that two stories with identical evidential profiles can receive opposite judgments, with the difference tracking the story's prestige rather than any stated historiographical principle. The move is not mere bias: scholars often explicitly justify the smaller kernel as the "most economical" explanation, yet they don't apply that economy consistently across cases. Is there an established concept for this calibrated, prestige-sensitive reduction—the practice of preserving a minimal historical residue for culturally central legends while allowing peripheral ones to be fully demythologized? How does it differ from ordinary source criticism, from euhemerism, and from what we might call "motivated minimalism"? If there is no standard term, what boundaries would distinguish it?
