After a long argument with a friend about whether moral psychology can ever cleanly separate what we genuinely prefer from what we have been biased to prefer, I was grading a midterm for my moral philosophy course and came across the following response. The exam asked:

Consider the case of a professional who consistently avoids leadership roles despite high competence, attributing this to a genuine preference for collaboration over authority. Some theorists interpret this through the lens of adaptive preference—socialized aversion to power—while others treat it as implicit bias against one's own entitlement. Drawing on Frankfurt's hierarchical model, the Sen-Nussbaum capability approach, and recent work on structural preference formation, explain whether these are competing explanations or complementary ones, and whether any of them can claim to identify a true preference beneath the bias.

The student wrote:

The Frankfurt model says a preference is authentic when it aligns with a second-order volition—when you want to want it. In this case, if the professional wants to want collaboration, then the preference is authentic, even if socialization shaped it. The Sen-Nussbaum approach, by contrast, treats adaptive preferences as false consciousness: people come to want what they can have, so the preference for collaboration is really just internalized gender bias, not a real choice. Meanwhile, implicit bias research shows that people automatically associate authority with negative traits, which means the avoidance isn't a preference at all but an unconscious aversion. These three frameworks are actually complementary: Frankfurt gives the formal structure of authenticity, Sen-Nussbaum identifies the social distortion, and implicit bias explains the mechanism. Together they show that there is no single true preference—only layered explanations. The only mistake people make is thinking Frankfurt's second-order endorsement can rescue an adaptive preference, but actually Frankfurt requires that second-order volitions themselves be free from coercion, which Sen shows they never are in unjust societies.
