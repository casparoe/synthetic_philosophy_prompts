Compare Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness with the Cartesian theater model he targets. Rather than rehearsing the familiar contrast between distributed narrative and a central spectator, examine both models specifically in terms of temporal binding: the integration of disparate neural events into an experienced present with a unified temporal order. First, explain how the Cartesian theater attempts to solve binding by locating it in a singular functional space, the stage, and why Dennett regards this as a homuncular fallacy that merely relocates the problem of integration rather than solving it. Second, analyze whether Dennett's distributed drafts can genuinely accommodate the phenomenological structure of temporal binding, or whether, in eliminating a central stage, the model dissolves the experienced now into a set of parallel, uncoordinated temporal slices that never come together. Third, consider whether a modified multiple-drafts framework could include a binding draft — a specialized narrative stream that integrates other drafts without functioning as a Cartesian observer — and assess whether such an addition saves the model from dissolving temporal unity or whether it collapses back into the theater it was designed to avoid. Conclude by stating which model, or which modified version, better accounts for the reality of temporal integration without invoking an inner spectator.
