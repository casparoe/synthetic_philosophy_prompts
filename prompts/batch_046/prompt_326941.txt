In preparing a lecture on distributive arbitration, I have come to hold a stringent view of the Kalai-Smorodinsky (KS) bargaining solution: it is not merely one reasonable division rule among rivals, but the only arbitration scheme whose normative foundation avoids both arbitrary cardinalization and counterintuitive invariance. Specifically, I take the monotonicity axiom to capture a minimal rationality condition—if the feasible set expands in any direction that can benefit one party, that party’s payoff should never fall—and I hold that this principle alone, without invoking Nash’s independence of irrelevant alternatives or any interpersonally comparable utility scale, is sufficient to determine a unique outcome in standard cooperative settings. I further maintain that rival solutions suffer from identifiable, specific defects: Nash’s axiom produces invariance to clearly available alternatives; egalitarian rules presuppose utility comparisons that bargaining theory ought to reject; and utilitarian aggregation collapses strategic autonomy into an aggregate welfare function that neither party has reason to accept.

I want an honest map of the exposure of this position. What formal results, concrete counterexamples in non-standard domains—such as non-convex feasible sets, discrete goods, or multi-attribute bargaining with lexicographic preferences—and intuitive distributional cases would genuinely count against it? In particular, can monotonicity generate outcomes that seem manifestly unfair, for instance by rewarding a party whose underlying bargaining power has not improved, and have defenders of KS tacitly insulated the view by restricting monotonicity to convex, continuous, or single-attribute settings? Please distinguish the defeaters that a committed KS advocate would concede as damaging from those they would dismiss as resting on misinterpreted axioms or illicit cardinalizations; rank the threats from fatal to merely awkward; and assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If it has been so insulated, say plainly whether that is a theoretical virtue or a methodological vice.
