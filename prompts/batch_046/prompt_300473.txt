I'm trying to determine whether there's any defensible sense in which history has a moral direction, and I'm testing my own judgments for consistency. The abolition of chattel slavery in the Atlantic world feels like genuine moral progress, not merely a power shift, because the institution was later condemned even by societies that once defended it. The collapse of authoritarian regimes in Eastern Europe in 1989 also feels like a bend toward justice, but the democratic backsliding in Hungary and Poland since 2010 makes me think the arc can reverse, which complicates any idea of inevitability. The Nuremberg trials strike me as partly justice and partly victors' law, since prosecution was selective and many perpetrators escaped; I don't think this counts as history correcting itself, only winners writing rules. The creation of the International Criminal Court looks like an institutional attempt to formalize such correction, yet its near-total failure to prosecute nationals of powerful states makes me judge it aspirational rather than evidence of actual progress. The global expansion of women's suffrage through the twentieth century feels monotonic and therefore like real progress, but concurrent rollbacks of reproductive rights in several democracies make me hesitate: if the arc can lose ground in one domain while gaining in another, is there a single axis? Decolonization in the mid-twentieth century strikes me as clearly just in ending direct imperial rule, but its replacement by economic dependency and debt structures makes me unsure whether justice was served or merely transformed. Meanwhile, the dramatic reduction in extreme poverty since 1990, driven largely by non-democratic development models, feels like material improvement that should count for justice, yet because it occurred without democratic self-correction I am unsure whether it belongs in the same category as abolition or suffrage. So my judgments split between cases that feel like self-correcting moral learning and cases that feel like contingent shifts with good outcomes. Is there one principle—perhaps distinguishing retrospective moral convergence from institutional durability, or democratic agency from outcome improvement—that captures most of these verdicts without forcing me to call every improvement justice or every reversal failure? If coherence requires abandoning one of my judgments, say which and why. Please also apply your principle to a case I haven't listed: the gradual legal recognition of same-sex marriage across many jurisdictions over the past three decades, followed by recent legislative attempts to reverse those recognitions in some of them.
