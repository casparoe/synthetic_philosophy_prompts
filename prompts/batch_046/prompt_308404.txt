For an advanced undergraduate seminar in political philosophy—fifteen students, seventy-five minutes—design a structured role-play and deliberation that makes participants feel the specific force of Hannah Arendt’s distinction between tactical deception and organized lying: the kind that does not merely mislead an opponent but destroys the shared factual ground that makes political judgment possible. Do not open with a lecture, and do not name Arendt or the concept until the debrief; the dynamic should emerge from the rules and roles first.

Structure the session around a simulated transitional-justice body in a fictional post-authoritarian state. Divide the group into three roles: Record-Keepers (who physically hold document cards and may reorder, delay-release, or reclassify them according to an access protocol they collectively draft), Office-Holders (who must issue public statements and cast votes on reconstruction policy using only the records made available to them), and a Citizen Assembly (who evaluate the Office-Holders’ consistency but have no direct access to the archive). Give each role hidden, non-monetary incentives tied to group reputation: Record-Keepers gain status if the transition appears orderly and legally coherent; Office-Holders gain if they can maintain narrative consistency across three rounds; the Assembly gains if it can expose a contradiction.

Design the rules so that no participant is ever instructed to utter a direct falsehood. Instead, build in a structural-opacity mechanism: Record-Keepers control not the content of documents but the taxonomy for retrieving them, and Office-Holders depend on that taxonomy to verify claims. Over three twenty-minute rounds—crisis briefing, public statement, assembly vote—participants should discover that coordination collapses not because anyone lied, but because the categories for evidence were altered, making verification structurally impossible. By the final round, the Assembly should face a situation in which they cannot determine whether a specific event occurred, not because records were destroyed, but because the protocol for accessing them has made the question unaskable in public.

Specify the materials needed (index cards, sealed envelopes, a round timeline, a brief role sheet for each group), the exact division of roles, the reputation-based payoff scheme, and a timeline that includes a silent ten-minute writing period in which each Office-Holder must defend a position without consulting the archive. Then provide the debrief: map the first round onto tactical deception, the second onto the shift to institutionalized control of access, and the third onto the destruction of shared factuality; identify the moment when citizens stop trying to verify and begin to treat coherence as a substitute for truth; and address Arendt’s claim that the liar must eventually deceive himself. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time—Record-Keepers will likely start inventing false cards rather than manipulating classification, or the Assembly will disengage into cynicism rather than experiencing epistemic paralysis—and explain how the facilitator should correct this without breaking the simulation frame.

Finally, include an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: the historical scale of bureaucratic lying, the long-term psychological adaptation by which people come to inhabit the false world they have built, and the specific violence that accompanies the collapse of a common reality. Note also that the scenario should remain concrete enough to require real choices with small stakes, but should not depend on any specific historical archive, real-world transitional justice case, or named contemporary event.
