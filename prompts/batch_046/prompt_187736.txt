I serve as the senior risk-and-ethics officer for a regional public-health authority, and every quarter I must review proposals to deploy interim health measures—temporary vaccination schedules, adjusted water-treatment protocols, emergency screening programs—when the available evidence is suggestive but falls well short of full clinical or epidemiological confirmation. I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in about thirty minutes on any proposal, grounded in the ethics of belief: specifically, James’s framework for permissible belief in forced, live, and momentous options, and Clifford’s evidentialist constraint that belief should never outstrip the available evidence. The deliverable should be a step-by-step checklist with ordered questions, explicit criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, clear stop points where the procedure recommends against adoption and where it approves adoption, and explicit hand-off points where the issue is one of values rather than epistemic risk and must go to a deliberative committee rather than being settled by me alone.

A crucial translation I want built in: the procedure should distinguish institutional belief-adoption—the authority’s internal commitment to a claim being true—from action-authorization—permission to act despite residual uncertainty. Apply Clifford’s stricter evidential standard to belief-adoption and James’s more permissive conditions primarily to action-authorization, and flag exactly where that distinction collapses in practice.

Please apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) adopting a temporary vaccination schedule for a novel respiratory pathogen based on a single forty-person immunogenicity study; (2) adjusting municipal chlorination levels based on preliminary but unreplicated biofilm data; and (3) an emergency screening protocol that is politically demanded but supported by weak, conflicting evidence, where the procedure should yield a recommendation against adoption despite institutional pressure to act. For each case, walk through the steps, show the intermediate judgments, and state the final recommendation.

In addition, identify where the procedure is likely to misfire—especially where James’s live-option criterion is ambiguous for collective institutional belief rather than individual belief, and where the belief-adoption versus action-authorization distinction breaks down. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedural rules, and state explicitly what kinds of decisions the procedure cannot settle and must always escalate.

Finally, in your response separate what is well-established in the James-Clifford literature from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers who work on belief and ethics; do not present James’s permissiveness as the consensus view or Clifford’s rigorism as obviously correct.
