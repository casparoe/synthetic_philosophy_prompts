I'm working through a problem in AI governance with a study partner, and we've reached an impasse that feels like it should have an obvious exit but doesn't. The context is whether supervisory legitimacy for high-capability systems must be process-based, outcome-based, or some hybrid, and my partner laid out the following chain.

(1) A deployment is normatively justified only if a human supervisor can, in principle, verify the system's safety for that specific deployment. (2) Verification is structurally exhaustive: it must operate either on the causal process generating behavior (process-based) or on the behavior itself measured against an independent standard (outcome-based). (3) In open-ended domains—long-horizon strategic reasoning, novel scientific synthesis—outcome-based verification is impossible ex ante, because by definition there is no available ground truth independent of the system's own output. (4) Process-based verification is also impossible in principle, not merely because of opacity, but because of what we might call counterfactual instability: to audit the reasoning process, one must check how it responds to perturbations in inputs, yet any such check either alters the system's state (breaking counterfactual identity) or requires running a duplicate instance that is no longer the supervised system. (5) Therefore, for the very domains where high-capability AI is most valuable, neither verification mode is available. (6) Consequently, no deployment in those domains is ever justified. (7) If we accept this, supervisory frameworks collapse into either restricting AI to trivial, ground-truth-rich tasks—defeating the purpose of development—or abandoning the justification condition and relying on unexamined trust. (8) So the process-outcome framework, taken consistently, undermines itself.

My partner claims this proves we need a third category—something like procedural escrow, in which outcomes are held in normative suspension while partial process indicators are monitored iteratively over time—but I feel the slip is somewhere between (4) and (6), or perhaps hidden in the move from (2) to (4). I want to preserve (1) and (3) if I can, and I'm skeptical of the counterfactual-instability claim in (4), though I don't have a clean rebuttal. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, if I deny (4) to save process-based supervision, am I forced to accept full transparency of complex reasoning, or can I defend a thinner audit condition? And if I instead reject (2) and allow for hybrid or iterative verification structures, what normative work is the new category actually doing—does it solve the problem or just rename the gap?
