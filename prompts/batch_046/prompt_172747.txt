I am currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on medical ethics and the philosophy of consent, and I have become fixated on a recurring pattern in our case discussions that I cannot map onto the taxonomy we have been taught. Our instructor emphasizes that consent is vitiated by deception, by direct coercion, or by incompetence, and we have spent considerable time distinguishing these categories and examining borderline cases within each. Yet I keep encountering scenarios that seem to fall outside all three. Consider two examples. First, a pharmaceutical study recruits participants from a community with high unemployment; the consent form is meticulously clear, there is no misrepresentation of risks, no explicit threat, and the participants demonstrably understand the protocol, but the stipend offered is large enough relative to local wages that refusing participation means passing up an economic lifeline that comes only rarely. Second, consider a patient in a publicly funded healthcare system who is told, accurately and without pressure, that a particular surgery is available and that an alternative conservative treatment exists; she understands both options, but she also knows that opting for the conservative path will place her at the bottom of a years-long waiting list for future care, whereas accepting the surgery keeps her within the active-treatment pathway. In both situations, defenders of the consent say that the subjects made a rational, informed, voluntary choice; critics reply that the background conditions make refusal so costly that the affirmative response does not carry the moral weight of genuine authorization. The critics are not alleging deception, because the risks and benefits were transparent; they are not alleging direct coercion, because no one threatened harm; and they are not alleging incompetence, because comprehension is intact.

Is there a specific philosophical term for this intermediate zone—consent that satisfies the standard triad of conditions yet is undermined by structural dependency or asymmetrical opportunity costs? I have heard phrases like structural coercion and undue influence mentioned in passing, but I am unsure whether either captures exactly this configuration, or whether they collapse back into ordinary coercion once analyzed carefully. If there is no established term, I would like a precise definition that distinguishes this phenomenon from the three standard invalidators and from mere regret after the fact, along with boundary cases that show where it ends and ordinary hard-choice consent begins. Please also indicate what would change your assessment: for instance, if it turned out that any background economic pressure counts as coercion after all, or if the concept of voluntary choice is simply inapplicable in conditions of scarcity, then my framing of the problem as a distinct middle category might dissolve entirely.
