Having spent three decades in experimental physics before turning to philosophy of decision theory, I find that standard discussions of the dollar auction recycle familiar behavioral labels without mapping the actual logical space of explanations. I want a decision tree—not a narrative comparison—that sorts explanatory positions by concrete diagnostic commitments rather than by author or school.

Start from the premise that the classic dollar auction (bidders compete for a reward smaller than the cumulative bids, with the loser also paying) genuinely exhibits escalation that demands explanation.

Build the tree from three branching levels, each phrased as a question answerable without knowing any theoretical label:

1. Valuation dynamics: Does the bidder’s subjective value of winning increase endogenously as sunk costs accumulate, or does the value of winning stay fixed while only the psychological cost of quitting grows?

2. Mechanism: Is the primary driver an individual-level cognitive process (such as consistency-seeking, anticipatory regret, or loss-aversion framing), a social-signaling strategy directed at an observable audience, or a hybrid that requires both?

3. Context dependence: Would the escalation pattern persist in a fully anonymous, single-shot protocol with no observable history, or does it depend essentially on iteration, reputation, or visible commitment?

This yields eight logical combinations. For each leaf, name the corresponding position if one is well-established; if the combination is unoccupied, mark it “unnamed” and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored.

Then insert a cross-cutting axis that intersects all eight: whether the escalation process is best modeled through a thermodynamic analogy—treating commitment as an order parameter that resists reversal near a critical threshold—or whether such physical analogies are categorically inappropriate for intentional action. Add the corresponding leaves and flag any pair that the augmented tree still cannot cleanly separate, explaining why.

Please present the result in American English, without citations, bibliographies, or references to specific scholars. Every node should be phrased so that a reader can answer it from intuitive judgment about the case alone. Note which combinations of answers yield positions that nobody currently defends, and say whether that absence looks like a conceptual impossibility or merely an unexplored niche.
