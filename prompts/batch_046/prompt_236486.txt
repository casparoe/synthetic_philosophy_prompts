I'm delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop titled "Memory, Amnesty, and Institutional Repair," hosted jointly by our law school and history department. The audience is split: roughly half are scholars who have worked on the South African TRC, Argentina's CONADEP, and Rwanda's gacaca courts, together with philosophers of moral responsibility; the other half are guests—including relatives of the disappeared—who are not academics but are precisely why the room is gathered. I need a speech of about seven minutes that does not use philosophy as decoration but lets a single conceptual framing shape what I can and cannot honestly say to everyone present.

Here is the framing, and I want you to commit to it rather than present it as one option among others. I want you to treat the sequential structure of amnesty-for-testimony in a truth commission as structurally analogous to Thomson's lamp: each individual grant of amnesty in exchange for disclosure toggles the institution's moral status between complicity with perpetrators and justice for victims, and if the process continues indefinitely—session after session, applicant after applicant—the commission never reaches a well-defined limit state. Take the definite position that this "supertask of moral evaluation" means we should reject the question of whether the TRC or CONADEP was "ultimately just or ultimately corrupt," because that question presupposes a determinate endpoint the process never achieves. Instead, argue that the proper ethical stance is local and processual: assessing each toggle on its own terms, honoring harm received and harm acknowledged without demanding a final institutional verdict. Let this constraint do real work—so that you genuinely cannot offer the family members present a comforting closure about the institution as a whole, but you can offer them a form of respect that does not depend on false finality, and so that the experts recognize you are not misusing the lamp logic but applying it seriously to transitional justice.

Write this as a polished, formal address, spoken at a measured pace, that takes the interpretive risk of claiming the absence of a limit-state is not a failure of these institutions but a feature that protects against premature closure. Do not lecture the non-experts; let the emotional weight fall on the idea that we do not need a settled judgment to bear witness. At the end, clearly separated, include a brief note identifying where your argument takes liberties with the actual procedural histories of South Africa's TRC and Argentina's CONADEP, and where it relies on a contested philosophical reading of Thomson's lamp—for instance, whether an infinite sequence of state-changes must lack a determinate endpoint, or whether the social meaning of an institution can be fixed independently of that logical structure. Do not include citations, a bibliography, reading suggestions, or any request for further sources.
