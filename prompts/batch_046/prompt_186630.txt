I'm preparing a conceptual framework for a seminar on synthetic creativity, and I want a rigorous adversarial-collaboration document that two opposing camps could both sign. The first camp—call them the Intentional-Source Theorists—holds that artistic creativity necessarily involves an agent with first-personal intentional states directed toward aesthetic ends, including the capacity for normative self-correction and counterfactual justification of specific choices; on this view, generative AI produces at best sophisticated pastiche, because no phenomenally conscious agent stands behind the output to own or revise its aesthetic commitments. The second camp—the Functional-System Theorists—holds that creativity is properly analyzed as a functional-structural property: the production of valuable, domain-appropriate novelty through adaptive navigation of a constraint space, where judgment is defined by the system's capacity to apply selection criteria that are not merely stochastic but responsive to emergent features of the output space; they argue that requiring human consciousness is an unjustified biological essentialism, and that an AI which invents a novel formal vocabulary and then applies its own learned evaluation metrics to curate a coherent body of work exercises synthetic creativity in a literal, not metaphorical, sense.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the formal architecture of diffusion-based and transformer-based image generation, the distinction between training-data interpolation and extrapolation, the fact that current systems lack continuous phenomenal consciousness, and the observation that human artists also rely on non-conscious processes—formulated in wording each side accepts; (2) the precise statements of disagreement, each phrased so that the camp named would endorse its own formulation without reservation; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side, which I suspect include whether counterfactual justification (the ability to explain or defend a specific aesthetic choice under hypothetical revision) is a necessary condition for artistic judgment, and whether an internal second-order selection loop (where the system applies its own evolved evaluation function to filter outputs, rather than relying solely on human post-hoc curation) constitutes genuine normative assessment or merely optimized sampling; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or thought-experiment both sides agree is relevant, together with each camp's explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates. One specific case to include is the hypothetical System M scenario: an AI generates a previously unknown visual syntax, applies an internally developed coherence metric (not human-labeled) to select a subset of outputs, and human critics subsequently judge the curated set to exhibit greater conceptual unity and innovation than both the unfiltered set and comparable human-curated sets. Format the deliverable as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and conclude by noting whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—specifically, whether creativity and judgment are being used with different extensions rather than different intensions—together with the reasoning both sides could accept for that diagnosis.
