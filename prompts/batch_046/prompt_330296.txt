The first one, Aisha, said that if God actually wants everyone to know Him, then the uneven distribution of religious belief is basically a design failure -- like installing streetlights everywhere except in one neighbourhood and then blaming residents for tripping. She added that hiddenness doesn't just affect atheists; it affects people across different cultures and cognitive styles, so it's not just about individual stubbornness.

Her friend, Jaden, pushed back with what he called the 'epistemic humility filter'. He argued that God might deliberately maintain ambiguity so people rely on trust rather than overwhelming proof, since proof-based belief wouldn't allow the relational vulnerability central to faith. Aisha replied that this sounds nice until you realise it applies selectively -- people with certain temperaments find belief easier, so the filter is biased. Jaden said that's like complaining some people are better at running; the point isn't equality of access but quality of response. Then Aisha brought up doxastic involuntarism: she said we don't choose to beleive in gravity or other minds, and if God-belief were genuinely available it should be similarly involuntary. Jaden countered that religious belief involves a different assent -- more like committing to a friendship than accepting a theorem -- so comparing it to gravity is a category mistake. Aisha said that's special pleading unless he explains why God-belief gets this exemption without assuming it's special.

They turned to me and asked who was winning, and honestly I'm stuck. Could you go through their exchange move by move, rate each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say what both are missing, and tell me who's more right overall?
