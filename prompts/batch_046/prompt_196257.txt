Write a parody of a peer-reviewed scientific abstract, approximately 600 words, formatted with standard sections (Background, Methods, Results, Conclusion, and a brief Conflict-of-Interest statement). The voice should be deadpan, jargon-heavy quantitative meta-science—complete with statistical language, institutional acronyms, and recommendations for "further multi-site replication"—and the venue should be a fictional journal of empirical philosophy.

The study reported should investigate whether Quine's well-known rejection of the analytic–synthetic distinction has actually eliminated that distinction from philosophical practice, or whether it survives as an officially disavowed but still operationally active "zombie concept". Satirise both the distinction itself and the tendency to treat conceptual debates as if they could be empirically falsified like scientific hypotheses. The specific target is the gap between explicit Quinean commitments—holism, the interdependence of statements within a "web of belief", and the denial that any statement is immune to revision—and the implicit institutional reliance on intuitive divisions between "purely conceptual" and "empirically loaded" work.

To keep the prompt in the weeds without requiring niche knowledge, inject the following invented concept: the study proposes a new metric called the Synthetic Residual Index (SRI), measuring the degree to which apparently analytic reasoning in a department still depends on unstated empirical assumptions about linguistic usage, disciplinary history, or sociolinguistic norms. The Results section should report the paradoxical finding that the SRI is highest precisely in departments with the strongest explicit Quinean commitments, and should discuss this using the language of "confounding variables" and "institutional path dependence" rather than philosophical argument.

The humour must come from applying bureaucratic measurement culture to a conceptual debate: the straight-faced treatment of philosophical positions as measurable institutional behaviours, the absurd quantification of holism, and the deadpan recommendation that philosophy departments "intervene" on their own conceptual practices. It should be legible to a reader unfamiliar with Quine—who will recognise the satire of academic metrics—while remaining fair and recognisable to someone who knows the arguments about reductionism and the second dogma.

Do not include actual bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for references. Mention Quine's critique only in general terms, without quoting verbatim or depending on specific publication details. Use British spelling throughout (e.g. behaviour, centre, programme, analyse).
