"Williams is right that reasons have to be internal because they need to connect to an agent's subjective motivational set, or S. If you don't have any desire that could be linked through sound deliberation, then there's no reason for you to act. Take the gin and tonic example: if you want a gin and tonic but the bottle has petrol, you don't actually have reason to drink it, because the belief is false and correct deliberation from your actual desires wouldn't lead there. So reasons depend on true beliefs plus existing motivations.

Now for the drowning child: if someone genuinely has zero motivation to help, Williams says they don't have an internal reason. But critics like McDowell say this is wrong because moral reasons are external—they exist regardless of what you want. McDowell thinks practical reason can just see that saving the child is good, and that recognition directly causes the motivation. So external reasons work by bypassing the agent's prior desires and going straight to rational recognition.

But I think this actually collapses. Once you admit that rational recognition can motivate, you've just added a new kind of desire—call it a rational desire—back into S. So McDowell's external reasons are really just internal reasons with extra steps. Therefore Williams wins: all reasons are internal, it's just that some are mediated by rational capacities rather than base desires."
