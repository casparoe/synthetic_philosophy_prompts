The debate over whether populism is compatible with liberal democracy has stalled in a familiar loop: one camp treats populism as a democratic corrective that restores sovereignty to a homogeneous people against corrupt elites and unresponsive intermediaries; the other treats it as an anti-democratic mode in which the leader claims an exclusive, unmediated embodiment of the popular will that cannot be falsified by electoral defeat or constitutional check. Both sides invoke the same empirical markers—moralized division between pure people and corrupt elites, rejection of pluralism, hostility to parties and courts—and yet reach diametrically opposed normative verdicts, with the same cases serving as Rorschach tests rather than shared data. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily empirical or normative but conceptual: the two camps are using "the will of the people" to do different work—one as a synchronic, institutionally checkable mandate that can be disconfirmed at the ballot box, the other as a diachronic, constitutive identity that persists through and is sometimes validated by defeat, analogous to a national narrative rather than a preference aggregation. Is this right, or is the stall actually masking a hidden substantive disagreement about what counts as democratic legitimacy—say, whether representation requires falsifiability or only symbolic identification? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says about defeat, representation, and pluralism, not a survey of movements or leaders, together with an assessment of whether progress is possible: could a formal distinction between falsifiable mandate and constitutive identity, an empirical study of how populist voters interpret electoral loss, or a revised normative framework actually move the debate, or is this an irreducible clash of bedrock intuitions about politics?
