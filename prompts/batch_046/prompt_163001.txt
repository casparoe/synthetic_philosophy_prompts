As a journalist who covers scholarship and the history of ideas, I have been reading recent attempts at genuinely global history—books that deliberately distribute narrative weight across East Asia, Africa, the Americas, and the Pacific rather than treating Europe as the default protagonist. A recurring pattern strikes me. The authors avoid explicit Eurocentric framing: they discuss Song-era commercial networks, Mali’s administrative innovations, or Andean record-keeping on their own terms, and they do not present the Atlantic world as the inevitable center. Yet the book’s underlying architecture—its periodization, its criteria for what counts as a significant transformation, its implicit causal grammar—still seems calibrated to a single tradition’s experience. “Modernity,” for instance, is often defined by Atlantic industrial and democratic milestones; other societies then appear not as independent centers of historical logic but as anticipations, resistances, or alternative routes to that same endpoint. The narrative surface is polycentric; the explanatory structure remains monocentric.

Is there an established concept—perhaps in historiography, philosophy of history, or postcolonial theory—for this precise failure mode, in which the form of explanation remains anchored even after the content has been decentered? How exactly does it differ from conventional Eurocentrism, from Whig history, and from the presentism that reads every past as a prelude to now? If there is no precise term, could you propose one, together with clear boundary conditions that distinguish it from mere omission of non-Western voices and from a genuinely plural framework in which no single region supplies the master categories?

Please separate what is widely accepted in the relevant literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—particularly on whether any unified narrative can avoid some implicit structural center, and whether the distinction I have drawn is itself sustainable—and do not present a disputed interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
