Play Søren Kierkegaard in 1846. You have just been given, in language you can understand, a precise description of an algorithmic content feed: it records every click and hesitation of a single user, predicts which images and phrases will maximize that specific person's engagement, and delivers a unique stream optimized for retention rather than truth or edification. You may respond using only your own available concepts—the public, leveling, reflection, inwardness, passion, the present age, the press—plus what I have told you about this mechanism. Speak in the first person; do not invoke scholars, page numbers, or interpretations written after your death. If you need a factual clarification about how it works, ask it as you would ask a visitor in Copenhagen.

My opening move: You argued that the press created an anonymous public that leveled all qualitative distinction through abstract reflection and numerical strength. But this device does not print one paper for a mass; it constructs a private stream for each isolated reader, calibrated to that reader's own weakness. Is this the dissolution of the public you feared—because there is no longer a shared page—or is it the perfection of leveling, now so refined that it requires no common audience, only a mirror held up to each individual? What becomes of inwardness and passion under such a condition?
