I have been working through Douglas Hofstadter's dialogue Ant Fugue (from Gödel, Escher, Bach), in which Aunt Hillary contemplates whether an ant colony—lacking any central controller, yet displaying coherent, adaptive, goal-directed behavior—might constitute a single mind rather than a mere aggregate of individual minds. I want to translate the core thesis of that dialogue into the vocabulary of predictive processing and the free-energy principle: treat the colony as a hierarchical generative model, individual ants as sub-personal prediction-error minimizers, and the colony-level dynamics as coupled inference processes distributed across a shared Markov blanket. Do this translation systematically, claim by claim. First, the claim that collective intentionality emerges from local interaction rules rather than being programmed centrally; second, the claim that there is no unified inner observer or single subject of experience at the colony level; third, the implicit claim that the colony's boundary conditions—the nest architecture, the pheromone field, the thermal gradient—define an organizational closure sufficient for cognition. For each, say what translates cleanly into active-inference terms, what must be distorted to fit (for instance, does treating pheromone gradients as shared priors smuggle representational content that the original argument deliberately avoids?), and what has no expression at all—perhaps the qualitative first-person dimension that Hofstadter's dialogue gestures toward without resolving. I am reasonably fluent with the free-energy framework but not an expert, so keep the technical vocabulary accessible. Then report whether the residue points to a genuine limitation of predictive processing (it can model distributed cognition but cannot adjudicate whether there is anything it is like to be the colony) or to an ambiguity in the original ant-colony thesis about what having a mind actually requires.
