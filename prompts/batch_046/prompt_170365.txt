For a jurisprudence seminar on post-war international criminal law, specify the full set of desiderata that a normative theory of retroactive justice must satisfy in order to justify the legal structures of the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals without collapsing into either strict legal formalism—which treats the principle of nullum crimen sine lege as an absolute prohibition on retroactive punishment—or the thesis of victors' justice, which reduces the trials to exercises of sovereign power by the victorious coalition. For each desideratum, identify the phenomenon or case it must accommodate—such as the criminalization of aggressive war and the doctrine of command responsibility at Nuremberg, the differential framing of crimes against humanity and the absence of certain indictments at Tokyo, and the problem of judicial selection by the Allied powers—together with the consideration that makes it a genuine constraint rather than a camp-specific preference. You should distinguish between retroactive application of a statutory prohibition and retroactive recognition that an act violated an existing moral-legal norm, preserve the distinction between individual culpability and collective guilt, and maintain consistency with the historical fact that no positive international criminal code existed prior to 1945. State which requirements are non-negotiable and which are negotiable preferences of natural-law, legal-positivist, or critical-realist camps. Identify whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly given the tension between procedural legitimacy and substantive moral urgency—and specify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding either formalist or realist accounts. Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; a brief scorecard of how major jurisprudential positions fare against the list is optional.
