I'm on this ethics review board at my university and we're trying to stay consistant, but our track record is kind of a mess. Here are our actual rulings: we approved a study that calculated group welfare by averaging individual wellbeing scores and then optimized conditions for the highest mean, even though that left a couple participants worse off than baseline; we rejected a proposal to reduce one volunteer's severe chronic pain by distributing it across ten other consenting people who'd each feel a tiny fraction, on the grounds that suffering isn't transferable like that; we approved with conditions a VR study where pairs of friends experienced a shared virtual environment and their emotional responses were pooled for analysis, since they kept seperate identities; we rejected a neural-link study that'd literally merge two people's emotional states into one averaged consciousness, even with full consent, because it dissolved the boundary between persons; and we okayed a standard hypothetical survey about the trolley problem and the footbridge case, treating them as purely abstract.

Now we have this new proposal: a drug trial for a "welfare equalization pill." If everyone in a group takes it, the total amount of happiness and suffering in the group stays exactly the same, but it's redistributed so everyone ends up at the group mean. So if one person is at +8 and another at -4, after the pill they're both at +2. No one is forced, everyone gives informed consent, and no physical harm occurs - it's just smoothing out the distribution. The researchers say this isn't utilitarian aggregation, it's just leveling.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our past cases is the closest analog - the pooled VR study, the rejected pain-distribution study, or the rejected merge study? I'm also wondering if our precedents genuinely underdetermine this. Can you lay out the two or three different principles we might implicitly be running - maybe pure aggregation, a separateness-of-persons view, or a consent-only view - and say what each predicts about the pill? And if they do diverge, what's the one further case we should see that'd actually tell them apart?
