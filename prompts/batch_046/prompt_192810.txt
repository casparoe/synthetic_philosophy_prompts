I am delivering the commencement address next Friday for the joint Philosophy and Environmental Governance graduating class; the room will hold philosophy faculty, policy practitioners, family members, and graduates who have spent the year debating whether individual carbon reduction is morally required when one’s contribution to climate change is causally negligible. I have just finished a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend—an economist who treats lifestyle choices as pure signalling unless they alter structural incentives—about whether the inefficacy objection actually dissolves our obligations or merely relocates them onto collective agents. I want the address to navigate this tension without collapsing into either a sermon on personal virtue or a dismissal of private action as irrelevant, and I want it written in British English.

Please construct a formal, warm address of about five to six minutes. Its architecture should be shaped by the idea of structural complicity without marginal contribution—the claim that one can be genuinely implicated in a harmful system even when one’s withdrawal makes no counterfactual difference to the outcome. This idea must do real structural work: it should determine how I distinguish congratulating graduates on their future professional and institutional roles from addressing their private consumption choices; it should allow the philosophers present to recognise the conceptual machinery without losing family members who have never encountered the inefficacy objection; and it should prevent me from saying anything that implies individual lifestyle change is sufficient, or that it is morally empty. The occasion is partly celebratory and partly reflective, so the tone must honour achievement while refusing easy consolation.

Finally, please include near the end a brief, explicit note on where your reasoning is most likely to fail: identify the specific premise about the relationship between collective causation and individual obligation that, if false, would cause the address to fall apart, and state what kind of argument or empirical finding would change your mind on that point.
