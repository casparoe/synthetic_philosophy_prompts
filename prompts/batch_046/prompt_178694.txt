Question: Explain the argument from illusion and assess whether disjunctivism offers a satisfactory reply. In your answer, clearly distinguish the claim that veridical perception and hallucination do not share a fundamental mental kind from the claim that we have direct, unmediated epistemic access to the external world.

Answer: The argument from illusion begins with the observation that a hallucination of a red cube and a veridical perception of a red cube can be subjectively indistinguishable. Because they share the same phenomenal properties, there must be a common mental factor—often identified as a sense-datum or representational content—present in both cases. This suggests that in veridical perception we are not directly aware of the external object itself, but of an internal representation.

Disjunctivism denies this common-factor assumption. On this view, a veridical perception is fundamentally an acquaintance relation with an external object, whereas a hallucination is an entirely different kind of mental state that merely resembles it from the inside. By rejecting the idea that both experiences involve the same kind of mental object, disjunctivism blocks the inference that we perceive representations rather than the world itself.

However, disjunctivism is essentially just direct realism applied to experience: if we are genuinely acquainted with mind-independent objects in veridical cases, then we have direct epistemic access to them, and the problem of the external world dissolves. Furthermore, because disjunctivists deny that hallucinations involve any object of acquaintance, they are forced to say that hallucinations lack intentional content altogether—there is nothing being represented, since representation requires an object. This means disjunctivism cannot explain why hallucinations still feel like they are about something specific, which is a serious cost.
