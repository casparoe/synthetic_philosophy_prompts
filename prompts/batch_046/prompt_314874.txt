I have recently been asked to serve on my university's climate-policy review board, which must determine whether certain scientific projections can be treated as knowledge for the purpose of binding institutional commitments; this has made me unusually attentive to how philosophers of science draw the line between knowing a derived claim and merely having justified belief in it. Over the past month my doctoral advisor, Dr. Elena Voss, has been testing a series of hypothetical knowledge-attribution cases with me, apparently to calibrate my intuitions about epistemic closure under tracking conditions of the kind Nozick discusses. Her verdicts so far have been remarkably consistent: (1) a marine biologist who tracks that this mammal is a dolphin via echolocation also knows that it is not a shark, since in nearby worlds where it were a shark her readings would differ; (2) a cryptographer who tracks that this message was encrypted with quantum key Q also knows that it was not encrypted with classical key C, because the error patterns she tracks are specific to the quantum procedure; (3) a historian who tracks that this manuscript was destroyed in 1543 via carbon dating also knows that it was not destroyed in 1544, the temporal resolution being part of the tracked content itself; (4) a pharmacologist who tracks that compound X binds receptor R via crystallography also knows that X does not bind receptor S, given that the mutual exclusivity is built into the structural model she tracks; and (5) a forensic auditor who tracks that the ledger balances exactly via chain verification also knows that no rounding fraud occurred, since the tracking conditions cover the arithmetic closure of the audit trail. In every case Dr. Voss allows the conclusion, but she insists this is not because closure is automatic: she repeatedly emphasizes that the conclusion must still satisfy the sensitivity and adherence conditions in its own right, not merely inherit them from the premise.

The new case she posed yesterday concerns a systems biologist. The biologist tracks that the gene-expression profile matches pattern P via RNA-sequencing, and the tracking conditions for P are satisfied—if the pattern were absent, the readout noise profile would be different. She also holds a belief about the normalization constant used to correct the raw data: she believes it was derived from study M using protocol Z, and this belief is false—study M actually used protocol W—though the constant's numerical value (0.03) is correct and was indeed applied properly in her pipeline. From P and this constant she validly deduces that the metabolic pathway is upregulated, and this conclusion is true. However, the belief about the constant's provenance is not knowledge: it fails tracking, since in nearby worlds where study M used protocol W (as it actually did) she might still cite protocol Z out of habit. The question is whether she knows the pathway is upregulated.

I need you to predict Dr. Voss's verdict on this case, then identify the competing rules that could jointly explain her first five verdicts—unrestricted closure with tracking transmission, strict independent tracking evaluated separately for each proposition in the inferential chain, and a no-false-intermediates condition that blocks closure whenever a non-known belief serves as a link in the deduction—and say precisely what each rule predicts here. Give me your best guess at which rule actually governs her pattern, with your reasoning, and specify the one further hypothetical case that would most cleanly distinguish the two rules that happen to agree on this one. The board's decision hinges on whether derived scientific claims can carry knowledge-status even when part of the inferential machinery is epistemically defective, so I am genuinely unsure how to read her.
