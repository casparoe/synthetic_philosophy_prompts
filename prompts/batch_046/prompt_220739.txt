I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a historian—let's call her Dr. S. V. Kline—who argues in her recent work that modern nations are best understood through what she terms modular anachronism: the claim that colonial bureaucratic instruments, specifically census classifications, ethnographic museums, and territorial mapping protocols, were originally developed as portable administrative modules for imperial governance and then retroactively implanted into European metropoles after 1850. She contends that this modular transfer produces the illusion of deep indigenous national roots and denies that print-capitalism or shared linguistic markets are sufficient for nation-formation without this bureaucratic scaffolding. She further holds that Hobsbawm's concept of invented tradition and Anderson's concept of imagined communities are both downstream of this prior bureaucratic architecture rather than independent explanations.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate the thesis in general terms. Include at least one question pressing her on how modular anachronism accounts for postcolonial independence movements that actively dismantled colonial census categories rather than adapting them; include at least one question probing whether her framework collapses the distinction between invented tradition and imagined community or instead reveals a hidden structural dependency between them; and include at least one question asking what empirical signature could distinguish her modular-transfer claim from a standard diffusion-of-modernity narrative. For each of these three, provide a sharp follow-up keyed to her most probable reply—respectively, that postcolonial actors selectively adapted rather than rejected modules; that bureaucratic scaffolding is conceptually prior to both imagination and invention; and that formal isomorphism between colonial and metropolitan administrative forms is the telltale evidence. Conclude with a final question about chronological implications: if modular anachronism is correct, should we date the emergence of specific European nations to the moment of bureaucratic retrofitting rather than to earlier linguistic or cultural consolidation, and does this entail that all modern nations are essentially contemporaneous regardless of when their ancient traditions were invented?
