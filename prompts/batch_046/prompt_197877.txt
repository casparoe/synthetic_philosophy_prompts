I serve on the Joint Ethics and Acquisition Committee of the Meridian Museum of Anthropology. We must reach a decision before our meeting on 12 June on whether to approve the proposed temporary exhibition 'Mirrors of Barbarism: Ritual Objects and the Limits of Judgement', which centres on a seventeenth-century ritual assemblage including modified human remains acquired in a Tupi-Guarani context. The curatorial proposal draws on Michel de Montaigne's essay on cannibals to frame the display: it uses his observation that each culture tends to label as barbarism whatever deviates from its own customs, in order to defend the inclusion of the objects and to challenge visitors' unexamined moral universalism. A descendant community group, the Guaraní-Tupi Cultural Council, has submitted a formal objection. They contend that deploying Montaigne's relativism in this context aestheticises ancestral violence, treats their dead as philosophical instruments for European self-critique, and erases the coercive conditions of acquisition. They request either full repatriation or the removal of the Montaigne framing in favour of a universalist interpretation grounded in human dignity, which would condemn the display of human remains irrespective of cultural origin. Our senior curator replies that stripping away the relativist framing sanitises colonial history and that returning the objects would forfeit a vital philosophical lesson about the contingency of moral judgement.

Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before Thursday's meeting. It must recommend a concrete course of action — approval with conditions, conditional deferral, or rejection — together with a clear time frame for implementation. The reasoning should treat both the descendant community's objection and the curator's philosophical defence with genuine seriousness, not merely restate legal title or museum policy. In particular, please address the following tension: if Montaigne's cultural relativism is applied consistently, it appears to require recognising the descendant community's interpretive authority over its own ancestral practices — an authority that undermines the museum's claim to use those practices as a philosophical mirror for European audiences. Your recommendation should explain how it navigates this asymmetry of recognition, and it should acknowledge the strongest consideration in favour of the position you ultimately reject.
