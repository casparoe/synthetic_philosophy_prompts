I've been thinking about this since a discussion with some friends about how medical institutions actually operationalize the concept of death, and I'm trying to figure out whether there's a coherent philosophy underneath or just institutional layering. In many legal systems, two independent criteria are recognized for declaring death: irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, and irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. Both are taken to establish that an individual has died. Yet the institutional protocols that apply these criteria are asymmetrical in ways that seem philosophically loaded. A patient who meets brain-death criteria can be declared dead while mechanical ventilation maintains cardiac circulation, allowing immediate organ procurement with a still-beating heart; by contrast, in donation after circulatory death, life support is withdrawn, the heart stops, and clinicians wait for a defined interval—often just a few minutes—before declaring death and beginning procurement, even though the heart could in principle be restarted in a recipient. Meanwhile, in some jurisdictions brain death is not fully recognized as legal death outside specific contexts, meaning a heart-beating body without brain function is not treated uniformly as a corpse.

What theory of death—of the organism, of personal identity, of the boundary between life and non-life—does this bifurcated practice actually presuppose? Does it require the view that death is the irreversible loss of integrated functioning of the organism as a whole, such that either the brain or the heart can serve as the critical integrator? Or does it tacitly treat brain death and circulatory death as two different kinds of event that happen to share a legal label, perhaps because one concerns the loss of consciousness and the other the loss of biological animation? Reconstruct the implicit ontology: say whether the practice is genuinely committed to one view or is philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival accounts. And tell me what would have to change—conceptually and in hospital protocol—if we replaced the underlying view with a strict biological-identity account on which the organism dies only when circulation permanently ceases, or conversely with a personhood-based account on which death tracks the irreversible loss of consciousness regardless of cardiac status. I'm not looking for a bibliography; I want the conceptual architecture reconstructed from the practice itself.
