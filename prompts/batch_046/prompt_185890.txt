As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been trying to understand the Darwinian theory of cultural transmission—the idea that memes are replicators subject to selection for copying-fidelity, fecundity, and longevity—through the lens of the systems I work with every day. I want a poem that does not merely mention this theory but enacts it formally. Write a sestina: six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi, using the classic rotated end-words fork, commit, merge, branch, clone, and decay. The rotation should perform the concept: in each stanza the same six terms must reappear at the line-ends in the prescribed order, but the surrounding context should shift so that each word accumulates mutated meanings—enacting replication with error. Ground the imagery in software ecosystems, distinguishing vertical transmission (inherited legacy dependencies, downward code inheritance) from horizontal transmission (viral snippets copied peer-to-peer without provenance or attribution). In the envoi, gather all six terms together so that the closing lines stage an unresolved tension between preservation and mutation—between merge and decay, clone and branch—rather than resolving it into a moral or conclusion. The speaker should remain an engineer observing the parallel precisely, without claiming philosophical authority or reducing culture to biology. The result should be accurate enough that someone familiar with memetics recognizes the three selection criteria at work in the imagery, and rigorous enough that the form itself is the argument.
