As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been working through the identity of indiscernibles by analogizing it to problems I know from distributed systems. The view I am considering is that Black's symmetric two-sphere universe does not actually violate Leibniz's principle, because the spheres differ in genuine relational properties—each stands in a unique distance and orientation relation to the other—so the thought experiment only defeats a version of the principle that arbitrarily excludes relational structure from what counts as a property. I take this to be similar to the error of claiming two processes are identical because their internal memory states match, while ignoring that they have distinct process identifiers, socket bindings, and positions in the network topology; the difference is not intrinsic in the narrow sense, but it is structurally real. I want a rigorous audit of what could defeat this view. Which arguments, conceptual distinctions, or formal results would be decisive, and which would only be awkward? Are there clear cases where relational differences fail to establish numerical distinctness? Does the position collapse into the tautology that whatever is distinguishable is distinguishable? What do defenders of the principle themselves concede as genuine failure conditions? And has this defense been insulated by qualifications to the point of unfalsifiability—if so, is that a problem for a metaphysical claim of this sort?
