A friend is deciding whether to commit a significant portion of her retirement savings to funding a first-of-its-kind carbon-capture installation in a country that has never regulated this technology. She has gathered extensive expert material—engineering feasibility reports, macroeconomic forecasts, comparative legal analyses, and analogies drawn from earlier industrial transitions—but the sources rest on incompatible methodological assumptions, and no statistical base rate exists for the particular failure modes she faces. Her advisor insists that because the volume of information is large, she should construct scenario weights and maximize expected utility. I have argued that accumulating heterogeneous evidence is not the same as refining a numerical probability, and that treating a structurally novel, open-ended situation as though it were a repeatable gamble risks substituting formal precision for genuine judgment. She asks which of us offers the more rational framework. Is my resistance to the expected-utility approach philosophically defensible, and does it depend on a distinction between calculable risk and a form of uncertainty that cannot be reduced to any assignment of probabilities?
