I've been stuck in this loop with the Great Divergence debate — you know, the old why Britain and not China framing, sometimes called the Needham question. In my history reading group it's gone totally circular. Someone brings up coal being near Manchester, someone else says the imperial exam system killed scientific curiosity, then someone mentions colonial extraction funding capital, and the rebuttals roll out like clockwork. Nobody actually changes position. I'm trying to figure out if this is a real disagreement about the past, or if it's one of those stalls where people answer totally different questions under one label.

Here's the split I see. One camp asks for necessary conditions: what had to be present for industrial takeoff? The other asks for a comparative explanation: why did those conditions cluster in northwest Europe rather than the Yangtze delta? Then there's this counterfactual problem — some people think asking would China have industrialized by 1800 if... only works if small initial differences don't cascade wildly, others think the cascade is the whole point and makes the counterfactual useless. And there's this framing people keep using where Britain gets selected out of a pool of candidates, which sounds neat but maybe sneaks in the assumption that every society was running the same race with comparable starting positions.

Can you diagnose the stall? Is it verbal — are they using explanation differently? Hidden standards of evidence, like whether institutional archives count more than ecological data? Or is it just that counterfactual stability hasn't been solved? And what would actually move things — a formal branching model, agreement on whether we're comparing states or ecological zones, or should we drop the why not China framing entirely because it assumes a single trajectory both were on?

There's also this specific case I keep going back and forth on. Some historians say China's high-level equilibrium trap was real and explains the stall, others say that concept was debunked and using it just assumes stagnation. Is that a factual dispute or a framing dispute about what counts as economic stagnation?

Also, I notice this debate has occured for decades with almost no movement despite tons of new data. Does that mean the disagreement is bedrock?
