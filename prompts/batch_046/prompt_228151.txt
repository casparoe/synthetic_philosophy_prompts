I'm preparing interview questions for a podcast episode with an unnamed political theorist who defends a refined, institutionally embedded version of performance legitimacy for authoritarian regimes—specifically for China's adaptive authoritarianism and Singapore's developmental-state model. She does not treat prosperity and order as merely instrumental benefits that purchase passive acquiescence; instead, she argues that sustained delivery of collective goods generates what she calls an "epistemic mandate," in which a regime's demonstrated capacity to produce growth, stability, and low corruption functions as a non-electoral form of political knowledge about the common good, inflected by a Confucian-inflected conception of authority as competence rather than consent. I want a sequence of six to eight questions that go well beyond asking her to restate this view. Please include at least one question on each of the following angles, paired with a follow-up keyed to her most probable reply.

First, the institutional separation between output legitimacy and procedural legitimacy: ask how her framework distinguishes genuine social order from coercive stability—anticipating her likely appeal to Singapore's legal-rational bureaucracy and China's internal feedback loops as filters that exclude mere repression—then follow up by pressing whether those same institutions can be empirically identified independently of the outcomes they are meant to explain, or whether the distinction collapses into circular reasoning. Second, the comparative challenge posed by South Korea and Taiwan, where rapid prosperity was followed by democratic transition rather than consolidation: ask whether this shows performance legitimacy is inherently transitional, anticipating her reply that those cases lacked the deep Confucian institutional embedding that locks legitimacy into competence rather than consent, then follow up by asking what observable institutional feature, other than the absence of democratization, marks that embedding. Third, the "epistemic mandate" claim itself: ask whether high GDP growth, infrastructure delivery, or corruption control actually demonstrate knowledge of collective welfare, or whether they might equally reflect effective extraction, surveillance capacity, and suppression of distributive conflict—anticipating her response that delivery metrics are intersubjectively verifiable in ways popular consent is not—then follow up by asking which specific welfare indicators she treats as non-negotiable and how she accounts for regimes that score well on growth while scoring catastrophically on specific dimensions like environmental health or ethnic inclusion. Fourth, the threshold problem: ask what happens to legitimacy when growth stalls, inequality spikes, or demographic crisis hits, anticipating her appeal to long planning horizons and institutional resilience—then follow up by asking at what quantitative or qualitative point a temporary downturn becomes a legitimacy crisis rather than a tolerated fluctuation, and whether that point is determined internally by the regime's own metrics or externally by popular experience. Fifth, the normative boundary: ask whether her view licenses any regime that delivers goods regardless of method, including ones that maintain order through mass internment, suppression of dissent, or elimination of rival information channels—anticipating her probable distinction between "developmental order" and "maintenance order"—then follow up by asking what independent criterion distinguishes the two, given that both can produce identical macro-level indicators of stability and growth.

The questions should treat her position with philosophical seriousness—pressing where the comparative-politics and Confucian-political-philosophy literatures actually press—rather than treating it as a straw man, and each follow-up should tighten the pressure specifically on the move she is likeliest to make. Avoid generic biographical or career-oriented questions entirely.
