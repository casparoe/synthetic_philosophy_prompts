I'm currently enrolled in a seminar on normative social theory, and we recently examined the so-called gender-equality paradox—the empirical pattern in which more gender-egalitarian societies often display larger gender differences in career specialization and field selection. During discussion, a distinction was drawn between two interpretive frameworks that I am struggling to keep conceptually separate. On one side is the autonomy-actualization view: the claim that when structural barriers and coercive norms are minimized, observed divergence in occupational choice reflects the authentic expression of stable, pre-social preferences, which implies that prior gender homogeneity was largely an artifact of suppression. On the other side is the institutional-reflexivity view: the claim that egalitarian policies and cultural narratives do not merely remove constraints but actively reshape the symbolic meaning of gender, generating new status-competition dynamics and identity-signaling mechanisms that amplify self-selection into gendered pathways, so that divergence is constructed by the very conditions of equality rather than revealed beneath them.

Please compare these two frameworks with particular attention to how each relates descriptive statistical patterns to claims about nature, culture, and distributive justice. I want to understand whether either framework can consistently derive normative conclusions—such as whether observed divergence justifies different resource allocations, educational priorities, or anti-discrimination measures—from aggregate data alone, or whether both risk committing a category mistake by inferring individual-level causation from population-level correlations. In your answer, clarify the precise points of conceptual conflict between autonomy-actualization and institutional-reflexivity, indicate whether they are logically incompatible or might be partially integrated, and explain what a principled adjudication between them would require without presupposing either strict biological essentialism or an unrestricted social constructivism.
