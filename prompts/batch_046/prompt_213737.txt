I have been trying to work out, on my own, a problem about rules that can rewrite themselves, and I think there are two serious ways to think about it that keep talking past each other. I want a document that treats them as partners in an adversarial collaboration.

The specific setup I have in mind is a legal charter with three provisions that create what I am calling a recursive amendment loop. Provision A states that the charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the governing body. Provision B states that the meaning of "two-thirds"—whether it means two-thirds of seats, of present members, or of eligible voters—is itself defined by the charter and may be revised by that same two-thirds process. Provision C states that any amendment which makes the amendment process incoherent or self-contradictory is automatically void. The loop is that the rule governing change is, in part, about its own conditions of change, and the coherence check in C applies to the results of B and A as well.

One camp—call them the hierarchical-resolution camp—thinks this loop is only apparent. They say that at any given moment there is a fixed, determinate rule in force, and that talk about "the rule changing itself" is really just a sequence of distinct rules across time, with an implicit meta-rule that governs the transition. The other camp—call them the grounding-paradox camp—thinks the loop is real and vicious. They say that because the conditions of valid change depend on the very rules being changed, there is no non-circular foundation for authority inside the system; either the loop collapses into contradiction (especially when B and C interact) or it must be grounded in something outside the charter, like an original political act or an external norm.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want it formatted as a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements table.

In the agreed-claims section, include: (1) the exact formal structure of the recursive loop as defined by A, B, and C; (2) what each camp agrees needs explaining—namely, whether authority can rest solely inside the loop, and what happens when an amendment to B triggers C; (3) the shared recognition that standard hierarchical answers (separating levels of rules) must address the temporal dynamics of B specifically; and (4) any results both sides accept, such as that if C is interpreted as applying to its own enactment conditions, a direct contradiction follows.

In the disagreements table, present the precise formulations of disagreement in wording that each side would accept for its own position. For the hierarchical-resolution side, phrase it so they can endorse it: for example, that temporal sequencing and implicit meta-rules dissolve the circle without external grounding. For the grounding-paradox side, phrase it so they can endorse it: for example, that any temporal sequencing already presupposes the authority it claims to explain, and that C's self-application reveals a contradiction that internal resources cannot fix.

Then identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect at least two cruxes: first, whether a rule can coherently apply its own coherence conditions to the act of its own revision (the C-crux); second, whether an "implicit meta-rule" is genuinely part of the charter or is an external addition smuggled in to avoid paradox (the meta-rule crux). There may be others you see.

For each crux, provide a concrete hypothetical case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the outcome or implication will be. For instance, for the C-crux, both might agree that a specific amendment scenario is relevant—say, a vote that redefines "two-thirds" downward to a simple majority, then uses that new definition to validate its own passage, triggering C. The hierarchical camp should predict that the temporal sequencing prevents C from applying retroactively to block the transition, while the grounding camp should predict that C must apply and therefore void the amendment, leaving the system without internal authority to resolve the conflict. For the meta-rule crux, both might agree on a case where the charter is silent about transition rules except for A, B, and C; one side should predict that the silence is filled by logical structure, the other that it reveals an unavoidable gap requiring external authority.

Finally, include a brief section on whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal—about the word "rule" or "authority"—and, if so, which part and why both sides can accept that diagnosis.

Write this from the perspective of someone who has no formal training in law or philosophy but has been thinking through the puzzle carefully, and keep the tone formal and polished. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask me to quote specific texts or scholars. The document should be something both camps could actually put their names to.
