I serve on the ethics oversight panel for a large agricultural research institute, and I am trying to understand the philosophical architecture of our own regulations. Our institutional policy requires full ethical review, harm–benefit analysis, and species-specific welfare safeguards for any study involving vertebrates, however small the sample: even a pilot study with twelve zebrafish must justify every lethal endpoint and document analgesia. By contrast, research programmes that deliberately expose hundreds of thousands of aphids, beetle larvae, or nematodes to novel pesticides, temperature shocks, or genetic modifications fall entirely outside our remit; they are governed only by biosafety and crop-protection regulations, with no welfare assessment whatsoever. In the policy’s rationale there is an explicit footnote stating that, although the cumulative number of invertebrates affected is enormous, the per-individual probability of sentience is presumed so low that multiplying the two does not generate a morally significant expectation of suffering worthy of regulatory attention.

What philosophical commitments about moral status, aggregation, and epistemic risk does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does it implicitly endorse a threshold view—that sentience, and therefore moral considerability, is an all-or-nothing property tied to neuroanatomical complexity, such that below the threshold there is literally nothing to weigh? Or could a gradualist or scalar view of sentience accommodate the same institutional division by treating expected welfare below a certain magnitude as practically negligible, even if non-zero? Furthermore, is the practice consistent with the institute’s parallel commitment, in its human-subjects protocol, that we should protect participants from low-probability but high-magnitude harms even when the base rate is uncertain? And if the footnote’s probabilistic reasoning were taken seriously—that vast numbers multiplied by a tiny probability still fail to trigger review—what does that imply about how the institution conceives of moral aggregation: does it reject additive aggregation of welfare across individuals, or does it merely apply a de minimis filter that it refuses to apply in the vertebrate case?

Please analyse the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify where the policy is philosophically underdetermined, such that rival views of invertebrate sentience and moral mathematics could still license the same regulatory split. Please respond in British English.
