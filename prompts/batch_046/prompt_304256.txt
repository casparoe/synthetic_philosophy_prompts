I'm a PhD student in moral psych and I've been stuck on this argument that keeps resurfacing in our reading group. The question is whether guilt is genuinely the better moral emotion and whether a culture can actually choose to be more guilt-oriented. One camp—let's call them the repair camp—says guilt is better because it's tied to specific actions, motivates amends, and doesn't attack the global self; they lean on the classic action-focus vs global-focus distinction. The other camp—maybe the cohesion camp—says shame is undervalued, especially in collectivist settings, because it binds people to social norms and the whole 'global self' framing is mostly Western bias. Lately some people have brought in this idea of digital 'shame archives,' where persistant social media traces make shame public and permanent in ways traditional communal shame wasn't, which maybe breaks the old repair-vs-cohesion framing entirely. But here's the thing: we've circled these same positions for decades, from the classic philosophy texts to recent cross-cultural work, and nobody ever moves. Is this actually a substantive disagreement about moral psychology, or is it verbal—like 'guilt' and 'shame' are just tracking different phenomena in clinical versus anthropological studies? Or are the two sides answering seperate questions: one about individual moral repair, one about social regulation? I keep wondering if there's a hidden empirical question about emotional plasticity we're all ignoring, or if the stall is just because we're treating 'better' as a univocal standard when it might depend on whether you're optimizing for individual wellbeing or group stability. What's your diagnosis—substantive, verbal, or misframed? And if it's misframed, what would actually move it forward: an intervention study, a formal model of emotional trade-offs, or just dropping the 'which is better' framing altogether?
