My opening move: I have no formal philosophical training, but I have been thinking through these questions on my own, and I want to test an idea I call "structural irreversibility." Rather than distinguishing analytic from synthetic by meaning or convention, I distinguish them by the architecture of belief-revision. Imagine the fabric of belief not as flat but as hierarchical: some threads are peripheral, and pulling them out only disturbs nearby cloth; other threads are load-bearing, and cutting them collapses the entire garment. I propose that analytic truths are precisely these load-bearing threads—beliefs whose abandonment would not merely adjust other beliefs but would force a complete reconstruction of the conceptual framework. Arithmetic and basic logic, on this view, are not true "by convention" in the old sense, yet they are analytic in practice because revising them is structurally irreversible: one cannot replace them with alternatives without rebuilding everything else. This seems to honor your insight that any statement can in principle be revised, since the revision is not logically impossible—only architecturally catastrophic. Does this save the analytic-synthetic distinction you rejected, or does your fabric of belief already contain everything needed to explain load-bearing resistance without introducing a separate category?
