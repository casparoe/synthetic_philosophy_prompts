I work in distributed systems, and a recent conversation with friends about narrative provenance made me wonder whether historians apply an implicit graph-theoretic threshold when they decide that a legend has become history. My intuition, shaped by thinking about source nodes and branching in data pipelines, is that a story crosses that boundary when its textual transmission shows sufficient independent branching — multiple attestations that cannot be collapsed back into a single origin node. Applying that framework, the Arthurian cycle should seem stronger than the Exodus narrative: Arthur survives across Welsh triads, Breton lays, Latin chronicles, and vernacular romances, whereas the Exodus account is overwhelmingly anchored in one dominant textual stream with comparatively sparse external branching. Yet the historiographical consensus appears to run in the opposite direction, treating Arthur as legendary while allowing the Exodus at least provisional historical status.

Is the discrepancy resolved purely by material corroboration — settlement patterns, epigraphic hints, or comparative Near Eastern texts — which somehow override the topology of textual transmission? Or is there a deeper epistemological rule that says network branching is irrelevant once archaeological correlation enters the picture? I have tried to formalise this as a provenance-weighted confidence score, but I am unsure whether the branching coefficient should dominate or whether material evidence always takes precedence. If the two criteria genuinely conflict, which one ought to govern our classification of narratives like the Trojan War, King Arthur, or the Exodus, and does that mean my distributed-systems analogy is simply misapplied to historiography?
