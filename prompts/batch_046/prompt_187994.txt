Here are my judgments about material persistence and constitution, and I want to know whether they cohere into one principle or whether some must be abandoned.

Consider a bronze statue in a plaza. The bronze is melted away entirely and then recast into the identical shape in the same location. I think the original statue survived; its matter was destroyed and restored, but its form and historical trajectory continue. Now consider the same statue gradually replaced atom-by-atom with titanium, preserving every contour without interruption. Once no bronze remains, I think the original statue is gone and a titanium replica stands there, even though no abrupt change occurred.

An ice sculptor maintains a horse-shaped statue by continuously adding fresh ice as old ice sublimates. After a full year, none of the original water molecules remain, yet the form and location never lapsed. Here I think it is still the same statue. By contrast, a wooden statue made from ship-timber has every plank replaced over decades with synthetic plastic that mimics wood exactly. At the end I think a different object exists, even though the replacement was gradual and the history traces back continuously—whereas if the replacements had all been wooden planks from other trees, I would say the statue survived despite total material turnover.

Two marble figures stand adjacent and are carved together until the boundary dissolves into one continuous form with two heads. I think neither original survives; a new fused object exists, and the two prior statues have been destroyed. That fused object is then split precisely in half, with each half carved into a perfect miniature replica of the original single-headed form. I think the fused object is destroyed, and there are now two new statues—neither identical to either pre-fusion original.

Finally, a bio-engineered sculpture is grown from a single seed-culture that regenerates its own tissue through a persistent regulatory system. Every cell is replaced monthly, yet the organism maintains the shape. I think this is one continuously existing statue, but I am unsure whether that judgment conflicts with my verdict about the wooden-to-plastic case.

Is there a single principle—perhaps about the kind of matter that can constitute the object, the presence of an organizing cause, or the conditions under which spatiotemporal gaps are permissible—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn and why? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed: a statue formed solely by a temporary magnetic field arranging iron filings into a shape, where the filings are continuously replaced but the field pattern itself persists unchanged. I also ask that you say explicitly what would change your mind—whether a particular argument about causal continuity, formal persistence, or material essentialism—and identify the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to counterexample.
