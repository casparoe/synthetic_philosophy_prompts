I am a retired field biologist who, since leaving active research, have begun studying philosophy in earnest. I am currently reading Augustine's treatise On Lying, and I find his classification of falsehoods both systematic and difficult to reconcile with any framework I know. The passage that puzzles me runs approximately as follows. Augustine distinguishes eight kinds of lies, ordered by severity: falsehoods in religious doctrine; lies that harm others without benefiting anyone; lies that harm one person to benefit another; lies told solely for the pleasure of deceiving; lies told to amuse in conversation; lies that preserve property without harming anyone; lies that save a life without harming anyone; and lies that protect bodily purity without harming anyone. He maintains that every one of these is a sin, not merely the malicious ones, and he insists that the lie told to save an innocent life from murder is still gravely wrong. His reason appears to be that false speech injures the speaker's own soul—an internal corruption that is worse than any external bodily harm that truthful speech might permit—so that even the most compassionate lie is self-destructive.

What I cannot grasp is the exact philosophical principle that drives this conclusion. Augustine writes as though the soul possesses a kind of internal coherence or signaling integrity: just as in evolutionary biology a costly honest signal maintains the stability of a cooperative system, false speech seems to disrupt an internal protocol regardless of its consequences in the world. Yet Augustine clearly does not mean this as a biological analogy; he treats the damage as metaphysical. Is the core principle that speech is constitutive of the speaker's being in a way that makes falsehood a kind of self-division? Or does the argument depend essentially on a theological premise—say, that the soul is created for truth and that lying therefore disorders its relation to God—that would render the conclusion unavailable to anyone who does not share that ontology? I am trying to understand the passage on its own terms, but I also want to know whether the underlying principle can be reconstructed without the theological framework, perhaps as a claim about the conditions for rational agency or communicative trust. Please explain the reasoning in the passage, identify the principle that unifies Augustine's rejection of all eight categories, and say whether that principle can stand independently of his theology. I ask that your response be written in American English.
