I have been working through archaeological case studies on Neolithic burial assemblages—specifically how interpreters use grave orientation and the differential placement of red ochre, marine shell ornaments, and curated toolkits to reconstruct cosmological belief-systems—and I find myself caught in what seems like an intensified version of the hermeneutic circle. Because material culture does not assert propositions in the manner of texts, any inference from a spatial pattern (for instance, an east-west orientation consistently paired with shell deposits but lacking ochre) to a dualistic world-view depends on ethnographic analogies; yet those analogies are typically selected because they render the observed material patterns coherent, which makes the reasoning circular. Some methodologists respond by advocating a principle of interpretive charity, reconstructing the belief-structure that makes the assemblage maximally rational, while others caution that this treats embodied practices as if they were discrete mental states, thereby projecting modern cognitive categories onto societies for which belief may not have functioned as a propositional attitude at all.

The problem becomes more acute once we grant that institutional beliefs are not merely reflected in material practices but are partly constituted by them. If a particular burial layout is not simply evidence for a cosmological doctrine but is itself one of the practices through which that doctrine is enacted, then there appears to be no independent standard—no belief behind the object—against which to judge whether the layout accurately represents it. Even so, archaeological reasoning routinely distinguishes between commitments that are doctrinal, and therefore vulnerable to counter-example (as when a grave violates the expected orientation), and those that are practical or habitual, where deviation is read as local noise rather than falsification. Given this constitutive relation, I want to ask whether there is a principled, materially grounded criterion—perhaps visible in patterns of ritual repair, the deliberate destruction of cultic vessels, or the generational reuse of grave goods—that allows an interpreter to determine whether a given assemblage encodes a propositional institutional commitment open to empirical refutation, or whether it expresses a tacit, embodied framework in which deviation is structurally impossible by design. If such a criterion exists, it would seem to allow us to read the ontology of belief directly from material behavior and thereby resolve the hermeneutic difficulty; if it does not, then I am left wondering whether archaeological inference must abandon the language of belief-reconstruction altogether, or whether we are forced to accept that every reconstructed doctrine is necessarily a retrospective imposition on practices that were never propositional to begin with.
