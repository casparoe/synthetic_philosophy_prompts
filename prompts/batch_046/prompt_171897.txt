Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of historiography with deep expertise in the ethics of historical interpretation and the logic of conceptual change. Before answering, do a brief internal audit: map the logical relations among the commitments listed below, identify hidden entailments, and only then deliver your assessment in a structured form.

I am preparing for a graduate seminar on presentism, and I have arrived at a cluster of views that feel individually plausible but may not cohere. Here is roughly where I stand. (1) Historians should avoid moral presentism: to evaluate past societies fairly, we must first understand their practices within the normative frameworks they actually inhabited, not judge them by contemporary standards they could not have known. (2) There is nonetheless a transhistorical moral floor—specifically, that inflicting severe, avoidable suffering on sentient beings is wrong regardless of cultural context—so condemning practices like institutionalized torture in ancient regimes is not anachronistic, even if those societies lacked any vocabulary for human rights. (3) Because historical description necessarily employs our present conceptual categories—terms like economy, gender, state, or agency—some form of conceptual presentism is unavoidable; we cannot describe the past without translating it into a vocabulary that did not exist for its inhabitants. (4) I endorse a principle of explanatory charity: we must reconstruct past reasoning on its own terms before any evaluation, but charity does not entail neutrality. Once reconstruction is complete, moral evaluation using our own standards is not only permissible but obligatory whenever a practice violates the transhistorical floor. (5) Recently, however, I have adopted a stricter constraint—call it the articulation condition: if a past actor or society could not have articulated the specific moral concept we now invoke to condemn them (for instance, human rights or systemic injustice), then applying that concept to them constitutes a category error, regardless of whether the underlying harm is objectively real.

Are these five commitments jointly tenable? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. Specify which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which only appear to conflict and can be reconciled with a minor clarification, and identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency—indicating exactly which commitment should be weakened or dropped and what that costs my overall position. In particular, be precise about whether the articulation condition in (5) undermines the transhistorical floor in (2), whether conceptual presentism in (3) collapses into the moral presentism I reject in (1), and whether explanatory charity in (4) survives once I permit evaluation after reconstruction.
