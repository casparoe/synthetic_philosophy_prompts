Design an 80-minute in-class simulation for 16 graduate students in a collective decision seminar that makes them experience the discursive dilemma—the risk that majority judgments on logically interdependent propositions yield an inconsistent collective result—without naming the paradox until the debrief. Frame the scenario around a municipal heritage board deciding whether a 1958 industrial complex should receive protected status, using three propositions defined in the board charter: P, that the complex satisfies an architectural-innovation criterion; Q, that it satisfies a socio-historical narrative criterion; and R, that protected status is warranted, where the charter defines R as holding exactly when (P or Q) holds and an emergency-demolition exemption D does not apply; in this round, no voter believes D applies. Assign each student one of three roles—voting member, procedural chair who enforces rules but does not vote, or recorder who manages public tally boards—plus a small pool of reputation tokens they may wager on whether the collective decision will be consistent. Give every voting member a hidden private ballot requiring sincere judgments on P, Q, and R separately, and forbid any open deliberation before the first aggregation so strategic adjustment is impossible. Run two sequential aggregation rounds using printed ballot cards and a visible tally board: first premise-based, computing majorities on P and Q and then deriving R by applying the charter logic; second conclusion-based, taking a direct majority vote on R alone. Include a precise timeline—5 minutes for role and token allocation, 10 minutes for silent individual judgment, 15 minutes for the premise-based round with derivation, 10 minutes for the conclusion-based round, and 30 minutes for debrief. The debrief should map the contradiction onto the formal tension between logical closure and majoritarian responsiveness, ask students which round felt more legitimate and why, explain that the first run will predictably fail because students conflate their direct vote on R with their derived judgment or because the chair reveals the logical link too early, and specify recovery by rerunning only the silent-judgment phase with stricter silence rules. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce: it cannot model the epistemic-competence argument that voters might be more reliable on discrete premises than on complex conclusions, since all judgments carry equal weight and no expertise differential is built in; the facilitator must supply this limit verbally. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
