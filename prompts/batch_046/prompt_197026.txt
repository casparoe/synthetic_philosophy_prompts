As a retired molecular biologist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I am composing a historical study of a 1950s virology laboratory whose experimental designs were, by the technical standards of the day, exceptionally rigorous, yet whose practices—including the acquisition of human tissue without documented consent and the use of animal surgical preparations that would now violate every institutional review protocol—are regarded by contemporary ethicists as clear violations. Several younger colleagues have accused me of moral whitewashing, arguing that my narrative should explicitly condemn these practices rather than describe them in the neutral institutional language of the era. I have replied that imposing present-day ethical categories risks a form of anachronism that obscures rather than clarifies historical causation, since the actors operated within a normative framework that was actively reinforced by funding bodies, peer reviewers, and regulatory norms of their own time. Nevertheless, I have been struck by a more subtle distinction: it seems there is a difference between holding historical agents accountable for failing to meet the moral standards they actually held, and holding them accountable for failing to meet standards that were epistemically available to them—that is, standards supported by arguments and evidence already present within their intellectual environment, even if not widely adopted. If the latter form of judgment is philosophically legitimate, then my neutrality may not be historical fidelity but a kind of evasion. I would value your guidance: is there a coherent philosophical basis for distinguishing explanatory contextualization from moral endorsement in historiography, and can one apply contemporary ethical criteria to past scientific practice without treating history as a courtroom for the present?
