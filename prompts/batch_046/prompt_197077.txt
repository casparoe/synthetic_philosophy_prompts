I am preparing the central argument of a term paper for my seminar on comparative constitutional federalism, and I have adopted the following position: Article 5(3) TEU's principle of subsidiarity should be read not as a procedural competence-allocation rule but as a substantive constraint requiring that any EU-level measure demonstrate that decentralized action by member states would produce not merely distributive inefficiency but specifically structural fragmentation—a breakdown of shared constitutional identity across levels—that centralized action uniquely prevents. I derive this reading partly from the German Federal Constitutional Court's Lisbon judgment's emphasis on democratic legitimation through state-level structures, and partly from the Swiss cantonal model's reliance on direct-democratic spillover control, while treating the U.S. anti-commandeering line running from New York v. United States to Printz v. United States as a negative foil that rejects precisely this substantive framing. I want a rigorous stress-test. What institutional observations, legal developments, or conceptual arguments would genuinely count against this interpretation? Specifically: what empirical patterns in multi-level governance—say, the actual operation of the German Bundesrat in blocking or enabling EU directives, or documented failures of Swiss cantonal autonomy to contain cross-border externalities—would force a defender of this view to concede that the structural-fragmentation criterion either collapses into ordinary procedural review or functions as a hidden centralization mandate? Which rival predictions does my view make that proceduralist accounts do not—for instance, about when the EU should abstain despite clear efficiency gains—and what evidence would show those predictions fail? I want you to distinguish defeaters that would be fatal from those that are merely awkward, to identify which qualifications I have already built in—such as limiting the criterion to constitutional-identity disruptions rather than ordinary policy disagreements—that might insulate the view, and to assess honestly whether this insulation is a virtue or a vice that renders the position unfalsifiable. In short, map the exposure: where is this interpretation actually vulnerable, and where is it protected by design?
