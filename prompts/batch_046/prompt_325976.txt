I am trying to stabilize a particular reading of Maimonides on animal sacrifice and need an internal consistency audit before I present it. Here are the commitments I hold together:

(1) The sacrificial cult (korbanot) is a pedagogical concession: God retained the form of animal slaughter within ritual boundaries not because divine being requires blood, but because the Israelites, emerging from cultures of animal worship and sacrificial idolatry, could not have been abruptly weaned to aniconic prayer without psychological rupture. The ritual shell was preserved; the idolatrous content was emptied out.

(2) Therefore, the normative value of sacrifice is purely instrumental and therapeutic. Its religious worth derives entirely from its transitional function—channeling an existing propensity toward blood-ritual into monotheistic worship. Once that transition succeeds, the practice should become dispensable; it possesses no intrinsic moral or metaphysical status.

(3) Nevertheless, I take Maimonides to be committed to the rationality of every commandment: each law has a purpose (ta'am), and apparent arbitrariness always signals incomplete human understanding rather than divine irrationality.

(4) I read the post-Temple rabbinic shift to prayer—the service of the heart—as the fulfillment, not the failure, of this trajectory. The concession successfully worked itself out of a job.

(5) At the same time, I want to claim that the sacrificial system operates as a technology of moral attention. By confining the killing of sentient beings within strict procedural, spatial, and temporal boundaries, the ritual does not suppress awareness of animal suffering but structurally amplifies it. The practitioner is compelled to confront the moral cost of dominion, making the violence of life-taking visible rather than invisible.

Do these five hang together? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. Specifically:

- For each genuine tension, state what would have to give—whether (2)'s pure instrumentalism, (5)'s claim about intrinsic moral friction, or (3)'s universal rational-purpose thesis—and what that revision costs the overall interpretation.
- Tell me whether there is one revision that resolves the greatest number of conflicts simultaneously, and whether that revision is philosophically affordable.
