I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but after a long discussion with friends about why some research programs feel like disciplined engineering while others feel like astrology with extra parameters, I have become convinced of a modified demarcation criterion I call “exposed computational risk.” On this view, a theory is scientific not merely if it is logically falsifiable in the Popperian sense, but only if its practitioners specify in advance the precise failure conditions that would trigger abandonment or core revision, and only if the algorithmic complexity of any ad hoc patch needed to absorb an apparent counterexample exceeds a threshold that the community itself declares ex ante. Pseudoscience, by contrast, is characterized not by absolute unfalsifiability but by low-cost immunization: it permits infinite repairs of negligible descriptive complexity—verbal adjustments, hidden variables with no independent constraints, or post-hoc reinterpretations—that never breach the predetermined abandonment protocol. I find this framing attractive because it mirrors how we actually evaluate production systems: we do not merely ask whether a model can fail, but whether its failure modes are specified, monitored, and costly enough to correct that we are forced to retrain rather than patch.

What I want to understand is what would genuinely count against this position—not just the standard objections that naive falsificationism is either too permissive or too severe, but the specific defeaters that target the computational-risk axis itself. Which intuitions about scientific practice, which historical episodes (theories saved by genuinely complex structural revisions versus those saved by cheap verbal adjustments), and which formal results—perhaps from algorithmic information theory, statistical learning theory, or the structure of auxiliary hypotheses—would undermine the claim that repair-cost is the right demarcation metric? Which of these objections would a committed defender of the view concede as damaging, and which could be deflected by refining the complexity measure or tightening the ex ante protocol? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate whether the view risks collapsing back into conventional falsificationism once the complexity metric is fixed, or whether it can be insulated so thoroughly that nothing ever counts as a cheap patch. I am especially interested in whether there are domains—quantum mechanics, psychoanalysis, evolutionary biology, or contemporary machine-learning research—where the criterion succeeds revealingly or fails in a way that exposes a hidden circularity. What, in short, would have to be true for me to be wrong, and which of those possibilities should I take most seriously?
