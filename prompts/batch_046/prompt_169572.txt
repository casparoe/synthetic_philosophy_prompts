I recently became a parent, and the long-horizon forecasts about food security and climate have made me want to understand how a scientific theory survives being wrong in the near term. Analyze Paul Ehrlich’s predictions in his late-1960s work The Population Bomb—specifically the claim that mass famine in Asia and elsewhere was unavoidable by the 1970s and 1980s—through the lens of Malthusian population theory and the philosophy of long-run scientific prediction. I am not asking for an ecological history, which I take as given: I want the episode treated as a case study in whether a theory can be “right but early,” falsified and then rescued, or genuinely refuted. Map the specific predictions (famine timelines, global yield ceilings, population curves) against what actually occurred with the Green Revolution, then apply the Malthusian framework together with the Boserupian counter-claim that population pressure drives technological intensification rather than collapse. Do the yield gains represent a genuine escape from the Malthusian trap, or do they merely postpone it by substituting fossil-energy and phosphorus inputs for land, turning positive checks into deferred negative ones? Turn the analysis back on the theory: does Malthus’s failure to anticipate the Green Revolution show that his mechanism was wrong, or only that his time horizon and auxiliary hypotheses were inadequate? In particular, address whether introducing technological innovation as an unanticipated variable constitutes a legitimate long-run rescue or an ad hoc immunization strategy that renders the theory unfalsifiable in principle. Note also where the Malthusian lens is silent—specifically on distributional and institutional failures that caused local famines even during aggregate surpluses. Finally, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
