As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy with the same rigor I once reserved for instrumentation, I have become fixated on a peculiar institutional design in modern central banking—one that seems technically coherent but philosophically unstable. Consider the following practice: a central bank issues fiat currency with no commodity redemption promise, treats this currency in its accounts as a liability matched against assets that include non-redeemable gold reserves and foreign exchange, remunerates commercial-bank reserves at a policy rate that can fall below zero, and simultaneously conducts public communication aimed at anchoring inflation expectations and preserving the value of money as if the unit of account were a durable container of labor or natural worth rather than a mutable social relation.

What philosophical commitments about the nature and value of money does this specific arrangement presuppose? Must it presuppose a Berkeleyan conception—money as a purely conventional counter or ticket representing labor and goods, with no intrinsic value of its own—or does it tacitly rely on a Lockean or commodity theory in which value requires ultimate anchoring in scarce material substance? Can the practice be rendered consistent with a strictly relational, immaterialist ontology of money, or does the combination of negative-rate reserve remuneration, gold-reserve confidence anchoring, and rhetoric about stored purchasing power commit the institution to contradictory premises?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of money—its ontology, its axiology, and its account of value preservation—that makes this practice intelligible. Take a definite, committed position: argue for one reconstruction over its rivals, state clearly whether you judge the practice philosophically coherent, and specify exactly what institutional features—reserve-accounting rules, the legal status of gold holdings, the framing of inflation-targeting mandates, or the treatment of reserve remuneration—would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced by a consistently Berkeleyan alternative, and what would have to change for a consistently commodity-based one. Do not lay out a menu of options; defend one interpretation and its practical consequences.
