Restate the core philosophical debate about collective memory versus critical history—specifically the normative conflict that arises when a community's living social memory and the archival record disagree about a past state central to group identity—using only the vocabulary and reasoning of restoration ecology. Translate the three principal positions: (i) that collective memory should prevail over the record for identity and policy purposes; (ii) that the critical historical record should correct and override false or distorted memory; and (iii) that the two serve incommensurable functions and must be held in productive tension rather than resolved. In each case, express the claim through ecological concepts such as reference conditions, proxy reconstruction, stakeholder knowledge, shifting baselines, counterfactual states, and management objectives, without relying on philosophical terminology except where it is unavoidable and explicitly flagged.

Then deliver the residue report: for each translated position, specify which elements map cleanly onto ecological practice, which survive only with distortion (for instance, translating moral authority or phenomenological presence into weighting criteria for stakeholder input), and which have no counterpart in ecological vocabulary at all (for instance, the idea of remembrance as an experience or the normative notion that one source should give way). Finally, apply all three translated stances to the following concrete case: a community's oral tradition describes a dense old-growth forest in a valley that sediment and pollen proxies do not confirm, yet local identity and current conservation funding depend on treating that forest as the legitimate reference state for restoration.
