I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I'm trying to determine whether a cluster of beliefs I hold about rational disagreement, belief formation, and normative judgment actually cohere. Here is my current position:

(1) I accept the common prior assumption in rational choice: if two agents share the same prior probabilities and update rationally on each other's beliefs, they should not be able to agree to disagree.

(2) I apply this framework to empirical disputes: when well-informed people persistently disagree about a factual matter, I tend to diagnose either divergent priors or some failure of rational updating.

(3) At the same time, I think moral disagreement is often intractable in a way that empirical disagreement is not; reasonable people can share all relevant non-moral facts and still reach opposing normative conclusions, and I do not think this always reduces to different priors or irrationality.

(4) I find evolutionary debunking arguments persuasive: they suggest our innate moral intuitions are unreliable guides to mind-independent moral facts. Nevertheless, in practice I still rely on my own moral judgments—especially about AI alignment and distributive fairness—as if they carried genuine evidential weight.

(5) Drawing on distributed systems, I take the Byzantine fault tolerance model seriously: consensus can survive malicious nodes provided they remain below a one-third threshold. I analogize social disagreement to this framework, treating persistent disagreement not as proof of irrationality but as a system that remains stable so long as "faulty" or biased reasoning stays under some critical fraction.

Are these five commitments jointly tenable? In particular, does (1) together with (2) commit me to explaining all persistent disagreement in terms of prior divergence or irrational updating, which (3) explicitly rejects for the moral case? Is (4) guilty of special pleading—rejecting the authority of evolved intuition in the abstract while appealing to it in concrete judgments—or is there a principled distinction that saves it? And does (5) genuinely resolve the tension in (3), or does importing a fault-tolerance model into epistemology conflict with the common prior framework in (1)? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify the least costly revision, and indicate whether a single change could resolve the majority of the conflicts.
