Design a 75-minute classroom activity for a philosophy-of-science seminar of about 15 undergraduates that makes participants feel the rational pull of retaining a false hard core within a Lakatosian research programme. Invent a fictional empirical domain—do not use historical cases such as Copernican astronomy or Newtonian mechanics—so that students reason from first principles rather than received history. Divide the class into two competing research programmes, each assigned an incompatible hard-core claim about the domain (for instance, “all signal decay is entropy-driven” versus “all signal decay is template-driven”). Over four rounds, reveal anomalous data cards one at a time. Before seeing the next round’s data, each programme may either register a protective-belt auxiliary hypothesis that also yields a specific, risky prediction about that upcoming data—earning progressive points—or adjust the belt only after seeing the anomaly to accommodate it—earning degenerating points—or abandon the hard core entirely. The scoring rules should make it genuinely optimal, across multiple rounds, to retain a hard core that is already contradicted by round-two data, provided the belt keeps generating correct novel predictions; but if the belt merely patches past anomalies without predicting new ones, the programme accumulates degenerating points and eventually loses rational standing. Do not lecture on Lakatos beforehand and do not name him until the debrief. After the final round, debrief by mapping each move onto the progressive versus degenerating problem-shift distinction, identifying exactly which feature of the rules made retention of the false core attractive, noting what happens if that predictive-reward feature is removed, and being explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—namely the multi-decade temporal scale and the social distribution of scientists across programmes—so the facilitator supplies those missing elements directly.
