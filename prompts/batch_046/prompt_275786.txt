I am a high school senior presenting at an interdisciplinary symposium on classical philosophy and contemporary epistemology. I am defending the following thesis: in Plato's dialogue the Meno, knowledge is more valuable than true belief not chiefly because it is stable or justified, but because it carries a distinctive social-epistemic capacity that I call an "epistemic vouching license." On this reading, knowing that p allows the knower to serve as a trusted node in testimonial networks—others can depend on the knower's assertion without performing independent verification—whereas someone who merely holds a true belief lacks the internal tether of having worked out the reason and therefore cannot reliably transmit belief without risking the corruption of the network, even when she happens to be correct. The audience will include: a classical philologist who reads the dialogue as being about metaphysical recollection and the immortality of the soul; an analytic epistemologist who will press Gettier-style counterexamples and ask why justification alone is insufficient; a social epistemologist who doubts that testimony requires any special license beyond statistical reliability; and a cognitive scientist who will ask whether neuroscience distinguishes the mechanisms of knowing from those of accidentally correct belief. Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually face, ordered from the one that could most seriously damage my argument to the one that is merely annoying, phrased in the vocabulary and tone each audience member would actually use. For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for about two minutes of speaking without notes, and explicitly note where that reply is weakest or concedes too much. Finally, include the single objection I am hoping nobody raises—the one that reveals the largest gap between Plato's original concerns and my modern framing about social transmission. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I will have no access to external sources during the presentation.
