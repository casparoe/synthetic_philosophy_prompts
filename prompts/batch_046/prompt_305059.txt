In a philosophy-of-action discussion, a colleague invokes the slogan that nobody errs willingly to argue that hyperbolic discounting cannot be a genuine case of weakness of will: if an agent truly judged a larger-later reward superior, she would not choose the smaller-sooner one, so apparent intertemporal conflict must be a failure of cognition rather than a conflict between judgment and desire. Could you separate the precise claims this slogan might be making — ranging from tautological readings to strong metaphysical theses about the unity of belief and desire — and for each indicate (a) whether it is defensible, (b) whether it is compatible with the empirical psychology of intertemporal choice, and (c) whether it actually supports the dismissal of hyperbolic discounting as akrasia? Lay the readings out as a menu, and include an honest treatment of the strongest interpretation that allows weakness of will to survive.
