I work as a quantitative risk analyst and am fluent in robust portfolio optimization, Knightian uncertainty, ambiguity-averse decision rules such as maximin and α-maximin, and sensitivity analysis across incommensurable asset classes, but I have no formal background in moral philosophy. I have encountered a summary of a position in the literature on moral uncertainty—specifically, decision-theoretic approaches to action under uncertainty about which moral theory is correct. The summary runs as follows. The core claim is that each credible moral theory can be treated as specifying a choice-worthiness function over available actions; the agent assigns subjective credences to these theories and should select the action that maximizes expected choice-worthiness, with expectations taken across the theories weighted by those credences. The summary then adds two further twists. First, inter-theoretic comparison of choice-worthiness requires a common currency—typically some measure of well-being—without which the expectation is undefined because the outputs of different theories are incommensurable. Second, the agent should apply an ambiguity-aversion filter that discards actions which are optimal only under theories assigned very low credence, or whose ranking is highly unstable under small perturbations to the credence distribution.

Please restate this entire position—both the core expectation-maximization thesis and the two added twists—using exclusively the vocabulary and conceptual machinery of robust quantitative decision-making: portfolio weights, model ambiguity sets, a numeraire, Knightian versus probabilistic uncertainty, maximin criteria, and robustness checks. Be precise about the structural mapping: for example, does assigning credence to a moral theory correspond to assigning weights to models within an ambiguity set, or does it differ in what the weights represent and how they are updated?

Then provide the residue report. Identify which philosophical commitments translate cleanly into financial or decision-theoretic analogues, which require distortion or fudge (and specify the nature of that distortion), and which—if any—have no counterpart whatsoever in how analysts handle model uncertainty. In particular, examine whether the demand for a common currency of well-being is genuinely analogous to requiring a numeraire for comparing returns across asset classes, or whether it introduces a normative constraint that has no parallel in robust optimization. Finally, assess whether the ambiguity-aversion filter is doing the same work as a maximin or robustness check, or whether it relies on a notion of moral credibility that cannot be captured by statistical model confidence or by standard ambiguity-set constructions. I want to know whether my suspicion that this is largely robust portfolio theory with normative vocabulary layered on top is well-founded, or whether the translation reveals a structural feature that makes moral uncertainty genuinely different from quantitative model uncertainty.
