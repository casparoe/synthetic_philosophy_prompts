In a graduate seminar on classical Chinese philosophy and the ethics of mortality, a classmate summarized Zhuangzi’s reaction to his wife’s death with the slogan “death is just transformation—like a caterpillar becoming a butterfly—so grief is a confusion about nature.” She deployed it to support two very different conclusions: first, that personal death is not a harm because the self does not persist across the change, and second, that mourning is therefore irrational in roughly the way mourning melted ice would be. The slogan seems to be doing all the work, yet I cannot tell whether it expresses a modest metaphysical observation, a denial of diachronic identity, a normative claim that natural change cannot be bad, an epistemic diagnosis of our fear of death as resting on a false dualism between being and non-being, or a more radical “open individualist” thesis in which the boundary between my life and the cycles of the ecosystem was always conventional. Could you lay out these distinct precise theses—perhaps including a “normative continuity” reading on which death harms only if it interrupts a process that should continue, and a deprivation-account counter-reading—state for each whether it is defensible on its own, whether it actually licenses dismissing grief, and whether it fits the texts we associate with Zhuangzi, particularly his drumming and singing at his wife’s death and the butterfly-dream passage that people often conflate with the transformation idea? I am especially interested in whether there is a coherent middle position that accepts transformation without collapsing into the view that no particular death matters, since that collapse seems to be the move my classmate needs but also the one that makes the slogan ethically hollow. Please also say what would change your assessment: for instance, would a strong deprivation-based argument about the badness of death, or a philological point about the use of hua in the Inner Chapters, lead you to favor one disambiguation over the others, and where in your reasoning is the risk of over-reading modern ecological metaphysics back into the text greatest?
