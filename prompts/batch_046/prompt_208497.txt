In an interdisciplinary reading group I have been running on geological time and environmental politics, a debate over formalizing the Anthropocene as a distinct historical-geological period has stalled in a way I find intellectually paralyzing. One faction, composed mostly of stratigraphers and Earth-system scientists, insists that any ratified epoch must be anchored to a globally synchronous marker—such as the radionuclide signature of early-1950s nuclear testing—because without such a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point the term collapses into mere political rhetoric. The opposing faction, drawing on historical ecology and postcolonial geography, counters that selecting a single start date—whether 1784, 1610, or 1952—inevitably flattens regionally asynchronous timelines of deforestation, irrigation, and extraction into one universal narrative, effectively granting geological authority to the industrial North Atlantic while marginalizing longer, non-industrial histories of land transformation elsewhere.

What makes the impasse frustrating is that both sides accept the empirical reality of profound human planetary change; they do not dispute climate data, extinction rates, or sediment chemistry. Yet after more than a year of discussion neither side has conceded an inch. Is this disagreement ultimately about hidden factual questions—say, whether a truly global synchronous signal even exists—or is it about what counts as legitimate authority over temporal classification? Could it be that the two groups are answering different questions under one name: one asking when Earth-system dynamics shifted measurably, and the other asking when specific human societies began restructuring local ecologies at scale? Or is the stall driven by bedrock normative intuitions about blame, agency, and who gets to name an epoch—intuitions that neither side can argue for without begging the question? Given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate? Would progress require a formal result about stratigraphic correlation, a conceptual distinction between geological and historical periodization, or an explicit acknowledgment that the disagreement is irreducibly political and therefore should not seek geological ratification at all?
