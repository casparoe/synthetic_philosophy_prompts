My friend and I spent several hours last night in a long, somewhat circular argument about Herbert Simon’s idea of satisficing, and she ended up staking out precise verdicts about whether particular decision processes count as rational despite falling well short of global optimization. She holds that an emergency physician relying on recognition-primed heuristics under extreme time pressure is fully rational; that a chess engine deliberately restricted to shallow search is rational only relative to its computational budget, not simpliciter; that a home buyer who stops searching once an apartment meets an aspiration level is rational precisely when that level was adaptively calibrated rather than arbitrarily fixed; that a climate-policy office using an imprecise but immediately actionable model is rational provided the model’s uncertainty is transparent to the decision-makers; and that a large language model using greedy decoding without any explicit search or revisable stopping rule is not rational at all, because it possesses neither a transparent constraint nor an adjustable aspiration threshold. I am trying to see whether these judgments cohere around a single principle or whether they split across competing interpretations of bounded rationality.

The case I want to test is a self-driving vehicle whose control system operates under a hard real-time compute ceiling. Its designers implemented a bounded-risk heuristic: if sensor fusion reports uncertainty above a fixed numerical threshold, the system executes the safest available maneuver—an immediate hard brake—rather than computing an optimal trajectory. In one documented incident this caused a low-speed rear-end collision with minor injuries, though an optimal planner with greater compute might have executed a safe swerve. The manufacturer defends the system as classically satisficing—rational given genuinely binding temporal and processing constraints—while critics argue the fixed threshold never updates after near-misses, making the system closer to her disapproved language-model case. Given her track record, what verdict should she deliver? Lay out the two or three rules that best accommodate her earlier judgments—say, rationality as optimization-relative-to-transparent-external-constraints, rationality as possession of an internal feedback loop that revises aspiration levels, or rationality as achievement of the best available outcome regardless of mechanism—say which predicts approval or rejection of the vehicle, identify the closest analog among her five prior cases, and propose one further hypothetical—perhaps an autonomous agent that dynamically raises its own risk threshold after each failure—that would force a decisive choice between the rules. I care more about the consistency of her implicit framework than about the vehicle itself.
