I'm meant to be giving the opening adress at a workshop next month called "Thick Memory, Thin Forgiveness" — it's in the town hall of a place that just had a horrible public row about whether to keep or destroy the local council registry of wartime evacuations. The room will be split right down the middle: philosophers and memory-studies people who know Margalit's distinction between thick and thin relations of remembering, and Rieff's point that forgetting can be moral labour rather than failure; and local archivists, community historians, and a few older residents who actually lived through it, none of whom came for a lecture.

I want the talk to be about eight minutes, and I need the tension between the duty to remember and the case for forgetting to actually structure what I say — not just be decoration. I'm particularly stuck on whether "choosing not to preserve" is ethically different from "choosing to erase," and I want to navigate that without either glorifying destruction or treating every forgotten record as betrayal. There's a niche idea I'm playing with — mnemonic hospitality — the thought that archives (and communities) have a kind of limited capacity, so selective forgetting might be an obligation to the living rather than just an insult to the dead. I don't want citations or a reading list, obviously, but I also don't want to sound like I'm just name-dropping for credibility. Can you write something that lands for both halves? It should welcome people and set the tone, make clear we won't pretend remembering is always virtuous, and maybe use one concrete image tied to the local dispute without inventing specific historical facts. Keep it casual but don't dodge the hard part: sometimes the most respectful thing is to let a memory stay unarchived.
