I am working on the normative structure of advance directives in progressive cognitive decline, specifically the long-standing dispute over whether a competent earlier self retains authority over a later self that has lost the psychological capacities—rational reflection, narrative continuity, recognition of prior commitments as one's own—that originally grounded autonomous choice. One camp treats the directive as a diachronic legislative act: the competent agent binds her future, and clinical obligations flow from respecting that act regardless of whether the later individual is content, disoriented, or affectively altered. The opposing camp holds that authority is an ongoing relational or executive status; once the psychological substrate that supported autonomous choice has eroded, the directive addresses a subject who no longer exists in the relevant moral sense, and present welfare interests, however minimal or diffuse, must govern care.

The debate has stalled structurally rather than empirically. Both sides have answered every standard objection—paradoxes of self-binding, third-party interpretation, adaptive preferences in dementia—without convergence. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not ultimately about personal identity, though both sides invoke continuity and discontinuity, but about two incompatible conceptions of normative authority across time. One side models authority on a testamentary or contractual logic: a competent agent can generate obligations that survive the loss of the capacities that made her an agent. The other models it on an executive logic: authority requires an ongoing capacity to authorize, revise, or at least recognize the directive, and its absence dissolves the obligation rather than transferring it to a different subject.

If that diagnosis is correct, the sides are not disagreeing about a single shared question—who should decide?—but pursuing different questions under one heading. One asks whether a past act of will can create present obligations for a being who cannot endorse it; the other asks whether present moral subjects have standing to be governed by past acts they can no longer recognize. Is this framing right, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about the metaphysics of personal identity, or about whether affective states in severe dementia constitute interests in the morally relevant sense—doing the real work?

Please diagnose the disagreement by engaging what each position actually commits to regarding authority, continuity, and welfare, not by surveying the literature. Specifically: is the impasse verbal (centered on competing uses of autonomy or person), substantive but masked (about identity or interests), or structural (two different normative questions pursued under one title)? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would a distinction—say, between legislative and executive authority over one's future, or between domain-specific authorities such as bodily integrity versus social identity—allow both sides to concede partial ground? Or is progress impossible because the case activates bedrock intuitions about whether suffering experienced by a severely demented patient is genuinely the suffering of the individual who signed the directive?
