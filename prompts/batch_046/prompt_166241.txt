I have just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether the nonidentity problem is really just a metaphysical curiosity or whether it undermines the way institutions actually think about future harm, and I want to see the tension laid out precisely. Take the institutional practice — common in biomedical ethics committees and long-range environmental policy assessments — of evaluating a proposed intervention by asking whether it causes 'net harm to the people who will live in the future', where the comparison is made between the actual welfare of those who will exist if the policy goes ahead and the counterfactual welfare of the different people who would have existed had it not. These same bodies typically reject the nonidentity reasoning that one cannot harm a particular person by bringing them into existence with a disadvantage, provided the alternative was never existing at all; they treat that reasoning as a dodge that should not block regulatory concern. What philosophical commitments about personal identity, the metaphysics of harm, and the scope of moral standing does that practice presuppose? Must it assume that there are 'slots' for future individuals that can be filled by numerically different people, or can it be reconstructed on a purely modal or counterfactual theory of harm that remains neutral on diachronic identity? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the person that renders the boards' reasoning coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say whether an institution could consistently adopt the nonidentity view without abandoning its concern for future welfare — and, if so, what structural changes to its evaluation framework that revision would demand.
