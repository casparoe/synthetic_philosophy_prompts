Next month I defend my dissertation chapter at an interdisciplinary workshop on geological epoch-making. My thesis is that the Anthropocene should be formally dated to 1986—not because of Chernobyl's radiological signature alone, but because that year marks the first global event that simultaneously produced a distinct stratigraphic marker (the transnational cesium-137 pulse detectable in ice cores and marine sediments) and triggered a permanent restructuring of international legal regimes governing ecological harm, including the revision of nuclear liability conventions and the first binding multilateral codification of transboundary pollution liability. I argue that epoch boundaries should be defined neither purely by stratigraphic signals, as the ICS demands, nor purely by historical causation, as environmental historians prefer, but by what I call "juridical-stratigraphic coupling"—the point at which a physical layer becomes inseparable from a new global legal ontology that treats Earth-system disruption as a governable event rather than a natural background condition. The audience includes two ICS stratigraphers committed to the 1950 bomb-spike golden spike, an environmental historian who advocates for the 1610 Orbis hypothesis, a legal scholar who thinks epoch-making authority should be transferred entirely from scientific bodies to the UN General Assembly, and several science-and-technology-studies graduate students skeptical that law and geology can be coupled without category error. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each of these people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—that selecting 1986 over 1952 or 1979 is arbitrary once you abandon pure stratigraphy—and suggest how to reply without sounding defensive.
