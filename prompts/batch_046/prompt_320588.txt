I’m facilitating a 90-minute workshop for about 12 philosophy graduate students on persistence through change. I want a structured, in-class game—not a lecture or narrated thought experiment—that forces participants to live through the tension between treating an object as wholly present at each time versus treating it as a four-dimensional worm composed of temporal parts. To avoid another Ship of Theseus retread, frame the exercise around a distributed “artifact network”: over six rounds, each student manages a cluster of property-bearing nodes that gain, lose, and exchange features (function, location, material composition) according to hidden transition rules drawn randomly each round. Half the group are assigned as Synthesis Guards (the endurantist analogue): they must collectively maintain a running ledger declaring which node-clusters are strictly identical across rounds, earning group points for consistency but suffering steep penalties whenever their identity claim violates a secret “binding constraint” (for example, no more than 40% feature overlap, or a break in causal dependency) that is revealed only after the vote. The other half are Trace Architects (the perdurantist analogue): they do not vote on identity at all, but instead build directed “continuity edges” linking nodes round-to-round, earning individual points for edges that survive a hidden “causal coherence” check, and losing points for orphaned nodes they failed to link.

The payoff structure should use only symbolic course-credit tokens, with a designed crisis in round four: Synthesis Guards discover that maintaining identity across high-turnover transitions forces them either to accept wildly counterintuitive sameness claims—a node-cluster retaining none of its original properties is still “the same artifact”—or to fragment every object into ephemeral micro-instances that make practical coordination impossible. Meanwhile, Trace Architects find that without some integrator-style commitment—without asking “which worm is this?”—they cannot justify why anyone should care about repairing or preserving a particular lineage rather than simply optimizing node-states independently. I want the breakdown to emerge from their own allocations and arguments, with the facilitator speaking almost not at all until the final 20-minute debrief. That debrief should map each round’s crisis onto the formal distinction (three-dimensionalism with strict identity conditions versus four-dimensionalism with temporal parts and relations), predict the two predictable failure modes (Synthesis Guards usually retreat into conventionalism or essentialism; Trace Architects often deny all diachronic concern), and be explicit about which feature—most likely the first-person phenomenological sense of being one continuous thinker—this mechanism-design exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not name endurantism or perdurantism until the debrief, and do not include any bibliography or reading list; the design should stand entirely on its internal mechanics.
