I have just finished a long and unresolved argument with a friend over whether leisure can possess any moral standing that is not derived from its utility for work. She reads Weber’s Protestant work ethic as showing that rest is always instrumentally justified, whereas I have been struck by the contrast between France’s 35-hour week—where reduced labour was framed as a social right—and Japan’s karoshi, where death from overwork is treated almost as a credential of moral seriousness. Tomorrow I am delivering the opening remarks at a workshop on the philosophy of labour; the audience will include both specialists and the family members of the organisers, so the speech must work for both without reducing the philosophy to mere decoration. I want the talk to turn on a specific conceptual tension: is there such a thing as a non-instrumental right to leisure, or is every claim to rest morally overdetermined by prior obligations to produce? The idea should structure what I can and cannot say—I must not resolve the contradiction, but let it sit there—so that praising the value of work does not automatically cancel the claim for rest, and vice versa. Please write a brief opening address of about five minutes, composed in British English, that holds this tension structurally for a mixed audience. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
