Write a parody of a software terms-of-service agreement—approximately 650 words, formatted as numbered clauses in deadpan corporate legalese—for a fictional "Universal Radical Interpretation Service" that provides real-time translation of unknown languages according to Davidsonian principles. The satire should target Davidson's theory of radical interpretation itself: the principle of charity, the holism of belief and meaning, the rejection of conceptual schemes and incommensurability, and the triangulation condition. Exaggerate real features rather than invent false ones: include a clause that treats charity as a binding contractual duty rather than a methodological constraint; a "No Conceptual Schemes" clause that voids any translation invoking an untranslatable worldview; a "Holistic Renegotiation" clause stating that disputing a single term requires reopening the entire agreement; and a "Triangulation" dispute-resolution mechanism requiring a third-party observer and shared causal interaction with the world. The humor should arise from pushing these philosophical commitments into bureaucratic literalism. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan—specialists should recognize the targets without the text winking, and non-specialists should still grasp the absurdity of treating interpretation as a legal contract. After the agreement, append a brief postscript of about 100 words stating how confident the parody is in its characterization of Davidson's view and identifying the specific point where it is least faithful or most reliant on a contested reading—particularly where it collapses methodological charity into a normative obligation, or where it treats the triangulation argument as a procedural rule rather than an account of the conditions for thought.
