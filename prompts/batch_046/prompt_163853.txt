I'm delivering the opening address at a museum exhibition titled Unseen Works, which displays paintings and sculptures that were never shown during their creators' lifetimes. The audience is split between philosophers who know Berkeley's master argument in detail and museum staff and artists who do not. I want a ten-minute speech in American English that lets the argument do structural work: use Berkeley's claim that to be is to be perceived, together with the specific strain introduced when he exempts spirits—perceiving minds—from that principle, to determine what I can and cannot say about whether these works existed before this exhibition. Don't treat Berkeley as decoration; let the tension in his exemption shape my claim about curatorial responsibility—whether hanging these pieces completes their being or merely reveals it. The specialists should recognize the philosophical machinery, while everyone else should grasp the stakes without any citations, reading lists, or references to secondary literature.
