I sit on my university's Joint Research Funding Council, which evaluates interdisciplinary grant proposals through a three-part peer-review framework: reviewers assess theoretical coherence, empirical feasibility, and ethical compliance as separate premises, and also cast a holistic vote on whether the proposal should be funded. We have recently encountered instances of the discursive dilemma—majorities endorsing each individual premise while rejecting the funding conclusion, and vice versa—which has undermined confidence in our collective rationality. To address this, our chair has proposed adopting "sequential premise locking": reviewers vote on the three premises in fixed order, and once a premise secures majority support it is locked; the funding conclusion is then derived automatically by logical closure rather than being subjected to an independent vote. Proponents argue this eliminates contradiction; opponents contend it suppresses legitimate holistic judgment, violates the independence of conclusions, and may fail when premises themselves rest on nested sub-evaluations. For Thursday's plenary, please draft the Council's formal decision memo: recommend whether to adopt sequential premise locking (possibly with modifications), to reject it in favor of conclusion-based aggregation, or to implement a hybrid protocol; specify a concrete implementation timeline; and provide reasoning that takes seriously the strongest objection to your recommended course, particularly the concern that locking premises ignores reviewers' epistemic authority over the overall proposal.
