In philosophical discussions of human extinction—especially those shaped by Samuel Scheffler’s claim that the expectation of a continuing human “afterlife” underwrites much present meaning—the dispute over whether extinction is bad chiefly because it eliminates potential lives or because it collapses the value-structure of current lives has reached a stubborn impasse. Defenders of the deprivation account treat the harm as an aggregate failure to realize future well-being; advocates of the afterlife conjecture reply that the harm is already internal to present agency, since long-term projects, cultural transmission, and moral commitments presuppose an ongoing human story. For years, seminar participants have agreed on the empirical premises—that extinction ends all future lives, that present people care about descendants—yet have not moved an inch, with one side treating isolated-survivor thought experiments as decisive and the other dismissing them as psychologically unrealistic. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden metaphysical dispute about whether value can depend on outcomes no present agent will observe; a disagreement about which cases count as data; or are the sides answering different questions under one name—one about intertemporal welfare aggregation, the other about the conditions of meaningful agency? Present your diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says, and propose what would count as progress given that diagnosis: for instance, a distinction between psychological dependency and structural dependency (the idea that the value of a project may depend not on anyone’s mental state but on the temporal architecture that makes it a project at all), an intertemporal rationality result, or a reframing of the question. Also say whether any feature of the disagreement suggests the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard, and be explicit about which feature of the dispute your proposed move cannot resolve and must be supplied by argument.
