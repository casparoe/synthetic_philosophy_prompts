I'm trying to figure out my actual view on honoring sunk costs, going by gut on cases, not theory. Buying a $200 nonrefundable concert ticket then getting sick: going feels wrong, like throwing good health after bad money. A small town keeps repairing an old bridge almost nobody uses because they already spent millions; that feels wrong too, but somehow less clearly wrong than the concert? A grad student spends four years on a PhD line that's going nowhere; quitting feels rational, but if she built a one-of-a-kind apparatus that can only run this exact failed experiment, spending one more year to publish the negative result feels okay, even kind of honorable. A couple stays in a miserable marriage because they've been together twenty years; that feels irrational and sad, full stop. But then there's a company that keeps funding a failing software rewrite because the team has now mastered the legacy codebase inside and out, and that knowledge is useless anywhere else. Here I waver -- is the deep knowledge a sunk cost or an asset? I keep going back and forth on that one. Is there a principel that captures all of these, or at least gets close, and what does it say about the case I can't settle -- my friend refusing to sell her house at a loss even though she needs to move for work, saying "I'd be locking in the loss"? I want the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on a couple cases I haven't mentioned (say, a artist finishing a huge canvas she hates because she already bought the expensive linen, and a city council finishing a half-built stadium with no team to play in it), and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it has to drop if it wants to stay simple rather than just adding an exception for every feeling.
