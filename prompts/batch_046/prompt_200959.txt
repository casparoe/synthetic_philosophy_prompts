Two doctoral students in philosophy of mind, Ravi and Sofia, argued over lunch about whether modern neuroimaging strengthens the classical analogical argument for other minds. (1) Ravi said the traditional argument is weak because it moves from my private consciousness to your outward behavior and then back to your inner life, but today we can observe analogical neural mechanisms—mirror-system engagement and global-workspace dynamics—directly in others via fMRI and EEG. From first-person knowledge that a particular prefrontal-parietal coupling pattern accompanies conscious deliberation in himself, he argued, observing the same coupling in Sofia gives mechanistic analogical grounds for attributing consciousness to her, not mere behavioral inference. (2) Sofia replied that this only pushes the explanatory gap one level down: seeing that a causal process runs in her brain tells us nothing about whether that process is accompanied by subjective experience for her. She accused Ravi of sliding from structural identity to phenomenal identity without justification, essentially repeating Hume’s warning against projecting the familiar onto the unknown. (3) Ravi countered that Sofia was presupposing an obsolete Cartesian dualism: on any credible embodied or functionalist account, a mechanism running with the right organizational properties just is the mental state, so observing the fully specified functional architecture is observing the mind itself. (4) Sofia answered that even functionalism must address phenomenal integration—whether distributed activity is bound into a single point of view—and that neuroimaging reveals correlation, not integration or ownership. She added that Ravi’s criterion, applied consistently, would compel us to attribute minds to artificial networks that replicate the coupling pattern, which shows mechanistic analogy cannot distinguish a conscious subject from a high-fidelity simulation.
