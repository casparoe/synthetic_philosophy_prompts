This has become personally relevant because I am currently drafting a dissertation chapter on determinism in general relativity, and I need to assess whether framing the hole argument primarily as a metaphysical dilemma—rather than as a straightforward physical indeterminacy—is defensible. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or a reading list; I need conceptual adjudication only.

Here is the exchange I heard between two colleagues, Sam and Riley, in a reading group. I am reporting it as a bystander and do not want to signal which side I favor.

(1) Sam said the hole argument proves that general relativity is incompatible with determinism if substantivalism is true. He described the setup: specify initial data on a Cauchy surface outside a matter-free region (the "hole"), then apply an active diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside but smoothly non-trivial inside. This produces two metrics, g and φ*g, that both solve Einstein's field equations, match exactly on the boundary, yet assign different curvature values to the same manifold points inside the hole. If those points are real entities with primitive identity, the theory fails to determine their geometric properties.

(2) Riley replied that Sam is mistaking gauge redundancy for physical multiplicity. In general relativity, metrics related by an active diffeomorphism represent the exact same physical geometry; g and φ*g are not two worlds but one world redescribed. Riley compared it to electromagnetism, where A and A + dΛ represent the same field. So determinism is untouched—the initial data uniquely determine the equivalence class of metrics, and that is all that physics requires.

(3) Sam came back that Riley's "redescription" defense only works if you deny that spacetime points have haecceities—primitive thisness—independent of their properties. A "thick" substantivalist holds that point p inside the hole is literally the same entity in both descriptions, bearing curvature R in one and R' in the other. Since the boundary conditions do not fix R versus R', determinism is genuinely violated. Sam insisted that invoking "gauge" without argument smuggles in an anti-haecceitistic metaphysics, which is a premise, not a consequence of the field equations.

(4) Riley answered that even a thick substantivalist has a response: if points are individuated partly by their qualitative roles, then assigning R versus R' to p is not assigning different properties to a pre-identified object, but describing two different ways the qualitative structure could be realized. Because the two descriptions are isomorphic with respect to everything observable outside the hole, there is no physical basis to prefer one realization. The "failure of determinism" is therefore only a failure to determine which haecceitistic labeling holds, not a failure of the dynamics. Physics remains deterministic; only metaphysical labeling is underdetermined.

(5) Sam then argued that Riley has saved determinism only by making point-identity depend on qualitative properties—which is just relationalism in disguise. If points have no identity apart from their place in the structure, then saying "there is a manifold of points" adds nothing over saying "there is a purely relational structure." So Riley has not defended substantivalism; he has shown that it collapses into relationalism when pressed by the hole argument.

(6) Riley said this collapse claim is too quick. There is a genuine middle position—"thin" substantivalism—which holds that points exist as parts of a container manifold but are individuated only structurally, lacking haecceities. This is not relationalism, because relationalism denies the container altogether and says only relations exist, with no underlying relata. Thin substantivalism keeps the relata (the points of the manifold) but denies them primitive identity, so there is a real manifold that is not merely relational structure.
