I was listening to two colleagues, Mara and Leo, argue about Carroll's tortoise and what it actually shows about the justification of deduction. They went back and forth like this.

Mara started by laying out the standard puzzle: Achilles accepts that if A then B, and he accepts A, but the tortoise refuses to accept B unless Achilles also accepts the further conditional — that if (A implies B and A holds), then B. Once that is added as a premise, the tortoise demands yet another conditional licensing the application of modus ponens to these three premises, and so on. Mara said the moral is that you can never derive a conclusion from premises alone; there is always a missing rule, and trying to supply it launches an infinite regress. So deduction cannot be justified from within, only assumed from without.

Leo replied that this misdescribes formal inference. In a deductive system, modus ponens is not a premise but a rule of transformation. The tortoise treats it as an extra premise, but that is a category error: logical rules belong to the framework of reasoning, not to the content being reasoned about. Leo said asking for a premise that licenses modus ponens is like asking for a chess move that licenses the bishop's diagonal motion; the rule is constitutive of the game, not a move within it.

Mara pushed back. She said Leo's answer just relocates the problem. If logical rules are outside the premises, then justifying deduction requires justifying the framework itself. And that justification cannot itself be deductive without circularity — you cannot use modus ponens to justify modus ponens. So we are left with either circularity or an arbitrary stipulation. She then introduced a sharper point: in non-classical settings — paraconsistent or relevance logic — we do suspend or restrict modus ponens under certain conditions. This shows the rule is not absolutely constitutive; it is framework-relative. Therefore, before applying it, one should justify the choice of framework. That generates not an infinite premise-regress but a framework-regress: every framework-selection needs its own meta-framework, and so on.

Leo conceded that framework-choice is real but denied it is vicious. He argued that frameworks are not justified by derivation from higher frameworks but by comparative criteria — explanatory power, coherence with practice, or pragmatic success in inquiry. The tortoise's error, Leo said, is demanding a deductive justification for the conditions of deduction. That demand is self-defeating, because any answer would have to employ the very rules being questioned. Mara countered that this makes framework-choice ultimately pragmatic or conventional, not rationally compelled. If so, deduction rests on a foundation that is not itself rationally grounded — it is merely useful. Leo responded that rationally grounded is ambiguous: internal to a framework, deduction is fully justified; across frameworks, rational comparison uses shared norms of coherence and evidence, which do not require a super-framework to be legitimate.

They ended without resolving whether framework-relativity dissolves Carroll's paradox or merely transforms it.

Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right at each specific step — not just who wins overall? In particular, assess whether Mara's framework-regress is genuinely analogous to Carroll's premise-regress, whether Leo's chess analogy holds up against her framework-relativity objection, and whether Leo's appeal to pragmatic coherence avoids circularity or just disguises it. Also, say what, if anything, both of them are missing — for instance, whether there is a distinction between justifying an inference-rule for a particular agent and justifying the normative authority of logic itself.
