As a novelist who has been tracing the formation of collective identity in my recent fiction, I am preparing the interview guide for a forthcoming episode of my podcast, The Unbound Margin, in which I will speak with an unnamed cultural historian defending a revisionist thesis at the intersection of Benedict Anderson’s theory of imagined communities and Eric Hobsbawm’s analysis of invented traditions. He argues that Anderson’s chronology—tying national consciousness to print capitalism in the early modern period—is too narrow, insisting instead that late-medieval vernacular pilgrimage narratives and court-sponsored chronicle translations already formed horizontally integrated “communities of sentiment” centuries before movable type. At the same time, he applies Hobsbawm’s framework retroactively: rather than treating invented traditions as a uniquely modern nation-state strategy, he regards them as a recurring dynastic technique of legitimation. On this account, nations are neither ancient organic entities nor exclusively modern fabrications, but recurring structural forms of political imagination whose age depends on whether one traces continuity through institutional ritual or through the vernacular networks that sustain collective identification.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press this position exactly where the existing scholarship presses it, with no generic biographical openings and no requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations that a listener without archival access could not evaluate. I want at least one question challenging his chronology—specifically, whether manuscript and oral circulation can genuinely replicate the anonymous, simultaneous co-reading that Anderson sees as definitive of the national imaginary—paired with a follow-up keyed to his likely reply that elite literacy still permitted a kind of horizontal awareness. I want a second that asks how Hobsbawm’s framework, developed to expose modern bureaucratic states manufacturing ancient pedigree, applies when the “inventors” are pre-modern dynastic courts rather than nation-states, with a follow-up anticipating his probable move that court ritual and vernacular chronicle serve the same structural function as modern flag ceremonies and public education. A third must address the conceptual implication: if traditions are repeatedly invented across centuries, does this render a given nation “older” in any meaningful sense, or does it dissolve the distinction between authentic antiquity and strategic fabrication? For each, include the anticipated answer and the precise follow-up that exposes the tension or cost within it. Every question should turn on the actual content of his revisionism and should be answerable without recourse to external sources.
