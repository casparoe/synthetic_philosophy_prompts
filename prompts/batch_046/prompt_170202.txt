As a novelist who recently audited a philosophy of science seminar, I have become preoccupied with the parallel between what I leave out of a story and what scientists leave out of a model. In both cases, something is deliberately false or incomplete, yet sometimes the result illuminates and sometimes it misleads. I want to know whether my judgments about these cases form a coherent pattern, or whether I need to overturn some of them. Here is where I stand.

In scientific modeling: treating planets as point masses to derive orbital mechanics strikes me as fully justified, since gravity depends only on total mass and separation, not on internal structure. The Ising model's assumption that atomic spins are strictly binary also seems acceptable, because it predicts real magnetization transitions despite atoms not being binary—the macro-phenomenon genuinely does not depend on that micro-continuity. But Milton Friedman's well-known defense of economic models that assume perfect rationality and unlimited computation makes me uneasy; if accurate predictions arise only because opposing behavioral biases happen to cancel, then the false mechanism is not causally inert but actively disguises the real causal structure. A climate model that averages away small-scale cloud convection for computational reasons strikes me as even worse—not simplification but suppression of a feedback loop known to drive the very warming being predicted. By contrast, a neuroscience model that treats neurons as simple on-off logic gates to explain consciousness feels unjustified, because graded potentials and continuous dynamics appear essential to the phenomenon in a way that binary spins are not essential to magnetization.

In fiction, I apply analogous verdicts. Omitting my characters' sleep, meals, and bathroom use is harmless, since those activities lie outside the causal and thematic structure I am tracing. But writing every conversation as perfectly logical and explicit—eliminating confusion, interruption, and emotional misdirection—feels like the neuroscience case: it suppresses the actual psychological mechanisms that generate the behavior I claim to be depicting. The case I keep reversing involves a historical narrative of a revolution written solely from official state documents, with private letters and oral testimony deliberately excluded; the author defends this by saying the official record alone is sufficient to predict the regime's collapse. Is this like the point-mass case, because only the structural features of the state matter for the outcome? Or is it like the climate-model case, because the omitted sources contain the actual causal drivers—rumor, fear, informal coordination—that produced the collapse? I am asking for a precise principle that distinguishes permissible idealization from misleading suppression, one that applies across both scientific and literary representation, delivers a clear verdict on that historical-narrative case and on any further case you construct, and states honestly which of my judgments—particularly the Friedman case versus the point-mass case, and the neuroscience case versus the Ising case—I must abandon if the principle is to remain coherent. Please respond in American English.
