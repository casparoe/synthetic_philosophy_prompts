Here are my verdicts on whether a long-run predictive theory has been genuinely falsified, rescued by an auxiliary adjustment, or merely “right but early,” and I want to know what principle—if any—captures the pattern.

(1) Malthus argued that population tends to grow geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically, implying eventual crisis. In nineteenth- and twentieth-century Europe, food production outpaced population due to synthetic fertilizers and mechanization, and no Malthusian crisis occurred. Verdict: the theory was not falsified outright, but its prediction failed because it implicitly assumed technological stasis; the mechanism was real but contingent on conditions that did not hold.

(2) A 1970s ecological model predicted widespread famine by 1990 based on linear soil-depletion curves. The famine did not occur, largely because the Green Revolution expanded yields through chemical inputs. The model’s defenders argue that depletion was merely masked, not solved, and that the crisis is deferred rather than canceled. Verdict: this feels like a legitimate rescue—the core mechanism (unsustainable extraction) remains intact, and the auxiliary claim about technological offset is independently testable and observable.

(3) In 1955, a sociologist predicted that television ownership would cause a collapse in adult literacy rates by 1980. Literacy rates remained stable, but defenders now claim that “deep literacy”—the capacity for sustained attention to long, complex texts—has eroded, and that this was the true predicted outcome all along, merely mis-measured initially. Verdict: this feels like an ad hoc immunization; redefining the measurable outcome after the fact protects the theory from any possible failure.

(4) A behavioral economist predicted in 1990 that hyperbolic discounting would produce universal retirement poverty in developed economies by 2020. Instead, automatic enrollment policies and shifting social norms increased savings rates, and poverty among retirees declined. The economist responds that the underlying cognitive bias is unchanged; institutional scaffolding merely overrode it. Verdict: I am genuinely torn—this looks structurally like (2), since an external factor interrupted the causal chain, yet it also looks like (3), because the observable outcome was negated and the theory is protected by invoking an unobservable “underlying” tendency.

(5) In particle physics, a symmetry-based prediction that a specific boson would exist was made in the 1960s, but the particle was not detected until 2012, after accelerator technology improved. Between 1960 and 2012, the theory was not considered falsified. Verdict: clearly not falsified; the delay was in observational access, not in the predicted mechanism itself.

(6) A climate model from 1990 predicted approximately 3°C of warming by 2020; observed warming was closer to 1.2°C. The modelers revise feedback-loop parameters downward and predict accelerated warming in later decades. Verdict: this is a partial disconfirmation of the specific parameterization, but not necessarily of the broader theoretical framework—though if every downward revision is followed by a new upward prediction without independent constraint, I worry we approach the immunization strategy in (3).

The case I keep returning to is (4). Is there a principled distinction between rescuing a theory by identifying an independent, testable interrupting cause (as in 2 and 5) and rescuing it by relocating the predicted effect into an unobservable substrate or an indefinitely deferred future (as in 3)? Or must I concede that all predictive rescue involves some auxiliary adjustment, and the only difference is our prior confidence in the auxiliary?

Please state the principle—or competing candidates—that delivers these verdicts, specify what it predicts for a case not listed (for instance, a medical theory that predicts high mortality from a virus, but mortality is low because an unanticipated mutation weakened virulence: is this analogous to 2 or to 4?), and be explicit about which of my verdicts—especially on (4)—the principle forces me to overturn if I want to keep the account simple and non-ad hoc.
