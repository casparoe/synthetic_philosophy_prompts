I've been thinking about philosophy from the outside, mainly through popular books, and I'm stuck on why some people treat thought experiments—the trolley problem, the Ship of Theseus, Searle's Chinese Room—as serious arguments, while others dismiss them as rhetorical tricks. I don't want a list of named philosophers; I want a decision tree that sorts the actual positions by what people believe about how these cases work.

Please build a taxonomy with exactly three branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete claim that a non-specialist could answer without knowing philosophical labels.

First node: When a thought experiment changes your mind, is it isolating one principle and stripping away distractions (like a clean-room test), or is it carrying hidden structure from a familiar story into an unfamiliar domain (like an analogical map that brings baggage with it)?

Second node: If a case seems to mislead—say, by framing a choice so that one option looks absurd—can that error be corrected by redesigning the case, or is misdirection built into using narrative to do logical work?

Third node: Does the case reveal an intuition you already possessed, or does it construct the intuition on the spot?

Map every combination of answers onto a leaf: name the position if it has a recognized defender or label, otherwise write "unnamed" and state its most awkward commitment in one sentence. Note any empty branches—logically possible combinations that seem unoccupied—and briefly diagnose whether each gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, explaining why the distinction collapses in practice.

Keep every node free of citation requests, reading lists, and jargon that requires knowing the literature. The audience is an interested lay reader trying to figure out which camp they actually belong to.
