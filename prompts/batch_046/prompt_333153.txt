I'm taking this philosophy of religion seminar and we're stuck on karma — not the instagram version, but karma as an actual theory of moral desert where your present situation is literally deserved because of past actions, maybe across lives. We keep asking whether it's just retribuitivism with reincarnation stapled on, and my prof threw out this idea about "karmic latency" (the delay between action and result) but then moved on. I want to run an activity for our study group — 8 people, 90 minutes, meeting in a classroom with movable chairs — that makes us feel why someone might think karma does explanatory work that ordinary punishment just dont cover.

Here's my half-baked idea: a two-round token game called The Ledger. In round one everyone negotiates trades and alliances with visible "resource tokens" but hidden "moral ledgers" (each ledger lists 3 past actions with weights). Theres also a "latency pool" — tokens sitting in the middle that represent outcomes already determined by past actions but not yet assigned, because the causal chain hasnt "matured." In round two all ledgers and the pool are revealed. Now people have to redistribute everything so that each agent recieves exactly the net weight of their own ledger, and heres the kicker: debts and credits are non-transferable. If Player A has a negative balance, Player B cant take it, even if B wants to volunteer. The latency pool has to be assigned back to the original agents too, but nobody knows for sure which token in the pool belongs to which past action, so people have to guess based on patterns. The rules should make the group naturally start acting like theres an invisible accounting system running underneath, and then they should hit the wall where they realize they have no expistemic access to anyone's past-agent identity, so the whole system collapses into guesswork.

Can you turn this into actual rules with a payoff scheme that doesnt use real money, a clear timeline for 90 minutes, roles or instructions for each round, and a debrief that maps the chaos of round two onto the difference between karma, retributivism, and consequentialism? I want the group to discover for themselves that the system requires perfect knowledge of past-agent identity and perfect non-transferability, both of which break in practice. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — like people ignoring the non-transferability rule to be nice — and how to handle it. And be honest about what feature of karma, maybe the cross-life identification problem, this just cant reproduce and I'll need to supply in the debrief myself.
