Write a proposal for a one-semester graduate seminar paper (approximately 6,500 words, due at term's end) addressing the absent-minded driver problem. The standard framing treats it as a paradox of dynamic rational choice under imperfect recall: the driver prefers to take the highway exit only if he will not stop earlier to check his progress, yet he anticipates that reaching the earlier intersection with the intention to continue will cause him to stop. I want to ask whether the paradox dissolves once we distinguish two norms governing belief revision—what I will call evidential stability (the requirement that a belief be retained only if the evidence supporting it persists across the relevant temporal perspectives) and procedural coherence (the requirement that one's reasoning procedures be endorsed from the first-person perspective of the procedure itself)—and, if so, which norm should govern self-locating beliefs about one's own future reasoning.

Existing treatments leave this open because both the classic decision-theoretic resolutions (treating the driver as a sequence of time-slices with non-transitive preferences) and the epistemic resolutions (treating the belief that he will continue as unstable) assume a single rationality norm is at stake. They do not ask whether the driver is subject to two norms that can come apart specifically for beliefs about one's own cognitive architecture. This gap is particularly acute given that epistemic utility theory assigns special scoring rules to credences about one's own reliability, yet no existing analysis applies that machinery to the driver case.

The approach should be conceptual analysis supported by a minimal formal model, not empirical research. First, formalize the driver scenario using centered-world semantics rather than standard possible-worlds, indexing the belief "I will not stop" to the driver's temporal perspective. Second, reconstruct the standard paradox and demonstrate that both the decision-theoretic and epistemic resolutions implicitly conflate evidential stability with procedural coherence. Third, develop a model of procedural coherence for self-locating beliefs and determine whether the driver violates it when he forms the belief that he will continue past the intersection. The paper should conclude with a determinate answer: either the paradox is dissolved (if procedural coherence permits the belief) or it is sharpened (if the two norms genuinely conflict and we must choose between them).

What counts as success is a clear distinction between the two norms, a demonstration that at least one standard resolution collapses them, and a specific verdict on whether the driver is rational in believing he will continue. What counts as failure is that the distinction turns out to be merely verbal, or that the formal model adds no explanatory power beyond the standard framing.

Scope and timeline: Month one—literature review and formalization of the centered-world framing; Month two—analysis of the two norms and reconstruction of standard resolutions; Month three—development of the procedural-coherence model and drafting; Month four—revision and conclusion. This is realistic for one semester because the project does not attempt a general theory of self-deception or a full decision-theoretic framework, only applies an existing conceptual distinction to one well-defined case.
