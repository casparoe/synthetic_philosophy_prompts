I'm drafting a scene where a salaryman in Osaka realises the firm he's given his life to is going to break its promise of lifetime work, and it got me sidetracked onto Weber — that old idea that Calvinist anxiety accidently built capitalism — and whether culture can ever really be the engine, not just the paint job.

Take Japan's Lost Decade. The shūshin koyō system, that unspoken contract of lifetime employment, basically collapsed in the nineties. The standard explanation is all structural: the asset bubble burst, banks went bad, global competition bit, demographics turned sour. But there's this other reading, almost Weberian, that says Japanese firms had their own spiritual economy — seishin, a kind of collective calling — and that when that faded, the economic model faded with it.

So analyse the breakdown through Weber's lens. Treat seishin as if it were Japan's version of the Protestant calling. Did the culture cause the rigidity that made the crash worse, or was it just a comforting story told after the banks had already failed? What should the firms and the state have done if they really believed in that value-system — and did they? Where does Weber's idea of elective affinity help, and where is it silent? Does the case prove culture can be an independant cause, or does it show that Weber's thesis only works in the particular soil of 17th-century Europe?

Also, I keep flipping on whether "seishin" is a real causal factor or romantic nostalgia. If the theory forces you to take it seriously when it shouldn't, say so. Don't bend the case to fit.
