I'm putting together a feature on personal identity for a science magazine and got stuck in an argument two of my collegues had over lunch. They were riffing on that Derek Parfit teleportation scenario -- you know, scan and rebuild on Mars -- but they twisted it into a slow-burn version. Imagine a clinic that replaces your neurons one by one over about two years with synthetic equivalents. Same electrical patterns, same connections. By the end none of your original brain tissue is left, but the transition was seamless; you never lost consciousness or memory.

My first collegue said this is obviously survival. She argued what matters is psychological continuity -- your memories, personality, intentions stay linked the whole way through, just like they do when you naturally learn and forget. The physical stuff is just hardware.

My other collegue said that's a sleight of hand. He said natural change is gradual and continuous, but this is an engineered swap-out. The original neurons get destroyed and replaced by artifacts. You're not persisting through change; you're being copied in slow motion. By the end it's a perfect replica living your life, not you.

Then she came back with: "But wait, your body already replaces almost every cell every decade or so. If physical continuity actually mattered, you'd be a different person every ten years. So physical continuity was never the right criterion anyway."

He replied: "Cell replacement in bodies isn't the same as brain replacement. The brain's organizational structure is maintained by the same substrate adapting, not being swapped for synthetic chips. And even if bodies change, endurantism allows an object to survive gradual part-replacement if there's spatiotemporal continuity. Here the continuity is faked -- each synthetic neuron is a new object occupying the space, not the old one transformed."

Then she said: "You're smuggling in a metaphysical distinction with no practical difference. If the synthetic brain behaves identically, has your memories, and everyone treats it as you, then for all legal and moral purposes it is you. The 'original' is just a ghost in your ontology."

He said: "That proves too much. By that standard a perfect AI clone of you running on a server is also you. But Parfit himself said psychological continuity without physical continuity might not be enough if branching happens. Here there's no branching, but there's also no original continuing. You've just described a very good forgery of survival."

Neither of them would budge. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step -- not just who wins overall? Rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. And tell me what both parties are missing: does this slow-replacement case actually map onto the standard teletransporter debate, or does it introduce something genuinely different that makes both of their arguments miss the point?
