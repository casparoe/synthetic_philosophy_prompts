For my rational-choice seminar I am trying to formalize an objection to bounded-utility resolutions of the St. Petersburg paradox, but the argument I have built seems to prove far more than I intend and I cannot locate the leak. I would like to keep the idea that the paradox genuinely threatens open-ended decision-making, yet I suspect there is a scope or level-confusion error in the middle of the chain. Could you diagnose which step fails, explain why it initially looked sound, and price the cost of rejecting it?

(1) In the standard St. Petersburg game with linear utility, expected monetary value is infinite, so rationality appears to require paying any finite price to play.
(2) If utility is bounded above by some U_max, the expectation becomes finite—approximately proportional to log(U_max)—so for any fixed entry fee there exists a large enough U_max that justifies playing.
(3) Now define a meta-game: with probability 2⁻ᵏ, the payoff structure is a St. Petersburg game scaled so that its effective U_max equals 2^(2ᵏ). The scaling parameter itself therefore has an exponential-tail distribution over the integers.
(4) Applying the bounded-utility rule recursively, the inner expectation at scale k grows like 2ᵏ, and weighting by 2⁻ᵏ yields a divergent outer sum.
(5) Hence bounding utility does not stabilize the paradox; it only relocates the divergence to the distribution over scaling parameters.
(6) To avoid divergence entirely, one must reject expected-utility maximization for any prospect with unbounded support, not merely for literal St. Petersburg lotteries.
(7) But prospects such as long-horizon scientific investment or existential-risk mitigation have unbounded support in principle, so this rejection undermines rational choice in precisely the domains that matter most.
(8) Therefore the paradox proves too much: it forces abandonment of EU theory for all open-ended endeavors, not just for one contrived game.

I am especially reluctant to give up (6) or (7), yet I feel something slips between (3) and (5)—perhaps a conflation of computing an expectation over a random parameter with applying the utility bound at the wrong level. Which step should I reject? Please separate what is settled in the decision-theory literature from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested view—such as whether bounded utility is the standard resolution, or whether my meta-game construction is well-formed—as if it were the consensus.
