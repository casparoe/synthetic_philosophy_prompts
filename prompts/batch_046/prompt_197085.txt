I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and I have noticed a recurring pattern in arguments about cross-cultural and inter-personal moral disagreement that I have not been able to map onto existing terminology. I will describe two instances concretely, then ask whether this configuration has a recognized name and how it should be bounded.

First instance: in debates over dietary practices, a speaker often classifies the meat-eating of another culture as a preference rooted in tradition and therefore not open to moral condemnation, but classifies the refusal to eat meat within their own culture either as a rational moral stance or, conversely, as a bias against social norms—without offering any independent criterion that distinguishes preference from bias except proximity to the speaker's own commitments.

Second instance: when comparing bodily-modification norms across societies, observers routinely treat restrictions in an out-group culture as manifestations of cultural bias, yet treat structurally analogous restrictions in their own society—those serving comparable functions of social cohesion or risk-aversion—as expressions of legitimate preference or custom, even when the harm profiles are similar.

What interests me is not ordinary motivated reasoning, but a specific taxonomic instability: the preference/bias distinction functions as a normative boundary-marker rather than a descriptive psychological category. The very same evidential features—emotional salience, social transmission, resistance to counter-argument—are invoked to place a judgment in the category of preference when it belongs to the in-group, and in the category of bias when it belongs to the out-group. The result is that the distinction can be reversed without contradiction whenever the speaker's allegiance shifts.

My question is whether there is an established concept—perhaps in moral psychology, philosophy of disagreement, or anthropology of ethics—that captures precisely this second-order failure: the asymmetric deployment of the preference/bias taxonomy to immunize one's own moral framework. If there is no dedicated term, how should the phenomenon be delineated from nearby ones such as the bias blind spot, in-group favoritism, or simple self-serving rationalization? In particular, does it involve a distinct failure in second-order moral classification, or is it reducible to first-order disagreement coupled with ordinary cognitive bias? I am not looking for a literature survey, but for the conceptual carving: what exactly this is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
