I have been developing what I call gradient prioritarianism. On this view, the moral weight assigned to a unit of well-being is not a fixed decreasing function of the recipient's absolute welfare level, as in standard prioritarianism, but is instead proportional to the recipient's distance from the current global welfare median. Now consider a downward-leveling case: we reduce the welfare of the best-off individuals by a small amount, benefiting no one. Because the global median falls slightly, everyone near the old median becomes relatively farther from the new median, which raises their assigned moral weights. Meanwhile, the harm to the wealthy is evaluated at a very low weight, since they remain far above the median. Aggregating these effects, it appears that the second-order increase in weight applied to the untouched majority can outweigh the direct harm to the wealthy, yielding a net gain in aggregate priority-weighted welfare despite nobody being made better off.

My question is whether this reasoning exposes a genuine vulnerability in prioritarianism—namely, that if weights are anchored to a distribution-relative scale rather than an absolute scale, the theory actually licenses the very leveling-down it was designed to avoid. Or is there a formal error in how I am computing the aggregate change?
