Over the past year my reading group—mostly rationalist-adjacent people who usually converge quickly on empirical questions—has been stuck on the balance-sheet debate over the British Raj. One side runs something like Niall Ferguson’s institutional-spillover argument: railways, legal codes, parliamentary habits, and English-language education left durable infrastructure that post-colonial states inherited. The other side runs Shashi Tharoor’s extraction-and-deindustrialization line: famine, textile collapse, fiscal drain, and the deliberate underdevelopment of indigenous industry. We all agree on the raw figures—miles of track, mortality data, budget allocations, export volumes—yet nobody has moved an inch. The disagreement feels less like a dispute over measurements and more like two people using the same ledger with incompatible accounting rules, but I can’t tell whether the problem is hidden empirics, a normative deadlock, or a conceptual mistake in framing “the Raj” as a single intervention at all.

My working theory is that the paralysis is partly caused by treating nearly two centuries of rule across the subcontinent as one uniform treatment, when in fact colonial governance was radically heterogeneous: direct-rule crown provinces with different fiscal-military regimes, hundreds of indirectly administered princely states with retained sovereignty, and presidency-level variation in land revenue, education spending, and infrastructure investment. If that heterogeneity is real, then both sides may be averaging over incommensurable institutional experiments—some extractive, some developmental, some both—while implicitly adopting different counterfactual baselines (Mughal continuity, fragmented sovereignty, or an earlier industrial takeoff). That makes me wonder whether the stalemate is really about what counts as a moral cost versus a developmental benefit, or whether it is a deeper category error: trying to compute a “net welfare effect” across counterfactual worlds we cannot observe and comparing institutional “gifts” that were imposed by coercion with wealth that was extracted through the same coercion.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this disagreement verbal—both sides saying “net harm” but defining the baseline differently? Is it substantive but hidden behind a shared vocabulary, with one side weighting autonomy and distributive justice and the other weighting institutional persistence and GDP trajectories? Or is it bedrock intuition about whether domination can ever be redeemed by downstream benefits, with neither side able to argue the other out of their prior? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal framework for counterfactual welfare comparison across heterogeneous colonial regimes, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the “balance sheet” framing entirely, or an honest admission that the question is ill-posed and should be replaced? Please answer in American English, and be direct about whether anything—an experiment, a theorem, a distinction—could realistically end it.
