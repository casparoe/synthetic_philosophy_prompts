As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mathematics, I keep returning to an argument about Skolem's paradox that seems to dissolve not only set theory but also the continuous mathematics I used for decades. Here is the chain as I understand it.

(1) By the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem, first-order ZFC has a countable model M.
(2) Inside M there is a set S that formally satisfies the definition of being uncountable: no bijection with ω exists within M.
(3) Because M itself is countable, there is a bijection in the meta-theory between S and ω; so from outside M, S is countable.
(4) Thus one and the same object is both uncountable and countable, with the difference lying only in the scope of quantification available.
(5) If a property can flip for a single object solely by restricting or expanding the domain over which we quantify, then that property is not intrinsic to the object but an artifact of descriptive limitation.
(6) So uncountability is not a genuine feature of sets; it is a phantom generated by first-order logic's failure to fully fix its domain.
(7) By direct analogy, physical continuity is defined inside formal systems via epsilon-delta conditions quantified over real numbers in a model; if that model is countable from the outside, continuity becomes similarly relative.
(8) Therefore we lack any non-circular reason to treat physical space or fields as genuinely continuous rather than discrete.

This proves far too much—if sound, it undermines both mathematics and physics. I want to preserve the intuition that formal systems should not manufacture phantom properties, yet I also want to retain both uncountability in mathematics and continuity in physics. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does abandoning (5) force me to accept that some properties are irreducibly model-relative, and does that salvage or destroy the physical analogy in (7)?
