Please grade the following undergraduate examination response. The exam was for a course in historical sociology and the philosophy of social science; students had studied Weber’s methodological essays and his thesis on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism, but they were not expected to cite secondary literature.

Question: Weber’s account of the relationship between Calvinist asceticism and the emergence of rational capitalism is often misread as a simple causal claim. Drawing on his concepts of Verstehen, ideal types, and elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft), explain how Weber attempts to grant culture explanatory weight without treating values as deterministic forces analogous to physical causes. Specifically, address whether his framework permits what philosophers of science sometimes call causal relevance without causal efficacy—that is, whether cultural meanings can be indispensable to a correct historical explanation even if they are not links in a deterministic causal chain. Conclude by assessing whether this middle position is coherent, or whether it collapses into either economic determinism on one side or a purely narrative, post-hoc rationalization on the other.

Student answer:

Weber rejects both materialist determinism and idealist determinism. He thinks sociology must understand action through subjective meaning, which is why he uses Verstehen. In the Protestant Ethic thesis, he argues that Calvinist beliefs about predestination created a psychological anxiety that led people to seek proof of salvation through disciplined labor and accumulation of wealth. This was not the only cause of capitalism—Weber mentions other factors like legal and administrative developments—but it was an important one.

Weber uses ideal types as heuristic tools to compare historical cases. The ideal type of modern rational capitalism is not a description of reality but a measuring rod. In this framework, elective affinity describes how the Protestant ethic and capitalist behavior reinforced each other. I think elective affinity is basically a functional explanation: the values survived and spread because they served the functional needs of the emerging capitalist system. This avoids treating culture as a direct cause, because it explains values by their consequences rather than by their internal logic.

Weber distinguishes between value-rational action, where people act according to ethical or religious commitments, and instrumentally rational action, where they calculate the best means to an end. In the historical transition he describes, value-rational religious motivation gradually gave way to instrumentally rational economic behavior. Once capitalism was established, the religious roots withered away, leaving only the rational calculation of profit.

Because Weber denies that values operate like physical forces, they can only have causal relevance in the weak sense of being part of the story we tell after the fact. They do not actually push history in one direction, but we include them in our explanation because they help us understand what actors thought they were doing. So culture is indispensable for understanding, but it does not have real causal efficacy. This seems coherent to me: it avoids determinism without falling into the view that economics alone drives history.
