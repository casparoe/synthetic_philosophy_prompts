I spent last evening in a protracted argument with a close friend over whether narratives like the Exodus, the Trojan War, or the Arthurian cycle actually cross from legend into history, and we kept circling a mechanism I can only describe as an archival feedback loop: once a story is classified as legendary, institutional incentives to preserve contradictory evidence collapse, yet if it is classified as historical, contradictions are reinterpreted as regional variants or corruptions, making falsification structurally impossible. I want my students to feel that loop physically rather than hear it named. For a seminar of 14 upper-level students in historical epistemology, meeting for 75 minutes, design a simulation—call it the Four Councils—in which successive groups represent oral transmitters, Hellenistic/Roman compilers, medieval monastic copyists, and modern critical historians. Each council receives an identical one-page packet about a single event (choose the Exodus, the siege of Troy, or Arthur’s consolidation) that contains embedded contradictions; they must vote to place the narrative in the Historical Record, the Legendary Corpus, or the Mythic Archive. Crucially, that vote determines which documents survive to the next round—legendary status triggers discarding contradictions, historical status triggers copying them as “regional variants,” and mythic status preserves everything but marks it symbolic. Provide exact rules, the packet structure, a role-based payoff scheme that rewards institutional consistency rather than accuracy, and a timeline in which the facilitator says almost nothing until the end. Participants should come away seeing that the boundary between legend and history is not merely evidential but institutional and self-reinforcing. The debrief should map each council’s behavior onto the feedback loop, say what will predictably go wrong the first time (likely anachronistic voting by the modern group), and be frank about which feature—the multi-century temporal sedimentation—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally.
