I'm fluent in the design vocabulary of distributed consensus protocols—Byzantine fault tolerance, proof-of-stake, slashing conditions, fork-choice rules, and reputation-weighted gossip—without formal training in evolutionary biology or moral philosophy. Restate Darwin's evolutionary account of moral sentiment and religious belief—specifically, the claim that cooperative social instincts arise through selection pressure on competing groups, and that religious cognition stems from an overactive agency-detection mechanism coupled with experiences of awe—entirely within that protocol vocabulary. Map each evolutionary dynamic onto its closest protocol analog: nodes, validators, consensus thresholds, malicious actors, reputation staking, and network partitions. Then deliver the residue report: which claims translate without distortion, which survive only if we force emotional reward into the shape of staking rewards or awe into the shape of a partition event, and which evolutionary claims have no protocol counterpart whatsoever. In particular, examine whether the normative weight Darwin gives to the welfare of the community can be expressed inside a purely descriptive mechanism-design framework, or whether that residue indicates a non-evolutionary normative commitment that the translation makes visible. Be precise about where the analogy collapses rather than smoothing over the gaps.
