I am designing a closing exercise for my graduate seminar on aesthetics and politics, and I need a villanelle—nineteen lines arranged as five tercets followed by one quatrain, with the two refrain lines alternating according to the standard rhyme pattern—written in the voice of W. E. B. Du Bois addressing an imagined formalist critic in 1927 who insists that the rigid strictures of traditional poetic form prove art can transcend political propaganda. The poem should take seriously Du Bois's argument, developed in his 1926 essay in The Crisis, that for an oppressed people all art is necessarily propaganda and that beauty is never politically innocent; it should also weave in his less-discussed comparison between the Negro artist and the Russian revolutionary writer, both using aesthetic form to forge collective consciousness rather than individual expression. Use one refrain that initially asserts the identity of propaganda and beauty, and a second refrain that asks whether any form can stand outside the struggle for recognition; allow these lines to shift in meaning across the stanzas so that the repetition itself performs the idea that form channels politics rather than escaping it. The conceptual turn should occur in the final quatrain, where Du Bois concedes to the formalist that disciplined form may refine propaganda but can never abolish it, leaving the tension deliberately open rather than resolved into either pure aestheticism or pure didacticism. The piece should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Du Bois's writings on Negro art recognizes the position rather than encountering a mere name-drop, and it should avoid any resolution that makes the conflict disappear. After presenting the poem, please explain in continuous prose only—without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—how the changing contexts of the refrains track the movement from the opening assertion through the middle stanzas to the unresolved concession in the quatrain.
