I'm trying to figure out where I actually stand on whether big discoveries are inevitable and whether credit even matters, just going by my gut on cases. Newton and Leibniz both hitting calculus around the same time feels totally inevitable, and I'm weirdly okay with both getting credit even though Leibniz's notation won out in the end. Darwin and Wallace with natural selection also feels inevitable given the intellectual climate back then, but Wallace getting sidelined feels like a real injustice, not just a naming quirk. Bell and Gray filing for the telephone on the same day feels like pure contigency to me, not inevitable at all, and Gray probably got robbed. Two different AI labs publishing transformer architectures in 2017 within a couple months: inevitable given the GPU situation, but it doesn't really matter who was technically first since the idea was in the air. A friend of mine says her lab and a rival lab both discovered the same protein folding pattern independently, she thinks it's inevitable because the data was already public, but she still wants her name first on the paper. Then there's this classroom thing: if two students solve the exact same proof problem in a seminar but one used geometry and the other used algebra, I think both deserve praise; if they used the exact same method, I'm less sure. The case I keep going back and forth on is one I'm kind of making up — suppose a philosopher in 1980 publishes an obscure argument about consciousness, and then in 2024 a totally different philosopher comes up with the exact same argument without ever having read the 1980 paper. Is the 2024 person a discoverer, or just a repeater? Does it change things if the 1980 paper was in a language the 2024 person can't read? Is there a principle that captures all these verdicts, or close, and what does it say about which of my gut feelings have to go? I'm especially unsure whether my sympathy for Wallace and my indifference about the 2017 transformers can live together.
