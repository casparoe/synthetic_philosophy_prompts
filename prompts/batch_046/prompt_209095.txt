I am preparing the final unit of my graduate seminar on value theory, and I want to pose a conceptual problem that I think gets obscured in the standard taxonomy of global versus local meaning. We have been reading Camus's classic formulation of the absurd—the confrontation between the human appetite for absolute, rational clarity and the world's unreasonable silence—alongside contemporary hybrid accounts, particularly Wolf's fitting-fulfillment view and the more recent fundamentality-based frameworks that treat meaning as generated when subjective attraction aligns with conditions of objective worth. Most of my students are inclined to read these positions as straightforward competitors: the absurdist denies that life has meaning tout court, while the hybridist grants that local projects can be genuinely meaningful even in the absence of any cosmic purpose. But I want to push them to consider a more fine-grained distinction—what we might call a scaling problem for absurdity. Rather than treating absurdity as a single global property, suppose we distinguish between thin absurdity, which is merely the absence of transcendent justification, and thick absurdity, which involves a specific structural mismatch in which the very form or intensity of an agent's normative commitments includes an unavoidable demand for cosmic validation that local achievements cannot satisfy without contradiction. On this framing, a life might be rich in local meaning—full of projects that are both subjectively engaging and objectively valuable—yet still be thickly absurd if the agent's life-form is constituted by a demand for absolute grounding that the structure of those projects necessarily fails to meet.

My question, then, is whether this distinction actually helps reconcile absurdism with hybrid theories, or whether it instead reveals that the two traditions are talking past one another by operating with different conceptions of what a life-form demands from the universe. Specifically, does the hybrid theorist have the conceptual resources to acknowledge thick absurdity without collapsing back into a global denial of meaning? Or is the disagreement ultimately not metaphysical—about whether the universe provides justification—but psychological and normative, about which demands are legitimate to place on the conditions of a meaningful life? I am especially interested in whether the hybridist can accommodate the idea that some lives are not merely lacking in meaning, but are absurd in a stronger, structurally conflicted sense, without abandoning the distributive, project-based model of meaning that defines her position. Please explain this conceptual terrain with care, indicating where the thin/thick distinction genuinely illuminates something that the standard global/local division misses, but also flagging the points where my framing may be importing assumptions—about normative inertia, about the scalability of existential demands, or about the unity of a life-form—that neither Camus nor the hybridists would explicitly endorse. Finally, please state how confident you are in your analysis and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
