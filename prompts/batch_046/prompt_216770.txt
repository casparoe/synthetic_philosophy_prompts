I am part of my school's robotics team, and we are programming an autonomous delivery bot to navigate the cafeteria hallway during lunch. To avoid collisions, we want the bot to enter a high-alert navigation mode whenever the space is crowded. We defined crowded using a LiDAR sensor that counts how many people stand within a three-meter radius of the bot. During testing, I noticed a puzzling logical pattern. If zero people within that radius is clearly not crowded, and adding exactly one more person never by itself transforms the space into a crowded one, then repeating that step seems to imply that even thirty people in the radius would still not be crowded—which is obviously false, since thirty people in that space is unmistakably crowded. At the same time, I cannot identify any precise number, such as exactly seventeen people, at which the hallway suddenly becomes crowded; any specific cutoff I program feels completely arbitrary. My teammate argues that we should simply set a hard threshold at twenty people and treat it as an engineering approximation, but I worry that if the underlying concept is genuinely incoherent, choosing a number is only disguising the problem rather than solving it. Is there a philosophically sound way to justify a sharp boundary in a system that tracks gradual change, or am I overthinking what should remain a simple programming parameter?
