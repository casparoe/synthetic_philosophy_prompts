This question has become personally relevant to me over the past month, as I have been assisting in drafting curricular materials for a comparative-government seminar and have noticed that students consistently conflate ceremonial monarchy with operative authoritarianism, which has led me to examine whether there is a coherent philosophical defense of retaining a hereditary head of state within an otherwise democratic framework—specifically in cases such as the United Kingdom, Scandinavia, and Japan. I have constructed the following argument, which seems to prove far more than I intend, and I need help locating the precise misstep without simply dismissing my discomfort.

Consider this chain of reasoning. (1) In a mature democracy, institutional authority is legitimate only to the extent that it derives from the consent—whether explicit or tacit—of those who are presently governed by it. (2) A constitutional monarch exercises symbolic sovereignty, representing the continuity of the state across time, yet this office is acquired by birthright rather than by any act of consent from living citizens, and it binds future generations who are not yet born and therefore cannot have authorized it. (3) Therefore, constitutional monarchy is democratically illegitimate in principle, not because the monarch wields coercive power—since in these systems operative sovereignty resides with elected bodies—but because the very presence of a non-consensual symbolic sovereign violates the temporal symmetry required by democratic autonomy. (4) However, the same temporal asymmetry applies to any long-binding institutional constraint: entrenched constitutional rights that future parliaments cannot easily repeal, multi-decade international treaties ratified by past legislatures, and even sovereign debt obligations all bind citizens who never personally consented to them. (5) If the presence of a hereditary symbolic office renders democracy illegitimate because of this asymmetry, then constitutionalism itself—including the frameworks that protect democratic rights and limit majoritarian abuse—is rendered illegitimate for the identical reason. (6) Yet abandoning constitutional constraints altogether is far more destructive to democratic governance than retaining a ceremonial crown, so (5) must be avoided. (7) Consequently, (1) must be false: democratic legitimacy does not require present consent for every feature of the institutional architecture, and the objection to monarchy collapses.

The conclusion that democratic legitimacy imposes no temporal consistency at all strikes me as excessively permissive; it seems to license arbitrary institutional inertia and to erase the distinction between operative and symbolic authority that I wanted to preserve. I suspect that something slips between (2) and (4)—perhaps an equivocation between being bound by coercive law and being represented by a continuous symbol, or between the amendability of constitutional provisions and the hereditary fixity of a royal office—but I cannot name it precisely. Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? In particular, if I wish to maintain that constitutional monarchy is not uniquely illegitimate compared to other long-term democratic structures, must I abandon the consent-based theory of legitimacy entirely, or can I salvage it by distinguishing the kind of authority—symbolic versus operative, amendable versus fixed—that a monarch actually exercises?
