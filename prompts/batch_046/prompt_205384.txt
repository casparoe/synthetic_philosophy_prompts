I have recently been appointed to an institutional review board evaluating a high-risk gene-therapy protocol, and an internal meta-analysis revealed that reviewers with my specific disciplinary background—molecular pharmacology combined with prior industry consulting—systematically assign greater weight to efficacy endpoints than to long-term adverse-event signals. After carefully re-examining the case files, I still judge that the protocol's risk-benefit calculus is favorable, yet I cannot dismiss the audit's finding that my judgment is precisely the kind susceptible to this bias. This puts me in what philosophers call a state of potential epistemic akrasia: I hold a first-order belief about the trial, but I also hold higher-order evidence that I am an unreliable judge about exactly this sort of question.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—discussing whether my position is necessarily irrational. Vasquez should defend the view that structured first-order reasoning, when accompanied by explicit procedural safeguards (such as pre-committing to weighting criteria, using blinded data extraction, and requiring adversarial peer checks within the board), can rationally resist defeat from general higher-order statistical evidence about one's cognitive profile; she should argue that global self-doubt is not automatically transferable to domain-specific judgments that have been methodologically insulated. Chen should defend the opposing view that such procedural safeguards are themselves executed by the very cognitive agent under suspicion, so that invoking them to protect a first-order belief is a form of epistemic self-deception that violates intellectual humility; he should maintain that the rational response to recognized higher-order unreliability is either suspension of judgment or a downward revision of confidence, not doxastic resilience. The dialogue should take place in a seminar setting where both have read the audit report, and they must engage with each other's actual arguments—particularly whether transparency about one's reasoning process can genuinely function as an epistemic insulator, or whether transparency merely relocates the bias without neutralizing it. The exchange should contain real back-and-forth, avoid strawman representations, and should not resolve into a simple agreement, though it may reach a precise point of principled disagreement about the conditions under which first-order belief can survive higher-order defeat.
