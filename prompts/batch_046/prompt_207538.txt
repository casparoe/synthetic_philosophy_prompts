I've been reflecting on whether anyone writing about the past can truly step outside their own era's values, or whether every historian is inevitably a prisoner of the present. To test this, I keep returning to a concrete shift I noticed in how American school textbooks, monuments, and public discourse treat the founding generation. In the early twentieth century, figures like Thomas Jefferson and George Washington were generally presented as visionary statesmen whose slaveholding was acknowledged as a moral failing but treated as an unremarkable feature of their historical context; by the early twenty-first century, the same figures are often framed as architects of a republic built on racial slavery, with that fact treated as central rather than incidental to their legacy.

This seems to involve two different kinds of presentism, though I don't know the standard terms. One kind uses knowledge available now—demographic data, economic records, recovered voices—to understand what actually happened then, even if people at the time didn't grasp its full significance. The other kind imports today's moral framework—our current judgments about human dignity, equality, and coercion—to evaluate people who lived under radically different ethical assumptions. Let's call the first kind evidential presentism and the second normative presentism.

Using this distinction, analyze the historiographical shift I described. Which parts of the change in how the founders are portrayed represent evidential presentism, and which represent normative presentism? If the claim is that historians can never escape the standards of their own time, does that apply equally to both kinds, or does one kind escape it? What features of the founders' world does the shift miss or misdescribe—are there aspects that neither framing captures well? And in turn, does this episode support the idea that presentism is unavoidable, or does it show that historians can separate explaining the past from judging it by present lights? Be disciplined about the mapping: say exactly where the distinction applies, where it blurs, and where it simply runs out of answers.
