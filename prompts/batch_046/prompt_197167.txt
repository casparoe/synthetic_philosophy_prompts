I am working with a reconstructed reading of von Neumann's later reflections on automata and technological growth—specifically, the claim that once technological systems achieve capacities for self-description and autonomous energy capture, they shift from being tools governed by human intent into self-sustaining evolutionary substrates, with human societies functioning more as maintenance environments than as designers. Within this framework I distinguish two survival mechanisms: active replication, in which a system propagates its own specifications, and structural embedding, in which a system persists because social and physical infrastructures have become irreversibly dependent on it. The reconstructed view treats these as phases of a single autonomous survival dynamic rather than as categorically different. Present this position straight, then map what would genuinely count against it. Which empirical developments, conceptual distinctions, or formal results—say, in evolutionary dynamics, systems theory, or the study of technological lock-in—would undermine the claim that technology acquires momentum decoupled from human purpose? Be explicit about whether the view risks unfalsifiability by interpreting every case of technological persistence as evidence of autonomous survival; if it has been insulated by such qualifications, assess whether that insulation is a virtue or a defect for a claim of this kind. Rank the defeaters from fatal—the position collapses into a rival account it was meant to resist—to merely awkward, requiring ad hoc adjustments. Say which of these a defender of the von Neumann-inspired view would themselves concede as damaging, and which they could plausibly dismiss. I want an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is.
