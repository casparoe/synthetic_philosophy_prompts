At the bar last night my friends Maya and Leo got into it about whether prosperity and order can actually justify authoritarian rule, mostly arguing over Singapore versus China. Maya said Singapore is the clearest case for performance legitimacy: high prosperity, low corruption, stable order, and people stay and build lives there, which amounts to a tacit contract. She said calling this illegitimate just because elections aren't competitive misses that legitimacy can come from outputs, not just procedures. Leo said that's ignoring "structural stickiness" — prosperity creates dependencies like property, family ties, and career capital that make leaving expensive, so staying isn't consent, it's inertia. Maya answered that stickiness exists in liberal democracies too; people don't flee Canada every time taxes go up. Leo said the difference is "cultivated dependency" — in authoritarian contexts the state actively designs policies (housing, pensions, business licensing) so prosperity is tied to political compliance, which means the contract isn't freely renewed. Maya then said Leo was smuggling in a hidden premise: that only procedural democracy counts as free renewal. She argued that if citizens genuinely prefer prosperity-over-participation, then the deal is legitimate even if exit is hard. Leo came back that preference itself is shaped by the system — you can't separate the goods from the mechanism delivering them — so performance legitimacy collapses into "they like the cage because it's gilded." They asked me to pick sides and I honestly couldn't. Can you adjudicate this round by round? Whos right about structural stickiness being different from normal attachment, about whether cultivated dependency undermines consent, and about whether performance can ever ground legitimacy without democratic procedures? Also what are they both missing?
