I have been preparing a session for a philosophy of mind seminar on the idea that the self is not an inner essence but a centre of narrative gravity — the abstract focal point of the stories we tell and are told about ourselves. I want to use Michel Gondry's Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind as a case study, but I am wary of reducing the film to a mere illustration. To push the discussion into less familiar territory, I want to introduce what we might call an "interpersonal narrative gravity well": the possibility that when two people's life-stories become deeply entangled, erasing one from the other's memory does not simply restore an original self but destabilises the gravitational field that held both identities in orbit.

Read the film through this combined lens. Where does Joel Barish's resistance to the erasure of Clementine Kruczynski show his self being pulled not by its own narrative centre but by a shared gravitational field? Does the scene in which he hides her inside childhood memories he never shared with her suggest that personal identity can borrow narrative mass from another person's past, or is it only an episodic fragment clinging to survival? Conversely, where does the film resist a purely Dennettian reading — for instance, in the recurring image of the Montauk beach house that seems to draw them back together even after complete erasure, which might hint at a bond that operates beneath or before narrative construction? I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that does not distort the philosophical concept to fit the plot, but also recognises what the film adds to the debate about whether narrative gravity can be genuinely shared, or whether it necessarily collapses without a singular centre.
