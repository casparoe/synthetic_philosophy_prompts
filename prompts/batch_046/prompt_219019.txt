I'm a software engineer with basically zero philosophy background, but I got obsessed with Parfit's hitchhiker problem — the one where you promise to pay a driver once he gets you to town, but after you arrive paying is irrational. I build commitment mechanisms in code all day (smart contracts, escrow, that kind of thing), so I see the same pattern: you want to bind your future self to do something your future self won't want to do. The philosophical debate around this feels totally stuck. One camp says you just need a stronger commitment device — change your preferences, sign an irreversable contract, whatever. The other camp says that's incoherent, rationality is always about maximizing utility right now, and binding yourself to do worse later is just self-sabotage. I'm wondering if the stall isn't really about hitchhikers at all but about what the word 'rational' is doing in the argument. Is one side using it for global consistency across a whole plan, and the other for local optimization at each decision point? Or is there a hidden disagreement about wether preferences can actually be bound? I don't want a literature review. I want a diagnosis: is this verbal, or is there a substantive disagreement hiding underneath? And if it's substantive, could a formal distinction — like separating the planning stage from the execution stage — actually resolve it, or is the impasse permanent because it comes down to bedrock intuitions about what it even means to be rational?
