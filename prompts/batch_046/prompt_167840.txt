I was recently reviewing the assessment protocols used by a large multinational consulting firm for its promotion and hiring pipelines, and I noticed a curious structural split that I cannot quite map onto the philosophy I have been reading. For entry-level and mid-tier recruitment, the firm relies almost exclusively on situational judgment tests: candidates are placed in hypothetical organizational dilemmas—resource shortages, conflicting stakeholder demands, ambiguous ethical boundaries—and scored on the adaptability and context-sensitivity of their responses, with explicit instructions that there is no single correct personality profile. Yet for admission to the senior leadership track and partner evaluation, the same firm mandates an entirely different instrument, an Aristotelian-derived character survey that ranks individuals on enduring trait-virtues such as practical wisdom, magnanimity, and temperance, treating these as stable dispositions that predict long-term executive judgment regardless of situational variance. The internal documentation justifies both tools with the same language of predictive validity and organizational fit, but they appear to rest on incompatible psychologies: one treating moral behavior as highly situation-dependent, the other treating it as the expression of globally stable character.

What implicit theory of moral responsibility and character does this bifurcated institutional practice presuppose? Does it commit the organization to a virtue-ethical framework only at the executive level, or does it presuppose something more specific—perhaps that practical wisdom is a second-order trait that becomes causally stable only once it has been acquired, while first-order responses remain situationally fluid? Can the practice be reconciled with the situationist critique advanced by social psychologists and philosophers of virtue—that global character traits are largely illusory and that behavior is driven by situational features rather than internal dispositions—or does the split implicitly concede that critique for junior staff while quietly exempting leaders from it? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical anthropology behind the policy, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate whether a consistent assessment regime—one that did not bifurcate by rank—would have to abandon virtue-ethical language altogether, or whether a modified virtue theory (say, one that distinguishes local from global traits, or that treats virtue as a kind of practical expertise) could license a unified protocol.

In particular, please address whether the firm’s design choice reveals an unacknowledged commitment to the idea that virtue is not merely a pattern of behavior but a kind of authority-conferring status that only applies to those already in power, and whether that commitment can survive if the situationist evidence is taken seriously across all organizational levels rather than being selectively bracketed. Tell me also where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could the same bifurcation be licensed by a purely instrumental theory of management, or does it genuinely need a normative theory of character to make sense of why leaders alone are measured by who they are rather than by how they respond?
