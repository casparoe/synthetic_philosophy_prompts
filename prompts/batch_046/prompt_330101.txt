I'm finalizing the conceptual framework for my graduate seminar on Marxist theory and want to check whether my commitments hang together before I present them. Here is my working profile: (1) I analyze exploitation through an analytic-Marxist, game-theoretic lens—differential ownership of productive assets permits unequal exchange—while explicitly rejecting the labor theory of value as either necessary or sufficient for this analysis; (2) I understand alienation phenomenologically, as an objective structure of estrangement from one's activity, its product, and one's fellow producers, but I deny that it is uniquely capitalist, holding that bureaucratic and technocratic social forms can instantiate it with equal severity; (3) I take the normative core of exploitation to be captured by republican freedom: the worker is dominated insofar as the capitalist holds arbitrary power to interfere with her choices, regardless of whether that power is ever exercised; (4) I maintain that in an economy approaching full automation, exploitation persists through the extraction of data-rents and platform surplus from users, even in the absence of direct labor-value extraction; and (5) I hold that worker-owned cooperatives operating within competitive markets eliminate exploitation—since the arbitrary internal power of capital is abolished—while failing to eliminate alienation, because market imperatives continue to subordinate democratic deliberation in production to external profitability constraints.

Please audit this set. For each pair of commitments, indicate whether they are genuinely in tension, only apparently so, or fully compatible. Where there is real tension, rank the conflicts by severity, specify what each side of the conflict would have to surrender for consistency, and identify the cheapest revision in each case. Then tell me which single change to one commitment would resolve the greatest number of tensions with the least loss to the overall view. Be explicit about the one pair that looks problematic but dissolves on closer inspection, explaining exactly why the appearance of conflict is misleading.
