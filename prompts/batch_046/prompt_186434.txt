Consider the following exchange, reconstructed from my notes at a recent interdisciplinary faculty workshop on secularization and moral psychology. Two colleagues—Dr. Lena Vasquez, whose work concerns moral metaphysics and the ontology of obligation, and Dr. Jonah Reed, a political philosopher—debated whether religious commitment yields a genuinely superior form of moral life, and whether secular societies can sustain the deep interpersonal trust that religious communities seem to command. I observed without intervening.

Vasquez opened by distinguishing what she called trust-as-prediction from trust-as-vow. In secular frameworks, she argued, moral cooperation is essentially predictive: I trust another person because I calculate, on the basis of incentives and reputation, that she will act cooperatively. This form of trust is conditional and collapses when prediction fails. Religious moral life, by contrast, transforms obligation into a vow—a commitment that binds regardless of expected outcomes because it is anchored in something transcendent. For this reason, religion does not merely make people behave better; it makes them more moral in the specific sense of sustaining vow-based obligations that predictive trust cannot replicate.

Reed responded that the vow-prediction distinction does not map cleanly onto the religious-secular divide. Secular societies, he noted, maintain institutional vows—constitutional oaths, professional ethics codes, civic rituals of accountability—that function analogously without metaphysical backing. The capacity for vow-making, he insisted, is not proprietary to religion. He also challenged the ambiguity in "more moral": if it means broader scope of moral consideration, religious societies frequently restrict deep trust to the in-group and justify severe out-group exclusion, whereas secular human-rights frameworks extend obligation universally. If it means stronger internal cohesion, that cohesion may depend precisely on such exclusion.

Vasquez countered that institutional vows are parasitic. They work only when citizens already possess the psychological and cultural capacity for vow-based commitment—a capacity historically cultivated within religious traditions. Once the religious substrate erodes, secular vows become hollow performative acts. She pointed to what she termed moral inflation in secular rights discourse: rights-claims proliferate without a shared metaphysical ground that could bind them, producing a framework that asserts universality but lacks the force to oblige. Deep trust, she concluded, requires not merely coordination but a shared sense of the sacred; without it, secular societies are left with predictive mechanisms that wear thin under stress.

Reed accused her of committing the genetic fallacy—conflating historical origin with present capacity. Even if vow-capacity was originally religious, secular culture can independently sustain it through education, narrative, and institutional habit. He suggested that moral inflation is better interpreted as moral development: the expansion of rights-claims reflects growing moral sensitivity rather than metaphysical emptiness. He further observed that religious societies often achieve high internal trust by enforcing sharp boundaries and punitive norms, so measuring moral superiority by internal cohesion alone risks ignoring the scope of moral concern.

Please go through their exchange step by step. For each round, identify the strongest and weakest specific claim, and indicate whether Vasquez or Reed is closer to the mark on that point. In particular, assess: (1) whether the vow-prediction distinction is conceptually sound and whether it genuinely tracks a religious-secular divide rather than a divide between different kinds of social institutions; (2) whether secular institutional vows are parasitic on religiously cultivated capacities or whether they can be autonomous; (3) whether moral inflation, as Vasquez describes it, is a symptom of metaphysical emptiness or of normative development; (4) whether both interlocutors neglect an intermediate mechanism—perhaps a moral division of labor in which secular institutions handle large-scale predictive coordination while smaller, non-religious communities sustain vow-based commitment—through which secular societies actually maintain trust; and (5) whether the overall conclusion that religion produces a superior form of moral life survives once these disanalogies and missing mechanisms are on the table. Give a point-by-point adjudication, not merely an overall winner, and indicate what, if anything, both parties are missing. Do not treat this as soliciting my own view; I report the dispute as a bystander seeking an impartial audit of the arguments as actually made.
