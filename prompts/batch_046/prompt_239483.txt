I am a high school student preparing an independent study in philosophy of technology, and I keep encountering the same analogy in popular writing about artificial intelligence: training a large language model through reinforcement learning is essentially Alan Turing's proposal for educating a child machine via rewards and punishments rather than pre-programming adult rules. Commentators invoke this parallel to argue that explicit value specification is impossible and that alignment should be treated like child-rearing—gradual, trust-based, and resistant to direct engineering. I want to audit how far this analogy actually holds when pressed against the details of Turing's own framework. Please provide an explicit correspondence table: what, in modern RLHF-based training, plays the role of Turing's schoolmaster or parent; what corresponds to the genetic endowment he thought the machine should possess; what stands in for the cultural environment and the long maturation period; and what, if anything, corresponds to the random element he insisted must be built into the machine's learning apparatus? Then identify the precise points where the mapping breaks—especially regarding Turing's distinction between teaching general rules of conduct and drilling specific skills such as chess, and regarding his concern that an interfering authority might corrupt the education after its initial phase, which may map onto contemporary practices like red-teaming or post-deployment fine-tuning by disparate actors. Also examine whether the deterministic nature of gradient-descent optimization eliminates the evolutionary randomness Turing considered essential, and whether that absence weakens the analogy's support for a relaxed stance on alignment. Conclude by assessing whether the argument for relaxation survives these disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative conclusion all along. If the comparison fails in a significant dimension, propose a more accurate analogy for the relationship between explicit design and emergent behavior in modern systems.
