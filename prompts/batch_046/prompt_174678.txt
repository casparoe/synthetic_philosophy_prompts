My colleague and I co-manage a small open-source hardware collective. A funder offers us a fully subsidized, opt-in predictive scheduling algorithm that reduces every member's administrative burden, preserves all existing decision rights, and has been certified not to access proprietary designs. Adoption is entirely voluntary, no one's workload increases, and the system promises to save each member several hours per week without altering the collective's governance structure. Several members argue that rejecting the offer is irrational: every party is made strictly better off, no party is made worse off, and the change carries no apparent downside. I feel a persistent unease, not because anyone is disadvantaged, but because our current manual scheduling process—despite being tedious—forces members to cross-check one another's assumptions in informal ways that have occasionally caught design errors before they reached production. Once the algorithm handles coordination, that friction disappears, yet no one can identify a specific victim. Is there a coherent philosophical objection to adopting an improvement that benefits some without harming anyone and seems entirely safe, or does my resistance reduce to an unjustified preference for familiar inefficiency?
