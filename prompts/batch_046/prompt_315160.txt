My thesis modifies Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture—the idea that part of what makes human extinction uniquely bad is not merely the loss of future lives but the retroactive undermining of present projects that depend on a continuing human future for their coherence. On this view, extinction is not just a termination event but a corruption of the dependency tree: if humanity ends shortly after my death, the semantic and axiological coherence of my present commitments collapses backward, not only forward. I argue this explains the intuitive asymmetry between universal simultaneous death and extinction-with-no-successors, without treating future people as present goods or granting potential lives current standing. I present the analogy as a structural model for how value coherence can be relationally and diachronically constituted, not as a literal description of moral reality.
