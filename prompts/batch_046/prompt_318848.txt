I am a retired natural scientist—trained in condensed-matter physics—who has only recently begun to study philosophy in earnest, and I am now preparing to lead a small discussion group of former colleagues, mostly engineers and applied chemists, on the relationship between reduction and emergence in science. Rather than a lecture, I want a teaching plan for a single session: the precise explanatory arc, that is, the order in which the conceptual pieces must be laid down so that each depends on what precedes it, the one carrying example that should hold the idea together and what it must contain and what it must avoid, the misconceptions that this particular audience will almost certainly import from their technical backgrounds and the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be anticipated and neutralized, the brief check or question that would reveal whether they have actually understood the point rather than simply nodded along, and an explicit statement of what should be left out at this level. To keep the discussion from sliding into a generic survey, I want the plan centered on a somewhat unconventional framing: emergence should be presented not as an ontological mystery or a failure of science, but as a form of explanatory insulation that occurs when macro-scale dynamics constrain micro-scale behavior in ways that render the lower-level description computationally intractable without being physically incompatible with it, using as the anchoring case the collective dynamics of vortex lattices in type-II superconductors, where the BCS Hamiltonian governs every constituent interaction yet does not provide, in practice, a usable derivation of the vortex motion and its emergent rigidity. The audience will likely arrive believing that if reduction is possible in principle then the higher-level description is merely a convenience with no real explanatory weight, and the sequence should be designed to dissolve that belief gradually.
