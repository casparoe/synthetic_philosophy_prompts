I am designing a branching taxonomy for a graduate seminar on whether maximal moral goodness is desirable — the moral-saint problem, but approached through a specific diagnostic angle: whether the obstacle to sainthood is a conflict among goods, a psychological impossibility, or a conceptual mistake about what moral perfection entails. The instrument should be built as a tree of concrete questions, not as a list of named authors, so that a participant can trace her own answers without knowing which philosopher they map onto. Begin at the root: "Is there any ground project or non-moral life-plan that could be rationally retained while one is maximally responsive to moral demands?" From there, branch first on whether retaining such projects is (i) a necessary condition for a life worth living, (ii) permissible but not required, or (iii) actually incompatible with true moral perfection because any competing attachment dilutes virtue. At the next level, split on how to understand the psychological cost of total moral dedication — call it moral hypervigilance — asking whether it is best interpreted as (a) a contingent clinical failure, (b) a necessary feature of one-dimensional virtue, or (c) an illusion produced by assuming that moral goods must always compete with other goods rather than restructuring them.

Then introduce a branch on supererogation: does moral obligation have an upper threshold, so that actions beyond it are admirable but not required, or is every failure to do the best possible thing a moral defect? From these answers, the tree should generate eight to ten leaves. The leaves must include: a Wolf-style position that moral saints crowd out non-moral virtues and personal projects; a Singer-style consequentialist maximalist view for whom total dedication is not only desirable but obligatory; a Williams-style integrity view that ground projects are constitutive of identity and therefore cannot be sacrificed; a virtue-ethical integrationist view that true practical wisdom includes proper self-love, so a "saint" who neglects herself is not maximally good; a threshold deontologist who holds that there is a ceiling to obligation; and an unnamed or "integrationist exuberance" position holding that moral self-erasure is not a loss but a transformation in which the agent's preferences are fully restructured so that moral and personal goods coincide. Include also a leaf for the view that "maximal moral goodness" is not a coherent concept because moral demands are always comparative and context-sensitive, leaving no stable point at which one becomes a "saint."

Every leaf must carry the position's name (or "unnamed / unexplored") and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the consequence or case it must accept that its rivals avoid. Build the tree from the logical space: list the combinations of answers the branches allow, place the named positions in the occupied ones, and flag any unoccupied combination with a brief diagnosis — whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely open for exploration. Specifically, flag the status of the combination that accepts supererogation as incoherent, treats ground projects as necessary, and treats hypervigilance as an illusion. Phrase every node as a question a seminar participant could answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to; do not ask "Do you agree with Wolf?" or "Are you a consequentialist?" The path through the tree itself should function as the scoring key.

Finally, note explicitly which pairs of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate — for example, if two positions share every diagnostic answer but differ only in rhetorical emphasis or in their account of moral motivation — and explain whether the ambiguity can be resolved with a further branch or is intrinsic to the debate. Keep the tree formal and compact enough to fit on a single handout page.
