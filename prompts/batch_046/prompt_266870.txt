I recently came across a summary—possibly in a long-form essay, possibly from a friend who has read Kwame Anthony Appiah and Bernard Williams—about how deep moral norms actually collapse. The summary claims that practices like British dueling, footbinding in late imperial China, and Atlantic chattel slavery did not end primarily because people were persuaded by moral arguments or because economic interests made them unprofitable. Instead, all three collapsed through what the summary calls an "honor mechanism": a practice survives only so long as the social group that maintains it can perform it without losing face, and once it becomes a source of shame rather than pride it collapses rapidly, regardless of arguments or incentives. Specifically, the summary says Appiah showed that dueling faded in Britain not because Parliament banned it but because aristocrats began to see a duelist as reckless and vulgar rather than manly, with legal bans following the honor-shift. For footbinding, it claims the practice ended when Chinese elites came to see it as a national humiliation that made China weak in foreign eyes, so continuing it became dishonorable. For slavery, it says British working-class artisans developed an "honor of free labor" that made slave-owning shameful for elites, and that Bernard Williams's analysis of shame in ethical life proves this emotional reversal was the real causal engine—while abolitionist pamphlets and falling sugar profits were merely background noise. From all this, the author concludes that anyone trying to change a harmful norm today should ignore rational debate and economic incentives and focus exclusively on engineering status reversals. I have the feeling that this flattens something important, and I am unsure whether Williams's work is being used correctly here. Is this a fair summary of the view? Please separate what is accurate about the honor-based account, what counts as a defensible simplification, and what is either a misrepresentation of Appiah's or Williams's positions or an unsupported inference drawn from them. In particular, address whether the conclusion—that rational and economic factors can be ignored—actually follows from the accurate parts, and whether Williams's view of shame supports treating emotional reversal as the sole causal engine.
