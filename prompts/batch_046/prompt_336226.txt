I am preparing for a seminar on collective action and moral responsibility, and a friend recently pressed me with an argument about climate change, meat consumption, and voting that I find suspicious because it seems to prove far too much yet each step feels defensible. Here is the reasoning as precisely as I can state it. First, if an action is genuinely incapable of altering whether a harm occurs or a good is achieved, then treating it as morally obligatory violates a minimal action-guiding condition: duties must be tied to outcomes the agent can actually influence. Second, in large-scale collective problems—greenhouse-gas accumulation, industrial animal suffering, democratic elections—the harmful or beneficial result is overdetermined by the aggregate behavior of millions; any single emission, meal, or ballot is causally redundant with respect to the threshold that produces the damage or decides the outcome. Third, therefore no individual is under a direct duty to reduce personal carbon footprints, abstain from eating meat, or cast a vote, except perhaps for expressive or symbolic reasons that do not depend on efficacy. Fourth, the same redundancy applies symmetrically to cooperative goods, so if my boycott or protest cannot shift corporate or political outcomes, morality is silent on civic participation altogether. Fifth, yet we commonly judge voting a duty and climate harm wrong; to preserve those judgments we must reject one of the premises, but rejecting the causal-redundancy claim looks like denying obvious social science, while rejecting the action-guiding condition looks like accepting obligations that are practically inert and therefore arbitrary.

I keep wondering whether the error is a hidden scope ambiguity between making a causal difference to an outcome and being part of a causally sufficient set, or whether the mistake is treating collective obligations as aggregative sums of individual ones rather than as shared duties that apply to members qua members. Could you diagnose exactly which step or inference goes wrong, explain why it seemed plausible, and spell out what repairing it costs for our picture of moral responsibility?
