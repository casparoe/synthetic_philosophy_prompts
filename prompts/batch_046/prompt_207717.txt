I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about genuine knowability, especially once self-reference, collective verification, and bounded cognitive architecture enter the picture. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete scenarios.

First, an ordinary unknown: the exact number of water molecules in Lake Michigan right now is some specific figure, no one currently knows it, but a sufficiently resourced survey could in principle determine it. I say this is genuinely knowable; its being unknown now is only practical.

Second, a self-referential unknown: consider the statement "This very statement is not known by anyone at noon today." If it is true, then knowing it makes it false; if false, then someone knows it, which seems to make it true that it is not unknown... I keep oscillating, but my settled verdict is that if it is true, it is not genuinely knowable—knowing it is structurally impossible, not merely difficult.

Third, the Fitch-style conjunction: take a specific unknown mundane fact p (say, that there are exactly 14,203 craters larger than 1 km on the far side of the Moon) and form the conjunction "p and p is unknown to anyone." If p is indeed unknown, this conjunction is true. But if anyone knew the conjunction, they would know p, contradicting the second conjunct. So my verdict is that this conjunction, if true, is in principle unknowable—and that feels importantly different from the first case, even though both involve unknown truths.

Fourth, a nested existential: suppose a logician proves "There exists at least one arithmetical truth that no human will ever verify." She knows the existential claim, and she knows it concerns ignorance, but she does not know which specific arithmetical truth it is. I think the existential claim is knowable, even though particular instances falling under it may not be. That feels consistent.

Fifth, the hard pair. Case A: an AI system is designed so that it can compute any first-order arithmetical truth but is hard-coded never to represent its own epistemic states; it outputs "7+5=12 is unknown to me" only when the computation has not yet run. I think the system genuinely knows 7+5=12 once computed, and its self-ignorance report is just a processing glitch—merely practical limitation. Case B: a human mathematician reflects on her own cognitive limits and asserts "There is a true statement about this very system that this system can never verify." I think this is genuinely knowable in principle by an outside observer, but not by the system itself. The tension is that in both cases we have a bounded system encountering a truth about its own ignorance, yet I want to say Case A involves no structural barrier while Case B does. I am not sure that distinction holds.

Finally, a tentative repair: perhaps the principle is that a proposition is genuinely knowable just in case it can be verified without forcing the agent to represent themselves as both knowing and not knowing the same content, where "verification" means producing a justified true belief whose justification does not require contradictory self-attribution of epistemic states. On this view, Case 1 and Case 4 pass; Case 2 and Case 3 fail because they require exactly that contradictory representation; Case A passes because the glitch is not a representational contradiction; Case B fails for the internal agent but passes for the external one.

Does this principle work? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? Is there a simpler principle—perhaps just banning self-referential epistemic operators altogether—that saves most of the cases at the cost of making Case 4 or Case 5B look wrong? Please state the principle (or a small set of rival principles) precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on cases I have not mentioned (e.g., knowing a disjunction when one disjunct is a Fitch-style conjunction, or knowing that all knowable truths are known), and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
