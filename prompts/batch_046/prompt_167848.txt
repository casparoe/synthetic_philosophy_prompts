In a long-running seminar on epistemic structure, the debate between foundationalists, coherentists, and infinitists about the regress of justification has settled into an unusual stasis. Every participant can reproduce the canonical objections—circularity for the coherentist, arbitrariness for the foundationalist, psychological unreality for the infinitist—yet none has revised their position in years, even though all sides accept the same formal models of belief-dependence and agree that human cognition operates with finite working memory. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but taxonomic: the three camps are using “justification” to answer different normative questions—one about the genealogy of belief, one about the stability of a system under rational revision, and one about the absence of arbitrary explanatory termination—while treating these as competing answers to a single question.

Please diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement. Is it a substantive dispute masked by shared vocabulary, a verbal disagreement about the extension of “justified,” or a case of philosophers answering different questions under one heading? In your diagnosis, engage concretely with what each side actually requires: for example, whether the foundationalist’s demand for non-inferential support is a claim about cognitive architecture, a normative claim about doxastic entitlement, or an intuition about explanatory termination; whether the coherentist’s mutual-support model presupposes a specific holism about meaning or belief revision; and whether the infinitist’s infinite chain is intended as a psychological description, a normative ideal, or a formal possibility condition. Given your diagnosis, specify what would count as genuine progress—whether a distinction between doxastic and propositional justification, an empirical result about belief fixation, a formal result about the completeness of finite justification structures, a conceptual clarification of “arbitrary,” or an argument that the regress question itself is ill-posed—and explain precisely why that intervention would succeed where decades of counterexample have failed.
