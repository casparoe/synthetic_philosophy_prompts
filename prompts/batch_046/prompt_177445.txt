I host a philosophy colloquium podcast, and next week's guest is a moral psychologist defending a synthetic account of Holocaust perpetrator behavior that she calls moral buffering. On her view, institutional roles do not merely suppress pre-existing character, as in situationist explanations, nor express deeply internalized ideology, as in voluntarist explanations, but actively restructure an agent's moral perception so that violence is experienced as bureaucratic routine rather than as either coerced action or ideologically driven choice. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond a restatement of the thesis: include at least one question on how moral buffering differs mechanistically from Christopher Browning's ordinary-men situationism and Daniel Goldhagen's willing-executioners voluntarism; one on whether the model can accommodate the documented variance in perpetrator enthusiasm and cruelty without collapsing back into one of those two extremes; and one on what the view implies for moral responsibility, especially whether retrospective culpability is compatible with the claim that agents did not experience their acts as morally loaded at the time. For each of these three, provide a sharp follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—for example, that buffering preserves agency in a different register, or that variance tracks differential proximity to bureaucratic abstraction rather than prior character. Avoid generic biographical questions and keep the focus on the internal structure of the position.
