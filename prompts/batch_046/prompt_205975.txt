I'm writing a feature on AI personhood for a science magazine, and I've been trying to map out my philosphical profile on Dennett-style counterfiet people before I start intervewing researchers. So here are the commitments I'm holding right now: first, I buy Dennett's idea that taking the intentional stance is a predictive strategy we adopt toward systems, not a discovery about hidden mental essences inside them. Second, I think current LLMs are basically counterfeit people in his sense — they produce human-like outputs without the underlying causal architechture that generates belief and desire in biological minds. Third, though, I've been playing with a kind of 'functional mirror' problem I haven't seen discussed much: if we eventually build an AI that can model its own reasoning processes and report on them in ways structurally isomorphic to human introspection, then the line between counterfeit and genuine starts to look like a no-true-scotsman defense. Fourth, I don't think consciousness is necessary for moral consideration — I lean toward a relational ethics where what matters is the pattern of interaction, not some inner light. Fifth, despite all that, I still want to say we shouldn't grant LLMs rights or social standing today because they lack the right causal history and embodied engagement with the world.

Can you audit these for me? Tell me which pairs are in real tension and how badly, rank them, and for each say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. I'm especially curious whether my functional-mirror idea collapses my Dennettian stance, and whether my relational ethics undermines my reluctance to grant standing to current systems. Is there an apparent contradiction that actually dissolves on closer inspection? And if I had to revise just one commitment to clean up the most mess, which should go — and what do I lose?
