I hold an unconditional normative reading of Good's theorem on the value of information: since the expected value of sample information is never negative, any rational agent—individual or collective—should always acquire costless information before deciding, and any institutional closure of inquiry while free observation remains available is thereby defective. I extend this to claim that satisficing under bounded rationality is never rational in its own right, only pragmatically excusable, and that democratic scientific bodies ought not vote to halt research programs on practical grounds alone if further data could still be gathered without direct expense. What would genuinely count against this position? I am especially interested in whether strategic or adversarial environments—in which the very act of gathering information alters payoff structures or reveals intentions to an opponent—undermine the theorem's normative force, or merely restrict its domain. Would empirical results showing that agents systematically degrade decision quality by accumulating excess free information—for instance, through amplified confirmation bias or framing distortions in large datasets—count against the normative principle itself, or only against an empirical assumption about human rationality? Is there a decisive formal result that introduces hidden structural costs into apparently free information, or does the position risk being insulated by defining "cost" and "information" too narrowly? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what committed defenders of the unconditional view should themselves concede from what only critics would press, and offer an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and, if so, whether that is a defect for a normative principle of this kind.
