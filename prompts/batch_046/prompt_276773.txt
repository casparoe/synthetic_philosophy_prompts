For a postgraduate philosophy reading group of twelve meeting for ninety minutes in a seminar room with movable tables, design a structured simulation—call it the Calibration Council—that makes participants discover the instability of holding both an inside view and an outside view on their own reasoning at once, rather than letting them collapse comfortably into either pole. Inject a specific, novel mechanism: the indexical exemption rule, which states that any statistical calibration a participant derives from observing others must be applied to the group forecast but is suspended for themselves, on the explicit grounds that their own reasoning is transparent to them in a way others’ reasoning is not.

Divide the room into three groups of four. Provide each participant with six forecast cards (e.g., probabilistic judgments about technological or social outcomes), a hidden cognitive-profile card describing a specific reasoning bias they are prone to but must not reveal, a reliability-score sheet for cross-group evaluation, and collective-consequence tokens. The timeline should be precise: ten minutes for private, individual forecasting; twenty minutes for groups to observe and score another group’s reasoning patterns; fifteen minutes for a calibration round in which groups must adjust their collective forecasts using the outside-view reliability data they have gathered; and a final ten-minute individual-modesty vote in which each person must state whether they apply the group correction to themselves, with the exemption rule enforced by the facilitator. The payoff should be non-monetary but consequential—collective reputation points that determine which group’s final forecast is adopted as the official group position, plus individual consistency points awarded only if a participant’s private forecast, group adjustment, and final vote align, thereby creating real pressure to maintain coherence rather than to win.

Arrange the rules so that the philosophical tension emerges before it is named: participants should initially try to apply statistical corrections to themselves, find the exemption rule blocks them, attempt to third-personalise themselves to escape it, and only afterwards recognise the problem they have enacted. Include a debrief that maps each phase onto the distinction—private forecasting as inside view, cross-group scoring as outside view, the calibration round as the demand for epistemic modesty, and the exemption/vote as its breakdown. Predict explicitly what will fail the first time: participants will either reject the exemption as irrational self-favouring or perform elaborate mental gymnastics to view themselves as objects, thereby collapsing the first-person perspective they were meant to preserve. Be frank about which feature of the inside/outside distinction the simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the genuine phenomenological opacity of one’s own reasoning processes that makes the exemption feel rational rather than merely strategic—and state precisely what the facilitator must supply verbally to make that gap visible.
