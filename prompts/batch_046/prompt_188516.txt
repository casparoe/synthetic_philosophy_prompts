I volunteer as a docent at a small maritime museum that also maintains a working boatyard, and I've been trying to understand an argument that keeps breaking out between our staff historians and our boatbuilders. The historians want to keep our nineteenth-century fishing vessels as static artifacts with original timber and iron, displayed behind ropes; the boatbuilders insist that preservation means keeping them seaworthy with modern engines, fiberglass, and replaced planking so they can actually sail. They talk past each other so thoroughly that I've started to think of it as two different worlds with no shared ruler. A friend mentioned Thomas Kuhn's idea of paradigm shifts and incommensurability — the claim that competing scientific frameworks can be so different that there is no neutral standard to judge between them. I want you to restate Kuhn's account of paradigm shifts and incommensurability entirely in the vocabulary and practical concerns of heritage boat preservation, using terms like original materials, seaworthiness, working use, museum display, and craft continuity, and mapping normal science, crisis, scientific revolution, and incommensurability onto the historian-boatbuilder conflict as precisely as you can. Then I need the residue report: which parts of Kuhn's theory translate cleanly into preservation practice, which parts only survive if I distort either the boatyard situation or Kuhn's meaning, and which parts have no counterpart at all. Finally, tell me whether the leftover pieces point to something Kuhn understood about conceptual change that heritage work is missing, or whether they reveal that Kuhn's framework carries hidden assumptions about scientific progress that do not actually belong in a museum boatyard.
