A study group has reconstructed Dennett's brain-transplant scenario and drawn what they take to be an inescapable conclusion about personal location and identity. Here is their chain.

(1) Hamlet's brain is surgically removed and placed in a vat, where it continues to control his original body via radio transmission. Call this brain B.

(2) Because Hamlet's conscious experience continues uninterrupted with B, Hamlet is now located where B is: in the vat, not in his original body.

(3) A perfect functional duplicate of B, call it B*, is constructed and installed in a new body. B* has identical mental states, memories, and functional organization.

(4) If the feature that makes B Hamlet is its present psychological profile, then B* has that same feature, so B* is also Hamlet.

(5) Therefore Hamlet is now wholly present in two separate bodies at once.

(6) But one person cannot occupy two non-overlapping spatial locations simultaneously.

(7) So (4) must be false: psychological profile alone is not sufficient for being Hamlet.

(8) The difference must be historical. B is Hamlet's original brain, relocated; B* is a copy with no direct causal link to the pre-surgery Hamlet.

(9) Hence personal identity requires direct causal or historical continuity with the original.

(10) But B itself was removed from Hamlet's original body. Its only continuity with pre-surgery Hamlet is neural and psychological, not biological-bodily. If causal continuity requires being the very same physical object in its original biological context, then B is no more Hamlet than B* is.

(11) So Hamlet died on the operating table, and neither brain is him.

(12) By extension, since human bodies constantly replace their constituent matter, we never survive any physical change; diachronic personal identity is an illusion.

They say this shows Dennett's puzzle does not merely make location mysterious—it dissolves the self altogether. I want to preserve that B is Hamlet (so Hamlet survives in the vat) and that B* is not Hamlet (the copy is a different person), but I cannot accept (12). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there an equivocation between kinds of causal continuity—say, spatiotemporal continuity of a particular brain versus continuity of a biological organism—that saves B without saving B*, or does saving B force me to accept some other counterintuitive consequence about exactly where Hamlet is?
