I'm working out whether my commitments on self-binding and institutional precommitment form a coherent package, and I want an audit, not a lecture. Here is my position:

(1) I accept Ulysses contracts at the personal level: it can be rational—sometimes required—to bind one's future self against anticipated weakness of will, because this honors the preferences of a single enduring agent across time.

(2) I reject the collective analog: democratic societies should not rigidly bind future generations through constitutional entrenchment, since there is no persisting collective "self" whose autonomy is thereby served.

(3) I nonetheless defend entrenched constitutional rights, but only on instrumental grounds: they protect individuals' synchronic autonomy from temporary majoritarian passion, not as expressions of any collective will binding itself.

(4) I hold that justified precommitment mechanisms should have revocability conditions that mirror the authority that created them. So personal contracts should be breakable only through a structured, reflective procedure—not by impulse—while constitutional provisions should require supermajoritarian amendment.

(5) I recognize that treating a future democratic majority's preferences as non-authoritative (when they conflict with entrenched rights) is structurally parallel to treating one's future impulsive preferences as non-authoritative in case (1). But I deny that this parallelism supports any collective-level analogy, and I insist it does not smuggle in a hidden collective agent.

Please tell me which of these pull against each other, which pairs only appear to conflict, and for each real tension what the cheapest revision is and what it costs. I'm especially concerned that I am implicitly relying on a notion of "authentic preference" in (1) and (3) that I officially reject at the collective level in (2) and (5), and that (4) may impose a binding force on future generations that (2) says is illegitimate.
