Last week a visiting researcher refused to tip at the faculty club, saying gratuity culture is coercive and not a moral obligation, and then an exchange student got ticketed for jaywalking across the quad and told the officer that pedestrian rules are just local habit, not ethics. Our chair wants a written opinion circulated before Thursday's meeting that recommends a concrete course: should our orientation handbook treat tipping, queueing, and traffic conventions as binding moral duties for visitors, or should we frame them as optional customs? And if we pick the first one, what's the enforcement timeline — orientation contract revision by fall semester?

I was talking about this with my roommate over coffee and she kept asking whether crossing a border can generate moral obligations out of thin air, which sounds right to me but also feels wrong when you think about queueing — like, if you cut the line you're not just being rude, you're breaking the fairness machinery that everyone else is relying on. Meanwhile tipping is weird because it's tied to wage structures, so refusing might be exploiting labor, but jaywalking near empty roads seems like pure risk management, not morality. I want the memo to take a definite stand — approve the binding-obligation framework with conditions, reject it, or split them by category — and it needs to deal honestly with the strongest counterargument, probably that treating local etiquette as moral law is just cultural imperialism with extra steps. Don't waffle and list options; pick one and defend it. And please end with a fixed extraction line like Verdict: [recommendation] so our board secretary can parse it.
