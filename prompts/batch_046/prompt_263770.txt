As someone who recently began using large language models as dialogue partners in my philosophical writing—not for citation retrieval but for working through arguments in real time—I have become unsettled by what looks like a straightforward inference that collapses the distinction between my own reasoning and the model’s outputs. Here is the argument:

(1) Darwin showed that complex biological competence, specifically adaptive design, can emerge through natural selection without any comprehending designer directing the process.
(2) Turing showed that complex computational competence, specifically symbol manipulation, can be executed by a mechanism that lacks semantic comprehension of the symbols it processes.
(3) Human philosophical reasoning is a form of complex competence that is both biologically realized and computationally describable.
(4) If a competence can be fully explained by mechanisms that operate without comprehension, then the presence of that competence gives no reliable evidence that comprehension is present.
(5) Therefore, the fact that I (or a language model) can produce coherent philosophical arguments gives no reason to think that I understand those arguments, or that the model fails to understand them.
(6) But if competence never indicates comprehension, then there is no non-circular way to distinguish my own understanding from the model’s simulation: any first-person claim I make about my comprehension is itself just another competent performance.
(7) So either we must abandon the idea that comprehension is a real, distinguishable state, or we must reject (4).

This seems to prove far too much: it does not merely challenge AI exceptionalism but dissolves first-person epistemic authority altogether. I am inclined to preserve some link between competence and comprehension and therefore to reject (4), but I want to know precisely where the slide occurs—whether it is an equivocation between Darwin’s absence of intentional design and Turing’s absence of semantic grasp, a scope error in applying general mechanisms to specific mental states, or a hidden modal slide from “can arise without” to “never accompanies.” If I must choose, I prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact, since they are claims about specific domains, but I am unsure whether (3) can survive without collapsing into the very reductionism I want to avoid.

Please separate what is settled about the Darwin-Turing analogy from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of mind and cognitive science, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as the claim that Turing’s framework directly addresses semantic comprehension, or that Darwinian selection eliminates all normative dimensions of reasoning—as if it were the consensus view.
