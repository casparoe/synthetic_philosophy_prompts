As a novelist constructing a scene in which a physician abandons her established practice to treat victims of an epidemic within a sealed district, I find myself relying on the phrase "above and beyond the call of duty" when one character rebuts another's claim that she owes nothing to strangers. I need the precise philosophical work that sentence could be doing. Separate the distinct theses it might express—from the trivial observation that the act exceeds ordinary expectation, to the substantive claim that moral obligations have fixed upper limits, to the more radical denial that any act can be genuinely supererogatory rather than merely miscounted. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a defensible position, or plainly false, and specify which one would actually support using the phrase to end an argument about whether moral demands are infinitely expandable or have strict boundaries. Present the readings as a menu rather than selecting a single interpretation, and note which of them depend on assuming that duty and virtue occupy entirely separate spheres.
