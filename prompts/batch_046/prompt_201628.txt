After recently attempting to translate verse in which rhyme, meter, and phonetic echo serve as argumentative structures rather than decorative frames, I have found both literalist and free-paraphrase accounts inadequate. Before committing to any theoretical framework, I want the conditions of adequacy made explicit. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of poetic translation under formal constraint must meet: for each, provide the motivating case or phenomenon, the distinction it requires, and your judgment of whether it is non-negotiable or revisable. Specifically address (i) the adjudication of cases—such as the one I encountered—where a source-language homophonic rhyme pair binding a term of divine favor to its acoustic near-twin of withdrawal carries thematic weight, yet the target language offers no equivalent homophony and the quatrain's rhyme scheme demands preservation of sonic identity at the corresponding syllabic position; (ii) whether formal fidelity can ever justify semantic distortion, or semantic fidelity justify formal rupture; (iii) the line between form that is meaning-bearing and form that is merely conventional; and (iv) any pair of these desiderata you judge jointly unsatisfiable, with the conflict spelled out. End with a brief comparative assessment—scored against your list—of semantic literalism, dynamic-equivalence functionalism, and strict formal-preservationism, noting precisely where each succeeds and where it collapses under the weight of its own commitments.
