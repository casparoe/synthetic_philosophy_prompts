You are an expert in philosophical logic and formal semantics, acting as senior advisor to the editorial board of a cross-disciplinary journal that publishes work at the intersection of formal semantics and practical reasoning. Before constructing anything, take a deep breath, think step by step, and briefly brainstorm in bullet form how deflationary (minimalist), hierarchical/Tarskian, revision-theoretic, and paraconsistent (dialetheist) theories of truth each treat sentences that apply a truth predicate to themselves—specifically noting where they agree that a sentence is meaningful, where they diverge on whether it is false, truth-valueless, or genuinely contradictory, and which of those divergences actually matter for an editor who is not a logician.

Once you have mapped those commitments, design a reusable, approximately fifteen-minute editorial evaluation procedure that translates the theories into concrete steps for submissions whose central claim structurally embeds self-reference—not merely citing the liar paradox as an illustration, but making the self-reference do argumentative work. The submissions in question include abstracts or core theses such as claiming one's own falsehood, asserting that no true statement in the present volume is assertible, or deriving a normative or methodological conclusion from the claim that correspondence truth fails for the very argument being advanced.

Your deliverable should be a checklist or flowchart in words, ordered as follows: (1) classify the form of self-reference (direct, indirect, or meta-linguistic); (2) for each of the four theories, specify whether the claim is licensed as meaningful, evaluated as false, assigned no truth-value, or accepted as a true contradiction; (3) state the exact question the editor should put to the author at each branch; (4) identify the precise points where the procedure must halt and escalate to a specialist rather than letting the editor decide; and (5) indicate which theoretical distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be safely collapsed for practical purposes. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls disguised as rules, and flag the conditions under which the procedure will misfire or produce an unstable verdict.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical submissions:

(A) A paper whose abstract reads, The central result proved below is false.

(B) A paper arguing that because self-reference undermines correspondence theories of truth, scientific claims ought to be evaluated by coherence alone, and offering the present argument itself as an exemplar of that undermining.

(C) A paper claiming that revision-theoretic semantics resolves every liar-type sentence without contradiction, and therefore concluding that the journal should reject any submission that assumes bivalence for self-referential statements.

In applying the procedure to (C), include the specific verdict that the procedure delivers but that the author will reject, and explain why the procedure yields it anyway despite the author's objection. Finally, conclude by stating clearly what the procedure cannot do—for example, whether it can adjudicate between the theories themselves, substitute for formal peer review, or eliminate all value-laden editorial judgment.
