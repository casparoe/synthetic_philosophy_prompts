Read Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon through the philosophical question of whether brain size—or, more precisely, organizational metrics such as encephalization quotient—is morally relevant to a being's status. The plot turns on Charlie Gordon's surgical alteration of his brain: his intelligence surges, then collapses as the tissue degenerates. The moral drama asks whether his enhanced cognition makes him more worthy of autonomy, affection, and respect, and whether his regression erodes those claims. I want a reading that takes seriously the distinction between absolute brain mass and structural neural complexity, asking whether the book implicitly treats moral standing as tracking cognitive capacity, or whether Charlie's dignity persists invariantly through his neural changes. Point to specific scenes—his relationship with Alice, the researchers' reduction of him to experimental data, and the letter he writes as his cognition declines—and show where they illuminate or resist the idea that only organizational brain properties carry moral weight. Does the novel suggest that biological metrics of intelligence miss something essential about dignity, or does it inadvertently confirm that moral relevance scales with neural architecture?
