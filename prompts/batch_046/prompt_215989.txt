I'm on the allocation board for our university's Social Impact Investment Fund and we have this proposal sitting in front of us that I need help framing for thursday's vote. It's a pilot program — let's call it the High-Earnings Pledge Pipeline — that recruits juniors into quant finance and consulting tracks with one condition: they sign a contract pledging 15% of post-tax income for ten years to charities vetted by an external "impact audit" panel. The twist is that the audit panel uses a measurability-weighted scoring system, so direct bednet-style interventions get full credit but systemic advocacy or local institution-building gets discounted by about 60% because outcomes are harder to attribute. There's also a "career-offset" clause where students calculate the estimated negative externalities of their job — market volatility contribution, regulatory arbitrage, that kind of thing — and increase their pledge to cover that gap.

Half the board loves it. They say it's just institutionalizing earning-to-give with rigor and that the offset clause solves the moral hazard problem. The other half thinks this is exactly the institutional critique people make of EA: the university becomes a recruiting arm for one moral calculus, measurability bias warps what counts as good, and the offset logic pretends you can commodify moral responsibility and buy your way out of harm your career actively produces. They also worry it's coercive — technically voluntary, but the mentorship and funding they recieve are tied to signing.

I need a memo I can circulate before the meeting recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It needs a concrete bottom line, not just "let's discuss more," and it has to take the strongest objection seriously rather than waving it away. The memo should address whether the measurability discount is defensible as a decision procedure or just bias baked in, and whether the career-offset clause solves the earning-to-give problem or makes it worse. If we go with approve-with-modifications, flag concrete conditions — like decoupling the pledge from university resources, or requiring the audit panel include critics of quantification. Don't give me bibliographies or citation lists; just reason it through.
