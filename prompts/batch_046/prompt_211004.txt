As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I have been trying to formalize how I actually make decisions when I am unsure which moral framework is correct—not just factually unsure, but normatively unsure about which theory applies. Below are five concrete work-related cases with my gut verdicts. I want to know whether they hang together under one principle of decision-making under moral uncertainty, or whether I must abandon at least one.

Case 1. I am about to deploy a predictive-policing feature. I am roughly 60 percent convinced that maximizing aggregate welfare is correct and 40 percent convinced that violating an explicit fairness commitment in the product spec is impermissible regardless of welfare gains. The feature improves aggregate metrics but breaks that commitment. Verdict: I should not deploy. Even at 40 percent confidence, the rights-like constraint feels like it should block the action.

Case 2. I am choosing a server architecture. Option A is cheap now but requires full replacement in two years; Option B is expensive now but lasts ten years. I am split between weighting present people heavily and giving strong weight to distant future generations affected by e-waste. I pick A without serious regret. Verdict: near-term practical considerations should dominate when uncertainty concerns distant-future moral status; I should not treat speculative long-range obligations as veto-level.

Case 3. I run a large language-model fine-tuning job for a temporary client demo. It consumes significant electricity, produces nothing lasting, and will be deleted in one month. I am genuinely unsure whether a non-sentient but capable system could have even minimal moral standing. I run the job but feel uneasy. Verdict: I should have refused, applying a precautionary default against treating a possibly-status-bearing entity as disposable—even though I think sentience is almost certainly absent.

Case 4. A teammate asks me to sign off on code modules I did not review, saying they are overwhelmed and the release is low-risk. I am unsure whether virtue-ethical considerations about professional integrity are genuinely normative or merely conventional. I sign off. Verdict: that was wrong. The misrepresentation corrupts my professional role in a way that feels wrong independent of outcomes, and that judgment does not depend on my being certain virtue ethics is correct.

Case 5. I discover a critical security vulnerability. Immediate disclosure risks panic and exploitation; a two-week delay allows silent patching. I am about 50/50 on whether transparency is an absolute duty or one weighted consideration. I delay disclosure. Verdict: that is permissible. The risk of harm makes transparency defeasible, and my uncertainty about its absolute status does not turn it into a veto.

Together these seem unstable: in Case 1 I treat a 40-percent-confidence constraint as blocking, but in Case 5 I treat a 50-percent-confidence transparency duty as defeasible. In Case 2 I discount distant-future obligations, yet in Case 3 I apply a precautionary principle despite near-certainty that the entity lacks status. Is there a unified account—perhaps based on reversibility, the kind of moral risk, or the structure of the uncertainty—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I drop, and why? Apply your account to one further case: I have already built an internal security tool; publishing it requires one command. I am about 70 percent sure it will help defensive engineers and 30 percent sure it will be weaponized. Should I publish?

Please lead with your bottom-line answer in the first sentence—stating whether a unified principle exists and, if so, what it is—then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Begin immediately with that bottom line.
