I have recently joined the advisory board of a museum that holds objects taken during the 1897 Benin Expedition and is now revising its restitution protocol, and I find myself unable to articulate precisely why the proposed framework disturbs me. The policy distinguishes between objects for which claimants can demonstrate membership in an unbroken ceremonial practice that continues unchanged today and objects that were always secular court art or whose ritual use was interrupted by colonial violence itself; only the first category qualifies for return, while the second remains under a mandate of global stewardship. The board treats this as a neutral, administrable filter, yet I suspect it conceals a substantive philosophical theory about the grounds of cultural property rights—perhaps that such rights depend on present-tense functional continuity with the pre-colonial community rather than on the wrongfulness of the original seizure.
