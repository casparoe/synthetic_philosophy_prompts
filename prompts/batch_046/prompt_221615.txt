I'm auditing a graduate seminar on democratic theory and keep returning to a disagreement that arose during our session on the boundary problem. One student, Mira, defended the principle that anyone whose interests are significantly affected by a collective decision should be included in the demos that makes it. She applied this to a concrete case: if a country's energy policy contributes to drought that displaces agricultural communities across a border, those displaced communities should have formal representation in the energy-policy demos, because the policy's effects do not respect territorial boundaries. Another student, Jonas, argued that this application reveals a deeper flaw in the principle itself. To determine who is affected by a decision, Jonas said, you must already have specified what the decision is and which outcomes count as relevant effects; but specifying the decision requires knowing whose preferences and welfare constraints are relevant to the collective choice set, which is precisely the boundary question the principle was meant to answer. Mira responded that this is only an epistemic circularity—in practice we can identify affected parties through causal analysis without presupposing membership—and that Jonas was conflating the difficulty of applying a criterion with its conceptual emptiness. Jonas countered that every causal analysis of "affectedness" already encodes normative judgments about which interests matter and which temporal horizons to include, so the "independent" identification always smuggles in a prior demos.

They asked me to referee, and I want a careful adjudication rather than a diplomatic compromise. For each round of their exchange—the cross-border displacement example, the circularity charge, the distinction between epistemic and conceptual failure, and the claim about smuggled normative judgments—please say who is right about what, whether either party is missing something important, and whether the "all affected interests" principle survives once these specific objections are on the table. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want the reasoning worked out precisely as it applies to this exchange, with the mapping between their claims made explicit.
