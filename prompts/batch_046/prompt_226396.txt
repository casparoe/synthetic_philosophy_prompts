I've been retired from a molecular biology lab for about three years now and lately I've been sitting in on a philosophy discussin group at the commnity college. We've been stuck for months on the is-ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy, and it's driving me a bit nuts. On one side there are people who say that any evolutionary story about why we have moral beliefs — like that cooperation was selected for because it improved group survival — is automatically committing Moore's fallacy, because you're going from 'this trait was selected' to 'this trait is good.' On the other side people say that's wrong, evolutionary explanations are purely descriptive and don't carry any normative weight unless you explicitly add a premise like 'survival is good,' which they admit is extra.

Everyone in the room seems to accept Hume's basic point that you can't derive an ought from an is, and nobody denies that describing a biological fucntion isn't the same as endorsing it. But we keep circling back: are evolutionary debunkers of morality secretly smuggling in a normative claim when they call a belief 'maladaptive' or 'dysfunctional'? Or is the anti-debunker side just being overly defensive about science?

My working theory, coming from years of talking about 'normal fucntion' in enzyme assays — where we definitely meant 'this is how it should work under standard conditions' without being moral about it — is that the disagreement isn't really about morality at all. It's about whether teleonomic descriptions in biology are already carrying a hidden normativity that makes the gap smaller than it looks. If that's right, then maybe we're answering different questions under the same name: one side asking about logical derivation, the other about whether scientific language is value-neutral. But I'm not sure. Is this stall actually a hidden empirical disagreement about what biologists mean by 'function'? Is it a verbal dispute about the word 'naturalistic'? Or are we just stuck because both sides have bedrock intuitions about whether science can ever touch ethics? And if your diagnosis is right — whatever it is — what would actually move us? A formal result? A clearer distinction? Or is this one of those debates where nothing will shift anyone because the real disagreement is about what counts as a reason in the first place?
