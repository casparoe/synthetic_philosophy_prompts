Instead, I want to investigate what I will call the welfare-replacement problem: when a high-turnover, high-biomass ecosystem is replaced by a low-turnover, low-biomass landscape, is the change best understood as a reduction in total suffering, a redistribution across temporal scales, or a transformation in the kind of suffering that occurs?

More specifically, existing debates tend to treat suffering as an aggregate that can be summed across individuals and compared across ecological states. I suspect this relies on an unstated continuity assumption — that suffering is a homogeneous quantity scaling linearly with the number of sentient lives. I want to test whether that assumption holds once we distinguish between (i) momentary welfare states, (ii) lifetime-integrated welfare trajectories, and (iii) what I am tentatively calling capacity-weighted suffering potential — the moral significance of the mere existence of neural architectures capable of negative experience, regardless of whether those capacities are actualized in a given ecological state.

The project will not collect ecological data. Rather, it will construct a formal conceptual framework — a welfare-accounting schema — applying these three metrics to standardized ecosystem-transition scenarios (e.g., primary tropical forest to pasture, or wetland to degraded brownfield). The aim is to determine whether the moral evaluation of habitat destruction depends essentially on metric choice, and whether any metric can be privileged without smuggling in a controversial axiological commitment.
