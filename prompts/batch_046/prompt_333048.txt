hey so i'm in a phd program in philosophy of psych and i've been tracking this pattern in the virtue ethics vs situationism debate that i don't have a clean name for. here's what happens: a study comes out showing people act totally differently in slightly different contexts -- like the classic good samaritan study or the bystander effect stuff. the situationist takes this as proof there's no stable character trait. then the virtue defender comes back and says virtue just is being responsive to context in the right way, so the variability is exactly what we'd expect from a virtuous person. i've seen this happen in responces to Doris's lack of character, and again in some more recent moral education literature. the weird thing is they're not just tweaking the theory, they're flipping the evidential valence: the data that was meant to kill the view becomes proof of it. but it's not exactly motte-and-bailey because there's no defensible core being hidden behind an extreme version -- it's more like redefining the trait so that its absence looks like presence. is there an established concept for this specific argumentative move? and if not, can we name it and draw the boundary lines? i want to distinguish it from ordinary theory revison, and also from cases where a theory genuinely predicts variability. also what's the boundary case where this is just legitimate refinement? a bare name won't do -- i need the carving: what exactly it is, what it isn't, and where it fails.
