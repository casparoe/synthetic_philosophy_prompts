I am a philosophy professor preparing a session for my graduate seminar in epistemology and the philosophy of science, with twelve doctoral students and ninety minutes available. We have been working through the problem of archaeological inference—specifically, how institutional beliefs and social hierarchies are reconstructed from material traces in the complete absence of textual corroboration—and I want the students to feel the conceptual force of this gap rather than merely hear it described. Please act as an expert in philosophical pedagogy with deep familiarity in both epistemology and the philosophy of archaeology. Before proposing any structure, take a moment to brainstorm the core tension: the distance between an object-assemblage and the institutional meanings projected onto it, including issues of evidential underdetermination, the theory-ladenness of observation, and the particular risk of importing modern categorical schemes into non-literate contexts.

Design a runnable classroom simulation or structured role-play that makes this tension unavoidable. Divide the seminar into small interpretation teams and provide each with an identical, deliberately ambiguous catalog of artifacts from a hypothetical non-literate site—fragmented ceramic patterning, arranged bone clusters, and the spatial layout of a putative silent sanctuary—without any written or oral contextual record. The rules should require each team to construct a fully coherent narrative about religious belief, power structure, and ritual practice using only these shared objects. Crucially, arrange the debate phase so that teams present their reconstructions and discover that their accounts are internally consistent yet radically incompatible, with no team able to defeat the others using only the common artifact-data. After this collision, conduct a debrief that maps each phase of the activity onto a specific philosophical problem—the underdetermination of institutional theory by material evidence, the analogy to radical translation applied to objects rather than utterances, and the anachronistic projection of contemporary categories—while being frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce, such as the actual temporal ambiguity of stratigraphic layering or the emotional stakes of real fieldwork, and instructing the facilitator on how to supply that missing dimension verbally. Specify the time allocation, group dynamics, predictable failures—such as one team dominating through rhetorical confidence rather than evidential superiority, or students treating the ambiguity as merely temporary pending more data—and exactly how to handle them. The students should leave seeing that archaeological inference is not a neutral reading but an active construction with irreducible alternatives, and they should arrive at this realization through the rules and payoffs of the game itself, not through a lecture delivered afterward. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the design must be fully answerable without access to external sources.
