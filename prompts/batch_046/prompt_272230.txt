Consider the following track record of verdicts given by a moral philosopher, Dr. V, on deontological puzzles. In the surgeon-transplant case she forbids killing one healthy patient to harvest organs for five, regardless of the net lives saved. She also forbids pushing an innocent bystander onto trolley tracks to stop a car that would kill five. Yet she permits flipping a switch that diverts the trolley onto a side track where one person stands, foreseeing but not intending that death. She further permits breaking a solemn promise when necessary to save a life, and permits lying to a murderer about a victim’s location. Now consider a new case that brings out what some call the paradox of deontology: an emergency physician holds exactly one dose of a life-saving drug and faces two patients, A and B, who are medically identical and both need the full dose immediately; there is no time to split it. Administering it to either will save that patient and let the other die. There is also a third option: administer a placebo to both so that both die, thereby honoring a self-imposed procedural rule—never be the direct causal source of an unequal life-or-death outcome among equally deserving patients. Predict Dr. V’s verdict: should the physician violate the rule and save one arbitrarily, or withhold the drug from both to preserve procedural equality? Identify the two competing principles her earlier rulings could be tracking—one centered on a categorical prohibition of intentional harm combined with overridable duties of truth and fidelity, and another centered on a deontic consistency constraint that forbids the agent from actively selecting among equals even when inaction produces greater harm. Give your best-guess prediction with reasons, and specify the further hypothetical that would most clearly distinguish the two principles—perhaps a scenario in which the agent can save both patients only by inflicting a minor, non-lethal harm on a third party, versus one in which saving one requires destroying a shared resource that both patients need equally but neither can claim prior entitlement to.
