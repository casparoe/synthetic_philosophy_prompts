The concept I need to explain is not merely the standard phenomenological distinction between guilt and shame, but the more specific normative claim that a culture can, through institutional design, calibrate which moral emotion dominates its public life — and that this calibration determines which moral failures become visible and reparable. The audience will likely arrive thinking that guilt is private and shame is social, and that emotional regimes are merely cultural accidents rather than design choices.

Plan the session's explanatory arc. Second, identify the single carrying example: I was considering Japan's post-war debates about accommodating haji within a modern legal framework, but I worry it demands too much cultural background for a fifty-minute session; if not that, propose an alternative case that shares the same structural features (a deliberate institutional shift in emotional framing with visible normative consequences) without requiring extensive context, and specify exactly what the example must include and what it must avoid. Fourth, provide one closing check — a question or brief exercise — that reveals whether they have understood the normative stakes of emotional calibration rather than simply reproduced the guilt/shame taxonomy.
