My philosophy advisor has rendered a consistent set of verdicts on comparative value across constructed cases, though she has never articulated her underlying principle. She judges that a life containing exactly one moment of intense well-being is better than a life containing none, and that a life with two such moments is better than one with one, maintaining this pairwise ordering without exception up through ninety-nine versus one hundred. Nevertheless, she denies that a life with one hundred moments is better than a life with none, treating that long-chain inference as an illegitimate accumulation. She also holds that when A is barely better than B and B barely better than C, A and C are incommensurable rather than ordered. Additionally, she maintains that adding a single trivial pleasure to an already excellent life never improves it, though adding the identical pleasure to a mediocre life does. Next week she must evaluate a case I have designed: life L contains fifty discrete moments of intense well-being arranged in a single uninterrupted cluster, while life M contains the same fifty moments distributed evenly across an identical lifespan so that no two are adjacent; every other feature is held constant. Predict her verdict—whether L is better than M, M better than L, or they are incommensurable—identify the implicit rule her earlier judgments jointly support, such as a non-transitive aggregation principle, a saturation-sensitive account of well-being, or a structural condition on the distribution of goods, and state which rule you take her to be running and why. Finally, specify the single further pair of lives that would most cleanly discriminate her actual rule from its nearest competitor.
