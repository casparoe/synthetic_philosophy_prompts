I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, currently working through an independent study course on philosophy of psychiatry. We've been discussing Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects—the way psychiatric classifications can change the very people they categorize, so that the category and its subjects co-evolve over time. In our discussion group, a disagreement has hardened around whether this dynamic undermines the reality of psychiatric kinds altogether, and it has become especially sharp now that someone proposed applying the framework to a new, narrowly defined condition: what they call "persistent feed-state dissociation," a hypothetical clinical profile describing individuals whose sense of continuous selfhood seems to fragment specifically across algorithmically curated information streams.

One side argues that looping effects are devastating for realism about psychiatric categories: if labeling someone with "feed-state dissociation" actually produces the fragmented self-experience it purports to describe—by giving people a narrative template, altering their self-monitoring, and reshaping their social interactions—then the category is more of a self-fulfilling artifact than a discovery. They point out that classic cases like multiple personality disorder seem to have proliferated precisely where clinicians looked for them. The other side replies that looping is compatible with realism: interactive kinds are still kinds. They note that diabetes classifications loop back on patient behavior (diet monitoring, insulin protocols) without anyone claiming diabetes is invented, and that suffering reported under the proposed profile is genuinely debilitating regardless of how the label arrived. Both sides agree on the empirical observations—people do report the symptoms, behaviors do shift after clinical framing, and the suffering is not feigned—but they have not moved in months. The first side insists that "real" requires some independence from the classificatory practice; the second insists that independence is the wrong criterion, and that what matters is whether the loop tracks a stable pattern of harm and response.

I am struggling to see what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a hidden empirical dispute about whether feed-state dissociation persists in the absence of clinical labeling? Is it a normative disagreement about what standards a category must meet to count as real? Are they answering different questions—one about the ontology of psychiatric kinds, the other about clinical utility—under a single heading?
