In a philosophy-of-mind seminar, a fellow student keeps invoking the line that "a symbol is meaningful only if it is physically grounded in interaction with the world." She deploys it to reject both classical symbol-manipulation theories of cognition and recent claims that large language models achieve semantic understanding through statistical learning alone, suggesting that without direct sensorimotor engagement, manipulation of signs can never amount to comprehension. Before our next session, I want to map the logical space this slogan actually occupies. Please separate the precise theses it could be expressing: (i) the tautology that every actual symbol token is a physical object with causal properties; (ii) the causal-informational claim that semantic content requires direct, unmediated causal chains linking token and referent; (iii) the embodied-functionalist claim that grounding requires the symbol to participate in a specific causal-functional architecture tied to perception and action, though not necessarily direct token-to-world causation; (iv) the phenomenological claim that genuine grounding requires conscious intentional directedness at the referent; and (v) a stronger embodied claim that continuous, real-time sensorimotor coupling is necessary, as opposed to a one-time developmental grounding through prior embodied interaction. For each thesis, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or self-undermining—for instance, whether (v) would also deny semantic competence to a blind mathematician or to a human using language after temporary sensory deprivation. Then say which of these readings the classic formulation of the symbol-grounding problem actually supports, which ones genuinely license her dismissal of statistical-learning models, and which ones she is likely conflating. Conclude by identifying the precise point at which her argument overreaches, if any, without offering references or a reading list.
