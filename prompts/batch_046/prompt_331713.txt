I'm preparing a graduate seminar on meta-normativity, and I'm trying to help my students see whether normative reasons can genuinely persist in the absence of any being for whom they are reasons, without letting the discussion collapse into well-worn slogans about moral realism versus anti-realism. To that end, I'd like you to compose a philosophical dialogue exploring "free-floating rationales"—reasons that remain fully normative even when there is no actual or potential reasoner to be guided by them.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them Althea and Ben—who debate this possibility in a concrete, slightly unconventional setting: imagine a distant epoch after the permanent extinction of all sentient life, in which certain structural asymmetries of value continue to obtain—specifically, the fact that suffering is intrinsically worse than non-suffering, arranged in a persistent cosmic pattern that no mind will ever register. Althea should argue that these asymmetries still generate genuine reasons—reasons against the instantiation of suffering, for example—even though there is literally no one to heed them; she should maintain that reasonhood is a non-relational property of states of affairs rather than a relation to agents. Ben should contend that without a subject whose practical reasoning could be guided, talk of "reasons" is a category mistake comparable to speaking of digestion in a universe without organisms; he should insist that normative language necessarily presupposes a reasoner and that Althea is merely describing causal or axiological structure in misleadingly agential terms. The exchange must feature real back-and-forth: Althea should directly address Ben's category-error charge, and Ben should directly answer Althea's claim that normative properties can be fully non-relational, rather than either speaking past the other. Neither interlocutor should be a strawman.

Please organize your response as follows:

The Dialogue: approximately 700 to 900 words, maintaining a formal, precise register appropriate to analytic philosophy.

Analytical Commentary: approximately 200 to 300 words, briefly identifying the strongest argumentative move made by each interlocutor and indicating precisely where the disagreement hinges—whether on the ontology of normative properties, the conditions for legitimate reason-ascription, or the aptness of Ben's biological analogy.
