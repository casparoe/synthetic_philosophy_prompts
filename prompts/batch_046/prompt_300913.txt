I have sketched a short argument about what I call “metric incommensurability,” a narrower form of Kuhnian incommensurability that applies not to whole conceptual schemes but to the internal calibration of evaluative standards within a shared domain. The core claim is that two research programs can share all their descriptive vocabulary and still fail to compare their explanatory successes, because the scale by which they rank explanations is itself paradigm-dependent. For instance, imagine two communities of physicists who both invoke “simplicity” but calibrate it differently: one treats it as syntactic economy relative to a fixed formalism, the other as ontological parsimony relative to a shifting ontology. They can translate each other’s sentences, yet when one asserts that a new theory is “simpler” and therefore preferable, the normative force of that assertion does not travel across the boundary. I want to suggest that this kind of incommensurability is both more common and more damaging to rational comparison than the global thesis, but also more tractable—potentially resolvable through meta-level negotiation about calibration rules, provided the communities recognize that they are disputing metrics rather than facts. Could you comment on three points? First, does this misrepresent Kuhn’s own account of incommensurability, particularly his emphasis on taxonomic change rather than on evaluative calibration? Second, is the distinction between metric and global incommensurability philosophically stable, or does any dispute over calibration eventually entail deeper conceptual divergence? Third, are there hidden assumptions in my suggestion that such disputes can be settled by negotiation rather than by empirical adjudication, especially if the calibration rules are themselves theory-laden?
