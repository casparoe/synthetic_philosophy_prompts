For a graduate seminar in metaphysics and epistemology, I want students to grasp a specific conceptual knot: why ontology identification becomes unstable when the information that distinguishes competing ontologies is structurally inaccessible rather than merely unknown. Please explain the tension between the claim that ontological commitment should track whatever best explains our accessible evidence, and the claim that if two ontologies generate identical observable structures but diverge only in modal or causal properties that lie beyond any possible epistemic access, then selecting one over the other seems to reduce ontological choice to convention rather than discovery. Do not simply rehearse the standard thesis of ontological relativity or the familiar underdetermination argument; instead, work through precisely how inaccessibility threatens to collapse the distinction between realism and pragmatic selection, and evaluate whether there is a coherent intermediate stance—let us call it epistemically modest realism—that permits us to assert that exactly one ontology is correct without maintaining that we possess, or could ever possess, the identifying information that would justify that assertion. Your explanation should be technically precise but accessible to students who understand basic modal logic and the distinction between epistemic and metaphysical possibility; avoid citing particular authors or texts, and do not include any bibliographic recommendations.
