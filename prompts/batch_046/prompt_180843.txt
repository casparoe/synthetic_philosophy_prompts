Here's a rough outline for an essay I'm playing with — does it hold water?

I want to argue that calling germs, climate shifts or volcanoes "historical actors" is useful but risky. Take the Antonine Plague: we know it killed maybe a quarter of Rome's population, but saying the virus "acted" in history smuggles in intentionality we don't actually mean. Same with the 536 volcanic winter — the aerosols didn't choose to cool Europe, yet chroniclers like Procopius and Cassiodorus (if I'm recalling right) treated the darkness as a kind of sign or punishment, which is it's own form of agency-ascription.

My middle path is "structural affordance" — these forces don't act, but they open or close possibilities for human action in asymmetrical, irreversible ways. So the plague didn't cause the Crisis of the Third Century, but it collapsed the tax base in a way that made military usurpation far more likely. The 536 event didn't cause Justinian's reconquest to stall, but it altered grain dynamics so holding Italy became strategically brittle.

The danger, I think, is environmental determinism dressed up in actor-network language. I'm trying to dodge both "everything is human politics" and "climate made them do it." Does that distinction actually do work, or am I just inventing jargon to feel better? Also, am I being unfair to recent ecological history — I feel like some historians do use "agency" in a purely causal, deflated sense, and I'm attacking a straw man.

Any thoughts on the argument structure, especially the affordance bit? And is there a glaring inaccuracy in how I've framed the 536 event?
