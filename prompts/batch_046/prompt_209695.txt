In my philosophy of science seminar, participants keep reaching for an analogy to defend standard Bayesianism against the old-evidence problem: they compare a long-known evidence statement E confirming a newly proposed theory T to finding a key in a drawer that was cast years ago but only now, with the manufacture of a matching lock, proves its shape was correct all along. The key (E) has not changed; the lock (T) is new; the fit is discovered rather than created. They use this to argue that there is no need for Jeffrey conditionalization, temporal indexing, or any revision to Bayes's rule—old evidence confirms just as new evidence does, because confirmation is a relational property that requires both relata to be present, not a temporal process that requires the agent to be ignorant of E.

Can you lay out the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the key, the lock, the drawer, the moment of manufacture, the discovery of the fit, and the supposed "activation" of confirmatory power—then identify precisely where the mapping breaks down? In particular, does the analogy quietly import a representational or causal story about theories (as artifacts deliberately designed to match data) that Bayesianism does not commit to? Does it conflate the agent's epistemic state (having long known E) with the logical relation between E and T? And once the disanalogies are separated into those that matter for the intended conclusion and those that do not, does the claim—that Glymour's problem dissolves without technical repair—survive, or was the analogy carrying the argument all along? If there is a better analogy for how old evidence relates to a new theory, suggest it.
