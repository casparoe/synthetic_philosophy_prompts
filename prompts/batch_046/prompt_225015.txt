I've recently been appointed to an ethics oversight committee for a regional autonomous infrastructure network, and after reviewing an incident in which the system's failure to issue an alert preceded a serious environmental breach, I find myself unable to settle whether that inaction genuinely caused the harm or whether attributing causation to an absence is a category mistake. Write a short story—set in a near-future regulatory agency—about an investigator tasked with writing the official causal analysis of just such an event. The protagonist should be grappling not merely with bureaucratic blame, but with the metaphysical question of whether omissions can enter into causal chains at all, and the narrative should feel personally urgent rather than detached, as if the investigator's own career and conscience hinge on getting the ontology right.

In the story, introduce a secondary character—perhaps a philosopher of science or a systems logician—who presses the investigator on the distinction between causation by omission and causation by commission, raising specific conceptual pressures: the asymmetry between doing and allowing, the difficulty of singling out one relevant absence from an infinite background of non-occurrences, and the possibility that treating silence as a causal factor collapses into a kind of negative-fact ontology that makes every non-event equally causally loaded. The narrative should avoid resolving these tensions with a tidy moral or legal verdict; instead, let the ambiguity deepen as the investigator realizes that assigning responsibility may depend on a prior, unsettled decision about what counts as a cause. I want the story to function as a genuine philosophical thought experiment rendered in narrative form—conceptually dense, personally unsettling, and resistant to the kind of neat instrumental-convergence fable that dominates so much fiction about autonomous systems.
