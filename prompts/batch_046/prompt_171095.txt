A blogger I follow summarized the view that reasons can float free of reasoners—what she called "free-floating rationales"—like this: she claimed that when a tree sends roots downward toward water, there is genuinely a reason for that growth, even though no mind is involved, and that this shows nature is saturated with unowned normative facts. She then argued that moral realists can apply the exact same structure to ethics, so that "causing unnecessary suffering is wrong" can be literally true with no human being required to recognize it. Finally, she said the main objection—that reasons must belong to someone—rests on a simple linguistic confusion between "having a reason" and "being able to articulate a reason," which means the critics have no real metaphysical challenge. I suspect this summary runs together different philosophical positions and overstates how smoothly the tree case transfers to morality, but I am not sure where the seams are. Which parts capture the view fairly, which parts conflate natural teleology with moral normativity, and does the dismissal of the ownership objection hold up?
