I'm studying Plato's Euthyphro for a course on ancient ethics, and I find myself returning repeatedly to the exchange in which Socrates presses Euthyphro on whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or whether it is pious because it is loved by them. As I understand the passage, Socrates distinguishes being carried from being led: the gods may love what is already pious, but that affection does not create the piety, since being acted upon is not the same as being defined by the action. Euthyphro then seems forced onto the second horn—that piety just is what all the gods love—which Socrates treats as reducing piety to an arbitrary preference rather than a stable normative property.

What confuses me is whether Socrates' framing silently assumes that every normative relation must run in one explanatory direction—either downward, where the property grounds the divine attitude, or upward, where the divine attitude constitutes the property. I have been wondering whether there is a coherent third shape available: one in which "piousness" and "being divinely loved" are not linked by causation or constitution at all, but are instead co-manifested under a broader descriptive pattern that lacks any single directional ground—something closer to a family-resemblance structure than to a definitional chain. Could you help me read the passage carefully on this point? Does Socrates genuinely exhaust the logical space of explanation between these two terms, or does his argument depend on suppressing a non-directional, descriptive-co-occurrence view? And if that third option survives, does the Euthyphro dilemma still have force against theories that deny normative properties must carry explanatory directions in the first place?
