I chair my university’s interdisciplinary research review board, which oversees proposals that combine evolutionary biology with human-subjects research. A team has submitted a study titled “Between-Group Selection of Cooperative Norms in Small-Scale Agricultural Communities.” The protocol measures reproductive success and cooperative behavior across ten villages, then uses hierarchical variance partitioning to attribute fitness differences primarily to between-village rather than within-village variation. The investigators argue that this design legitimately treats villages as units of selection and that participants should be informed the study examines “group-level evolutionary dynamics.”

Half the board accepts this framing: they say multilevel selection theory is well-established, and specifying the group level is methodologically transparent. The other half objects on two grounds. First, they claim that treating human villages as evolutionary units is a philosophical commitment disguised as empirical method—if selection is really operating on individuals or genes within those villages, then informing participants about “group-level dynamics” misrepresents the causal structure of the study and undermines informed consent. Second, they worry that the statistical model—partitioning variance into between-group and within-group components—presupposes rather than demonstrates that groups are causally decoupled enough to count as genuine units.

The investigators respond that they are not taking a metaphysical stance; they are testing whether the conditions for group-level selection are satisfied, and that their consent form can be revised to say “evolutionary dynamics at multiple levels” rather than specifying one unit.

We need to reach a decision—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—before next Thursday’s meeting. Please draft the board’s written decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a specific time frame for any required revisions, engage seriously with the strongest objection (whether about informed consent or about methodological presupposition), and address whether the board should require the investigators to adopt a particular philosophical position on the unit of selection in their protocol. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the memo should stand on its reasoning alone.
