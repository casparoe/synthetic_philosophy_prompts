You serve as an appeals referee for a mid-sized city's zoning board, issuing binding recommendations on objections to upzoning proposals. You hear cases weekly, have thirty minutes per case, no staff support, and must sign each decision personally. Write a reusable decision procedure that operationalizes the following synthesized principle: zoning objections are legitimate only when they demonstrate that a proposal reduces existing residents' real spatial capabilities—access to mobility, livelihood, assembly, and shelter—rather than protecting positional goods such as exclusivity, aesthetic control, or value capture. To apply this, distinguish "capacity-reducing" objections (admissible) from "extractive preservation" objections (inadmissible), using the "capacity asymmetry" test: the objector must show that the change specifically diminishes their freedom to access an urban good they currently enjoy, not merely that it alters neighborhood composition.

Present the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions, with criteria for sufficient answers, explicit stop points that yield a recommendation, and clear markers where judgment cannot be eliminated. Apply it to three proposals: (1) upzoning near transit for affordable housing, objected to on school crowding and parking loss; (2) allowing fourplexes in a single-family zone, objected to on "neighborhood character" and property values; (3) a luxury high-rise near a public market that expands housing supply but eliminates informal vendor space used by current residents—where the procedure should yield a recommendation you personally find unwelcome. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot decide and where it will predictably misfire.
