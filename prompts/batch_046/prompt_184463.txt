People often contrast the Cartesian theater with Dennett's multiple drafts model by likening the first to a single-screen cinema — one projector, one audience, one continuous 'now' — and the second to a newspaper editorial floor, where many versions of a story circulate simultaneously with no master copy and no central reader. A colleague is pressing this newsroom analogy hard: she claims it proves there is no unified present moment in consciousness, only competing publication deadlines, and that subjective unity is merely the retrospective illusion that one edition was authoritative. Please audit the analogy systematically. First, lay out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the projector, the film, the audience, the screen, the reporters, the editions, and the newsstands? Second, pinpoint where the mapping breaks — particularly regarding the absence of an audience and the treatment of temporal binding as distribution rather than experience. Third, ask what values or features the newsroom picture quietly imports (objectivity, public verifiability, retrospective arbitration) that consciousness may lack. Fourth, determine whether the conclusion — no present moment, no authoritative self — survives once those disanalogies are exposed, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along. Finally, evaluate whether a modified analogy works better: treating the drafts not as news stories but as competing contractual commitments that bind future behavior, with coherence enforced retrospectively rather than by an editor. Does that revision repair the model, or does it smuggle a hidden theater back in through the mechanism of retrospective arbitration?
