As a novelist drafting a long-form narrative about technological extensions of consciousness, I have settled on a cluster of philosophical commitments I want my characters to embody consistently. I need an audit of whether they actually cohere. Here is my profile: (a) I am a reductionist about personal identity—there is no soul or bare particular, only psychological continuity with an appropriate causal connection. (b) I think gradual replacement of biological neurons with synthetic equivalents preserves identity, provided the transition is continuous and the functional architecture is maintained without interruption. (c) I think the causal connection that matters must remain physically continuous at the level of information-bearing structures; a discontinuous scan-and-destruct upload severs this chain, which is why instantaneous uploading is death, not survival. (d) I think there is no further fact of “being me” beyond this continuity—if two branches both continue my psychology, neither is uniquely me. (e) I think substrate independence is real: if, during gradual replacement, the synthetic components shift to operating via a non-classical, distributed coherence protocol that abandons neuronal topology entirely but preserves every memory, trait, and narrative integration without gap, I still survive. (f) I accept evolutionary debunking arguments that our intuitive sense of a unitary self is an adaptive fiction, but I do not think this undermines psychological continuity as a criterion of identity.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. In particular, I worry that (c) and (e) pull in opposite directions—if physical continuity of information-bearing structures is essential, how can I survive the total abandonment of neuronal topology? I also wonder whether (f) undermines the normative weight I implicitly give continuity in (a) through (d). For each real tension, rank its severity and say what the cheapest revision would cost me narratively and philosophically. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most problems. Finally, apply your best reconstructed principle to a case I have not listed: suppose replacement proceeds gradually for a decade, but at the midpoint an external technician pauses the process for six months, leaving a hybrid brain in stasis with no conscious experience, then resumes with identical synthetic architecture—do I survive the resumption, and does your answer depend on whether the stasis involved preservation of structural continuity or merely potential for it?
