I've been working out for myself, without any formal study, what makes something a real kind of thing versus just a label people happen to use, and I'd like to see whether my judgments hang together. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

A substance synthesized in a lab with the atomic structure of gold: I say it is gold, full stop. A hundred-dollar bill printed with perfect fidelity but without treasury authorization: even if every machine and person accepts it, I say it is not really money, only a fake that functions like money. A racial classification invented by a government census office in the 1920s, with no prior cultural existence, that people eventually adopted as a deep part of identity: I say it is now a real race, despite its purely bureaucratic origin. A person assigned male at birth who lives consistently for decades in a female social role, recognized by everyone around them, with medical alterations to their body: I say they are a woman, and the birth assignment was the error. But I keep going back and forth on Bitcoin: it has no physical form, its value exists only because people agree to treat it as valuable, yet its issuance rules are fixed by mathematics and it behaves like gold in crises. Is it real money or a collective fiction?

Two pairs feel especially hard to square. First, shell currency on an isolated island: originally scarce, but now shells are common everywhere; the islanders keep using them openly, by agreement, with everyone knowing the scarcity is gone. I think this is still real money, just backed by agreement rather than nature. Yet the counterfeit bill is also backed in practice by universal acceptance—everyone treats it as money—yet I call it fake. Second, two people of the same sex who make lifelong mutual commitments in a private ceremony, with no state document: I think they are genuinely married, even if the law doesn't recognize it. But a group of close friends who sign a written "family contract" declaring they are now siblings: I think they are only playing with language; they haven't actually made a family.

So is there one principle—perhaps about whether the kind can survive open awareness of its origins, or whether deception is essential, or whether there is an irreversible social commitment—that captures all of these? Or must I choose between a simpler principle that saves most verdicts and a more complex one that saves them all? Please state the principle precisely, show which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed—specifically, a digital image certified as unique by an encrypted ledger, where anyone can copy the image exactly but only the certificate-holder is treated as owning the "original"—and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
