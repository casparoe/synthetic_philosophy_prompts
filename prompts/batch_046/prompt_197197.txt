I've come to accept the strong incoherence thesis about qualia—roughly, the view developed in the quining tradition, particularly in the form articulated by Chase and Sanborn—that qualia are not merely elusive or redundant posits, but conceptually contradictory. On this view, the defining marks of a quale are (i) intrinsic, non-relational phenomenal character; (ii) first-person epistemic privilege that is in principle incommunicable; and (iii) causal efficacy in physical processes such as behaviour and neural feedback. The argument is that (ii) and (iii) are jointly unsatisfiable: if a state is genuinely incommunicable, it cannot enter into the causal nexus required for (iii), and any relaxation of (ii) to allow causal interaction dissolves the intrinsic character required by (i). I therefore treat qualia as analogous to a round square—not something we fail to detect, but a combination of conditions that cannot be co-instantiated.

Tell me what would genuinely count against this position—not merely the standard response that we just know we have them, but which specific intuitions, empirical results, or conceptual distinctions would force a retreat. In particular:

- Which of the three conditions—intrinsicality, incommunicability, causal efficacy—would have to be abandoned or weakened, and which combinations of weakened conditions remain stable?
- Are there conceivable cases (perhaps involving split-brain subjects, artificial systems with opaque architectures, or anomalous causal loops) that separate incommunicability from causal inertness?
- Would a result from cognitive science or philosophy of mind—say, a rigorous formalisation of phenomenal concepts or a demonstration that first-person reports are causally mediated in a way that preserves privacy—undermine the contradiction, or would defenders of the incoherence thesis absorb it by redefining the conditions?
- Which rival theories (eliminativism about phenomenal concepts, relationalism, representationalism) would survive if the incoherence thesis collapsed, and does the thesis risk collapsing into one of them?
- Finally, rank the potential defeaters from fatal (the position is internally inconsistent or empirically refuted) to merely awkward (it must concede that some common descriptions of experience are misleading but not false).

I'd like you to distinguish what defenders of this specific incoherence claim would themselves concede as damaging from what critics typically allege but defenders dismiss.
