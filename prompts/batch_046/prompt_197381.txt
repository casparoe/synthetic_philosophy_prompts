I have been drafting an essay for a seminar on literary translation and aesthetic obligation, and I would be grateful for your comments on its coherence, its fidelity to the theoretical positions it invokes, and whether its central distinction actually does the work I ask of it. The essay takes up the problem of translating formally constrained poetry—specifically the rondeaux of Clément Marot—through a framework that tries to steer between the rigid formalism often associated with Douglas Hofstadter’s Le Ton beau de Marot and the pragmatic dismissiveness one sometimes encounters in contemporary functionalist translation theory. Rather than arguing that the translator must literally reproduce the rondeau’s ABaAabAB rhyme scheme and metrical pattern in English, which frequently produces stilted or semantically distorted results, or that such formal architecture is merely ornamental and therefore dispensable, I defend a middle position centered on what I call structural nostalgia. By this I mean the particular cognitive friction or pattern-recognition pleasure that the original form generated for its contemporary audience—an effect that can sometimes be preserved even when the specific rhyme scheme is not. For Marot’s rondeaux, I suggest, this might involve substituting a different but analogously restrictive form in English, such as a tightly constructed villanelle or a modified sestina, provided the substitution is justified by a shared affective logic—circular return, compressed variation, the sense of wit under pressure—rather than by mere translator convenience. I then anticipate an objection: that this appeal to structural nostalgia is too subjective to guide practice, since we lack direct access to sixteenth-century French readers’ psychological states. In response, I propose that we can infer the relevant affective profile from the historical reception and social function of the form—its association with courtly play, mnemonic challenge, and rhetorical display—without claiming to reproduce any individual mental state. The essay concludes by testing this framework against a hypothetical case: an English rendering of one of Marot’s rondeaux treating the conventional theme of a young wife and an aged husband, rendered in free verse yet retaining the poem’s recursive rhetorical structure and its ironic juxtaposition of youth and age. I claim that this rendering fails by my own lights not because it lacks rhyme, but because it removes the very constraint that makes the irony feel like a performed witticism rather than a plain statement; yet I worry that my example may merely smuggle in an unstated preference for rhyme rather than genuinely supporting the thesis. I also fear that my distinction between literal formal replication and structural nostalgia collapses upon inspection, since both seem to require the translator to make normative judgments about which formal features are essential. Would you please comment on whether the argument is internally consistent, whether the notion of structural nostalgia is sufficiently distinct from existing concepts in translation theory—such as Christiane Nord’s functional loyalty or Lawrence Venuti’s foreignization—to merit further development, and whether the hypothetical example is fair?
