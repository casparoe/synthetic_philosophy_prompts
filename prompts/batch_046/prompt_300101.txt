I'm working through some old puzzles in philosophy of language and mathematics with a study group, and we've noticed a recurring configuration that doesn't quite match the textbook examples we're familiar with. The pattern involves pairs of expressions that everyone agrees denote exactly the same thing—not hidden, not unknown, but fully transparent—yet substituting one for the other in reasoning or evaluation still seems to change the outcome in a non-trivial way. For instance, in mathematics, once one proves that the real numbers built via Dedekind cuts are identical to those built via Cauchy sequences, the two constructions are treated as one object; nevertheless, in actual proofs, mathematicians often resist replacing one construction with the other unless an explicit isomorphism is invoked, as if the path by which the object was reached retains some authority over what inferences are legitimate. A second instance comes from institutional discourse: if everyone knows that "the corporation" and "the board's decision to restructure" refer to the very same state of affairs, substituting the latter for the former in an assignment of moral responsibility still feels like a shift in subject matter rather than a mere redescription. In both cases, the identity is not a discovery that resolves a puzzle but a settled fact that fails to eliminate the practical or inferential difference between the two expressions.

We have tried to frame this using the standard distinction between how an expression presents its object and what object it presents, but that framework seems designed for cases where the identity is initially unknown—like the morning star and the evening star—whereas here everyone knows the identity from the start, and the divergence persists anyway. So I am asking whether this configuration—transparent co-reference combined with persistent substitutability failure in reasoning or normative assessment—has an established name in the literature, and if so, how it is distinguished from ordinary context-sensitivity, ambiguity, or the classic puzzle about informative identity statements. If there is no precise term, I would like a proposed definition that specifies its boundaries: in particular, whether it requires that the two expressions belong to different conceptual domains, or whether it can arise within a single domain, and what conditions make the substitutability failure rational rather than merely psychological. What exactly is this phenomenon, what is it not, and where do its edges lie?
