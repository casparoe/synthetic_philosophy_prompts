Having recently sat through my sister’s evaluation for epilepsy surgery involving corpus callosotomy, I find the familiar “two minds in one skull” framing less urgent than a more specific problem: how a single conscious system can maintain unified executive authorization when its hemispheric channels can issue contradictory commands that neither side can cross-verify. For a philosophy-of-mind graduate seminar—ten students, one seventy-five-minute session—design a structured simulation in which pairs jointly operate one “body” through separate left- and right-channel input streams, with a communication link that the facilitator can degrade or sever without warning. The payoff scheme should reward coherent, unified action rather than speed, so that participants feel the practical pressure of negotiating authorship when their channels conflict. Provide concise rules that require only paper and pens, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief mapping specific conflict moments onto the unity-of-consciousness debate, and an honest note about which feature—likely the first-person phenomenology of being the single author of action—the exercise cannot reproduce. Write in a formal, direct voice; do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
