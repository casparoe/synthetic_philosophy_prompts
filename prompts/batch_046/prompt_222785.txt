I'm working through Weber's methodological essays for a seminar on causal explanation in the human sciences, and I've become convinced that the popular version of the Protestant Ethic thesis—culture causes capitalism—misses the actual argument. The position I want to stress-test is more specific: culture can enter historical explanation only through elective affinity, and only when an account satisfies both meaningful adequacy (actors orient their conduct by the value-complex) and causal adequacy (the outcome is not overdetermined by material conditions alone). On this view, explaining with values is not like explaining with forces; it is like explaining with a selection mechanism that makes certain structural possibilities actual by giving actors reasons to seize them. I want to add a further qualification that is rarely emphasized—call it the asymmetry thesis. Material conditions can exist without the corresponding value-complex and produce different outcomes, but the value-complex without the material conditions produces only fantasy or frustration, not historical change. So values filter and activate, but they do not generate ex nihilo.

What would have to be true for this qualified Weberian position to fail? I am not asking for generic criticisms of Weber's historiography; I want to know which conceptual commitments are genuinely exposed. Could there be a convincing demonstration that values sometimes operate as direct, unmediated causes—that a belief-system can generate sustained economic behavior even in the absence of the structural opportunities Weber thought necessary? Or is there a live possibility that the distinction between meaningful and causal adequacy collapses, so that demonstrating one automatically demonstrates the other? More concretely, suppose a study of a modern secular "hustle culture" in a particular tech sector showed that an ascetic work ethic produced rapid capital accumulation in conditions where no structural market incentive existed—purely through internalized discipline. Would that refute the asymmetry thesis, or could a Weberian absorb it by redefining the "material conditions" broadly enough to include organizational architecture? I want an honest map of where this view risks something real and where it has been insulated by definitional moves: what predictions does it make that rival accounts do not, and what observations would its defenders themselves concede as damaging rather than merely awkward?
