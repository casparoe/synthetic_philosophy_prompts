Read Toni Morrison’s Beloved through the philosophy of state apologies for historical wrongs, but with a specific focus on the problem of institutional performativity: can a collective entity that did not experience the harm sincerely apologize, or does sincerity require the kind of embodied, first-person suffering that Sethe and the ghost of Beloved represent? Examine the novel’s treatment of public versus private acknowledgment—Paul D’s attempt to build a shared future, the community’s shunning of Sethe after the killing, and Baby Suggs’s sermon in the Clearing—asking whether these scenes dramatize the failure of collective contrition or point toward a non-institutional form of witnessing that avoids the performative contradictions philosophers identify in state apologies. Take seriously both the philosophical distinctions (between regret and apology, between symbolic and material reparation, and between successor responsibility and direct guilt) and the novel’s narrative resistance to abstraction: does Beloved ultimately suggest that historical wrongs can only be addressed through individual, bodily acts of remembrance rather than through the formal speech acts of states, or does it leave room for a collective acknowledgment that does not claim to speak for the dead? Offer a reading that does not reduce the novel to an illustration of the debate, and identify precisely where the literary text complicates or exceeds the philosophical framework.
