I serve on the Student Policy and Ethics Council at my high school. Our administration has proposed a cafeteria and study-period "choice architecture" program: meal trays will default to excluding desserts unless a student actively requests them, and study-block enrollment will switch from opt-in to opt-out. The proposal frames these as libertarian-paternalist nudges that preserve freedom of choice while steering students toward healthier and more productive outcomes. A student petition, however, raises a specific philosophical objection: because the nudges are designed to be invisible, they do not merely influence the execution of pre-existing preferences but actively construct the preferences themselves. If our desires are endogenous to the architecture, then measuring welfare improvement against "pre-nudge" preferences is circular, and the standard defense—that nudges help us achieve our own true goals—collapses. The Council must issue a formal recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with binding conditions—to the principal before Friday's meeting. Please draft the memo I will circulate: it should state a concrete course of action with a clear time frame, treat the preference-endogeneity and circularity objection as the strongest argument against approval rather than a misunderstanding, and propose specific institutional safeguards—such as transparency requirements, periodic preference audits, or structured opt-out mechanisms—that could address the concern without rendering the program pointless. The reasoning should be rigorous enough that both the administration and the petitioners can respond to it directly.
