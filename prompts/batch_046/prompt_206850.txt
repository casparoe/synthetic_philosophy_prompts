Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 10–14 graduate students in philosophy, game theory, or AI ethics. The aim is to let them experience why source-code transparency can stabilize cooperation without repetition or punishment—while also feeling exactly where it breaks when “source code” is ambiguous natural language rather than formal syntax.

Structure and rules. Phase 1 (15 min): each student writes one “commitment protocol” on an index card in constrained plain English—an if-then rule that specifies an observable feature of another student’s card and prescribes either Cooperate or Defect. The condition must depend on the content of the other card, not on reputation or past rounds. Phase 2 (25 min): random pairing. Partners exchange cards, silently apply their own rule to the other card to determine their action, record it privately, then reveal simultaneously. Payoffs per round: mutual Cooperate = 3 points each; mutual Defect = 1 each; unilateral Defect = 5 for the defector, 0 for the cooperator. Points accumulate for ranking only; there is no external reward. Phase 3 (20 min): one revision round—students may rewrite their card, then pair with a new partner and execute again. Phase 4 (30 min): debrief.

What should happen before it is named. Pairs whose rules are structurally symmetric—e.g., each promises to cooperate exactly when the other’s card contains an equivalent promise—should lock into mutual cooperation without trust-building. Pairs with vague wording, hidden asymmetry, or conditions that reference interpretation rather than text should fall into exploitation or mutual defection, often despite both participants intending cooperation. Only in the debrief should you name program equilibrium, map the writing phase to program selection, the exchange to source-code transparency, and the execution to simultaneous running. Identify which pairs achieved FairBot-style outcomes and which failed due to “interpretation errors” rather than malice.

Debrief requirements. Map each activity moment onto the corresponding part of the theory. Predict the failure modes you will actually see: intentional loophole crafting, false syntactic symmetry (identical intent, different wording), and conditions so vague that each partner applies them differently. Say how to handle each (keep example cards ready, allow one “clarification” round after the first execution, do not resolve disputes for them). Then be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: formal verification, self-referential loops, the computational cost of inspecting source, and the critical distinction between syntactic and semantic equivalence in real code. The niche point to inject is that natural-language transparency can increase conflict precisely because it supplies too many interpretation dimensions—something formal program equilibrium avoids by collapsing semantics into syntax, but which any human commitment device must confront.
