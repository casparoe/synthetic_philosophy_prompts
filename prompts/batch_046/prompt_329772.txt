Consider the position sometimes called lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: suffering and pleasure are not commensurable on any cardinal scale of value, yet suffering occupies a lexical tier that takes unconditional precedence over pleasure, regardless of magnitude. A critic advances the following argument that the view undermines itself. (1) If two values are genuinely incommensurable—lacking any shared metric—then assigning one lexical priority over the other is not a rational ordering but a category mistake, since hierarchical ranking presupposes at least minimal comparability. (2) Defenders of the lexical view typically ground it in the phenomenological incommensurability of pain and pleasure: they claim there is no experiential currency that can translate a unit of pleasure into a unit of suffering relieved or avoided. (3) Therefore the lexical ordering is itself grounded in incommensurability. (4) From (1) and (3) it follows that the ordering is incoherent: it applies a strict decision-rule to values that, by the view’s own lights, cannot be situated within the same evaluative hierarchy. (5) To escape incoherence, the defender must either concede that pleasure and pain are commensurable after all—thereby collapsing the view into standard weighted negative utilitarianism—or deny that lexical priority requires comparability, in which case the “threshold” becomes an unmotivated stipulation with no normative force. (6) Hence lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism either collapses into a different theory or forfeits its justification. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the step you would reject, explain why it appeared plausible, and state what abandoning it costs. In particular, is there a coherent way to maintain that suffering and pleasure are phenomenologically incommensurable while still defending a non-arbitrary lexical priority? If you accept the argument as valid, say which horn of the dilemma in (5) is less damaging and why.
