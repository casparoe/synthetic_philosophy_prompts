I have recently been asked to advise a local interfaith council on whether religious citizens may invoke theological premises in public debates over school curriculum without translating them into secular terms. To think through the problem, I want to examine John F. Kennedy’s 1960 campaign address to Protestant clergy in Houston as a case study in Rawlsian public reason—specifically through the inclusive versus exclusive interpretations of the proviso, and the asymmetry that Kennedy was pressed to disavow Catholic ecclesiastical influence while Protestant interlocutors, including the subsequent public objection by Norman Vincent Peale that no Catholic should hold the presidency, faced no parallel demand to secularize their own commitments.

Analyze the episode through this framework: identify the relevant public, map the actual options and objections, and determine whether Kennedy’s response satisfies an accessibility requirement, a translation proviso, or neither. Then ask what the case reveals about the theory—does the asymmetry in who must translate show that public reason is genuinely neutral among comprehensive doctrines, or does it expose a hidden secular burden? Where does the appeal to the burdens of judgment explain or fail to explain why only one side was required to bracket its theological commitments? Finally, say whether the episode confirms the inclusive view, counts as an anomaly for the exclusive view, or demonstrates that the framework was designed for symmetric pluralism rather than for cases of asymmetric religious power. Be disciplined about the mapping, and say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
