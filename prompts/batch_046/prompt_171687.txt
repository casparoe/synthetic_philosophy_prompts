I'm preparing material for an advanced philosophy-of-mind reading group, and we've become stuck on a particularly sharp version of the substrate-independence problem that keeps resurfacing in our discussions. In The Mind's I, Hofstadter and Dennett present a well-known thought experiment in which Einstein's brain is perfectly mapped—every neuronal connection and functional relation preserved as printed symbols—into an enormous book, so that an external reader could, in principle, trace the same causal patterns that once constituted Einstein's cognition. Some in our group take this to show that consciousness is essentially structural: if the relational architecture is intact, the substrate—whether neural tissue, silicon, or ink on paper—is irrelevant, and the book is, in some meaningful sense, Einstein thinking in extraordinarily slow motion. Others object that this mistakes representation for instantiation, arguing that consciousness is not a static pattern but an ongoing, self-modifying process requiring temporal dynamics, continuous feedback loops, and perhaps biological metabolism; on their view, the book is no more conscious than a blueprint is a building, because it entirely lacks the diachronic causal integration that binds mental states into a unified stream.

Please write a dialogue between two equally knowledgeable interlocutors—call them A and B—who defend these opposing positions. A should argue that functional isomorphism across substrates is sufficient for consciousness, and should specifically address the worry that a static medium cannot sustain temporal binding by proposing that the temporal structure can be reconstructed through the reader's sequential traversal, or through some equivalent dynamic interpretation of the printed relations. B should resist by distinguishing sharply between representing a causal process and actually instantiating it, and should introduce the further claim—perhaps drawing on recent embodied-cognition literature—that mental states require not merely internal relational structure but continuous sensorimotor coupling with an environment, which a closed book entirely lacks. Crucially, neither should be a strawman: B should concede that the book might preserve something genuinely analogous to Einstein's cognitive architecture, and A should acknowledge that the absence of real-time interaction and metabolic maintenance creates a serious explanatory burden rather than dismissing it as mysticism. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth, with each speaker responding to the specific point just made rather than repeating an opening monologue.

To keep the exchange from collapsing into the standard functionalism-versus-biological-naturalism script, please inject one somewhat niche conceptual distinction—perhaps the difference between token-token identity of mental states across media versus type-type realisation, or the idea that consciousness requires rate-independent causal topology rather than mere pattern preservation—so that the argument moves into less familiar territory. The response should be written in British English, and please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific page numbers that a reader without direct access to the original texts could not verify.
