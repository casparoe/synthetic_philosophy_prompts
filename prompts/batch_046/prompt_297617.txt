I am writing a seminar paper for a graduate course on early modern rationalist ethics, and during our discussion of Leibniz's moral philosophy I found myself stuck on an apparent tension between his identification of justice with caritas sapientis—the charity of the wise, grounded in rational love of perfection in others—and the logic of an emerging judicial technology that has no real historical parallel: real-time biometric sentiment-analysis systems integrated directly into trial proceedings, which continuously update sentencing recommendations based on algorithmically parsed facial micro-expressions, vocal stress markers, and galvanic skin response, calibrated against aggregate recidivism statistics rather than against any discernible quality of the defendant's rational nature. My advisor suggested I avoid the generic framing of "What would Leibniz think about artificial intelligence?" and instead ask whether his ethical system can even accommodate a practice in which charity is replaced by statistical optimization and in which the judge's rational discernment is supplemented—or supplanted—by a feedback loop that treats remorse as a quantifiable input variable.

More specifically, I want to understand how Leibniz's corrective, perfection-oriented theory of punishment—which holds that penalties should primarily serve the moral improvement of the offender—would evaluate a penal architecture that uses live physiological monitoring to dynamically constrain the range of expressions available to a defendant during trial. Given his commitment to the principle of sufficient reason and his metaphysical view that moral qualities are ultimately rooted in the clarity and distinctness of a monad's perceptions, would he regard such biometric mediation as a legitimate extension of the wise judge's rational discernment, or as a category error that substitutes aggregate correlation for the direct perception of another's substantial form? And given his account of freedom—that free actions are those that flow from an agent's own substantial form rather than from external determination—how might he assess a practice that alters, in real time, the external conditions under which that substantial form could manifest? I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want a reconstruction of the implicit commitments of his ethics that yields a specific judgment, and I want you to identify precisely where his system is genuinely underdetermined—where two different Leibnizian principles pull in opposite directions—rather than forcing a single answer.
