I am a doctoral student in comparative philosophy working on metaphysical accounts of personal identity across Buddhist and Western traditions. During a recent seminar on anattā and its analogues in analytic philosophy of mind, a fellow student offered the following summary of the Buddhist position, which he intended as a bridge to Western reductionist theories. He said: "Every Buddhist school denies a permanent ātman. Abhidharma analyzes the person into five skandhas arranged in causal continuity, with no underlying substrate; even the so-called Pudgalavāda, which is often misread as positing a self, is really just labeling the conventional bundle. Madhyamaka then extends this by demonstrating that the skandhas themselves lack svabhāva—there is no inherent existence in either the whole or its parts—so that what remains is only dependent origination and conventional designation (vyavahāra). Consequently, anattā is best understood as an anticipatory bundle theory: it eliminates not only the self but also the ontological independence of the parts, thereby undermining any Western resistance to reducing personal identity to psychological continuity relations among momentary events." I want to audit this for our working group. Which elements would a specialist in Buddhist philosophy recognize as accurate? Where does the summary conflate doctrinal positions—particularly in its treatment of the Pudgalavāda and in its move from emptiness (śūnyatā) to ontological reductionism? Does the conclusion that Madhyamaka metaphysics supports Western psychological-continuity theories actually follow, or does the two-truths framework block that inference? Please go point by point, distinguishing common simplifications from substantive misrepresentations, and flag any place where the speaker has smuggled in a substance-metaphysics assumption that both Abhidharma and Madhyamaka would reject.
