You are a professional philosopher of mind and ethics with particular expertise in panpsychism, the combination problem, and the normative implications of extending sentience into fundamental physics. Before constructing the instrument, take a moment to brainstorm the logical space: map the independent axes on which positions in this debate genuinely diverge—ontological commitment to micro-experience, presence or absence of intrinsic valence, the coherence of aggregation or combination, the relative moral weight of macro versus micro welfare, and whether any physical parameter (such as information integration, entropy production, or quantum coherence) is treated as necessarily or only contingently correlated with suffering. Think carefully about which combinations are coherent, which are unexplored, and which are internally contradictory.

Now design a formal diagnostic taxonomy—a decision tree with labeled branch nodes and named or unnamed leaves—for a graduate seminar titled "Pan-sentience and suffering in fundamental physics: the ethical implications of panpsychism." The instrument should sort a respondent into an actual position based on answers to concrete cases and claims, not on which philosophical label they happen to prefer.

Root node (answerable without knowing interpretations): Do you take seriously the live possibility that fundamental physical entities—fields, particles, or spacetime regions—instantiate experience or proto-experiential properties?

From there, branch sequentially on:
1. Whether such micro-level experience, if present, carries intrinsic valence (pleasure or suffering) or is phenomenally neutral and structurally empty.
2. Whether there exists a coherent aggregation or combination mechanism by which micro-level valence can contribute to macro-level moral significance, or whether it remains isolated.
3. If aggregation is coherent, whether the resulting ubiquity of micro-suffering generates an overriding moral obligation—e.g., to minimize computation, energy use, or particle interaction—or remains subordinate to conventional macro-level welfare.

The tree must include at least these terminal positions: (a) Conventional macro-sentient ethics, in which panpsychism is rejected or treated as ethically irrelevant; (b) Valence-neutral panprotopsychism; (c) Isolated micro-valence, which accepts micro-suffering but denies aggregation; (d) Hierarchical panpsychist ethics, which accepts aggregation but holds that macro welfare dominates; and (e) Fundamental-scale utilitarianism or micro-asceticism, in which aggregation is accepted and generates decisive, overriding obligations. For any logical combination in the space that is unoccupied, label the leaf "unnamed" and diagnose in one sentence whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Every internal node must be phrased as a concrete claim or decision scenario that positions genuinely come apart on, answerable by someone who does not already know the taxonomy's labels. For each leaf, provide the position name (or "unnamed") followed by exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—the conclusion it must swallow that its rivals avoid.

In addition, inject one under-discussed distinction into the tree—such as whether micro-valence correlates necessarily with information-integration measures or only contingently with thermodynamic gradients—so that the taxonomy stays in the weeds without requiring lookup of obscure sources. Do not hinge any branch on ultra-niche theories unknown to standard philosophical training, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

Finally, flag explicitly at least one pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining where their commitments overlap. Keep the tree compact (no more than six terminal nodes) and present it in clear hierarchical form, followed by a brief meta-note on its diagnostic limitations and any unoccupied regions of the logical space.
