So I'm in this philosophy of time seminar and we just hit Gödel's 1949 essay on rotating universes — the one where he finds a solution to Einstein's equations with closed timelike curves and no global time coordinate. I tried to turn his worry into a formal argument about why the 'lapse' of time might be illusory, but now I think it proves way too much. Here it is: (1) Gödel demonstrated that GR allows a rotating universe in which observers can loop back to their own past and in which no universal time ordering exists. (2) If a physical theory allows solution-models both with and without some property P, then that theory gives us no epistemic reason to think P holds in the actual world. (3) GR allows Gödel-type rotating universes (no global time) and also standard FLRW cosmologies that do possess a cosmic time parameter. (4) Therefore GR gives us no reason to believe in an objective lapse of time. (5) Gödel took this to mean that temporal passage is subjective or illusory. (6) But apply (2) consistently: GR also permits spatially flat, positively curved, and negatively curved universes, with and without a cosmological constant, etc. So by (2), GR can never justify belief in flatness, curvature, or even cosmic expansion, since each feature is missing in some permitted model. (7) That'd make physics basically useless for knowing which world we live in, which is absurd. (8) So (2) must be wrong. (9) But then Gödel's specific move — using the rotating-universe model to deny objective time — loses its force.

I feel like I want to keep Gödel's intuition that there's something fishy about treating time as just another dimension when the equations let it collapse, but I dont want to end up a skeptic about black holes and expansion. Which step do I actually reject? Is (2) the problem, or is there a hidden slide between (3) and (4) about what counts as 'giving a reason'? And if I ditch (2), do I have to pay by admitting that physics can justify belief in time after all, or is there a cheaper way to save Gödel?
