I want to understand whether a specific bureaucratic practice is philosophically coherent or merely pragmatically inconsistent, and I need help reconstructing its implicit commitments without simply labeling it hypocritical.

Consider the review process used by several municipal development boards for large-scale zoning proposals. Each project is scored on a one-to-ten scale across three dimensions—heritage preservation, ecological resilience, and affordable housing contribution—and approval is granted whenever the arithmetic mean of these scores reaches or exceeds seven. At the same time, the boards' governing charters contain an explicit non-compensatory provision declaring that heritage value is not fungible with economic or environmental gains, a principle board members routinely invoke when they reject high-scoring proposals that would destroy protected structures. Nevertheless, in ordinary cases they apply the linear mean without exception and have never articulated why arithmetic aggregation is appropriate for goods they officially treat as lacking a common measure.

What view of value, comparison, and practical reason does this institutional design presuppose? Must it assume there is an underlying cardinal metric—perhaps aggregate welfare or preference satisfaction—that renders the arithmetic mean legitimate despite the rhetoric of incommensurability? Or can the practice be reconstructed in a way compatible with the idea that rational comparison across these domains does not require quantification, with the rubric functioning only as a defeasible heuristic? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, assess whether its procedural parts are mutually consistent, and describe what would have to change in the board's decision rule if the underlying philosophical view were replaced by one that denies any rational basis for comparing heritage, ecology, and housing altogether.
