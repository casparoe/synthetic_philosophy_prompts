Construct a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies philosophical positions on whether deductive inference stands in need of justification, using Carroll's tortoise-and-Achilles regress as the central diagnostic framework. Begin with a root commitment that a respondent accepts the validity of modus ponens yet asks whether that entitlement requires further grounding. From there, build exactly four binary branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete question about the Carroll scenario that can be answered without knowing any philosophical labels: (1) Must a logical rule be accepted as an explicit premise in order to license an inference? (2) Is an infinite hierarchy of meta-rules that justify lower-level rules tolerable? (3) Can ground-level justification be circular—i.e., can the rules that justify deduction include deduction itself—without viciousness? (4) Is the demand for justification misapplied, because basic inference rules are constitutive conditions of rational thought rather than substantive claims that require defense?

Map the resulting combinations onto named or precisely characterizable leaves—such as strong logicism, rule-circular coherentism, logical infinitism, pragmatic or conventionalist deflation, constitutive rationalism, and primitive-entitlement views—while noting any combinations that remain unoccupied. For each empty quadrant, briefly diagnose whether it is likely incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by answers to these four questions, flag them explicitly rather than inventing a spurious distinction. Restrict the tree to no more than six leaves, phrase every node so that a respondent answers based on intuition about the regress rather than by selecting a familiar label, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific scholars; the taxonomy must be derivable from the logical structure of the regress problem itself.
