Consider the philosophical debate over historical explanation applied to the rise of National Socialism: would a Nazi Germany—defined not merely as an authoritarian state but as the specific racial-bureaucratic apparatus with its Führerprinzip, its particular racial-state administration, and its expansionist Lebensraum ideology—have emerged without Adolf Hitler?

Exam question: Ask students to analyze this dispute by clearly distinguishing (i) counterfactual dependence of the actual regime on Hitler's presence, from (ii) the structural robustness of severe authoritarian rupture in interwar Germany, and (iii) whether the disagreement is empirical or partly conceptual—specifically, whether the two camps are measuring individual causal weight against different baseline counterfactuals (the exact regime versus any comparable fascist or authoritarian outcome). The answer should address whether "structural explanation" requires that the specific institutional form was inevitable, or only that some severe rupture was highly probable, and it should avoid treating structural pressure as deterministic.

Student answer:

Structuralist explanations treat Hitler as largely replaceable. Because the crisis of Weimar democracy, the Versailles settlement, and the economic collapse created conditions of extreme institutional vulnerability, any leader of a radical nationalist movement could have produced a comparable authoritarian rupture. This means structuralists regard individual agency as largely epiphenomenal to deeper forces; the specific institutional form—the Führerprinzip and the racial-state apparatus—was the inevitable expression of those conditions, not genuinely contingent on one person. Therefore, Hitler was not a necessary condition for the regime that actually emerged; he was merely the contingent vehicle through which inevitable structures expressed themselves.

By contrast, Great Man theorists insist on strict counterfactual dependence. Without Hitler's particular oratorical skill, strategic ruthlessness, and willingness to escalate racial policy, the NSDAP would not have captured the state in 1933, and certainly not in its actual form. They concede that some authoritarian outcome—perhaps a conservative presidential dictatorship—was structurally probable, so they do not deny structural pressure entirely. Rather, they deny that the specific Nazi state was overdetermined.

The dispute is therefore largely empirical. Once we distinguish the two baselines—"this exact regime" versus "any severe rupture"—both sides agree that Hitler was necessary for the actual path but not for every authoritarian path. The remaining question is whether a replacement leader could have consolidated comparable power by 1934 and implemented a racial-bureaucratic state of similar scope. Given that the party's organizational architecture was built around a charismatic Führer figure, the Great Man view gains direct support: the structural need for a leader-figure does not show that Hitler was replaceable, but rather that only a figure with his specific authority could have filled that structural niche. Hence, structuralists must concede that the particular institutional form was not fully replaceable after all.
