I'm writing on behalf of the University's Committee on Digital Pedagogy and Institutional Autonomy. The administration proposes mandatory adoption across all first-year courses of a proprietary system—designated here as CLOE—that continuously restructures course material in response to real-time biometric feedback (eye-tracking, keystroke dynamics, and galvanic skin response) collected from student devices, routing each learner through algorithmically generated "optimal pathways." The vendor asserts pedagogical neutrality: the system merely optimizes delivery without altering curricular content.

Our committee must issue a written opinion recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, suitable for circulation before Thursday's vote. The opinion must include a concrete recommendation with a clear time frame—whether a conditional pilot with mandatory audit milestones, a moratorium pending independent evaluation, or full adoption with safeguards—and it must treat seriously the administration's argument that personalized optimization at scale serves equity, retention, and resource efficiency.

The philosophical issue embedded in this case concerns technological determinism and whether artifacts have politics independent of user intent. CLOE incorporates a "predictive normalization" module: it compares each student's biometric profile against a training cohort drawn largely from high-stakes manufacturing-certification programs, flagging statistical deviations as "cognitive risk indicators." The concern is structural rather than merely privacy-related—namely, whether the system deterministically enforces a particular normative model of attention, temporal discipline, and compliance that instructors cannot override by redesigning course content. This raises the question of whether CLOE conditions pedagogical possibilities (soft determinism) or reshapes the educational relationship regardless of instructor intent (hard determinism), and whether algorithmic form and curricular content are genuinely separable when the delivery mechanism itself classifies neurodivergent learning patterns as dysfunctions to be corrected.

Please draft the memo with the following elements: (1) a precise recommendation and an associated time frame; (2) reasoning that applies the hard versus soft determinism distinction to CLOE's architecture, specifically addressing whether predictive normalization embodies values that persist independent of the content it delivers; (3) an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side—likely either the administration's equity-and-scalability claim or the counter-claim that all pedagogical tools embed norms and CLOE differs only in degree; (4) a clear statement of the general principle the committee would be adopting, so that future technology proposals can be assessed consistently.

Finally, please conclude by stating how confident you are in the recommendation you advance, and identify the specific point about which you are least certain—whether that concerns the empirical likelihood that the manufacturing-cohort training data will systematically misclassify neurodivergent students, the philosophical claim that algorithmic form and curricular content are inseparable in cases of predictive normalization, or another element of the argument.

Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for circulation to an institutional body. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
