At a recent workshop on moral psychology, two colleagues—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Webb—disagreed about whether a hiring committee’s preference for collegial harmony over disruptive competence could be separated from an implicit demographic bias. Vasquez argued that framing the preference as a legitimate taste for cooperative deliberation was merely rationalization: any preference that correlates strongly with homogeneity and resists counter-evidence is structurally a bias, regardless of subjective justification. Webb replied that correlation is not constitution, and that cooperative deliberation is a genuinely valuable institutional good; dismissing every preference that happens to align with homogeneity collapses the distinction into a genetic fallacy about origins rather than content. Vasquez countered that Webb’s defense relies on an unexamined moral-ecology thesis—the claim that certain preferences are justified because they preserve the conditions of shared reasoning—but that this thesis is circular, since the ecology itself was formed under the very biases in question. Webb then proposed a diagnostic of indexical reversal: ask whether committee members would maintain the preference if they themselves occupied the marginalized position. Vasquez objected that reversal tests only egoistic bias, not the kind of constitutive attachment to one’s own community that can be both partial and morally defensible. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate each move in turn—Vasquez’s structural-bias claim, Webb’s correlation-constitution distinction, Vasquez’s circularity charge, Webb’s reversal test, and Vasquez’s final distinction between egoistic and constitutive partiality. Say who is right about what, note what both miss, and indicate who is more right overall about distinguishing preferences from biases in this domain.
