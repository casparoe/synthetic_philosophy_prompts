In debates over whether DNA is literally a code, I keep seeing the same speaker do two things that sit uneasily together. First, they insist that "genetic code" is only a heuristic metaphor—chemistry with no author, no semantics, no message—especially when blocking inferences to design or vitalism. Second, in the same discussion they speak of mutations corrupting information, of CRISPR rewriting instructions, and of selection optimizing the message, treating the sequence as if it carries normative content that can fail or succeed at representing functional states. The switch tracks rhetorical need rather than context: the metaphor is deflated precisely when a literal reading would support an unwanted metaphysical conclusion, and inflated precisely when literal reading makes biotechnology or evolutionary explanation more intuitive. Is there an established name for this selective literalization of a scientific metaphor—this pattern where a term is defended as purely decorative in one argumentative context and genuinely descriptive in another, with the boundary determined not by consistency but by which interpretation serves the immediate point? How does it differ from ordinary polysemy, from strategic ambiguity, or from a simple failure to notice one's own equivocation? If no specific term exists, what conditions would a precise definition need to include in order to distinguish it from merely talking past one's interlocutor?
