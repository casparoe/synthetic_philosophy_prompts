I am preparing a workshop presentation defending the view that the familiar divergence between gradual-replacement and abrupt-replacement intuitions in personal-identity and object-persistence cases—such as the Ship of Theseus, brain-transplant narratives, and teletransportation scenarios—does not track genuine metaphysical conditions on continuity, but instead reflects a cognitive mechanism I call "narrative anchoring decay". On this account, the initially described state serves as a working-memory anchor whose salience decays at different rates depending on the temporal framing of the replacement story, not on any ontological property of the entity replaced; philosophers have therefore mistaken dynamics of representation for data about persistence.

The audience is a joint philosophy-psychology workshop. Among them are two developmental psychologists who study object-file tracking in infants and are sympathetic to cognitive explanations of philosophical intuitions; three analytic metaphysicians—two committed animalists and one four-dimensionalist—who maintain that psychological explanations miss the normative and ontological force of persistence questions; and two philosophers of language who worry that explaining away an intuition collapses into a genetic fallacy and undermines philosophical methodology. Several graduate students in philosophy of mind will also attend; they know the standard literature on intuition pumps but are uncommitted on personal identity.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually receive, listed in roughly the order they will come, and phrased as each subgroup would phrase them. For each objection, specify what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the objection I am most hoping nobody asks, together with a concise reply I can deliver under time pressure. Pay particular attention to how the metaphysicians will challenge my inference from representational dynamics to the absence of a metaphysical fact of the matter, and to how the psychologists will ask whether "narrative anchoring decay" is empirically distinguishable from simpler order-effects or familiarity biases. The response should be in British English.
