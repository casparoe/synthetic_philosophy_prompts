As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun attending a philosophy of action seminar, I observed a disagreement between two fellow participants about a modified Frankfurt case, and I ask you to adjudicate it.

They discussed Dr. Elena Voss, a physician who decides to administer an experimental drug. Unbeknownst to her, a neural implant—wired deterministically into her pre-conscious pathways—will redirect her motor commands if her neural signals indicate she will refrain. In the actual sequence, her conscious deliberation reaches the decision to administer the drug, and the implant remains entirely inactive. Marcus argued that because the mechanism operates deterministically at the pre-conscious level, Voss never genuinely had an open alternative: even her conscious reasoning was causally constrained by that deterministic architecture, so the case revives the principle of alternative possibilities rather than undermining it. Elena replied that Frankfurt’s central insight applies precisely here—since the backup never actually intervenes, its deterministic nature is causally irrelevant, and Voss’s own reasons remain the sufficient cause of her action. Marcus then distinguished Frankfurt’s classic external blocker from this internal, deterministically integrated one, claiming that internal wiring collapses the distinction between actual and counterfactual causation. Elena answered that labeling the mechanism internal is merely a physical description; responsibility tracks the actual causal history of reasons, not the metaphysical possibility of deviation.

Please assess each of these four moves in turn—Marcus’s claim about pre-conscious determinism, Elena’s defense of actual-cause logic, Marcus’s internal/external blocker distinction, and Elena’s appeal to causal history—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Then say who has the stronger overall position, and identify one significant issue about responsibility and determinism that both participants neglect.
