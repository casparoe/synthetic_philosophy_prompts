I'm leading a twenty-minute discussion section in my upper-division moral psychology course on the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and my peers mostly arrive with the reductive slogan that character traits do not exist. I want to build the explanatory arc around Doris's distinction between global and local traits rather than around knock-down experiments. I plan to use the Good Samaritan study as the carrying case, but not to prove that people are callous—rather to show how situational urgency can suppress the expression of a trait that may still be locally present. Before I open with that study, what conceptual dependency must be secured first: should I establish what virtue ethics requires for cross-situational consistency, or should I begin with the local-global trait distinction and derive the consistency requirement from it? In what order will my peers form misconceptions—likely first that situationism is environmental determinism, then that virtue ethics has been empirically falsified, then that local traits are merely excuses for bad behavior—and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head off each? What properties must the carrying example have: it needs to display both the virtue-ethical expectation of stable helping behavior and the situationist result of dramatic variance, without letting either interpretation dominate the framing. How can I check at the end whether they understand the critique as a challenge to the explanatory scope of global traits rather than as proof that morality is an illusion? And what should I openly refuse to cover—perhaps the interactionist synthesis or recent personality-psychology defenses—while still signaling that the debate continues?
