As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing a feature on how expert knowledge claims survive, or collapse, when they travel from low-stakes laboratory contexts into high-stakes public debate. A colleague sketched the following argument for me. It seems to prove that pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism are jointly impossible, because together they generate what I will call epistemic mirroring failure. Please diagnose it precisely.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) Pragmatic encroachment is true: whether a subject S knows that p depends partly on the practical stakes S faces if p turns out false.
(2) Epistemic contextualism is true: the semantic content of "S knows that p" shifts with the context of the speaker attributing knowledge.
(3) Call the combination encroaching contextualism: the standard expressed in an attribution is set by the attributor's context, but whether the subject satisfies that standard depends on the subject's own stakes.
(4) This produces epistemic mirroring failure: the attributor applies her own context-sensitive standard to evaluate a subject whose required evidential threshold is fixed by a different practical situation.
(5) Consider Dr. Alvarez, a biologist in her lab facing low personal stakes, who sincerely and correctly asserts, "I know the compound is stable," based on strong but not conclusive evidence.
(6) A policymaker, facing high public stakes, reports: "Dr. Alvarez knows the compound is stable."
(7) By (2), the policymaker's utterance expresses a high evidential standard.
(8) By (1), Dr. Alvarez's actual knowledge depends only on her own low stakes, so she meets only a lower evidential bar.
(9) Therefore the policymaker's report expresses that Alvarez meets a high bar, but Alvarez in fact meets only a low bar; the report is false.
(10) Yet direct reports of sincere first-person knowledge claims are routinely true when the original claim is true.
(11) So encroaching contextualism entails that routine cross-context knowledge reports are systematically false.
(12) Hence we must reject (1), (2), or the assumption that they can be combined.
