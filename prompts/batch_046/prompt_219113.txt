I'm delivering the opening remarks at a one-day retirement symposium for my former advisor, a philosopher of music whose career was devoted to Schopenhauer's aesthetics. The room is split evenly between specialists who know his arguments about art in detail and family members, musicians, and students from other fields who have never read him. Please write the remarks so that Schopenhauer's central claim—that music is unique among the arts because it does not copy Platonic Ideas but is instead the direct objectification of the Will—does real structural work, not decorative reference. Specifically, use the distinction between melody, which Schopenhauer treats as the conscious, striving life of the will, and the bass, which expresses the inorganic substrate of gravity and matter, to frame what can and cannot be said about her life's project. Her work was precisely to translate musical experience into conceptual language, yet on Schopenhauer's view music is exactly that which escapes conceptual capture. The speech must navigate this tension: it should honor her writing without claiming she "solved" the mystery of music, and without dismissing conceptual analysis as futile. It needs to land for both halves of the room—no unexplained jargon, immediate translation for every technical point—while including one specific, non-obvious observation about harmonic structure, such as how Schopenhauer reads the movement from dissonance to resolution as an analogue of the will's temporary satisfaction, to show the specialists that the philosophy is being taken seriously. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading suggestions. Do not quote any text verbatim; paraphrase freely. The philosophical idea should determine the shape and limits of what the speech can claim, not serve as ornament.
