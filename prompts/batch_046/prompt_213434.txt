Write a deadpan parody of an institutional health-insurance denial letter and its attached clinical-neurophilosophical assessment, approximately 650 words, in the voice of a bureaucratic insurer adjudicating a post-operative identity dispute. The claimant—an invented patient named Yorick—has undergone a scenario drawn from Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment: his brain was extracted, scanned, and used to initialize two functioning emulations, one running in a remote server farm (Claimant A) and one installed in a synthetic body (Claimant B). Both file claims under the same identity-continuation policy. The denial letter should apply standard philosophical criteria—spatial continuity, psychological continuity, and Dennett's conception of the self as a narrative construct rather than a Cartesian substance—not as resolutions but as bureaucratic checkboxes that fail to localize the insured party. The insurer should deny both claims on the ground that the policy requires a single determinable billing address for the self, which duplication renders impossible. Include an appendix listing three invented precedent cases—e.g., a branching upload with delayed synchronization, a consciousness split across two hemispheres housed in different legal jurisdictions, and a partial upload that preserves only narrative fragments—to show how each criterion collapses under administrative pressure. The satire should land the genuine philosophical problem—whether first-person location is determinable at all after brain duplication—without breaking character to explain the joke. The tone should be dry, technically precise, and institutionally impersonal; the target is the practice of treating personal identity as a property that can be administratively verified. The piece should be readable to someone unfamiliar with Dennett's puzzle, but should reward a reader who recognizes the specific moves in the brain-in-a-vat and duplication arguments.
