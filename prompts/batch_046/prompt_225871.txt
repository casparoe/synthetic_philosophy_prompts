I have to defend a paper next tuesdy at a workshop on blame and hypocrisy. The audience is mostly analytic moral philosophers who work on responsibility — they know the standard objections about tu quoque and standing, they care about consistency with legal practice, and they get suspicious if you sound like you're making blame too cheap. There's also one legal theorist in the room who thinks philosophers over-intellectualize courtroom dynamics. My thesis is that in collective or institutional settings, hypocrisy doesn't just fail to undermine your standing to blame; instead, being a fellow-violator gives you a kind of peer standing that outsiders lack. I call it normative co-implication: if you're implicated in the same normative failure as the target, you can't distance yourself, and that shared vulnerability is what makes the blame stick rather than bounce off. I'm applying it to institutional cases — like a university comittee on academic integrity where half the members have sloppy citation practices themselves but still sanction a student.

The sketch of the argument: hypocrisy only undermines standing when the blamer pretends to be outside the norm. When they acknowledge their own co-implication — like a committee member admitting they also cut corners — the blame becomes peer-correction rather than hierarchical judgment. Outsiders actually lack authority here because they aren't vulnerable to the same charge. So standing isn't cancelled; it's relocated.
