I have been trying to map a particular failure mode I keep encountering in discussions of cross-cultural moral psychology — specifically debates about WEIRD populations, folk morality, and what they imply for moral objectivity — and I am unsure whether the pattern already carries an established label.

Consider the following two interpretive moves, which I have seen applied by the same commentators within a single discussion. First, when a moral intuition — say, the salience of purity or loyalty — shows sharp divergence between societies, this divergence is taken as evidence that the intuition is a culturally contingent construct, not tracking any objective moral feature. So far this is a familiar line from the literature.

But then, when confronted with a near-universal pattern — for instance, that every documented society distinguishes harm-based norms from other kinds of norm, even if they calibrate the weight differently — the interpretive frame is reversed. The universality is not read as undermining the contingency thesis; instead, it is treated as evidence of a deep, shared moral architecture that merely expresses itself in locally calibrated forms. The very feature — cross-cultural variation — that was previously assigned an anti-objectivist significance is now assigned an objectivity-compatible one.

What interests me is that the choice between these frames — call them "variation-as-falsification" and "variation-as-local-expression" — does not seem to be governed by any consistent meta-criterion about what degree or kind of divergence undermines objectivity. Rather, the frame appears to be selected according to whether the underlying moral claim is one the commentator finds palatable. I have observed this in debates over trolley-style dilemmas, over distributive intuitions, and over the status of deontic taboos: the hermeneutic is applied asymmetrically, not strategically in the sense of a conscious bait-and-switch, but systematically enough that it functions as a stable argumentative reflex.

This is not quite the motte-and-bailey, since there is no defensible core being protected by an indefensible extension; both frames are genuinely available in the literature, and neither is being withdrawn under pressure. Nor is it straightforward confirmation bias, because the mechanism is specifically interpretive: the same datum (cultural divergence) receives opposite epistemic significance depending on the domain. Ordinary theoretical pluralism does not capture it either, because pluralism permits different theories for different domains, whereas here a single commentator deploys incompatible interpretive schemas across domains in a way that tracks normative preference rather than domain-specific structure.

Is there an established concept for this selective framing of cross-cultural moral variation? If the answer is no, I would value a coined term together with clear boundary conditions: for example, whether the phenomenon requires that the asymmetry be unconscious, whether both frames must be recognisably present in the academic literature, and how precisely to distinguish it from strategic ambiguity or from a genuinely domain-sensitive pluralism. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want to know whether the carving already exists, and if not, how best to draw its edges.
