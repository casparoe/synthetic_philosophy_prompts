I was discussing the foundations of rational agency with a colleague who works at the intersection of formal epistemology and artificial intelligence safety, and she offered me the following summary of what she called the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents: "If an agent employs a formal system strong enough to represent its own reasoning, then Löb's theorem yields that the agent can prove, for any statement X, that if it proves X then X is true. But the agent can establish its own consistency only if it already trusts its future reasoning, which generates a vicious circularity. Consequently, any sufficiently rational agent must either be logically inconsistent, or must permanently withhold full trust in its own successor reasoning, rendering long-term planning and self-improvement impossible. This is why even a flawlessly designed system could never fully endorse the outputs of a future version of itself without collapsing into paradox." She added that the obstacle applies specifically to agents using classical first-order logic with an explicit syntactic provability predicate, and that it vanishes entirely if one abandons proof-theoretic self-reference in favor of a purely probabilistic framework such as Bayesian updating without a formal derivability operator. I am inclined to believe she is overstating the "permanent withholding" and "impossibility" parts, but my grasp of modal logic is too weak to locate the error precisely. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what accurately reflects the logical result, what is a common simplification that retains the core idea, and what appears to be a misrepresentation—whether of Löb's theorem itself, of the conditions required for self-reference, or of the implications for rational planning. In particular, does the obstacle genuinely compel an agent to withhold trust globally and permanently, or is the constraint more localized and conditional? And is her claim that probabilistic frameworks dissolve the problem correct, or does a structurally analogous difficulty survive even without an explicit syntactic provability predicate?
