As a retired biologist who has only recently begun reading political philosophy systematically, I find myself stuck on what seems like a basic inconsistency in how modern democracies draw their boundaries, and I would like your help in reconstructing the implicit theory behind it. Consider the following institutional pattern, which I take to be common enough in outline, though its parts rarely appear together in any single jurisdiction. At the municipal level, legal residents who lack citizenship—including long-term temporary workers and undocumented migrants who have lived in the district for many years—are permitted to vote in local elections and to sit on participatory budgeting panels; yet at the national level, citizenship is required, and citizens who emigrated decades ago retain the franchise in national elections despite having no present connection to the territory. Meanwhile, citizens aged sixteen or seventeen may vote in some regional referenda but are categorically excluded from national ones. In parallel environmental governance structures—such as watershed management boards or climate deliberative assemblies—selection is made by sortition from the resident adult population with demographic quotas, but the frame excludes anyone under eighteen, anyone without legal residency status, citizens living abroad, and, notably, any representative of future generations or of non-human ecological interests, though these latter parties may submit written testimony but hold no binding vote. Some boards, however, do appoint an ecological guardian who holds a veto over decisions that would violate river-health thresholds but has no vote on economic allocations.

What philosophical view of democratic membership—of who belongs to the demos—does this patchwork presuppose? Is it a single principle, such as all-affected interests, territorial presence, citizenship, or stakeholder status, or does it silently combine incompatible ones? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether the exclusion of future generations and ecological representatives is consistent with the same logic that justifies including long-term non-citizen residents locally. If the underlying view were replaced with a straightforwardly territorial or affected-interest principle, what specific institutional changes would have to follow? In your answer, please separate what is relatively settled in democratic theory from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view—such as the claim that presence alone grounds democratic rights—as though it were the consensus.
