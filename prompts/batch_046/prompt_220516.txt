I coordinate emergency response for a mid-sized municipality, and over the past two years we have faced prolonged infrastructure failures—multi-week power outages and supply disruptions—during which staff and residents repeatedly propose actions that extend well beyond any contractual or legal duty: volunteering for hazardous rescue rotations without overtime, donating personal medical equipment to public clinics, or sheltering strangers at significant personal risk. I need a reusable decision procedure that my office can apply in under thirty minutes per proposal, requiring no formal philosophical training, to classify each proposed action into one of three categories: obligatory (we must require or facilitate it), permissible but not required (we may allow but should not pressure anyone), or supererogatory (it exceeds duty and should be praised but not demanded, and possibly restricted if it generates systemic risks).

The conceptual framework to be operationalized should not rely on a simple binary between duty and what lies beyond it. Instead, the procedure must preserve two distinctions that are rarely explicit in practical ethics but are decisive for our context. First, a distinction between agent-borne costs and system-borne costs: an action may be supererogatory when the burden falls mainly on the individual agent, yet become collectively obligatory once the same burden, if socialized, transforms into a shared responsibility. Second, a distinction between a moral remainder—an unfulfilled specific obligation to an identifiable party that genuinely persists after inaction—and a diffuse moral aspiration, where failure produces no particular wronged individual but only a general deficit of good. The procedure should translate these into operational tests rather than theoretical definitions, using ordered questions with explicit stopping points and clearly marked judgment nodes where the decision must be handed to a human value judgment rather than resolved mechanically.

Please construct the procedure as a sequential flow in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a fixed classification or a clear negative, and where it flags a misfire that an officer should override. Include a small set of diagnostic scenarios or sentences that a practitioner can apply to determine whether a given case involves agent-borne versus system-borne costs, and whether a moral remainder is present. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) a firefighter offers to work a seventh consecutive sixteen-hour shift during a blackout, accepting acute fatigue risk with no compensation; (2) a resident donates her private generator and fuel reserves to a public warming center, leaving her own household without backup heat; and (3) a staff member proposes to rescue a stranded family in a flooded zone using her personal vehicle, an action legally permitted but exposing her to liability and endangering other responders if she requires extraction. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a classification you expect the coordinator to reject—explain the nature of the misfire and how the judgment node should function. Finally, specify exactly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of proposals fall outside its scope, which value conflicts it deliberately does not resolve, and what institutional safeguards must accompany its use so that it is never treated as a substitute for democratic deliberation about risk distribution.
