I have recently begun consulting on governance reform in post-colonial states where clientelist networks are not hidden deviations but the visible architecture of local politics, and I find myself returning to an analogy that my interlocutors keep deploying: they say that applying Western anti-corruption metrics to these networks is like applying the rules of market exchange to a kinship system—specifically, like judging a ceremonial potlatch by the standards of a commercial contract. The claim is that both involve calculated reciprocity, that labeling one "corruption" and the other "economics" is pure cultural framing, and therefore that demands for transparency and impersonal administration are just imperial impositions. I want you to audit this analogy with precision. First, lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the kinship obligation, what plays the role of the ceremonial redistribution, what corresponds to the expectation of future return, and what corresponds to the public office that is allegedly being privatized. Second, identify the disanalogies that actually matter for the normative conclusion—particularly whether the potlatch analog has an equivalent to an electoral mandate being redirected, whether reciprocity in clientelism is bounded by communal norms or open-ended and coercive, and whether the analogy conceals the fact that state resources are being diverted from non-participants who never consented to the exchange. Third, tell me whether the conclusion—that Western standards are ethnocentric—survives these breaks, or whether the analogy was doing all the work. If the analogy collapses, suggest what a better comparison would be: perhaps clientelism is less like a potlatch and more like a private tax levied through political loyalty?
