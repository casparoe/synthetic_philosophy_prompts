Construct a unified decision-theoretic framework with three branches that isolates why the inefficacy objection applies with different intuitive force to climate mitigation, democratic voting, and meat consumption, despite all being collective-harm problems. In Branch A (climate), model the agent’s carbon reduction as contributing to a linear, continuous harm pool where individual causal efficacy is probabilistically negligible but physically non-zero; specify the payoff matrix and the aggregation function so that expected harm is smooth in individual inputs. In Branch B (voting), model the same agent casting a ballot in a large electorate where the harm (e.g., a harmful policy being enacted) is discontinuous, turning on whether the vote creates or breaks a tie; keep the stakes identical to Branch A but change only the aggregation rule. In Branch C (meat consumption), model the agent’s purchase as influencing harm through a market-elasticity mediator—specify a demand curve and supply response—so that causal efficacy depends on counterfactual producer behavior rather than on direct physical aggregation or on a discrete threshold. After presenting the three branches, demonstrate for each: (i) the precise structural feature (linearity, discontinuity, or mediated counterfactual dependence) that generates or undermines the inefficacy intuition; (ii) the verdict of standard causal-decision-theoretic reasoning versus evidential-decision-theoretic reasoning; and (iii) how a rule-consequentialist evaluation of the maxim “if everyone in my reference class acted this way” produces a different result from act-level evaluation in exactly one of the three branches, and identify which feature of that branch makes the divergence occur. Conclude by stating the minimal change to the decision framework—whether altering the probability assignment, the utility aggregation, or the level of evaluation—that would make all three branches yield the same normative verdict, and explain whether that change is independently defensible or ad hoc.
