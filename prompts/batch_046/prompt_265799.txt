Consider the paired institutional practices that govern how modern administrative bodies—government agencies, corporate compliance divisions, and legal institutions—produce language about harm, failure, and accountability. On one side stands the widespread mandate, exemplified by the U.S. Plain Writing Act of 2010 and its analogues in corporate disclosure rules, that all public-facing documents describing institutional failure must use short sentences, active voice, and everyday vocabulary so that citizens can understand exactly who did what. On the other side stands the equally widespread internal norm—visible in military after-action reports, corporate audit summaries, and legal risk assessments—of framing the very same events through agentless passives, nominalizations, and systemic abstractions: "a use-of-force discrepancy was identified," "process errors contributed to the adverse outcome," rather than "the officer struck the detainee" or "the manager approved the false entry." These two linguistic regimes apply to the same domain of institutional harm but pull in opposite directions, and both are enforced with bureaucratic rigor. What philosophical commitments about the relationship between linguistic form, cognitive transparency, and political reality do these paired practices jointly presuppose? Do they presuppose an Orwellian thesis—that corrupt language is both symptom and cause of corrupt thought, so that simplifying syntax for the public restores democratic truth while obscuring syntax for insiders protects power? Or can they be explained by a purely instrumental view of language, on which grammatical packaging is neutral with respect to the beliefs and intentions it conveys, so that the same "content" can be rendered transparent or opaque without any philosophical significance? Is the pairing consistent with a determinist view—sometimes associated with Orwell's broader argument—that specific grammatical structures can literally render certain thoughts unavailable to the speaker? In answering, distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose about language and politics from what they merely happen to accommodate, identify whether the practices are philosophically underdetermined (so that rival theories of language could endorse the same pairing for incompatible reasons), and say whether replacing the underlying view of language with its rival would require changing only one of the two practices or both.
