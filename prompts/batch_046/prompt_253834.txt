I've been trying to formalize Gödel's worry about time in rotating universes, but my version seems to collapse into a general skepticism. Here is the argument:

(1) For a feature to be genuinely real, it must be present in every physically possible world governed by our laws.
(2) Objective global time requires a consistent, loop-free ordering of all events.
(3) Gödel's rotating universe satisfies Einstein's field equations with a cosmological constant and uniform rotation; current bounds on cosmic vorticity do not strictly exclude it.
(4) In that model, closed timelike curves destroy any global ordering.
(5) So global time is absent in a physically possible world.
(6) By (1), global time is not genuinely real.
(7) We lack observational access to whether our universe rotates this way, since the relevant conditions lie beyond our horizon and depend on inaccessible boundary conditions.
(8) Hence we are not justified in attributing global time to our world.
(9) The same schema applies to any essential feature: if a law-compatible world lacking it is observationally indistinguishable from ours, the feature is unreal here too.
(10) That proves far too much — it threatens space, causation, and other minds.

I want to preserve (3), (4), and (7), because dismissing Gödel's model or our epistemic limits feels like cheating. Is the error in (1), confusing metaphysical necessity with epistemic security? Or is there an equivocation in (9) between absence in one model and indistinguishability in ours? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for Gödel's original concern about time?
