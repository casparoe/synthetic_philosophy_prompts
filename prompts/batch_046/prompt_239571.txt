In my sixth-form philosophy and economics reading group, we have spent the term on the Allais paradox and the independence axiom, yet our debate has stalled in a way that feels familiar from the wider literature. Every member accepts the experimental patterns—subjects prefer certainty in one frame and risk in another despite a shared common consequence—and every member can derive the contradiction with expected utility theory. Nevertheless, the division between those who diagnose irrationality and those who reject the axiom as normatively inappropriate has not narrowed at all. I have begun to wonder whether the real disagreement is hidden: not about psychology or mathematics, but about what consistency demands across different temporal parts of an agent's life. To test this, I proposed an intertemporal variant in which the "common consequence" is not a simultaneous outcome but a shared state of affairs ten years hence, so that the choice is between a certain career trajectory and a gamble over paths that converge on that later state. Even with this framing, our group split along the same lines. So my question is this: what kind of disagreement is the Allais debate actually? Is it a hidden empirical question about how people represent probabilities over time? A normative dispute about whether rationality requires consistency across diachronic choices? Or an unarguable bedrock intuition about the unique value of certainty? And given your diagnosis—supported by what each side actually says—what would count as progress? A formal result distinguishing synchronic from diachronic independence? A conceptual distinction that dissolves the paradox? Or is the impasse permanent because the two sides are answering different questions under the single name "rational preference"?
