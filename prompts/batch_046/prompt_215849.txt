Having spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of history and social science, I am now assisting with an upper-level seminar on comparative historical explanation. I want to set a closed-book final essay examination—ninety minutes, one question carrying forty marks out of one hundred—on the logical and methodological status of the contrastive formulation “Why Britain and not China?”, the framing that underwrites both the Great Divergence literature and the Needham question. The question must not be answerable by reciting standard causal inventories—coal, colonies, competitive markets, or cultural rationalism—nor by summarizing familiar accounts of Qing stagnation versus British industrial breakthrough. Instead, it should force students to treat the framing itself as problematic, particularly in light of a conceptual tension I have noticed between natural-scientific comparison and macro-historical comparison: in physics, a meaningful comparative claim requires an explicit shared baseline or boundary condition, whereas in macro-history the “shared” pre-industrial conditions are precisely what remain in dispute.

Please construct the exam question, specifying an approximate word limit of 800 words and instructing students not to include bibliographies, citations, or external references, since the exam is designed for conditions without web access or library consultation. The question should require students to confront two specific conceptual difficulties. First, whether treating China’s non-industrialization as a single determinate outcome implicitly assumes a counterfactual uniformity that collapses distinct possible trajectories—an issue I have come to regard as the problem of overdetermined absence, where the absence of an event is treated as if it had the same causal determinacy as its presence. Second, whether the descriptive categories used to characterize “British advantages”—such as secure property rights, rationalized bureaucracy, or market competition—function as anachronistic projections when mapped onto Qing institutional arrangements, rather than as neutral comparative variables.

Please also provide:
1. A model answer of approximately 700 words that demonstrates the required analytical moves: identifying the baseline-condition problem in comparative macro-history; diagnosing at least one specific risk of category-projection; and advancing a reasoned judgment on whether the contrastive form should be abandoned, retained with reformulation, or replaced by a non-contrastive explanatory structure.
2. A marking scheme that clearly separates first-class answers—which must explicitly address both the baseline-condition problem and the anachronism risk, and must take a defensible position on reformulation—from upper-second answers, which handle the historical material competently but treat the framing as methodologically unproblematic or address only one of the two conceptual difficulties. The scheme should list specific moves that earn marks and specific omissions that should cost them.

The assessment is intended for students who have studied the Great Divergence debate and the Needham question in general terms, but who have not encountered the particular synthetic framing of “overdetermined absence” or the explicit analogy to experimental baseline conditions. The prompt should therefore inject these framing devices without relying on obscure technical literature that a small language model would be unlikely to have properly internalized.
