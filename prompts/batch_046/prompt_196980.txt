Having recently retired from experimental physics and begun working through Plato in translation, I keep returning to an apparent contradiction about intoxication. In the Symposium, Alcibiades' drunken arrival produces an apparently truthful, uninhibited account of Socrates and of erotic desire itself; in the Laws, the Athenian Stranger treats wine as a dangerous pharmakon that must be confined to state-regulated symposia precisely to test citizens' virtue without dissolving rational order. The controversy is whether Plato thereby assigns alcohol a genuine philosophical function — a ritualized lowering of inhibition that reveals character — or whether the Symposium scene is dramatic irony and the Laws gives the real doctrine: wine is a civic poison requiring legal restraint. Write an essay that weighs these readings through the lens of structured epistemic vulnerability: the idea that truthful disclosure under intoxication is only cognitively valuable when the social architecture of the symposium preserves enough rational scaffolding to interpret the revealed content, whereas unregulated drunkenness collapses into noise. Reach and justify a specific conclusion about whether Plato should be read as endorsing alcohol as a philosophical instrument. In your conclusion, state explicitly how confident you are in the overall verdict, and single out the one interpretive move in your argument — for instance, your reading of the Laws' regulatory intent, or your claim about the Symposium's dramatic structure — about which you are least sure.
