I'm trying to run reflective equilibrium on the demandingness of consequentialism, but my verdicts on concrete cases don't line up with any standard formulation I've encountered. Here are my judgments, case by case. One: you're walking past a shallow pond and can save a drowning child at the cost of ruining an expensive suit. Required, without ambiguity or excuse. Two: you can donate ten percent of your disposable income every year to an effective intervention that saves roughly one life per donation cycle. I think this is genuinely required, but failing to do it is not as blameworthy as walking past the pond; the obligation feels real yet somehow "softer," as if continuous extraction from the same budget line eventually hits a saturation point. Three: you could work eighty-hour weeks for a decade on a project with very high expected value in lives saved, but doing so eliminates any meaningful personal life, relationships, or projects outside the work. Not required; this is heroic, and I wouldn't blame someone for declining. Four: a billionaire with a hundred million dollars can give away ninety percent, leaving ten million, and thereby save thousands of lives. Required, and blameworthy to refuse—despite the absolute magnitude being far larger than case two. Five: you are one of ten thousand people whose collective carbon footprint causes one statistical death; you can eliminate your contribution for about two thousand dollars per year. I think you should do it, but the obligation feels thinner and more negotiable than case two, even though the individual cost-benefit ratio is comparable.

The tensions I'm stuck on are between case three and case four—both involve enormous sacrifice, yet one is obligatory and the other is not—and between case two and case five, where similar individual costs yield different strengths of obligation. My own tentative framing, which I'm sure is incomplete, distinguishes what I call a "structural budget" (time, identity, close relationships) from "discretionary surplus" (money above a comfort threshold). Obligations that invade the structural budget seem to saturate and become supererogatory, while obligations drawing on discretionary surplus remain binding even at large scale. That explains three versus four, but it doesn't explain why five feels thinner than two, since both draw from surplus.

What principle, if any, captures all five verdicts? Please offer one precisely stated principle, or a small family of competing candidates, and for each clause specify which case motivates it. Show what each principle predicts for cases I haven't listed—say, a middle-class person donating five percent of income continuously for life, or a wealthy person giving away fifty percent but retaining enough for luxury. Be explicit about which of my verdicts each principle must overturn if they cannot all be accommodated, and say whether reconciliation requires appealing to collective-action structure, identity-preservation, some form of moral fatigue curve, or a factor I haven't named. I want an honest assessment of whether my saturation intuition should be kept, modified, or abandoned.
