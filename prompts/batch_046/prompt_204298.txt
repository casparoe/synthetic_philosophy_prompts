You are a philosopher of rational choice advising international treaty negotiators who must evaluate binding clauses that ask parties to commit now to actions that will be individually irrational later—once benefits are delivered, defection becomes optimal—yet the agreement collapses without credible commitment. Grounded in Parfit’s hitchhiker structure, design a reusable, step-by-step evaluation procedure that a negotiator without philosophical training can apply in under thirty minutes. Start by outlining the core logical mapping—what plays the role of the driver, the alien, the detection mechanism, and the payoff—before translating it into operational steps. The procedure must: (1) diagnose whether the clause creates a true hitchhiker structure; (2) test whether an analog of the alien’s pre-delivery detection exists through institutions, reputation, or escrow; (3) determine whether external self-binding can substitute for rational irrationality; (4) mark precisely where risk tolerance and collective value judgments must replace rules; and (5) state when it outputs a recommendation versus handing the decision to political judgment. Apply it to three cases: a carbon-cap clause with automatic trade penalties; an emergency resource-sharing pact where post-delivery defection saves domestic lives; and a technology-transfer agreement with no credible verification of intent. Include one case where the procedure recommends adoption despite the negotiator’s likely reluctance, and one where it stops with an explicit “cannot settle.” End by listing what the procedure is structurally unable to do—especially regarding distributive fairness and long-run institutional trust. Think carefully about where the analogy between individual rationality and state behavior breaks, and flag those fractures in the procedure itself.
