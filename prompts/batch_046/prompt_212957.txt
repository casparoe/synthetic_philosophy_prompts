Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level bioethics seminar of 12 postgraduate students that makes them feel the force of the problem of precedent autonomy in advance directives—specifically, the tension between treating an earlier self's directive as a binding exercise of authority over a later self, rather than merely as evidence of what that person now wants. The setting should involve paired role-play: in the first 30 minutes, each student writes a directive for a hypothetical future version of themselves after a specified cognitive or affective change (for example, a shift from valuing intellectual rigour to valuing emotional immediacy following a neurological intervention). The directive must contain both an executory clause (a concrete refusal or consent) and a constitutive clause (a statement about which values ought to govern future deliberation). Then, in a second phase, students are randomly assigned the later-self role with altered preference profiles and must decide whether to honour, invoke an escape condition, or override the directive, with the constraint that they must justify their choice using either authority of the earlier agent or welfare of the present agent, but not both. The rules should include a scoring mechanism—perhaps shared integrity points for consistency across phases and individual flourishing points for present satisfaction—so that the conflict is felt structurally rather than merely discussed. Include a debrief that maps escape-clause invocations onto philosophical distinctions (precedent autonomy versus present best interests, and executory versus constitutive binding), predicts what will go wrong in the first run (for instance, students conflating sympathy for the earlier writer with genuine deference to authority), and notes frankly which feature of the philosophical problem—the metaphysical question of whether the later self is numerically identical to the directive's author—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not ask for readings or citations.
