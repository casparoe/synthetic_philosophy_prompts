Construct a realistic, one-paragraph decision scenario—set in an ordinary institutional context such as public-health resource allocation, workplace-safety regulation, or urban infrastructure planning—engineered so that lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and standard aggregative negative utilitarianism deliver opposing verdicts, with the disagreement turning specifically on whether severe suffering and mild discomfort are commensurable on a single scale. The scenario must meet these specifications: (i) Option A prevents exactly three instances of intense, unambiguous, physically localized agony but unavoidably produces a vast number—tens of millions—of barely perceptible discomforts that are qualitatively different (for instance, a chronic low-grade aesthetic or cognitive irritation rather than bodily pain); (ii) Option B eliminates the widespread mild states entirely but leaves the three severe instances untouched; (iii) the two negative experiences must be described so that reducing them to a shared cardinal metric feels conceptually strained, not merely arithmetically unwieldy; (iv) no option may rely on science-fictional technology, perfect predictors, non-human moral patients, or exotic physics; (v) the setting must be one a normal decision-maker could actually face. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it satisfies each specification by providing: (a) the precise verdict each theory delivers, with step-by-step reasoning; (b) an explicit identification of which structural feature—lexical priority, qualitative incommensurability, or sheer scale—does the real work in generating the divergence; (c) an honest assessment of what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed, for example by making the mild states clearly homogeneous with the severe ones or by reducing their number to a tractable magnitude; and (d) the strongest objection available to the defender of the theory that yields the counterintuitive result in your case, together with one clear reason that objection might fail. Do not cite specific authors, texts, or page numbers; build the argument from the conceptual structure of the theories themselves.
