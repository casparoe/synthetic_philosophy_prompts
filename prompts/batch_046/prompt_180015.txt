I'm constructing a small anthology of philosophical fiction that treats metaphysical commitments not as decorative metaphors but as conditions under which characters actually suffer, decide, and perceive. I would like a short story—perhaps only its premise and an opening scene of about five hundred words—centered on Erwin Schrödinger's specific philosophical commitment, developed in his essays on Vedanta, that the multiplicity of individual minds is an illusion comparable to the quantum superposition of physical states, and that the only coherent ontology is one in which every apparent self is a modal expression of a single, undivided consciousness identical with what Vedanta calls Atman-Brahman.

The story should be set in Zurich during the winter of 1926, but its speculative premise should be grounded in a precise conceptual claim rather than vague mysticism: the protagonist, a fictional physics student named Clara who is assisting with wave-mechanical calculations, begins to experience episodes in which her first-person perspective fails to maintain boundary conditions. Rather than framing this as psychosis, the narrative should take seriously the idea—often dismissed by later commentators as poetic overreach—that quantum mechanics removes the conceptual necessity for separate observers, since a wavefunction describing mental states might in principle be non-separable across what we conventionally call different people. The crisis should culminate not in a laboratory but in an ordinary moment—Clara buying bread—when she realizes that the baker's intention and her own are not causally linked but identically instantiated, an experiential echo of the Vedantic teaching that the individual self and the universal ground are one.

Please write the opening scene, and then append a brief author's note, separate from the fiction, that distinguishes what is well documented about Schrödinger's actual interests and writings—his serious study of Vedanta, his rejection of Western substance dualism, his view that consciousness is fundamentally singular—from what remains genuinely contested among scholars, such as whether he intended this monism as literal metaphysics, as an interpretive heuristic for quantum mechanics, or as private mystical consolation. In that note, clearly flag where the story takes liberties with historical fact or with contested readings of his philosophy, separate settled interpretation from disputed ones, and do not present any one contested view as the consensus among people familiar with the topic.
