A friend who reads popular philosophy summarized Tolstoy's essay on art—commonly called What Is Art?—in this way. She said Tolstoy defines art as emotional infection: the artist feels something sincerely, puts it into a form that others can perceive, and the audience then catches the same feeling, much as one catches a cold. She claimed that Tolstoy measures artistic value purely by how widely and intensely this infection spreads among ordinary people, with no special education required, and that he therefore divides art into true art, which is clear and democratic, and counterfeit art, which is obscure, technically elaborate, and made to please critics and the wealthy. She went on to say that Tolstoy applies this so strictly that he rejects complex symphonic music and modern painting alike, since both demand trained taste rather than immediate feeling. Finally, she argued that the view is incoherent: if the standard is pure emotional transmission, then propaganda posters and advertising jingles should be masterpieces, yet Tolstoy clearly wants to exclude them, which shows he is secretly relying on a moral criterion—universal brotherhood—that he never actually includes in his definition of infection. I feel she has turned Tolstoy's religious and moral purpose into a simple sociology of accessibility, but I am not sure exactly where the distortion lies. Is her summary fair? Please separate what is accurate to Tolstoy's argument, what is overstated or subtly wrong, and whether her charge of incoherence holds once the summary is corrected.
