I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science and me and some friends from the ethics seminar keep arguing about James and Clifford after class. Last tuesday two of them got stuck in a debate about whether a public health official can justifiably believe a new virus variant is highly transmissible when the data's still noisy but delaying action would be catastrophic. (1) One said this is exactly James's point: it's a forced option (act now or dont), live (both outcomes are real possibilities), and momentous (lives at stake), so the official has a right to form belief on insufficient evidence, and Clifford's blanket prohibition that belief without adequate evidence is wrong everywhere and for everyone is too rigid for crisis decision-making. (2) The other said that's not Clifford at all -- his shipowner case targets belief-driven action, but you can implement lockdowns while genuinely suspending belief, saying we are being precautionary rather than convinced. Clifford allows cautious action; he just bans claiming knowledge you dont actually have. (3) The first came back that suspending belief is a fiction here -- if you actually do not believe it is dangerous, you wont spend political capital on costly measures. James is honest that belief drives action, and in forced, live, momentous cases we choose belief via our passional nature when intellect cannot settle the matter. (4) The second replied that momentous is just emotional urgency in disguise, and without Clifford's evidential brake every official will claim crisis to justify hunches. Plus James's live option criterion is culturally relative, so it cant ground a universal ethics of belief.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say who is more right overall and what both are missing -- especially whether theres any principled difference between accepting for action and actually believing that saves Clifford's position here, or whether James's framework applies differently to institutional belief than to individual belief.
