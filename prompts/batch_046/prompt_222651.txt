For an upper-level philosophy of probability seminar with ten graduate students and seventy-five minutes, design a runnable classroom game that makes the participants feel the force of a Dutch-book argument applied not to an individual agent but to a collectively pooled belief set. Inject the following twist: the students work in triads, each triad holding partial credences over a set of jointly exhaustive but not pairwise-independent propositions describing outcomes of a hypothetical non-standard die with overlapping categorical properties. Within each triad, members must combine their individual credences using a linear opinion pool to produce group betting odds. A rotating “bookmaker” student then offers separate and combined bets against those pooled odds using a point-based payoff scheme with small but tangible stakes. Structure the rules, roles (pool members, bookmaker, auditor tracking payouts), and timeline so that the triads actually suffer collective losses—discovering through play that linear aggregation of individually coherent credences can yield a group credence set that is Dutch-bookable even though no individual member is vulnerable. Specify exactly how the betting rounds proceed, what the payoff matrix looks like without real money, and how the auditor reveals the vulnerability. Include a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal distinction between individual coherence and aggregate coherence, states what will predictably go wrong on the first run (for example, participants attributing the loss to individual miscalculation rather than to the aggregation rule itself) and how the facilitator should correct this in real time, and be explicit about which feature of the classic single-agent Dutch book the activity cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied verbally by the facilitator. The participants should leave seeing that coherence is not automatically preserved under belief pooling, and they should have constructed that insight themselves before the concept is named. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; do not invoke obscure named theories that depend on specific textual attribution; and avoid framing the exercise around well-known textbook examples of single-agent betting.
