I'm applying for a six-month fellowship at a philosophy of law center and I need a 2-page research proposal. I want to work on communitarianism vs liberalism but not the usual Sandel/MacIntyre versus Rawls replay that everyone has seen a million times. My half-baked idea is this: both frameworks treat moral disagreement as either conflict over values or conflict over shared goods, but neither really handles what I'll call interpretive deadlock—when a community can't agree on what one of its own practices means, not just whether it's good or valuable. Think of a religious schism where one side reads a ritual as covenant and the other as coercion. Liberalism tends to translate that into individual belief or preference, which feels like it misses the collective nature of the breakdown. Communitarianism can say the tradition is fractured, but it struggles to describe the failure without just asserting that shared meaning has dissolved—it's descriptive but not explanatory. I want to ask whether there's an asymmetry here: can liberal resources like public reason or neutrality model interpretive deadlock without reducing it to private choice? And can communitarianism explain it without circularity?

The proposal should include: a precise statement of the question; why existing answers in teh standard debate don't settle it; the method—probably conceptual analysis plus one or two historical case studies of internal schism (I'll fill in the referances myself, don't include a bibiography); what a result would look like for people working in legal/political theory; what counts as failure; and a realistic scope for six months. Write it like a real grant pitch: the framing should argue that the work is worth doing and can actually be done in the time. Don't ask for citations or a bibiography, I'll add those later. Keep it casual—this is a draft, so a typo or two is fine.
