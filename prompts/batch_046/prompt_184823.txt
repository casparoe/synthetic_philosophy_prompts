Consider the appeals protocol of certain algorithmic governance boards, where a system-wide policy may be overturned only if an individual affected party demonstrates that the specific burden placed on them exceeds what could be justified to them as an equal; the board explicitly prohibits "cumulative harm" arguments that pool many individually sub-threshold grievances to block change. The same institution, however, distributes its operational budget through aggregate cost-benefit analysis, treating distributed small costs and benefits as fully summable. A participant in this system notices the tension—individual rejection is treated as categorically decisive here, yet summed effects are treated as decisive there—and asks what implicit theory of moral justification makes both practices coherent. What philosophical commitments does this bifurcated design presuppose? Does the appeals rule commit the institution to a contractualist view that wrongness consists in unjustifiability to particular individuals rather than in aggregate harm, or can an aggregationist treat the rule as merely a tractability constraint? Is the bar on pooled objections internally consistent with the contractualist framework, or does it smuggle in an aggregative baseline to determine when an individual burden crosses the rejection threshold? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined—so that either contractualism or a suitably modified aggregationism could license the design—and specify what would have to change in the protocol if the underlying view of numbers and justification were replaced by its rival.
