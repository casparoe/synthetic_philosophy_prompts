hey so i'm drafting a short essay for my blog about Plutarch's parallel lives and whether they're actually good for moral learning. my take is that everyone treats them like instruction manuals — imitate the good parts, avoid the bad — but i think the real pedagogical value is in what i'm calling biographical dissonance: the gap between the life narrated and the comparison plutarch forces on it. take nicias and crassus — he pairs them to talk about greed and superstition destroying capable men, but if you actually look at the sicillian expedition details, nicias's hesitation isn't just piety gone wrong, it's strategic paralysis that doesn't map cleanly onto the moral label. and crassus has real competence before parthia that the frame just ignores. so the reader doesn't learn virtue by copying either guy; they learn by noticing where the propogandist frame cracks.

i want to extend this to modern hagiography — not saints, but science biographies like curie or einstein that present pure dedication and suppress contradiction. my claim is that modern biography suppresses dissonance, smoothing out contradictions, whereas plutarch leaves them visible, which makes him a more honest moral teacher despite being a worse propogandist. does this hold water? i'm definatly unsure about the nicias/crassus mapping — am i misreading the expedition? and is it fair to say science biography suppresses contradiction, or am i setting up a strawman? also should i even use the word hagiography for plutarch, or does that confuse religious and secular biography? comments on coherence and whether this sounds interesting or just contrived would be great. no need for sources, just your take.
