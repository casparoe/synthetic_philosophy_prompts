I'm closing out a seminar on testimony and the norms of assertion—the dispute between knowledge norms, belief norms, and truth norms—when a student raised a worry I haven't been able to set aside. She noted that in nearly every domain where she participates—climate science, medicine, mathematics—her assertions depend on testimony she does not personally verify, yet she still speaks with the vocabulary of knowledge ("we know that..."). This creates what I want to call an "assertoric debt": every claim made without first-person justification accrues a standing obligation to defend or retract it, and the debt compounds differently depending on whether the speaker asserts from belief, acceptance, or borrowed authority. I want a poem that stages this precise discomfort, not as a lecture but as a lived moment of speaking.

Write a sonnet—fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, with a Shakespearean rhyme scheme (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG) and a clear volta at line 9—spoken in the first person by someone who has just made a specific public claim (for instance, a statistical projection about regional climate patterns or a computer-verified mathematical result) based entirely on expert testimony she cannot herself reproduce. In the first eight lines, build her confidence through vocabulary that quietly slides between "I know," "I believe," and "they have shown," letting the rhyme words reinforce that slippage—pairing words like "known" with "loan" or "proof" with "truth" in ways that sound secure but feel hollow. At the volta on line 9, she should suddenly recognize that applying a strict knowledge norm would require her to fall silent on nearly everything she says, yet abandoning that norm leaves her carrying an unpayable assertoric debt—each spoken claim now a liability rather than a contribution. The rhyme in the third quatrain and the couplet should feel strained or half-resolved, as if the form itself buckles under the pressure. The final couplet must not resolve the paradox; instead, it should leave her suspended between the silence demanded by epistemic rigor and the speech demanded by participation, with pronouns shifting from "I" to "we" to "they" to show the diffusion of responsibility. Render the distinction between knowing-that and knowing-how, and between testimony and first-person justification, precisely enough that an epistemologist recognizes the structure, but write it so that a reader unfamiliar with the knowledge norm of assertion or the division of epistemic labor still feels the philosophical weight in the rhythm and the rhyme. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
