As a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun attending philosophy seminars, I keep encountering a particular one-liner in discussions of space ethics: the warning that we must not export our sins to the stars. In the session that prompted this, an interlocutor invoked it to reject outright any Martian governance model that permits private extraction rights, contending that proprietary claims to extraterrestrial resources reproduce the logic of terra nullius and therefore carry the same moral stain. I found the move puzzling because the phrase seemed to slide between an uncontroversial historical reminder and something far stronger—perhaps a categorical prohibition on any proprietary framework, or even an obligation to leave celestial bodies untouched. I would like you to lay out, as a clear menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing. For each reading, indicate whether it amounts to a platitude, a defensible but substantive moral claim, or a false or overreaching one; specify which reading actually supports dismissing proprietary extraction rights on analogical grounds (as opposed to supporting that dismissal only through independent distributive or ecological arguments); and say which version the speakers in such seminars usually intend when they deploy the phrase to end debate. I am not asking for a bibliography, a survey of the literature, or any citations—only the disambiguation with verdicts, since I need to bring a precise map of the options back to my discussion group.
