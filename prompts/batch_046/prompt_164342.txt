I am designing a single fifty-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have read Kant and Mill but have no background in Chinese philosophy. I want to teach Confucian role ethics as a live alternative to both virtue ethics and the liberal picture of the unencumbered self. Give me a teaching plan—not the lecture itself—structured as follows. First, the dependency order: which conceptual pieces (the relational constitution of the self, asymmetrical reciprocity, the distinction between achieving a role and merely occupying it) must be introduced in sequence, and what each depends on having been secured first. Second, identify the one modern, non-familial carrying example—such as a long-term mentor-mentee research collaboration or a clinical supervisory relationship—that can bear the weight of the idea; specify what features it must possess (asymmetry, reciprocal obligation, no contractual exit clause) and what features it must lack (symmetrical exchange, universalizability, voluntary association pure and simple). Third, map the misconceptions: that role ethics is authoritarian family ideology, that it denies moral autonomy, that filial piety means blind obedience, and that it is incompatible with criticizing one's own role; state the exact moment in the sequence where each misconception should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, propose a brief check for understanding that requires students to apply the framework to a scenario outside the family or workplace hierarchy without falling back on Kantian universalization or utilitarian calculation. Fifth, state explicitly what historical texts, ritual specifics, and contemporary political applications must be set aside and acknowledged as beyond the scope of this session. The aim is that students leave seeing role ethics as a coherent moral psychology rather than an anthropological report on traditional societies.
