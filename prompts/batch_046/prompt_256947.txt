The dispute over free-floating rationales—whether normative reasons can obtain in the absence of any actual or counterfactual reasoner—has reached an impasse that now extends well beyond metaethics into applied design. On one side stand the source-dependence theorists, who hold that a consideration counts as a reason only if it relates, however remotely, to an agent's motivational structure, a social practice, or at least a possible grasping mind; for them, the claim that suffering is bad absent all sentience is not false but conceptually malformed, like attributing color to a number. On the other side stand the structural realists, who maintain that normative relations hold between states of affairs—such as the relation between gratuitous harm and its being-to-be-avoided—independently of anyone's cognitive or practical engagement with them; they regard the demand for a reasoner as an illicit projection of epistemology onto ontology. For years the exchange has cycled through familiar moves: the internalist challenge that free-floating reasons are causally and motivationally inert; the realist reply that explanatory power does not require motivational efficacy; the internalist counter that this renders the notion of a reason idle. Recently the deadlock has taken a concrete form in debates over legacy autonomous systems: consider a decommissioned ecological governance protocol that continues to adjust water distribution according to optimization criteria set by a dissolved regulatory body, where current stakeholders neither designed the criteria nor endorse them, yet the system's outputs consistently prevent ecological collapse. Source-dependence theorists deny that these outputs generate obligations for present users; structural realists affirm that they do, provided the outputs track genuine normative structures, regardless of provenance. My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily an ontological disagreement but a masked divergence about what a theory of reasons is supposed to explain—whether it must preserve the link between recognizing a reason and being moved by it, or whether it need only preserve the authority of normative judgment. I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey. Is the disagreement verbal, with both sides using 'reason' for different explanatory targets? Is it substantive but hidden behind shared examples? Are there different standards for what counts as data—perhaps intuitions about motivation versus intuitions about authority? Or is there a hidden formal question about the conditions for representation or agency embedded in the legacy-system case? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, tell me what would count as genuine progress: a distinction that dissolves the conflict, a formal result, an empirical finding about institutional cognition, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed and should be abandoned.
