I've just come away from a long argument with a friend about whether the Löbian obstacle really kills the possibility of genuinely self-trusting rational agents, and I'm trying to hold onto a stronger claim: not merely that an agent with sufficient logical strength cannot prove its own consistency without thereby being inconsistent, but that this creates a principled, inescapable asymmetry—any such agent can rationally trust a strictly weaker version of itself, but can never rationally endorse its own soundness at the same level of logical strength. My friend thinks this overstates the obstacle, because she distinguishes between logical endorsement and practical epistemic deference: an agent might lack a formal proof of its consistency yet still be warranted in treating itself as reliable by a kind of default operational stance, much as we treat induction without deductive justification.

What would count against my stronger claim? I want to know which assumptions it actually depends on—whether it is tied specifically to first-order arithmetic with a particular proof predicate, whether bounded or modal variants of self-reference escape it, and whether there are coherent formal models where an agent endorses a consistency statement about itself without triggering the Löbian contradiction. More concretely, would a counterexample require showing an agent that genuinely trusts itself at full strength without circularity, or is it enough to show that my distinction between trusting a weaker copy and trusting oneself collapses under closer analysis? And if the obstacle turns out to be avoidable only by weakening the agent's logical resources below the threshold needed for the result, does that save my view or undermine it?

I'm particularly interested in whether there are live, non-merely-logic-chopping escape routes: for instance, if an agent uses a non-standard model of arithmetic to interpret its own consistency statement, or if there is a meaningful sense in which trust can be indexed to a different proof system than the one the agent uses to reason. Which of these would genuinely defeat my position, and which would just be ways of redefining self-trust so that my claim becomes true by stipulation? I want an honest assessment of whether my view risks being insulated—if every apparent counterexample can be dismissed as not real self-trust at full strength—and whether that insulation is a feature of the logical landscape or a bug in my argument.
