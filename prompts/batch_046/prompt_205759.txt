the hard part is she genuinely lived this view, not just published about it. so i can't really say she 'believed deeply' in her work or that she 'desired' to change the field, because from her own framework those mental-state attributions are exactly the false folk talk she's spent her career trying to eliminate. but i also don't want the toast to sound like a cold behaviorist lecture or like i'm mocking her career. i need it to honor what she actually did - build the models, run the experiments, shift how people think about cognition - without falling back on the psychological language she critiqued.

can you write a five-minute toast that navigates this? it should land for the philosophers in the room (maybe a nod to churchland-style arguments without being a lecture) but stay warm and clear for her sister and nephews. and it shouldn't just avoid the words belief and desire - it should actually use the eliminativist framing constructively, like explaining why her commitment wasn't an inner mental state but something she embodied or enacted. also its tricky to do this without sounding like i'm saying she was just a robot, so the tone needs to stay affectionate.
