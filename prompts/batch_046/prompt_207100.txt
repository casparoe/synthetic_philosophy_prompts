I have recently begun collaborating on a project that models belief revision in contexts—scientific forecasting and legal reasoning about counterfactual liability—where agents must update on indicative conditionals despite having only imprecise probabilities. This has drawn me into the philosophical logic of conditionals, and specifically into the problem posed by Lewis's triviality results: any assignment of probabilities to indicative conditionals that respects standard independence conditions seems to collapse into triviality. I am trying to determine, not merely what the standard responses are, but which unresolved questions in this area actually deserve sustained effort over the next several years, and in what order.

Among the live options—defending a restricted version of Stalnaker's thesis, abandoning Adams's thesis entirely, constraining the domain of conditionals subject to the result, or reconceiving the relation between indicative and counterfactual conditionals—which lines of work are upstream of the others? Which are tractable for someone with a background in logic and formal semantics but not in the specialized literature on conditionals? And which, if settled, would most change how we should build practical belief-revision frameworks? Please defend a specific prioritization: state the criteria you are using—whether tractability, downstream dependency, or relevance to imprecise-probability contexts—and explain what kind of result over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list. I am not asking for a survey or a reading list; I want a defended agenda for where limited research time should go, and whether the triviality result is primarily a technical obstacle or a deep challenge to probabilistic reasoning about conditionals.
