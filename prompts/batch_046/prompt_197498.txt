As a retired physicist who has recently begun auditing seminars in philosophy of history, I have been discussing with colleagues in a reading group whether the long-running dispute over presentism is genuinely substantive or structurally misaligned. The specific question we keep returning to is whether historians can escape the normative standards of their own time when evaluating past societies—say, when judging whether the institutional practices of the Roman Republic constituted an unjust distribution of welfare rather than merely a culturally different arrangement.

One position, which I will call the Invariance Thesis, holds that certain normative properties—much like physical quantities that remain constant across coordinate transformations—can be identified in historical contexts without importing the evaluator's contemporary framework; on this view, a historian achieves neutrality not by abandoning judgment but by tracking properties that do not depend on the observer's normative reference frame. The opposing view, which I will call the Indexicality Thesis, maintains that all historical evaluation is necessarily indexed to the evaluator's own moral and conceptual coordinates, so that apparent neutrality is actually a hidden projection, and the best historians can do is make their coordinate system explicit. Despite decades of debate, both sides invoke the same case studies and methodological examples without convergence.

I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication: is this deadlock rooted in a hidden empirical disagreement about the cognitive psychology of historians, a verbal dispute over whether escaping presentism means achieving metaphysical neutrality versus achieving methodological transparency, or a clash of bedrock intuitions—perhaps analogized to debates about whether physical laws are frame-independent—that neither side can argue for? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal distinction in the philosophy of language, an empirical study of historiographical reasoning, or should we conclude that framing the problem through the analogy to physical reference frames reveals the original question to be ill-posed?
