For an upper-level philosophy seminar on existential risk and cosmic sociology, draft a 90-minute essay exam question on the dark forest hypothesis—the argument that rational civilizations in a resource-limited universe with discontinuous technological growth must treat any detected signal as an existential threat and preemptively destroy its source, on the grounds that survival is the primary axiom and the future power trajectory of strangers is radically unpredictable. The question must require students to move beyond narrative summary and instead analyze the argument’s logical structure, its game-theoretic analogies, and its policy implications.

The exam question should instruct students to do four things:
A. Reconstruct the inference from the survival axiom and technological unpredictability to the conclusion that disclosure is always irrational, identifying any suppressed premise about credible commitment or information asymmetry.
B. Evaluate whether the scenario is better modeled as a prisoner's dilemma or a stag hunt, and explain whether mutual silence could be a stable equilibrium given discontinuous growth.
C. Assess the objection that ontological humility—the recognition that one's own rational and axiological architecture may not be universal—undermines the predictive premise, considering both the weakening interpretation and the risk-amplification interpretation.
D. Derive one concrete implication for SETI disclosure protocols if the reasoning holds, and defend it against the objection that human societies routinely violate pure survival rationality through altruistic or self-sacrificial behavior.

Then provide:
1. The exam question itself, clearly framed for students.
2. A model answer outline of approximately 600 words that displays the specific philosophical moves required for a top answer: the explicit reconstruction with the suppressed commitment premise; the precise payoff-structure comparison and why discontinuous growth breaks stag-hunt symmetry; the recognition that ontological humility is ambiguous rather than resolving; and a SETI policy recommendation tightly linked to the premises, paired with a reply that distinguishes individual-level morality from civilization-level selection dynamics.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, allocating points across parts A–D plus coherence, specifying for each part the move that separates excellent from adequate answers, and listing the specific errors that should reduce marks—such as conflating the two game-theoretic models, treating ontological humility as automatically dissolving the problem, offering a generic SETI recommendation with no logical connection to the dark-forest premises, or substituting plot summary for philosophical analysis. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not quote verbatim from any published text.
