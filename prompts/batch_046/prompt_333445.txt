Read Stanley Kubrick’s Dr. Strangelove through the lens of nuclear-deterrence theory, focusing on the commitment problem embedded in the idea that credible brinkmanship may require cultivating an appearance of irrationality. The film’s Doomsday Machine is an automatic retaliation system—an irreversible commitment device meant to solve the credibility gap of mutual assured destruction—yet it is hidden from the adversary, which neutralizes its deterrent purpose. Use this tension to interpret key sequences: General Ripper’s unauthorized strike as a failure of delegated brinkmanship; the War Room debate over whether to warn Moscow as an illustration of the paradox that full transparency undermines strategic ambiguity; and the Doomsday Machine’s activation as the logical endpoint of removing human choice from retaliation. Does the film suggest that making deterrence mechanically credible necessarily destroys the political conditions that make it rational? Where does the black-comedy treatment of sexuality, death, and bureaucratic language resist reduction to strategic logic—does it imply that human drives are the real destabilizer, or that they are inseparable from the very irrationality that makes deterrence work? End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about commitment and credibility that formal analysis alone misses.
