I work as a software engineer without any formal philosophy background, though I’ve been reading informally about social psychology and cultural theory. I recently encountered the framework that distinguishes “tight” cultures—which enforce strong norms and sanction deviation severely—from “loose” ones that tolerate greater variability in behavior. The usual account, as I understand it, treats tightness mainly as a matter of normative strength: how many rules exist and how harshly they are enforced. But I’m wondering whether there is a second, less-discussed axis we might call “semantic tightness”: the degree to which a culture requires shared, unambiguous interpretations of symbols, rules, and collective goals in order for cooperation to function at all. A society could be normatively loose—few explicit rules, mild sanctions—yet still be semantically tight, in the sense that members must agree precisely on what basic terms like “order,” “rescue,” or “justice” mean; conversely, a normatively tight society might enforce conformity rigidly while still allowing competing interpretations of what the rules actually signify, so long as outward behavior aligns.

I’m preparing a discussion for a reading group of colleagues, and I’d like to read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies through this two-dimensional lens—not simply asking whether the boys form a tight or loose society, but whether the novel shows Ralph’s democratic group failing primarily from normative looseness (too little enforcement) or from semantic looseness (the conch, the signal fire, and the very concept of “rescue” become unstable in meaning as the boys drift apart). Does Golding dramatize a situation in which behavior could remain loosely regulated yet still functional, except that shared meaning has already fractured? Conversely, does Jack’s authoritarian tribe achieve not just stricter norms but a kind of forced semantic closure—where “hunting,” “strength,” and “the beast” become the only permitted interpretations—such that normative and semantic tightness collapse into one another? I want a reading that takes both the novel and the cultural-psychology framework seriously, without reducing the book to a mere illustration: where does the text resist being mapped onto these categories, and what might it add—perhaps about how symbolic breakdown precedes normative collapse in small-scale societies—that a purely sociological argument about tightness and looseness tends to overlook?
