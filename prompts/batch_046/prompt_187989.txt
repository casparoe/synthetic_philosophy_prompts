I take pain asymbolia to establish that the badness of pain is not built into its phenomenal sensory character—localized heat, sharpness, intensity, and bodily location—but is contributed entirely by a separable affective-evaluative component. On this view, a subject could in principle undergo the complete sensory profile of a severe burn with zero negative valence and suffer no harm whatsoever; the sensory state is unpleasant only when the evaluative module tags it as bad. What would genuinely count against this separation thesis? I am not asking for the familiar observation that asymbolic patients still withdraw their hands; I want the deeper defeaters. Which observed or constructible cases—say, an "inverse asymbolia" in which a subject reports intense suffering and exhibits robust avoidance behavior despite lacking any detectable nociceptive sensory quality—would undermine the claim that affect and sensation are independently variable? Which neural or behavioral findings would show necessary coupling rather than mere correlation? Rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which ones would a committed separationist concede as damaging, and which could they absorb by redefining terms? Finally, assess whether the separationist can defend themselves by insisting that sensory pain without negative affect simply is not pain at all, and whether that defensive move renders the view unfalsifiable or merely analytically precise.
