I'm in a seminar on strategic reasoning where we've been working through the Platonia dilemma and the associated Luring Lottery experiments with superrational agents. During discussion, one participant kept comparing the two Platonia agents to two instances of the same open-source chess engine running on physically separate servers: they share identical weights and evaluation functions, so if one selects move X the other selects X too, not because of any network message but because the computation is literally duplicated. He pressed this analogy to argue that superrational coordination is just deterministic replication in disguise, and that the luring mechanism is therefore redundant—the agents aren't genuinely reasoning about each other's choices, they're replaying a single computation with no counterfactual independence. Another participant pushed back: chess engines lack preferences, don't face payoff structures, and the server separation is a physical boundary that Platonia's purely logical separation doesn't replicate, so the analogy smuggles in causal isolation that isn't actually available. I want a proper audit. Please lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the shared weights, the separate servers, the evaluation function, the absence of a TCP connection, and the absence of stakes? Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the determinism-reduction claim from the ones that are merely decorative noise. In particular, does the engine picture preserve the counterfactual dependence that the dilemma takes to be central—where each agent asks what they would do if they were the other—or does it replace that reasoning with literal identity? Finally, if the chess-engine analogy fails precisely where the philosophical interest lies, suggest whether a better analogy exists—perhaps drawn from symmetric distributed computation or from identical twins in separated environments—that captures the symmetry without importing hidden causal coupling, and say whether that improved version still supports the conclusion that superrationality collapses into determinism.
