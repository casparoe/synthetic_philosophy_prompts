I was sitting in on a graduate seminar on comparative ethics last term when two doctoral students, Priya and James, became locked in a disagreement about whether Confucian role ethics contains the internal resources to condemn nepotism in political appointment without importing foreign normative frameworks. Their exchange went through several rounds, and since I was only observing, I have no stake in either side; I genuinely could not determine who had the stronger position.

Priya opened by arguing that Confucian morality is constituted by specific, graded relationships rather than universal rules, so the charge of corruption is largely an anachronism imported from Western legal-rational models. She maintained that the ruler’s obligation to his family — extending from filial piety to fraternal care — does not vanish upon taking office, and that promoting a genuinely capable cousin strengthens the relational web that sustains harmony. She even proposed what she called a “role-transference principle”: obligations nurtured in the family sphere can migrate into institutional contexts provided the underlying relational structure remains intact, so appointing kin is not a betrayal of public duty but a continuation of role-constituted care. James countered that this collapses the distinct architecture of Confucian political roles. The ruler-subject relationship is defined not only by care but by impartial justice, and classical sources treat the confusion of private affection with public duty as a primary cause of disorder. He granted that Confucian care is graded, yet insisted that grading operates across domains — closer to kin at home, but differently structured within government — so there is no contradiction in separating family love from impartial selection. He then pressed a deeper worry: if every role obligation ultimately reduces to maintaining harmony, the theory can criticise a ruler who fills offices with relatives only by appealing to bad consequences, which makes it covertly consequentialist rather than genuinely role-based. Priya replied that James was imposing a false dichotomy. Maintaining harmony is not an external outcome but an internal criterion of correct role performance; a ruler who appoints incompetents fails in his specific role not because disorder follows, but because the role itself includes cultivating and placing worthy people, so the check is internal. James offered a final rebuttal: if the criterion is “worthy people,” then we need an independent standard of worth not defined by family ties, which means the ethics must borrow from outside its relational logic. Without that borrow, Priya’s view cannot explain why appointing a capable cousin is ever wrong, since capability and kinship may align; with it, the theory admits that non-relational norms constrain roles, undermining her claim that morality is purely constituted by them.
