In debates over the gender-equality paradox — the finding that more gender-egalitarian societies often display stronger gender segregation in career choice — defenders of a biological or preference-based interpretation frequently invoke an analogy: gender norms function like a rigid mold pressed onto pliable material, and egalitarian policy is the act of breaking that mold away so that the underlying substance can settle into its natural contours. The observed increase in gender-typical career patterns in highly egalitarian countries is then taken as the substance revealing its intrinsic shape, licensing the conclusion that the segregation reflects innate, pre-social dispositions rather than persistent cultural pressure, and that justice is served by letting revealed preferences stand rather than by forcing parity.

Audit this analogy thoroughly. First, lay out the explicit mapping: what plays the role of the mold, the material, the breaking, and the settled form, and what work each part is doing in the argument. Second, identify where the mapping fractures: for instance, whether human preference formation is genuinely like passive material settling, whether the "mold" can be partly constitutive of the "material" rather than merely constraining it, and whether breaking one mold might expose people to other, less visible molds. Third, isolate the features the analogy smuggles in — particularly the assumption that there exists a stable, pre-social preference profile that survives the removal of explicit norms — and ask whether those features hold for career choice specifically. Fourth, consider whether an alternative analogy (say, preference formation as path-dependent navigation through a landscape of available cues, or as language acquisition in a specific linguistic environment) does better at explaining why egalitarian contexts might produce more gendered outcomes without invoking innate difference. Finally, determine whether the conclusion that justice requires accommodating these revealed preferences actually follows, or whether the disanalogies open space for the view that egalitarian societies have not eliminated culture but transformed it into a different kind of signaling environment, leaving the nature-culture-justice question genuinely open.
