I'm preparing a reading guide for an advanced undergraduate course on the history of moral philosophy, and I want to test whether students can diagnose a sophisticated but flawed summary. Below is a summary of Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, drawn from what a colleague calls the best short introduction they've seen. Please audit it.

Summary: Smith does not treat sympathy as emotional contagion—feeling exactly what another feels—but as an imaginative act in which we place ourselves in the other's situation and form an idea of what they feel. This imaginative projection is then corrected by reason. The result is not mere agreement in feeling but agreement in judgment about the propriety of the feeling. The impartial spectator is therefore not an actual person or a social survey, but an internalized normative perspective: we imagine how an informed, disinterested observer would view our passions. Crucially, Smith holds that this spectator applies a strict proportionality rule—our sympathy must match the objective severity of the cause in exact quantitative proportion—and that justice is enforced by the state only because sympathy for the victim is too weak to motivate punishment on its own. From this it follows that Smith's spectator is functionally identical to Rawls's veil of ignorance: both require stripping away particular social positions to reach an objective standpoint, and both treat moral judgment as a calculation of what a rational agent would choose without knowing their place. The summary concludes that for Smith, sympathy can never justify violating a general rule, because the spectator's judgment is always categorical rather than context-sensitive.

Please tell me: which parts of this summary a careful reader of Smith's work would accept, which parts involve subtle conflations or overstatements, and whether the conclusion about categorical rules and the Rawls comparison actually follows from the accurate parts. Structure your response as follows:

1. Verdict in brief (about 3 sentences): Is the summary broadly trustworthy or systematically misleading?
2. Accurate core (about 150 words): Identify the parts that capture Smith's view correctly, with brief explanation of why they hold.
3. Subtle errors and conflations (about 250 words): Go point by point through the misrepresentations—especially the proportionality rule, the enforcement argument for justice, the Rawls comparison, and the claim about categorical rules. For each, say what Smith actually holds and why the summary's version is off, without relying on verbatim quotations.
4. Does the conclusion survive? (about 150 words): Take the summary's final claim that Smith's view is categorical and equivalent to Rawlsian constructivism. Show whether the accurate parts support it, or whether removing the errors collapses the argument.
5. Unoccupied logical space (about 100 words): Note one coherent interpretation of Smith's sympathy or spectator that this summary does not represent—perhaps a particularist reading, or one that emphasizes the affective rather than judgmental dimension of sympathy—and briefly explain why its absence matters for assessing the summary's scope.

Write for a philosophy instructor deciding whether to assign this summary as supplementary reading. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
