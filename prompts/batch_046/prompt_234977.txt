In the manuscript I presently have in progress, there is a scene in which two evolutionary theorists debate whether human musicality is an adaptation or what one character terms an architectural leftover—a feature that exists not because selection favored it directly, but because the cognitive architecture required for language necessarily produces rhythmic pattern-recognition as an unavoidable structural byproduct, much as the triangular spaces between arches in certain basilicas arise from the geometry of vaulting rather than from decorative intent. The first theorist argues that because musicality is now evidently functional—facilitating social cohesion, emotional regulation, even mating displays—it must have been refined by selection and therefore qualifies as a genuine adaptation, regardless of how it originated. The second theorist insists that if a trait's origin is purely structural, it retains the status of a spandrel forever, and that attributing current utility to it is merely constructing a just-so narrative that confuses consequence with cause.

Reading around this disagreement, I have become convinced that the underlying issue is conceptual rather than empirical. The spandrels critique, as I understand it, seems to presuppose that every biological feature must be assigned a single, exclusive ontological category—either a direct product of selection for its present role, or a non-adaptive byproduct of some other design constraint—and that these two categories cannot overlap. But I worry I may be misreading the argument. Does the Gould-Lewontin critique actually require such a rigid binary? If a trait begins as an unavoidable structural consequence of an adaptive architecture—say, the expansion of the neocortex producing surplus associative capacity that is then co-opted for abstract reasoning—does its subsequent maintenance and refinement by selection transform it into an adaptation proper, or does its spandrel genesis permanently exclude it from that classification? More generally, is the critique aimed primarily at the overuse of adaptationist narratives, or does it entail the stronger claim that origin-determination is epistemically prior to functional analysis—that we must first establish a trait's architectural genealogy before we are licensed to speak of its evolutionary significance at all?
