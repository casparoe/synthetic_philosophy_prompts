I am presenting a paper that challenges the standard fideist reading of Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief in the later lecture notes. My claim is that he does not merely place religious belief outside rational appraisal by treating it as a language-game with its own internal grammar; rather, he tacitly relies on a normative distinction between authentic religious commitment and superstitious practice that he never defends, and that this distinction sits uneasily with his broader anti-essentialism about language-games. The audience is a philosophy of religion seminar: mostly analytic philosophers sympathetic to Wittgensteinian non-cognitivism, one historian of the later Wittgenstein who is sceptical of systematising interpretations that ignore manuscript context, and a cognitive scientist of religion who regards the belief-superstition boundary as empirically unstable. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to come, phrased in the voice of each faction, with what I ought to concede, what I must hold, and the weakest point in my argument that I should defend openly rather than hope is missed. Include the objection I most dread — likely from the historian about the reliability of student notes — and note where my reply is softest.
