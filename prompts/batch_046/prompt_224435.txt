As a retired molecular biologist who spent decades interpreting fluorescence micrographs—images that never speak for themselves but require calibration, theoretical framing, and the explicit exclusion of observer artifacts—I have recently turned to philosophy of mind and encountered what seems to be a methodological mirror-image in the heterophenomenological approach. The dispute between those who treat first-person reports of conscious experience as raw data requiring a neutral descriptive framework, and those who insist that every such report must be interpreted as an intentional narrative shaped by belief, culture, and cognitive architecture, appears to have reached a peculiar impasse: both sides accept the same neurophysiological findings, both concede that subjects can be systematically mistaken about their own experiences, and yet for decades the disagreement has not shifted. The defenders claim heterophenomenology rescues the science of consciousness from introspective mythology; the critics charge that it dissolves qualitative experience itself into a hermeneutics of self-narrative, leaving nothing for consciousness science to explain.

My working hypothesis is that this stall is not a substantive ontological disagreement about consciousness, but rather a concealed clash over the epistemic status of first-person reports—specifically, whether being the subject of an experience confers a non-transferable form of authority that survives interpretation, analogous to the authority once claimed for direct microscopic observation before the full recognition of instrument-mediated theory-ladenness. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical question—perhaps concerning the cognitive penetrability of introspection, or the neural mechanisms that generate verbal reports—actually driving the disagreement? I want an honest structural diagnosis, not a survey of positions: is the impasse verbal, centered on competing uses of “phenomenology” and “data”; is it a conflict of bedrock methodological intuitions carried over from different scientific cultures; or are the two sides answering different questions—one about the semantics of mental language, one about scientific methodology—under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a formal distinction between epistemic authority and descriptive access, an empirical result about introspective error rates, a reconceptualization of what consciousness science aims to explain, or is this an impasse that should be accepted because the underlying question is ill-posed?
