Analyze the collapse of the Newfoundland northern cod stock and the 1992 federal moratorium as a case study in the prisoner's dilemma, treating the open-access fishery before Canada's 1977 extended jurisdiction as an anonymous one-shot game and the post-1977 total-allowable-catch regime as an iterated game among identifiable players. Identify the actual strategic actors—independent inshore harvesters, offshore trawler fleets, and the Department of Fisheries and Oceans—and specify what actions counted as cooperation (restraining catch, reporting accurate data) and defection (exceeding quotas, misreporting, free-riding on conservation) in each institutional phase. Apply the conditions for the evolution of cooperation—iteration, reciprocity, and identifiability—to explain why cooperation failed to stabilize despite the quota system, paying particular attention to how scientific uncertainty about biomass and the credible anticipation of a terminal moratorium altered the shadow of the future. Then examine the limits of the framing: account for the role of government misestimation of stock size and the 1989 quota reduction, ask whether these represent exogenous shocks or endogenous strategic deception that reshapes payoffs, and consider whether the episode is better explained by a breakdown in collective action than by rational defection in a prisoner's dilemma. Finally, state what the collapse implies for evolutionary theories of cooperation—specifically, whether it confirms that cooperation requires an indefinite horizon, or whether it demonstrates that institutional identification alone is insufficient without credible punishment mechanisms, and whether indirect reciprocity through public reputation could have altered the outcome.
