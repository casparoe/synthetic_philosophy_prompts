Gödel's first theorem applies to any formal system that is consistent, effectively axiomatizible, and strong enough to represent basic arithmetic. Outside mathematics people love to cite Gödel to claim there's always unknowable truth, or that human minds can't be computers, or that every system has hidden contradictions. Most of these are overblown. But the result does travel beyond pure arithmetic, provided those three conditions are met.

Consider a fully formalized AI safety framework expressed in first-order logic, like the kind discussed in value-alignment work. If that framework can encode elementary number theory — which it probably can, since it needs to represent counts, probabilites, or resource allocations — then Gödel applies directly. That means there will be specific safety properties that are true but unprovable inside the framework. So no automated system can ever fully verify its own safety, which is why human oversight is unavoidable. It's basically the same point Penrose made about human mathematicians seeing truths that formal machines miss, except applied to safety rather than arithmetic. Also, Gödel's second theorem tells us the framework can't prove its own consistency, so self-certification is doubly impossible.

The usual mistake is thinking Gödel applies to weak or inconsistent theories, or to systems that can't do arithmetic at all. But as long as the framework is rich enough, the limitation is real and not just a technicality.
