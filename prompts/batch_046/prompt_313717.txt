I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've drafted some notes for a short essay on whether the classic tragedy-of-the-commons framework actually applies to digital public goods like open-source dependencies. I would appreciate conceptual feedback—particularly on whether my framing is coherent, whether I'm misusing philosophical distinctions, and whether I'm unknowingly reinventing existing categories.

Here is the gist of my argument. In the physical cases—pastures, fisheries, groundwater—the harm is rivalrous depletion: each additional user subtracts from what remains. But software libraries are non-rivalrous in consumption; one more download does not degrade the code. The danger, I think, is not over-extraction but under-maintenance: security patches, compatibility updates, and bug fixes require scarce human labor, and the pool of maintainers can be depleted by burnout or abandonment. I am calling this a "maintenance commons" collapse, as opposed to an extraction commons collapse. It seems structurally closer to a public-goods failure with a hidden positive-duty dimension—users freely benefit while contributing little or nothing to upkeep—rather than to the standard negative-duty story of "don't overgraze."

From there, I try to connect this to philosophical talk about negative versus positive duties. In the pasture case, the moral instruction is largely prohibitive: restrain your own use so others aren't harmed. In the dependency case, the instruction feels more like an obligation to contribute labor or resources, which sounds like a positive duty. I then ask whether institutional design for digital commons should therefore aim not at limiting consumption—there is no need to ration downloads—but at converting passive users into maintainers, perhaps through governance structures that make contribution visible and expected. Otherwise we risk what I call a "tragedy of abandonment," where the commons doesn't collapse from overuse but from neglect.

My specific requests are these. First, is the distinction between an extraction-driven commons and a maintenance-driven commons philosophically substantive, or is it merely a change of vocabulary applied to the same underlying economic problem? Second, does importing the negative-duty versus positive-duty framing distort either concept—perhaps by treating voluntary labor contribution as a moral obligation in a context where no direct harm is caused by omission? Third, am I missing standard philosophical or economic categories—perhaps around club goods, common-pool resources with institutional rules, or collective action problems—that already capture this dynamic more precisely? I am not looking for a bibliography or reading list; I want an internal critique of the reasoning, pointing out where the analogy breaks or where the moral analysis slips. If the framing is confused, I would prefer to know exactly where and why rather than to be pointed toward literature I should read.
