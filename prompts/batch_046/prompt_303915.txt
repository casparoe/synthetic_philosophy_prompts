I want to operationalize consequentialist cluelessness—the problem that for many actions we cannot meaningfully assign probabilities to distant outcomes—into a practical procedure for regulators.

Write a reusable decision checklist, with ordered steps and explicit stopping rules, that an infrastructure regulator could apply to proposals whose causal chains extend decades and involve feedback-sensitive systems. Ground the procedure in the distinction between predictive cluelessness (insufficient models or data) and structural cluelessness (intervention alters the system being predicted). Include: (i) an initial triage question that sorts proposals into tractable, predictively clueless, or structurally clueless; (ii) for each branch, specific conditions yielding approve, approve-with-binding-revision-clauses, defer-to-deliberative-body, or reject; (iii) markers showing where quantitative expected-utility reasoning must be replaced by qualitative robustness checks; (iv) an honest flag for cases where judgment is ineliminable and the decision must be handed upward rather than computed.

Then run the procedure on: (A) a 60-year tidal barrage in an estuary with poorly modeled sediment dynamics; and (B) a decade-long vaccination and habitat-modification program for a migratory species facing a novel pathogen, where intervention may shift selection pressures.

Conclude whether the procedure avoids both paralysis and false precision, and identify its single worst failure mode. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific named literature; work only from the conceptual structure.
