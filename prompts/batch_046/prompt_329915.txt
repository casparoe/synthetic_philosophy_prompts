As a retired experimental physicist who spent three decades in condensed-matter research before turning seriously to philosophy in retirement, I find myself returning to Alfred Hitchcock's Rear Window with a question that the standard privacy literature does not seem to address directly. The usual frameworks—privacy as control over access to personal information, or as the preservation of intimate spaces free from intrusion—are adequate for discussing data protection or domestic seclusion, but they treat observation primarily as a breach of boundaries. I want to explore a different conception: privacy as the preservation of what I will call interpretive sovereignty, meaning the agent's authority to determine the explanatory and normative framing of her own actions. On this view, the harm of being watched is not merely that hidden facts become visible, but that the observer appropriates the narrative structure of the observed life, reclassifying gestures, routines, and relationships within an alien interpretive scheme. The observed agent does not just lose concealment; she loses ownership of the meaning of her behavior.

Read Rear Window through this lens. The protagonist, confined to his apartment with a broken leg, transforms the courtyard into a field of observation, using a telephoto lens and binoculars to construct elaborate narratives about his neighbors—particularly the salesman Thorwald, whose actions Jefferies interprets as evidence of uxoricidal guilt. Meanwhile, Lisa Fremont moves from being Jefferies' romantic partner and occasional observer to becoming an active participant inside the observed space, breaking into Thorwald's apartment and thereby shifting from interpreter to interpreted. Consider the scenes in which Jefferies watches the composer, the dancer, and the lonely woman: in each case, his gaze imposes a story—artistic struggle, romantic performance, despair—that may or may not correspond to the subjects' own self-understanding.

Does the film support the idea that privacy's core value lies in protecting this interpretive sovereignty? Where does observation in the film function as mere information-gathering, and where does it become narrative appropriation? How does Lisa's transition from observer to observed complicate the ethics of Jefferies' surveillance? Conversely, where does the film resist this reading—for instance, in suggesting that Jefferies' interpretations are partly correct (Thorwald is indeed guilty), or in implying that some lives invite interpretation by others? I am not asking for a plot summary or for citations from the philosophy of privacy literature; rather, I want a critical reading that takes both Hitchcock's drama and the concept of interpretive sovereignty seriously, and that concludes by saying what the film reveals about privacy that an abstract argument about informational control could not capture on its own.
