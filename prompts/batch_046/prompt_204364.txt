I take the interpretive position that Augustine's unqualified prohibition on lying—developed in De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium—is best explained not as a deontological rule about harm or duty but as an entailment of his semiotic ontology: human speech is instituted to communicate the contents of the mind, and a lie deliberately fractures the sign-significate relation that makes language intelligible, making lying metaphysically parasitic rather than merely immoral. On this reading, Augustine's refusal to permit lies even to save innocent lives follows not from rigid moralism but from the claim that corrupting the sign-relation undermines the very institution of truthful communication upon which human community depends, a view reinforced by his broader account of signs in De Doctrina Christiana and his analysis of the will's relation to truth in De Libero Arbitrio. I want an honest map of what would count against this interpretation—not just the standard objection that Augustine is an inflexible rigorist, but which passages, which conceptual distinctions in his corpus, and which results from neighboring domains (say, medieval theories of mental language or modern philosophy of language) could genuinely undermine it. Which defeaters would a defender of the semiotic-ontological reading concede as damaging, and which could they absorb by reinterpreting Augustine's vocabulary of signum and verbum? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether the view risks being insulated from falsification by ad hoc adjustments to his theory of signs—if so, is that insulation a problem for an interpretive claim of this kind? I am especially interested in whether any internal tension in Augustine's treatment of metaphor, allegory, or pedagogical accommodation in Scripture could serve as decisive counterevidence.
