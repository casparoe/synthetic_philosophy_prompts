For the opening session of my graduate seminar on epistemic self-knowledge, I want to move the students away from merely summarizing the standard literature and toward constructing the evaluation framework themselves. Specifically, I am dissatisfied with how discussions of luminosity and the KK principle often proceed as if the only live question were whether Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument succeeds or fails, without first asking what an adequate theory of first-person epistemic transparency must actually be able to deliver.

Please write the desiderata that any satisfactory account of how mental states become epistemically accessible to their subjects—particularly regarding belief and knowledge—must satisfy. Your specification should include: first, the classic motivating cases for luminosity, such as immediate phenomenal awareness of feeling cold or being in pain, together with the intuition that a subject should be able to know she is in such a state if she is; second, the conditions that make the KK principle seem plausible for some doxastic states and implausible for others, with attention to the distinction between having knowledge and being in a position to know that one has it; third, the requirement that the theory respect empirical constraints on self-monitoring, including the fact that introspective access is resource-limited, sometimes systematically distorted, and not uniformly available across cognitive architectures; fourth, the distinction between phenomenal luminosity and doxastic luminosity, which popular presentations tend to conflate; and fifth, a further constraint I want you to treat as central—call it the gradience requirement: the theory must explain how epistemic transparency can come in degrees, accommodating states of partial belief, uncertain self-attribution, and intermediate confidence levels without collapsing into either full luminosity or complete opacity. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or theoretical consideration behind it, flag which are genuinely non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences, and identify whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given standard no-go results in the area. Then, briefly, assess how the main positions—Williamson's anti-luminosity view, modified internalist transparency accounts, and KK-friendly externalist frameworks—fare against your list. Keep the weight of your answer on the criteria and their interrelations rather than on the scorecard, since my purpose is to have the students see the standard of evaluation before they apply it.
