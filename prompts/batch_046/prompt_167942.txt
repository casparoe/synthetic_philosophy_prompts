Construct a diagnostic decision tree—intended as a “which position do you actually hold?” instrument—for views in which moral normativity is indirect, mediated specifically through idealized deliberation rather than applied directly to actions. Start from the root commitment: first-order moral requirements bind not actions themselves but some feature of practical reasoning (a deliberative disposition, a counterfactual endorsement, or an institutional procedure). Build the tree from the logical space upward: first pose concrete diagnostic questions that a respondent can answer without knowing which philosophical label they point to, then locate named positions at the leaves.

Use exactly these four branch dimensions (each node should be one yes/no or either/or question framed as a concrete case):

1. Transparency: If an agent knows she will not reach the idealized deliberative state, does the norm generated by that state still bind her present action? (Concrete case: she recognizes her own partiality and refuses to correct for it, yet the corrected view condemns her choice.)

2. Scope of idealization: Does idealization alter only the agent’s information set, only her motivational structure, or both simultaneously?

3. Layering: Is the procedure of idealization itself subject to a second-order normative assessment (e.g., must the idealization be justifiable to all affected parties), or is it taken as a primitive methodological given?

4. Mediation target: Does the indirect norm apply to an individual agent’s counterfactual state, to a collective deliberative design, or to the structural conditions that shape reasoning?

The leaves must include at least: procedural constructivism; a counterfactual-endorsement view (ideal-advisor or fully-informed-rational-agent type); a structural-institutional mediation account; and a direct-action fallback view that accepts indirectness in theory but collapses it in practice. Flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate—if two views share every branch answer but differ only in rhetorical framing or in a distinction your four nodes do not capture, say so explicitly rather than papering over it.

Also identify one logically possible combination of answers across the four nodes that appears unoccupied in the literature, and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored (for example, a view that demands both full motivational transformation and collective second-order justification but applies the result only to individual counterfactual states).

Design the instrument for a graduate philosophy audience already familiar with the basic idea that norms might apply indirectly, but who need to locate their own view precisely. Keep the tree to no more than six decision nodes, do not ask for bibliographies or specific textual citations, and do not invoke obscure named theories that depend on access to a particular monograph. End your response by stating how confident you are in the taxonomy’s accuracy and singling out the specific node or separation about which you are least sure.
