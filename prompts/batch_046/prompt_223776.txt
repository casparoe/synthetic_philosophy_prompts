I am in the middle of constructing a historical novel that traces three families across 1820s Naples, 1860s New Orleans, and a fictional Parisian silk-weavers' quarter in 1872, and I find myself stalled on a conceptual problem about how moral norms actually die. I have been collecting my own verdicts on concrete episodes, and I wonder whether they cohere around a single principle or whether I must abandon one to save the rest. In the decline of dueling among Southern planters, my intuition is that economic cost and legal risk did the work, not any genuine moral persuasion; yet in the erosion of foot-binding among certain Qing elites I think honor itself was redefined—what conferred status on a family shifted, and the old practice became dishonorable rather than merely expensive. The abolition of Atlantic slavery strikes me as a mixed case: neither pure interest nor pure argument seems sufficient alone, since plantation economics declined in some regions while moral argument gained force precisely where slaveholding had become socially degrading, though I keep reversing myself on whether the moral shift could have occurred without the economic one. By contrast, the persistence of child labor in industrial Britain despite widespread moral condemnation suggests that interest can keep a norm alive even when honor and argument have turned against it.

What breaks my tentative pattern is the rapid disappearance of communal shunning in certain nineteenth-century Protestant settlements, which collapsed after a localized theological dispute about grace—seemingly pure argument—without any visible economic incentive or honor-based mechanism driving it away. Is there an account—perhaps along the lines associated with Bernard Williams, who emphasizes the role of interest and social function, against Kwame Appiah, who stresses honor and the grammar of social recognition—that captures all five judgments? More precisely, I want a principle that states the conditions under which a norm dies by argument, by interest, or by honor, and that tells me which of my five verdicts I must overturn if consistency demands it. Finally, apply the principle to the unresolved case in my novel: in the 1872 weavers' quarter, masters maintain an unwritten rule that they must never acknowledge an apprentice as an artistic equal, sustained not by law or explicit honor ritual but by a silent grammar of deference embedded in workshop practice. If both economic pressure and explicit moral argument begin to push against this rule, yet the weavers' collective self-conception remains intact, does the norm survive, and does that outcome show that honor-based explanations are ultimately reducible to material interest, or that they operate with genuine independence?
