I have been working through some problems in institutional ethics for a graduate seminar on autonomy and collective decision-making, and I constructed the following argument with some colleagues over coffee. At first each premise seemed defensible, but the conclusion strikes me as clearly wrong, and I cannot locate the precise misstep without abandoning commitments I want to keep. The chain runs as follows.

(1) For consent to be morally valid, the consenter must possess an accurate understanding of the specific act or state of affairs being authorized, not merely an absence of explicit false statements. (2) In the normative sense required by (1), understanding demands the absence of any systematically induced false belief about the causal structure that connects the authorized act to its foreseeable consequences. (3) Every institutional consent procedure—whether clinical, contractual, or democratic—necessarily employs framing: information is sequenced, weighted, and categorized by the institution rather than by the consenter, and this structural selection is unavoidable. (4) When such framing alters the consenter's beliefs about causal structure without the consenter's explicit awareness of that alteration, it satisfies the conditions for deception, even if every discrete proposition presented is literally true. (5) Because framing is unavoidable in institutional design, every instance of institutional consent involves deception about the causal structure of the authorized act. (6) Therefore all institutional consent is invalid. (7) But if all institutional consent is invalid, then democratic authorization, contractual obligation, and clinical authorization all collapse into mere acquiescence under manufactured ignorance—a conclusion I find unacceptable.

My intuition is that the slip occurs somewhere between (3) and (5), possibly involving an equivocation between the inevitability of framing and the inevitability of belief-distortion, or between influence and deception proper. I would strongly prefer to preserve (1), since it captures the idea that consent is genuinely epistemically demanding, and (7), since democratic legitimacy should not reduce to bare acquiescence. Which premise should be rejected, and what does that rejection cost for a theory of valid consent? In particular, if I give up (4), do I lose the ability to criticize subtle institutional manipulation, or can that criticism be reconstructed without treating all framing as deceptive?
