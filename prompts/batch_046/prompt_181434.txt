I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on moral epistemology, and after our unit on testimony I've become stuck on a problem that seems to collapse the distinction between learning a moral fact and being told what to do. My study group recently discussed whether an AI ethics advisory system could ever provide genuine moral knowledge, and I tried to formalize our intuition that something is lost when normative guidance comes secondhand. Here is the argument I sketched; it strikes me as valid, yet its conclusion seems to undermine the very possibility of moral education.

(1) If a belief is genuinely moral, then holding it requires possessing the corresponding practical disposition—what philosophers call motivational internalism—so that recognizing a reason to apologize just is being disposed to apologize. (2) Testimony, whether from a person or an algorithmic advisor, can only transmit representational content; it cannot transplant practical dispositions into the listener. (3) Therefore, if someone acquires the belief that they ought to apologize solely through testimony, they lack the corresponding disposition and do not genuinely hold a moral belief, only a detached report about morality. (4) The same reasoning applies across all second-personal normative domains—prudential, aesthetic, and etiquette judgments—where the belief's authority depends on the holder's own practical stance rather than on external evidence. (5) Consequently, moral education, legal instruction, and even clinical ethics consultation are systematically hollow: they produce beliefs that wear the costume of morality but lack its practical heart. (6) Yet we plainly think children can learn justice from parents and patients can learn autonomy from counselors, which suggests moral testimony is not only possible but indispensable. (7) So one of the premises must fail, but I cannot see which.

If I had to choose, I would keep (1) and (2), since they capture exactly why an AI advisor feels different from a conscience. But then I must reject either the generalization in (4) or the inference from (3) to (5), and I'm unsure what each rejection costs. Where does this argument actually break down? Is there a way to preserve the idea that moral belief is essentially practical without rendering all secondhand moral learning impossible?
