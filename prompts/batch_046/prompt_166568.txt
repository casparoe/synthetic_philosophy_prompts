You are the senior philosophical advisor to a university's Committee on Civic Distinction, tasked with drafting the committee's formal written opinion for circulation before Thursday's vote. The committee must decide whether to confer this year's Distinguished Integrity Award on Dr. Elena Vasquez, a senior administrator who exposed a serious research-funding fraud. The fraud is confirmed, and Vasquez filed the detailed whistleblower report. However, internal communications show she had recently been denied promotion by the perpetrator and, in private messages, spoke of getting back at him; she also told a confidant that she would have let it slide had she received the promotion. At the same time, her report meticulously cited institutional ethical obligations, and she refused a settlement that would have required her silence. The committee's charter requires that recipients act primarily for the right moral reasons, but does not specify how to evaluate motives when moral and non-moral reasons are both present.

Please draft the committee's decision memo recommending whether to confer the award, deny it, or confer it with a qualified citation and explanation. The document should be actionable by an institutional body: state a clear bottom line, lay out reasoning the members can apply, and treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with intellectual honesty — especially the view that any significant non-moral motive corrupts moral worth entirely, and the opposing view that moral worth depends only on whether the agent's practical reasoning was genuinely responsive to the right considerations. To avoid a generic analysis, frame your evaluation through a specific conceptual criterion you should call counterfactual reason-responsiveness: the question is not whether Vasquez would have acted without her grievance, but whether her recognition of the fraud's wrongness functioned as a stable, non-derivative reason in her deliberation — one that would have retained its motivational force even if the grievance had been independently resolved. Apply this criterion to the evidence, then conclude with the concrete recommendation and a brief timeline for implementation. Think carefully and begin by mapping the conceptual tension between motivational purity and reason-responsiveness before turning to the case details; do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or external citations, and do not invoke obscure named theories that the committee could not apply without further study.
