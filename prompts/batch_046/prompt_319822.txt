For roughly half a century, philosophers of science and working scientists have cycled through the same exchange about reduction and emergence: reductionists hold that every macro-phenomenon is in principle derivable from micro-physical laws and that macro-disciplines are therefore merely convenient approximations, while emergentists maintain that domains such as consciousness, superconductivity, and evolutionary adaptation possess autonomous explanatory structures—sometimes with downward causal efficacy—that are not merely computationally intractable but conceptually irreducible. The empirical agreement is broad—both sides accept statistical mechanics, both accept that macro-patterns stabilize—yet the disagreement never moves; each side reinterprets the same examples to fit its framework. My working hypothesis is that part of the stall is verbal—the word 'explanation' does different labor for each camp, one meaning derivability-in-principle and the other meaning predictive and manipulable autonomy at the macro-scale—but I also suspect a hidden formal question is operating, specifically whether the independence licensed by renormalization-group techniques in physics amounts to ontological autonomy or only epistemic separation, and whether downward causation requires genuinely novel forces or merely organized constraints. Diagnose the disagreement precisely: is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two disciplines answering different questions—metaphysicians about ontology, scientists about predictive utility—under one heading? Be concrete about what each side actually claims: do reductionists deny only strong downward causation, or do they also reject lawlike independence for macro-sciences? And if the diagnosis is correct, what would count as progress—a distinction among weak emergence (computational intractability), strong emergence (irreducible causal powers), and what we might call structural emergence (scale-invariant pattern stability without new forces); a result from effective field theory; a shift in the framing; or should we conclude the impasse is permanent because it rests on bedrock intuitions about what science owes us?
