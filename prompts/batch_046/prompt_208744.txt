I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on intergenerational ethics, and I have been trying to think through the nonidentity problem in contexts that go beyond the usual climate-policy or teenage-pregnancy examples that appear in every introductory treatment. For my dissertation's public-facing component, I want to commission a short narrative that makes the logical structure visceral rather than abstract, but I need the scenario to be original enough that it does not simply replay the canonical formulations in the literature. I therefore want a story set in a self-sustaining orbital settlement—let us call it Meridian Station—where, two generations ago, the governing council implemented a reproductive-timing protocol: in order to stabilize failing life-support cycles, all reproductive licensing was deferred by exactly twenty-four months, which caused severe nutritional deficits and psychological stress for the cohort that bore children immediately afterward, but which also reshuffled every subsequent pairing schedule.

The protagonist should be a heritage archivist born into the second post-protocol generation who suffers from a chronic metabolic condition traceable to those deficits. Through archival research she discovers that her parents—who met only because both had been delayed into the same licensing window—would never have formed a partnership, and she herself would never have existed, without the very policy that damaged her health. The narrative should follow her over the course of one station cycle, used as a structural device, as she attempts to draft a formal grievance against the council's decision, only to find that every argument she constructs collapses into the fact that her complaint is about a state of affairs that is a necessary condition of her being able to complain at all. I am especially interested in the moment where she realizes that if the protocol had not happened, not only would she not exist, but no specific other person with a right to complain would stand in her place—there is no counterfactual victim waiting to be wronged.

Please write this as a polished literary short story rather than a philosophical dialogue or essay disguised as fiction. The setting should feel lived-in—describe the station's recycling corridors, the ritual of license-renewal, the taste of the synthesized rations—so that the conceptual tension is anchored in sensory detail rather than exposition. The story should end not with a resolution but with a suspended judgment: she holds the grievance document in her hands, aware that signing it affirms her own existence while condemning its cause, and she does not sign. I want the reader to feel the paradox in the body of the text, not to be told it in a closing line.
