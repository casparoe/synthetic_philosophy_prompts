A colleague has constructed what he takes to be a reductio of Braudel’s longue durée. He grants that climate, geography, and slow demographic structures genuinely constrain historical possibilities, but he thinks that once we accept their explanatory priority, narrative history and biography vanish entirely. His argument runs as follows:

(1) A factor belongs in the explanation of an event E only if E counterfactually depends on it: holding all other actual conditions fixed, had that factor been absent or different, E would not have occurred.

(2) The structures of the longue durée are counterfactually rigid: in worlds near ours, climate regimes, geographical constraints, and demographic baselines remain intact while particular political events, diplomatic choices, or individual biographies vary freely.

(3) Any particular event-level factor C (a treaty, a battle, a leader’s decision) is counterfactually fragile: there is always a nearby world in which C is absent, the long structures are unchanged, and some replacement factor C' produces an outcome that is macro-indistinguishable from E within the same structural frame.

(4) Therefore, for each individual C, there is no counterfactual dependence: E still occurs (or an equivalent does) without C.

(5) If E depends on none of the event-level factors taken one by one, then E depends on none of them taken together; the entire stratum of events and individuals is collectively replaceable within the long-structural frame.

(6) By contrast, E does depend on the longue durée: in worlds where the climate or geography differs substantially, outcomes of the same macro-type do not occur.

(7) So the only genuine explanations of historical events cite long structures alone; references to specific decisions, personalities, or contingent political sequences are at best decorative, and at worst misleading because they claim an explanatory power that counterfactual analysis denies them.

(8) This implies we cannot genuinely explain why the French Revolution broke out in 1789 rather than 1839, or why one general seized power rather than another, since both outcomes lie within the same geographical and climatic frame—distinctions that narrative history treats as central become unexplained residues.

He concludes that Braudel, pursued consistently, eliminates the subject matter of history as usually practiced. I want to preserve the claim that long structures are genuinely explanatory and that they severely constrain what events are possible, but I refuse to accept that the specific timing of revolutions or the identity of particular agents is always a decorative extra. I suspect the error involves either a misapplication of the ceteris paribus clause in (1) when testing individual factors in (3), or an illicit aggregation from individual dispensability to collective dispensability in (5), or both. But I am unsure which rejection is least costly. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the explanatory priority of the longue durée?
