I am putting together a taxonomy for a philosophy-of-memory workshop that focuses on the tension between an ethical duty to remember and the argument that forgetting can be a moral or political good. Rather than asking students to identify philosophers by name, I want a decision-tree instrument that sorts actual commitments: each internal node should be a concrete claim about remembrance that a participant could endorse or reject without knowing which label it carries, and each leaf should be either a named position (such as a Margalit-style thick-relations ethics of memory, a universalist moral duty to preserve extreme wrongs, or a Rieff-style defence of strategic forgetting) or marked 'unnamed' if the combination is logically available but apparently unoccupied. The root question should concern whether a community that has suffered a grave wrong owes remembrance to anyone beyond its immediate members; from there, branch through (i) whether the primary beneficiary of remembrance is the victim, the perpetrator, or the wider collective; (ii) whether remembrance requires active institutional preservation — monuments, archives, curricula — or can be satisfied by private mental or ritual acts; and (iii) whether there exists a threshold of 'mnemonic saturation' beyond which additional commemorative labour becomes ethically harmful rather than redundant. You should also inject the non-standard distinction of 'commemorative labour asymmetry' — the idea that performing archival or curatorial work carries a different moral weight from merely consuming commemorative narratives — and show exactly where this splits one of the named leaves into two sub-positions. Please flag any pair of leaves that the tree genuinely cannot separate, and identify one branch-combination that yields an apparently incoherent view (for example, affirming a universal moral duty to remember while denying that any institutional form is ever permissible), briefly diagnosing whether the problem is logical contradiction, practical impossibility, or mere unattractiveness. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the instrument should be usable by someone without web access. At the end, say how confident you are in the taxonomy's logical completeness, and explicitly name the branch point you are least certain belongs in the tree. Conclude with a single-sentence summary of the taxonomy's core insight, presented as the very last line.
