I run an ethics seminar for grad students—about 14 people, 75 minutes next thursday. We're on price controls, specifically rent caps and minumum wages, and I want them to feel the philosophical tension in their bones, not just hear it in a lecture. The idea is: you protect people from being priced out, but then allocation shifts to hidden queues, quality cuts, and personal connections, often hurting exactly who you meant to help.

So design a three-round in-class simulation. Materials should be cheap—paper, index cards, maybe some hidden "social capital" cards I deal out secretly. Roles split between providers (landlords/employers) and seekers (tenants/workers), with hidden "need level" cards so not everyone is equally vulnerable.

Round 1: free pricing, let them trade and record outcomes. Round 2: hard price cap with immediate allocation by lottery—no side deals. Round 3: same cap, but now there's a hidden layer I'm injecting, call it "merit tokens." When a seeker gets the capped good, they also recieve invisible merit tokens they can cash in for hidden quality upgrades or faster access, but only if they hold one of the secret social-capital cards. The students shouldn't hear the phrase "merit token" until debrief.

Payoffs shouldn't be real money—use course-participation points or just status markers within the game. The rules should be arranged so that by round 3 the group discovers on their own that the cap created a two-tier market they never intended, with the well-connected getting hidden perks while others wait in the open queue.

In the debrief, map round 1 onto entitlement-style reasoning, round 2 onto Rawlsian protection of the worst-off, and round 3 onto the unintended outcome that undermines both. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—I'm guessing collusion among providers in round 2, or students breaking character when they realize their hidden cards matter. And be honest: which feature of real-world price-control ethics can this not reproduce? Probably long-run supply collapse or macro-level employment spillovers, since we're stuck in one room for an hour. Make it runnable, not theoretical.
