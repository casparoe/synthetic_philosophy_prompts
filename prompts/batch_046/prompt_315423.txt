I'm pitching a three-month conceptual project to a rationalist-adjacent research fund. The precise question: when habitat destruction eliminates an ecosystem, does it reduce, increase, or leave unchanged the net welfare of wild animals, and does the answer depend on treating the ecosystem as having collective welfare that is not reducible to the summed lives of its current occupants? Existing conservation ethics treats ecological stability as intrinsically valuable, while the wild-animal-suffering literature counts only present suffering; neither addresses the population-axiological problem that destroying a breeding ground may kill thousands of present, suffering individuals while also preventing millions of future suffering individuals from coming into existence. I need a proposal that frames this as applied population ethics rather than empirical ecology: a conceptual taxonomy that distinguishes welfare-reducing destruction (where replacement lives are not morally weighted) from welfare-neutral or welfare-increasing cases (where they are), with explicit conditions for each. The method should be conceptual analysis—mapping analogies between ecosystem dynamics and standard puzzles about procreation asymmetry, replacement, and aggregation—rather than field biology. State what a usable deliverable looks like for people deciding on habitat interventions, identify the failure mode (e.g., if the framework collapses back into a standard person-affecting view with no distinctive ecological implications), and give an honest scope assessment for three months of solo work. Don't include a bibliography or literature review by citation; I'll add references myself. The proposal should be rigorous but accessible to a philosophically trained generalist without specialized ecological training.
