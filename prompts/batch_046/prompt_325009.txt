I serve as senior ethics review officer for a national emergency-management agency. Roughly four times a year my office receives proposals that involve deliberately violating ordinary moral norms or legal constraints—deceiving the public, authorizing foreseeable harm to innocent parties, breaching international agreements—to avert large-scale catastrophe. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the dirty-hands problem in political ethics, that my team (non-philosophers with legal and policy backgrounds) can run in about forty-five minutes using only the proposal documents and a standard risk assessment. The procedure should take the form of ordered questions or tests, each with a clear criterion for a sufficient answer, explicit stop-points where the answer is no and the proposal is rejected, and explicit hand-off points where the remaining issue is one of values rather than assessable fact and must be decided by the director rather than the procedure. Crucially, the procedure must include a “residue accounting” step: if an act is classified as genuinely dirty-handed rather than merely corrupt, it must identify the specific moral remainder generated, determine whether that remainder is dischargeable through subsequent restorative or transparent action or instead constitutes a permanent institutional stain, and specify who must formally acknowledge it. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) authorizing deliberately false public-health communications to prevent mass panic during an infectious outbreak; (2) ordering the destruction of civilian agricultural land to contain a toxic chemical spill; and (3) a case where the procedure approves the action but classifies the residue as a permanent stain—explain why the procedure yields this unwelcome result and what it reveals about the boundary between procedural ethics and unavoidable moral cost. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot settle on its own and where human judgment remains ineliminable.
