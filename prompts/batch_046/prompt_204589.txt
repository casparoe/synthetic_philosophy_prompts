A fellow student in my philosophy of religion seminar offered the following reconstruction of the classical Nyāya theory of knowledge (pramāṇa-śāstra): she claims that Nyāya philosophers treat perception (pratyakṣa) as the sole genuinely independent source of cognition, because every other alleged pramāṇa—inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda)—can be analyzed as a complex arrangement of prior perceptual judgments. On her view, inference depends on perceiving the invariable concomitance (vyāpti) between a reason (hetu) and a property (sādhya); comparison requires prior perceptual acquaintance with both the known object and its similar; and testimony ultimately relies on perceiving the speaker's reliability and the grammatical structure of the utterance, after which the content is adopted only if it coheres with what one already perceives. She therefore concludes that Nyāya's canonical list of four pramāṇas is best read as a taxonomy of heuristic aids rather than as four irreducible cognitive capacities, and that the school's defense of Vedic authority via śabda is really a defense of tradition-confirmed observation rather than an appeal to an autonomous epistemic instrument. I suspect this collapses distinctions that Nyāya explicitly maintains—perhaps conflating the specific causal conditions (such as sannikarṣa for perception and liṅga-connection for inference) that define each pramāṇa with a generic empiricist reduction—but I am not sure where the misrepresentation lies or whether her conclusion survives correction. Would a careful defender of Nyāya accept any part of this reconstruction? Which claims misrepresent the school's actual commitments—especially regarding the irreducibility of anumāna and śabda, and the role of direct contact in defining pratyakṣa alone? And does the argument that testimony is reducible to perception actually follow once those corrections are made?
