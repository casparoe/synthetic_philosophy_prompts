A colleague of mine serves on the planning committee for a municipal archive commemorating a 20th-century state atrocity executed not through mob violence but through census registries, rail timetables, and interdepartmental memos—an event whose scale depended on the routine machinery of modern administration. The committee is deadlocked over the narrative architecture of the permanent exhibition. One faction proposes a comparative layout: visitors move through galleries that place this case alongside other bureaucratically organized mass killings, with panels highlighting shared structural features—file systems, logistics networks, and the division of labor that lets perpetrators treat killing as an administrative output. They argue that this framing reveals the danger is not exceptional evil but replicable institutional design. The opposing faction demands an isolated, non-comparative wing, insisting that situating the event within a taxonomy of state violence dissolves its particular moral gravity into a generic sociology of atrocity and implicitly normalizes it by making it one entry among others. My colleague has been asked to cast the deciding vote. She feels that the comparative approach is necessary to show how ordinary modern life can become lethal, yet she also fears that reducing the event to an instance of administrative rationality risks dissolving specific victims into an abstract structural diagram and treating a singular moral rupture as merely one data point in a pattern. She has asked me whether uniqueness and comparability are genuinely incompatible, and whether comparing this event to others constitutes a moral failure or a necessary form of understanding. I am unsure how to advise her. Could you help? In particular, explain whether the insight that modern bureaucratic rationality can enable mass violence is itself an argument for comparative framing or for isolating the event, and whether there is a coherent way to preserve the sense of singular moral rupture without treating the case as metaphysically incomparable to other state crimes.
