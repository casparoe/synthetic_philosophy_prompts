ok so I've been trying to work out my stance on karma as a theory of moral desert and I'm not sure my pieces actually fit together. Here's roughly where I'm at, with maybe a typo or casualness included:

(1) I think karma makes sense as a non-theistic, structural account of desert—actions carry a kind of moral valence that gets expressed in teh quality of experience later on, transmitted across mental continuities without any divine accountant or cosmic ledger.

(2) I reject any robust soul or personal identity across rebirth. What's preserved is at most a causal-psychological thread, not a "self" that literally survives death.

(3) But I still want to say that when someone suffers now because of past wrongs, they genuinely deserve it—the causal thread alone is enough to ground backward-looking desert, even if there's no enduring subject.

(4) At the same time, I think if I encounter someone suffering from their past karma, I should help them, and withholding aid feels morally wrong, not like respecting justice.

(5) I also think the karmic accounting is deterministic—given the past act, the present suffering is fixed, and intervention can't truly erase the debt, only redirect it somewhere else in the system.

So does this hang together? I'm especially worried that (3) smuggles in the very identity I denied in (2)—if there's no self, in what sense is the present sufferer the right target of desert? And there's tension between (1)/(5) and (4): if suffering is the appropriate moral response to past wrong, alleviating it looks like breaking someone out of prison, not like charity. But maybe that's only an apparent tension if we distinguish desert-tracking from overall welfare? Also, (5) seems to make (4) either futile (the debt remains regardless) or contradictory (if helping does erase it). I don't want to just drop one arbitrarily—can you audit which commitments actually pull against each other, what's only surface conflict, and what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me?
