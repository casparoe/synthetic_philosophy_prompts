I have recently begun assisting with an oral history initiative that collects testimony from families whose relatives held administrative or clerical positions in occupied territories during the Second World War, and this work has made the explanations offered for ordinary perpetrators urgently personal rather than merely academic. In a discussion group associated with this project, two fellow researchers—Mara and Jonas—became locked in a disagreement about whether the situational account associated with Christopher Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 or Daniel Goldhagen’s cultural explanation of perpetrators as willing executioners driven by eliminationist antisemitism provides the better framework, and they have asked me to adjudicate their exchange as a neutral bystander.

Mara began by arguing that Browning demonstrates the perpetrators were genuinely ordinary middle-aged family men whose conduct is best explained by situational mechanisms—peer conformity, incremental commitment, career incentives, and the diffusion of responsibility within bureaucratic structures—rather than by any pre-existing deep hatred of Jews. She insisted that Goldhagen’s emphasis on a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism therefore overstates the role of culture and risks rendering the perpetrators alien and monstrous when they should remain uncomfortably familiar.

Jonas replied that this mischaracterizes Goldhagen, who does not deny situational pressures but contends they are insufficient to explain the degree, enthusiasm, and voluntary nature of the killing—particularly actions like participating in unsanctioned Jew hunts or shooting children without direct orders. Jonas noted that Browning’s own evidence reveals a small minority consistently refused without suffering catastrophic punishment, which suggests that situational pressure was not determinative and that individual or cultural variation must therefore carry real explanatory weight.

Mara then introduced a distinction she considered decisive: there is a difference between dispositional willingness, meaning a standing psychological desire to harm, and occasional willingness, meaning the ability to overcome moral resistance in a specific context through alcohol, group dynamics, or framing. She argued that Goldhagen needs the former to make his cultural claim distinctive, but that Browning’s data supports only the latter, and that occasional willingness remains primarily situational in origin. If all we observe is that people can be made to kill under pressure, she said, we have not shown that culture made them uniquely ready to do so.

Jonas answered that this distinction actually strengthens Goldhagen’s position rather than undermining it. He pointed out that explaining occasional willingness requires accounting for why the moral aversion was so weak in the first place compared to other populations or other crimes, and that explanation is precisely the long-term cultural dehumanization Goldhagen describes. Without that cultural background, Jonas argued, the situational mechanisms Browning identifies would not have encountered such fragile resistance. He also pressed Mara on the refusal rate: if behavior were purely driven by external situation, one might expect either near-uniform compliance or near-uniform refusal, yet the actual pattern was a small minority of resisters amid broad participation, which looks more like variation in underlying disposition or cultural internalization than like a uniform response to identical pressure.

Neither has convinced the other, and I find myself genuinely divided. Could you walk through their exchange move by move—rating each of Mara’s and Jonas’s interventions from clearly mistaken to clearly right, explaining briefly where each succeeds or fails, and then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both are missing?
