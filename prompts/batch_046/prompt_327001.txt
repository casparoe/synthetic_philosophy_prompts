I'm giving the opening remarks tomorrow at a small workshop called Fault Lines — it's half the engineering team that just deployed those autonomus harbor-pilot tugs in the Oslofjord pilot program, and half are philosphers of tech plus partners who came for the free dinner and don't know a neural net from a fishing net. The idea I want to actually structure the talk around is the responsibility gap: these tugs have no captain, the hull company blames the navigation vendor, the vendor points to a federated learning pool, and when something goes wrong there's genuinely no one person or firm you can point to. I don't want to decorate the speech with this; it has to shape what I can and can't say. I can't offer the engineers a comforting line about 'just being more careful,' since in this architecture carefulness isn't localized like that. And I can't tell the partners there's a clear chain of command they can trust — there isn't, and pretending otherwise would be false. So I need remarks that land for both halves without false reassurance or pure techno-pessimism.

I'm anchoring it on a near-miss from the pilot — not a public disaster, just the documented cascade where three modules disagreed on right-of-way in fog and the human override wasn't triggered because each vendor's interface showed green. They talk about it in their safety reports. I want to use that as the carrying example, but I also want to inject something slightly off the beaten path: frame the gap not just as missing blame, but as a kind of distributed absence that calls for a new social practice — maybe a collective apology protocol or shared audit ritual — rather than just a code patch. I don't need citations or a reading list for this occassion; no one can look things up live, and the engineers won't sit through bibliography. But the philosophical point should do real structural work: the speech needs to build to the idea that recognizing the gap is a design requirement, not an ethics afterthought.

Make it work for the philosphers in the room so the concept isn't dumbed down, but also for the family members who need to see why 'who's responsible?' is the wrong first question.
