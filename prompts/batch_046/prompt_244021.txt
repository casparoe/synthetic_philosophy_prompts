A colleague recently shared with me a summary they encountered in an online essay concerning David Lewis's Principal Principle. They paraphrased it as follows:

"The Principal Principle states that if one knows the objective chance of an event is x, one's rational credence in that event should be x. Lewis introduced this within his Humean account of law and chance, where objective chances supervene on the best systematisation of actual-world regularities, with no dependence on belief. He then extended the principle to centred worlds—worlds indexed to a particular location and time—thereby formulating the New Principal Principle, which requires deference to self-locating chances as well. Yet this exposes a concealed circularity: because the best system is partly chosen for its simplicity and strength, and rational agents may weigh those virtues differently, the resulting 'objective' chances are not genuinely mind-independent. Consequently, deferring to objective chance amounts to deferring to one's own theoretical commitments, and Lewis's principle ultimately collapses into subjective Bayesianism with an additional label."

I am unsure how far this can be trusted. Could you provide a careful accuracy audit? Specifically, please distinguish: (a) the elements that are accurate or acceptable simplifications of Lewis's view; (b) the subtle misrepresentations, misattributions, or conflations; and (c) whether the conclusion—that the principle collapses into subjective Bayesianism—follows from the parts that are correct. I should also be grateful for an explanation of what a defender of Lewis would object to here, and whether the appeal to centred worlds materially affects the evaluation. Please respond in British English, with a point-by-point structure rather than a broad impression.
