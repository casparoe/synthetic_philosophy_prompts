A municipal ethics body—the Committee on Non-Reducible Urban Goods—has issued four rulings that I want tested for consistency. First, they permitted relocating a traditional communal bathhouse to a new district, but only if every original stone, water pipe, and spatial proportion was reproduced exactly; they held that location is fungible but material-formal identity is not. Second, they blocked digitizing an oral-history archive that required live, intergenerational storytelling in specific seasonal gatherings, ruling that digital reach multiplies access but annihilates the practice’s embodied value, and that reach is not a proxy for the good in question. Third, they approved funding a near-extinct craft apprenticeship that produces no market goods, on the grounds that its value depends on a master-apprentice relation sustained over decades with no economic return, and that this institutional fragility creates a protective obligation not shared by more resilient goods. Fourth, they required that a climate-adaptive housing project and an existing acoustic heritage site—whose unique reverberation depends on specific uninsulated wall materials—occupy separate zones with separate governance, refusing both integration and substitution with a simulated replica.

These rulings seem jointly consistent with at least two principles. One is formal-condition preservation: the specific material, relational, or spatial conditions that constitute an incommensurable good are non-negotiable, while location, scale, and economic efficiency are negotiable. The other is institutional-fragility protection: goods that depend on fragile, non-substitutable human practices deserve protective priority and structural separation, regardless of whether their literal formal conditions are preserved in every detail.

The new case is this: the city proposes consolidating its restorative-justice circles and its formal adversarial courts into a single plural justice center with shared judges, unified case management, and adjacent rooms—defendants could choose either track, but both would operate under one administrative culture. Restorative circles depend on informal, non-hierarchical, emotionally open interaction; adversarial courts depend on formal role separation, procedural rigidity, and emotional restraint. Proponents argue consolidation improves accessibility and reduces waste; critics say the administrative culture of one will generate axiological friction—an unavoidable corruption of the other’s conditions—even with physical adjacency rather than full integration.

What should the committee decide if it wants to be consistent with its own record? Identify the closest analog among the four rulings, state what each of the two candidate principles predicts about consolidation, and give your best guess at the actual verdict with the reason for preferring that principle. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, specify exactly what principle the committee would be adopting with each possible choice. Then propose the single further case—involving two specific incommensurable values and one concrete structural decision—that would best distinguish formal-condition preservation from institutional-fragility protection.

Please treat this as a reasoning exercise, not a request for legal citation or institutional history. If my framing rests on a confused premise—for example, if the distinction between the two principles collapses under analysis, or if axiological friction is defined in a way that makes the consolidation case incoherent—say so plainly rather than complying politely.
