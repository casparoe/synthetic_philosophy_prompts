Buddhist anatta denies that there is a permanent, unitary atman underlying the five skandhas—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—which are instead impermanent and dependently originated. This is structurally parallel to Parfit's reductionism, where personal identity is analysed into psychological continuity, or Relation R: overlapping chains of mental states without any further fact. Both positions reject a Cartesian ego and substitute a bundle of processes.

There is, however, a crucial divergence. Parfit maintains that Relation R provides a form of quasi-substantial continuity that justifies special prudential and moral concern for one's future self; he therefore retains a normative framework for diachronic ownership, even if he denies that identity is what matters. Buddhism, by contrast, holds that pratītyasamutpāda reveals all phenomena as mechanistically interdependent, so karma operates without any owner whatsoever. Consequently, anatta entails that there is literally no subject to whom past actions can be ascribed; moral responsibility is merely a conventional fiction (sammuti-sacca) with no ultimate reality. Thus, while Parfit reduces the self but preserves rational concern across time, the Buddhist view rejects diachronic ownership entirely, making it a more thoroughgoing eliminativism about moral agency.
