I am developing an essay on Émilie du Châtelet’s conception of happiness, tentatively organized around what I call “dynamic monadic equilibrium.” In her Discourse on Happiness, she seems to reject both Stoic suppression of passion and a Lockean associationist reduction of pleasure to the absence of pain, arguing instead that happiness is an active, sustained harmony between intellectual clarity and affective engagement. My central interpretive claim is that this harmony is not merely psychological but metaphysically structured: on my reading, her Leibnizian commitments imply that the soul’s well-being tracks the distinctness of its perceptions, yet she insists—against a purely intellectualist reading of Leibniz—that passion is not an obstacle to happiness but a necessary modality through which the monad achieves relational coherence with other monads and with the divine order. I therefore propose that her account amounts to a relational ontology of well-being, in which happiness is neither an internal property of an isolated subject (as in contemporary subjective well-being research) nor an objective state of virtue (as in Aristotelian eudaimonia), but rather a dynamic equilibrium sustained through the soul’s active participation in a pre-established harmonious order. I also want to argue that her pairing of study and love as co-constitutive conditions for happiness introduces a gendered philosophical voice that frames intellectual labor not as an escape from embodiment but as a particular mode of embodied relationality.

I would welcome your critical comments on three specific dimensions. First, is my attribution of a “relational ontology” to du Châtelet defensible, or does this framing risk anachronistically importing conceptual machinery from later metaphysical debates? Second, does my contrast with Locke and with modern well-being studies genuinely illuminate her position, or does it obscure the specifically early-modern theological context within which she writes? Third, I am considering extending the argument to suggest that her view implicitly resists a form of environmental determinism—namely, that happiness cannot be reduced to external conditions because it depends on the soul’s active perceptual organization—but I worry this stretches the text beyond what it can bear. Is this extension conceptually plausible in light of her metaphysics, or should it be abandoned?

Please respond with comments aimed at sharpening the conceptual architecture and historical plausibility of the essay. I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations or a survey of the secondary literature; I am interested in whether the interpretive moves hold together and whether the niche framing I have introduced distorts or clarifies her view.
