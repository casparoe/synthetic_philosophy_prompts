I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been trying to understand a design pattern I see in secular liberal societies: the state runs ethics education and civic norms as universal, rational, and non-sectarian, yet it often treats religious communities as irreplaceable backend infrastructure for deep moral commitment, addiction recovery, and long-term social trust. Meanwhile, empirical comparisons of religious and secular populations—charitable giving rates, volunteer retention, survey-based trust metrics—are used both to claim that religion makes people more moral and to claim that secular societies can replicate the same outputs through different mechanisms.

What philosophical commitments does this dual-layer institutional architecture presuppose?

Specifically, I want to know whether the practice implicitly treats "being more moral" as a measurable functional property—like reliability or throughput—that can be compared across populations regardless of what produces it, or whether it smuggles in a normative theory about what moral improvement actually consists in. Does the separation of public ethical education from religious moral formation assume that reason can specify norms but only transcendent commitment can sustain them? Or can a control-based, motivation-only view explain why secular institutions handle the interface layer while religious ones handle the persistence layer?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of value, responsibility, and trust that makes this division of labor coherent. Is the system actually consistent, or does its public layer depend on one view of morality (behavioral and metric-driven) while its subcontracted layer depends on an incompatible one (that genuine morality requires metaphysical grounding)? And is the practice philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories—or does it strictly rule out the view that secular societies are fully self-sufficient for both norm-specification and motivational infrastructure?

Finally, tell me what would have to change in institutional design if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforwardly secular-sufficient model: would civic education need to be rebuilt, or is the current outsourcing pattern merely a contingent historical artifact that carries no deep philosophical commitment? I am not asking for empirical studies, bibliographies, or reading lists—just the reconstructed philosophical architecture, with attention to where its parts depend on compatible or incompatible theories.
