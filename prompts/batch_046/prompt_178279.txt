I'm trying to pin down my view on moral sainthood before I write a paper and I'm not sure these pieces fit together. Here is where I stand:

1. I basically buy Wolf's point: the moral saint whose every decision is optimized for moral output ends up crowding out friendship, play, and aesthetic experience, and that's a genuine loss—not just a different lifestyle.
2. But I also think moral reasons are structurally overriding in a strong sense: if I can prevent serious harm at the cost of a personal project, the moral reason doesn't just outweigh, it dominates.
3. I don't think anyone is morally required to become a saint. Supererogation is real; falling well short of maximal productivity is permissable.
4. I think real moral motivation has to be intrisic—acting for duty's sake—not strategic or reputation-driven.
5. I think human flourishing (eudamonia) is not seperate from moral excellence; you can't genuinely flourish while systematically neglecting moral demands, though I also worry about an "optimization trap" where treating every choice as calculus makes personal relationships irreversibly instrumental.

Do these hang together? Specifically, I'm worried that (2) and (1) pull in opposite directions: if moral reasons always dominate, then the saint who sacrifices art and friendship is just reasoning correctly, so why call it a loss? And (3) seems hard to square with (2) if domination aggregates across a life. Meanwhile, (4) and (5) might seem to clash with (1) if the saint is the one who truly flourishes, and the optimization-trap idea adds another layer I'm not sure fits. But maybe some of these are only apparently in tension. Can you audit the pairwise tensions, rank them by severity, and say for each what'd have to give? I want specific conflicts, not a general lecture on Wolf. Also, is there one revision that'd clean up the most mess with the least cost?
