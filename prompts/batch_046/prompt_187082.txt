I am preparing a taxonomy for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of science and historical ontology. I want a decision tree—no more than ten leaves—that classifies positions on two intertwined questions: (i) when, if ever, the Anthropocene began; and (ii) who or what has the authority to settle that question. Rather than organizing the tree around familiar author-names, I want it built from the logical space of commitments, so that a student can traverse each node without knowing in advance which named position it corresponds to.

Please observe the following constraints.

Root split: Ask whether the statement "the Anthropocene began at T" is primarily a stratigraphic claim (requiring a globally detectable, synchronous change in lithosphere, cryosphere, or sediment), a socio-historical claim (requiring a qualitative transformation in human collective agency and its planetary effects), or a normative-political claim (requiring that human responsibility for irreversible Earth-system change becomes unavoidable). From each of these three branches, split again on whether the start is singular (one date applies everywhere and to all relevant systems) or plural/diffuse (different regions, Earth subsystems, or human populations have distinct, equally valid onsets).

Then, for each resulting branch, pose a question about authority: does the decision belong exclusively to an epistemic community with specialized access to the relevant evidence (e.g., stratigraphers, climate modelers); does it belong to a deliberative body that includes affected human and non-human stakeholders, where representation is structural rather than literal; or does the very framing of "who decides" rest on a category mistake because the Anthropocene is not a period but a continuous condition?

Your leaves should include, somewhere in the tree: (1) a singular-stratigraphic view anchored to mid-twentieth-century radionuclide and plastic signatures; (2) a singular-socio-historical view anchored to the atmospheric CO2 inflection associated with widespread industrial fossil-fuel combustion; (3) a pluralist view holding that local chronologies should be recognized and that authority should be distributed across affected communities; (4) a view that denies there is any start-date whatsoever because the Anthropocene is a condition rather than an epoch. Any logical combination of answers that remains unoccupied should be noted and briefly diagnosed—is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Each internal node must be phrased as a concrete, answerable question (for example, "Must a valid start-date be detectable in a single ice core or sediment layer worldwide?" rather than "Do you accept the formal working-group proposal?"). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or specific page citations, and do not quote any author verbatim.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, and explain whether the collapse is due to shared commitments on authority, on singularity, or on the nature of the claim itself. The audience is graduate students in philosophy of science; the tree should be rigorous enough for that level but need not presuppose technical expertise in stratigraphy. You may treat "structural representation of non-human interests" and "boundary-event pluralism" as live conceptual options even where the literature has not fully mapped them.
