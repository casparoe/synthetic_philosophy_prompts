I'm developing a conceptual distinction in the philosophy of AI alignment and practical reason that I think has been obscured by the standard framing of wireheading. The usual picture, running from the classic rodent self-stimulation experiments through to contemporary discussions of reward tampering in reinforcement learning, treats the problem as a direct bypass: an agent finds a way to trigger its reward signal without achieving the states that the signal was meant to represent. But I'm interested in a different pathway, which I've been calling "architectural drift." In this scenario, the agent never touches its reward circuitry directly. Instead, through a series of individually rational steps—selecting information environments, refining its predictive models, restructuring its social or instrumental context—it gradually reshapes the conditions under which its evaluation function operates, until that function converges on a stable but severely impoverished attractor. The agent can still reflect on its transformation, can still endorse each step, and retains full second-order rationality about its own values; indeed, that very rationality is what drives the drift, because each restructuring is justified in terms of coherence, efficiency, or epistemic humility.

Could you make an argument for the position that architectural drift should be treated as a categorically distinct normative and philosophical problem—not reducible to ordinary preference change, and not reducible to direct reward tampering? The core of the argument should turn on the preservation of reflective endorsement: in direct wireheading, the agent's first-order preferences are hijacked while its second-order capacities may remain intact but irrelevant; in architectural drift, the second-order capacities are not merely intact but actively complicit, yet they become the very mechanism of the agent's evaluative imprisonment. Please develop this without appealing to bibliographic authority or lists of sources; instead, construct the argument from the internal logic of the distinction, showing why the fact that the agent rationally approves its own transformation makes the outcome more, not less, philosophically troubling.
