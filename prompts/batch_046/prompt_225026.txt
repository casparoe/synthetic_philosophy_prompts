I'm organizing a philosophy-of-science workshop for a small reading group—about eight people who've been in rationalist and adjacent spaces long enough to know the standard Darwin and Turing stories but are tired of just nodding along. We have 90 minutes, a table with index cards and tokens, and no slides or lecture up front. I want a structured classroom activity that makes the participants physically experience the two inversions usually called competence without comprehension: Darwin's inversion, where a process with zero understanding produces structures that look intelligently designed; and Turing's inversion, where we build processes with zero understanding that produce comprehension-like outputs, then turn around and use those very outputs as our model for what comprehension is.

Design the rules, roles, and incentives so the group generates predictions and explanations through a mechanism none of them fully understand, then discovers they've been treating that mechanism as an authoritative explanation for the phenomenon it predicts. Specifically, divide participants into three rotating roles: Constructors, who build and run a hidden stochastic selection mechanism (using weighted card draws or token pools with undisclosed weights that even they may not fully grasp); Interpreters, who must produce verbal explanations of an evolutionary trait or a linguistic pattern using only the Constructors' outputs; and Auditors, who score whether an explanation feels satisfying without knowing whether it tracks real causation.

The payoff structure should make it rational for Interpreters to rely on Constructor outputs before knowing the internal logic—award points for predictive accuracy but penalize requests to inspect the mechanism, while Constructors score only when their black-box outputs are adopted into an explanation. Include a timeline in 15-minute blocks: silent setup and mechanism construction; a first prediction round with full opacity; a forced explanation round where Interpreters must speak; a partial reveal of the weights; a second round where Interpreters now explain the mechanism itself using only its prior outputs; and a brief closing reflection.

In the debrief—where you first name the Darwin and Turing inversions—map each phase onto the corresponding move: blind selection producing design-like competence, then the model becoming its own object of explanation. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce: the subjective absence of comprehension inside the mechanism, and the normative question of whether predictive success ever justifies treating a black box as explanatory. Tell me exactly how you will supply those missing pieces in discussion.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: likely the Interpreters will anthropomorphize the Constructors, or the group will collapse "it works" into "it understands." Give concrete fixes—perhaps a mandatory silence rule during the reveal, or a scoring penalty for mental-state language about the mechanism. No bibliography, no citation requests, no reading list; this should run entirely from the basic arguments. Inject the specific niche framing—the tool-becomes-theory loop—into the rules themselves, not only in your explanation afterward, so that participants run into it before it is named.
