I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks tomorrow morning for a workshop titled "Background Conditions: The Frame Problem Beyond AI Planning," jointly convened by our philosophy and computer science departments. The audience is deliberately mixed: AI researchers who work on action representation and automated planning, moral philosophers concerned with practical reasoning under rapid technological change, and several university administrators and donors who made the workshop possible but do not work in either field; the remarks need to engage all three groups without reducing the idea to a decorative label. I want the frame problem—the difficulty, familiar in planning formalisms, of specifying which conditions remain unchanged after an action without being forced to enumerate every non-effect—to do genuine structural work in the speech, shaping both what I can say and what I must leave unsaid. To resist pure regurgitation, please build the address around a comparative framing I'm developing: that identifying the "fixed points" in normative reasoning when we confront unprecedented technologies shares a logical structure with the computational frame problem, since both require distinguishing background commitments that should stay constant from those implicitly available for revision, and both fail if they attempt exhaustive enumeration. The speech should be conceptually precise enough that a planning researcher recognizes the formal difficulty and a moral philosopher recognizes the architecture of practical reasoning, but it should not cite specific papers, invoke obscure formalisms, or depend on bibliographic recall—since the point is to test whether the comparison holds on its own conceptual merits. Structurally, please begin by naming the problem itself rather than the occasion, then use the impossibility of complete enumeration to justify why this workshop proceeds through focused case studies rather than comprehensive frameworks, and conclude by explicitly identifying one background commitment—perhaps regarding the stability of conditions for agency across technological change—that we are treating as fixed for the next two days, while leaving open whether that commitment might need revision if our case studies press against it. The tone should be formal but not stiff, intellectually serious without becoming a lecture, and appropriate for an occasion at which we are also honoring the recent retirement of a senior colleague who first brought these two departments into sustained collaboration two decades ago. Please aim for roughly 650 to 750 words, or about five minutes spoken.
