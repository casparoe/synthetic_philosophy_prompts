As a journalist writing about science and the philosophy of work, I recently interviewed a researcher who liquidated her equity in a biotech venture and now lives on investment returns that render labor unnecessary for generations. Rather than reducing her commitments, she chairs nonprofit boards, advises venture capitalists, and describes any day without measurable output as morally wasted. Her peers admire her discipline; I find myself wondering whether she has solved the economic problem only to remain imprisoned by it. Is there a philosophical distinction—perhaps in Keynes's reflections on the good life once scarcity is overcome—that separates virtuous purpose from a compulsive deferral of living? I am not asking for career guidance, but for the conceptual architecture that would clarify whether her refusal to treat economic security as a threshold rather than a floor is a form of excellence or a captivity to an obsolete problem.
