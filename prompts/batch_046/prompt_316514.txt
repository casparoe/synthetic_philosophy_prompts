I am a high school student working on an independent project about how societies name and classify mass violence. After studying Raphael Lemkin’s original formulation of genocide and the 1948 Convention that followed, I have come to hold that the Convention’s narrower definition—restricting genocide to acts intended to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as such—is not merely a political compromise but a conceptually defensible boundary. I maintain that expanding the category to cover forced cultural assimilation, the suppression of language or religion, or the elimination of political groups collapses the distinction between genocide and related but distinct wrongs, weakening both the moral specificity and the legal enforceability of the term. I want an honest assessment of what could genuinely defeat this view. In particular, I am interested in whether there are cases in which cultural destruction is inseparable from physical destruction—such that destroying a group’s institutions and identity is not just a precursor to killing but is itself a mode of group annihilation that the narrow framework misdescribes. I also want to know whether Lemkin’s broader vision, which included social, economic, and cultural dimensions alongside biological and physical ones, offers internal reasons to reject the Convention’s limits, or whether that broader vision can instead be read as supporting a layered taxonomy in which genocide remains a precise subset rather than an umbrella term. Furthermore, I ask whether the strategic reluctance of states to invoke the genocide label—precisely because it triggers specific obligations to prevent and punish—provides a moral argument for broadening the definition, or whether it shows that legal-moral categories should be insulated from political pressure. Please rank potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish arguments that defenders of the narrow view could concede without abandoning their core claim, and address whether my position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every contested atrocity can be redescribed as falling outside the four protected groups, does that insulation make the view conceptually virtuous or intellectually vacant?
