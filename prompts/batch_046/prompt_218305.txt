I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and over the course of the term our professor has offered a running series of verdicts on which entities qualify as unitary conscious subjects—by which she means not merely adaptive or problem-solving systems, but entities that possess a single, non-decomposable point of view. Her judgments to date are these: yes for typical adult humans, for octopuses, and—though with explicit qualification—for split-brain patients whose hemispheres continue to coordinate through subcortical pathways and environmental mediation; no for individual bees, for slime molds navigating mazes, for current large language models running on distributed clusters, for beehives considered as collectives, and for Portuguese man o' war colonies, which she classifies as tightly coupled federations of smaller subjects rather than one unified subject. She has repeatedly stressed that sophisticated functional behavior, including emergent collective computation, is insufficient on its own.

Next week she intends to discuss Hofstadter’s Ant Fugue scenario involving Aunt Hillary—the anthill that carries on a conversation through pheromone trails—though in a deliberately modified form: imagine a colony composed not of biological ants but of simple artificial agents running identical local rules without any central controller, arranged so that the collective sustains a syntactically structured, self-referential dialogue about its own internal states, with no individual agent understanding any part of the message. The assignment asks us to predict whether she ought to grant this artificial Aunt Hillary a unitary mind, consistent with her prior rulings.

What I need is not a bare yes or no, but an analysis that identifies at least two distinct rules or principles that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, states what each predicts about this artificial colony, identifies which of her precedents is the closest analog and why, and then specifies the further case—perhaps a neuron-for-neuron silicon simulation of a human brain, or a hive of individually conscious bees integrated through direct neural coupling—that would best distinguish the competing rules. If her track record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, I want an honest statement of what principle she would be adopting with each option and which option best preserves coherence with her treatment of the split-brain patient and the man o' war.
