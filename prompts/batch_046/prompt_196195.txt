I've been volunteering at a wildlife rehab center near me and got pulled into a weird policy debate. The center takes in injured native red foxes and spends serious money on them — including one purebred with congenital issues that keeps needing surgery — but if someone brings in a healthy fox-coyote hybrid pup, we're required to euthanize it immediately. The state lists pure red fox populations as protected, while hybrids count as invasive regardless of local origin or health. Meanwhile there's no funding at all for wild-animal welfare outside these protected categories; a suffering common raccoon has zero standing.

What theory of value or species-identity does this pattern actually presuppose? Is it that species are real natural kinds with intrinsic worth, that individual welfare only counts inside a protected taxon, or that tractability drives everything? Could a phylogenetic, morphological, or ecological-functional view of species justify killing thriving hybrids while saving sick purebreds? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy, say whether it's strictly required or just fits well with several rival views, and tell me what would have to change in intake rules, funding priorites, and euthanasia protcols if we replaced it with a population-thinking approach that treated ecological role as primary. I want to know if the practice is philosophically coherent or just bureacratic inertia wearing a conservation label — and whether it even needs one specific biology of species, or if multiple incompatible ones could license exactly the same rules.
