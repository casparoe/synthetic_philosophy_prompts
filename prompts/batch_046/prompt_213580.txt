I was arguing with my study group about Jain logic for a philosophy of religion course I'm studing, and one guy built this argument that anekāntavāda collapses into trivialism. I cant pin it down but I think there's a slip. Here it is:

(1) Anekāntavāda says every claim is only partially true — true from one naya (standpoint) and false from another.
(2) So for any specific claim p, both p and not-p are genuinely true, just in different respects.
(3) If p and not-p are both true, then a contradiction is satisfied.
(4) In classical logic, a contradiction entails every proposition (ex contradictione quodlibet).
(5) So anekāntavāda implies that every proposition is true.
(6) But if everything is true, then nothing is false — which directly contradicts the doctrine that claims are false in some respects.
(7) Therefore anekāntavāda entails its own negation and universal trivialism.

He says this shows the Jain view is self-defeating. I want to keep (1) and (6) if I can, since they capture the doctrine and the problem. But I'm not sure if the mistake is smuggling classical logic into (4), or if (2) misreads how syādvāda uses the qualifier syāt, or if there's a hidden scope shift between (3) and (4). Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Does saving the Jain view require giving up classical logic, or giving up the idea that contradictions are genuinely satisfied?
