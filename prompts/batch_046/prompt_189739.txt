I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about whether honoring sunk costs is invariably irrational, and I left convinced that my intuitions are more nuanced than either of us could formalize. I want to know whether there is a single principle behind my judgments, or whether I am committed to an inconsistency I should resolve.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, the classic ones: if I buy a non-refundable ticket to a concert and then come down with the flu, going anyway to justify the expense is plainly irrational. If a municipality has spent half its budget on a rail line that new technology has made redundant, finishing the project to avoid having wasted what was already spent is also plainly irrational. And if a couple has endured twenty years of unhappiness and stays together solely on the grounds that they have already invested too much to leave now, that too strikes me as a clear fallacy.

But then there are cases where honoring past investment feels not just permissible but required. I promised my dying mentor that I would complete and publish her unfinished monograph; I have now spent four years on it and discovered that its central argument is irreparably flawed. Finishing the work and seeing it into print, despite knowing it will not advance the field, strikes me as rational — indeed, as a fulfillment of commitment rather than a mistake. By contrast, an athlete who trained for fifteen years, suffered a career-ending injury, and now refuses to coach young players because doing so would admit the training was wasted, living in bitterness rather than redirecting his expertise, strikes me as suffering from exactly the fallacy in its purest form. And in the political domain, I think a democratic state that has maintained an international treaty for sixty years, with enormous institutional investment, should not withdraw from it solely on the ground that abandoning it now would render decades of diplomatic capital wasted; honoring that continuity seems rational, not fallacious, even when the treaty is now slightly disadvantageous.

So my verdicts are: going to the concert (irrational), finishing the rail line (irrational), staying in the marriage (irrational), publishing my mentor's flawed book (rational), the athlete's refusal to coach (irrational), and maintaining the treaty (rational). Is there a coherent principle — perhaps one that distinguishes resource-traces, which should never guide present choice, from commitment-traces or diachronic obligations, which can — that captures all six? Or does saving every verdict require an ad hoc patchwork? Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause of it is motivated by which case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Also tell me what it predicts for the case I keep returning to: a scientist who has spent a decade defending a theoretical framework that has since been empirically falsified, and who continues to defend it not because she finds new evidence persuasive but because her entire professional identity is built on this and walking away now would render those years meaningless. Is she closer to the mentor's executor or to the bitter athlete?
