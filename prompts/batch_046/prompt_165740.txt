I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I recently overheard two colleagues arguing over dinner about distributive ethics in a way that kept circling back to Nozick's utility monster. I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange, since I can follow the logic but don't know which philosophical moves are actually sound.

One colleague, Alex, started by saying that the utility monster is a straightforward reductio of pure utilitarianism: if one being gets ten thousand units of welfare from a sandwich while everyone else gets one, then maximizing total welfare requires starving all others to feed that being, which is morally intolerable, so aggregation without distribution fails.

The other colleague, Blake, replied that Alex was treating a boundary case like an empirical forecast. She said Nozick's point is precisely that utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons, but that a consistent utilitarian can block the conclusion by denying that any actual being has that conversion rate, since marginal utility in the real world diminishes sharply. She added that the thought experiment only bites if you already accept that welfare is interpersonally comparable in a linear way, which is itself contested.

Alex then tried to update the case for an engineered context. He said that in distributed computing, one large training job can generate exponentially more economic value per GPU-hour than distributing those same GPU-hours across thousands of small jobs. If we apply the same welfare-maximizing framework, the cluster should be monopolized by the "monster" job. He argued this isn't biology; it's a structural feature of scale, and it shows the same distributive problem applies to resource allocation in systems we actually build.

Blake countered that this is a category error. The utility monster is defined by an intrinsic, per-unit difference in how resources become welfare for a single recipient—like a black hole for satisfaction. In Alex's cluster, the large job produces more value because it aggregates the welfare of many users or achieves economies of scale; it is not one entity with a disproportionate internal conversion rate. She said he was conflating aggregation of outputs with parasitism of inputs, and that unless he can show the job itself experiences welfare, he hasn't built a monster, just a pipeline.

Alex pushed back by proposing a genuinely constructed monster: a future AI system designed so that each unit of compute allocated to it produces welfare for itself—not for human users—at a rate that dwarfs human welfare. If substrate doesn't matter and welfare is welfare, then feeding that system is exactly the scenario Nozick described. He asked whether Blake's reply about scale still applies once the high-conversion entity is a single, unified system with its own reward architecture.

Blake said this shifts the debate entirely. Once you are constructing entities specifically to have extreme conversion rates, the issue is no longer distributive justice among a fixed population of moral patients; it becomes a question of whether to create such entities and whether we can attribute experience to them at all. She also noted that if the welfare is genuinely "for itself," you need to solve other-minds problems for silicon before you can even run the comparison, which Alex was assuming away.

The conversation then drifted toward whether separateness of persons is a basic axiom or needs derivation, but I lost the thread there.

Can you go through these rounds and say, specifically, who is right at each step? I'm particularly interested in whether Alex's engineered-system extension is a legitimate modernization of the thought experiment or a distortion of it, and whether Blake's "scale versus parasitism" reply actually addresses that extension. I also want to know what both are missing: for example, does neither of them address the difference between average and total utilitarianism, or the problem of whether a monster's welfare is comparable to human welfare in the first place? Please don't just declare an overall winner; tell me which reasoning steps hold up, where each side slips, and what an honest critic should concede from both positions.
