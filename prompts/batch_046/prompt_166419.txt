As a retired field ecologist who spent thirty years studying population dynamics in disturbed watersheds, I have recently been appointed to the Allocation and Ethics Committee of the Mid-Atlantic Watershed Restoration Foundation. Our mandate is to distribute a fixed annual endowment between ecological remediation and direct human welfare projects in post-industrial river basins, and I have found—rather to my surprise—that our committee's debates mirror a problem I had not expected to encounter outside philosophy: the tension between giving greater moral weight to benefits for the severely disadvantaged and the so-called leveling-down objection. I need a formal decision memo for our plenary board, drafted in language that project officers and researchers can respond to concretely, not in purely abstract jargon.

The case before us involves three competing pilot proposals operating within the same budget envelope, none scalable without losing efficacy. Proposal A is a broad-scale wetland buffer restoration that improves water quality and recreational access for roughly 8,000 residents in moderately disadvantaged rural communities; the per-capita welfare gain is modest, and the pre-intervention baseline, while poor, is not catastrophic. Proposal B targets intensive soil and groundwater remediation at a single heavily contaminated site adjacent to a severely disadvantaged urban settlement of about 1,200 residents; the per-capita welfare gain is substantially larger, and the baseline deprivation is extreme. Proposal C is a mandated hybrid option: splitting the endowment evenly between A and B, which reduces each project's scope so that neither achieves its primary welfare target—the urban settlement receives only partial remediation, leaving residents severely disadvantaged though somewhat less so, while the rural communities receive only minimal ecological benefit—yet the disparity in welfare gains between the two beneficiary groups is minimized.

Several committee members, drawing on prioritarian reasoning, argue that we should apply a weighted evaluation in which benefits to the worst off count more heavily—what I have come to think of as a "keystone disadvantage" principle, analogizing the moral structure of the allocation to an ecological system in which the stability of the whole depends disproportionately on the condition of the most vulnerable node. Under this weighting, Proposal B is strongly favored. Others object that selecting Option C—deliberately reducing the welfare gains that the severely disadvantaged urban group could have received under full funding of B, solely to ensure that the moderately disadvantaged rural group also receives some benefit—constitutes a form of leveling down: it makes the worst off worse off than they could have been, without any compensating absolute gain, merely to reduce the gap between groups. They contend that if our principle permits this, we have not protected the worst off but have smuggled in an implicit egalitarianism that values equality of outcome over actual welfare.

The memo should be addressed to the Foundation Board, contain a clear recommendation (approve B, approve A, approve C, or approve B with conditions), and be organized with explicit sections: Recommendation, Background, Analysis, and Response to the Strongest Objection. It should treat the "keystone disadvantage" framing seriously—asking whether it genuinely avoids the leveling-down problem or merely disguises it—and should be decisive rather than equivocal. It should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since it must function as a standalone institutional document that researchers can answer on its merits. Please write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a scientific foundation's governance records, and include an honest account of why the losing side might still be right.
