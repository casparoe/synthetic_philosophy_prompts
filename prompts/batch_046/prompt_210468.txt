Two of my friends got into a weird argument about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, not just psychologically hard to avoid. (1) Maya said yes, in a narrow case: if you've trained for a marathon for 18 months, quitting now doesn't just waste the training, it wrecks your "commitment architecture" — you've taught yourself that past investment never binds you, which undermines thier ability to ever stick with long projects. She said the reason isn't the past cost itself, but protecting future agency. (2) Leo said that's just smuggling the sunk cost back in through psychology: the damage to your architecture is only bad because you already invested in building it, so it's still the past cost doing all the work. If you genuinely don't care about marathons anymore, there's no rational reason to finish. (3) Maya replied that Leo assumes a "thin" self that can switch projects costlessly, but real people are diachronically extended — our past commitments are part of who we are, so abandoning them is a kind of self-betrayal with independent normative weight, not just nostalgia. (4) Leo said that sounds like mystifcation: "diachronic selfhood" is just a fancy name for not wanting to feel like a quitter, and feelings of betrayal are precisely the irrational attachments we should overcome. By her logic, he said, someone who'd invested years in an abusive relationship should stay because leaving betrays thier past self. They asked me to referee.
