I've been comparing English translations of short sixteenth-century French lyric poems—pieces in the style of Clément Marot, where rhyme and meter are tightly fixed—to the originals, and I keep seeing the same pattern. A translator needs to preserve the rhyme scheme and the syllable count, so when a literal word won't fit, they substitute a near-synonym that rhymes: âme becomes mind, néant becomes emptiness, éternel becomes lasting. Each change is small enough that reviewers praise it as preserving the beautiful tone, and no single line looks like a mistake. But read across the whole poem, the philosophical claim has quietly reversed: the original seems to defend the soul's survival of the body, while the translation seems to defend consciousness as a temporary material pattern. The form is flawless, yet the meaning has been rebuilt, substitution by substitution, without anyone counting it a mistranslation. Is there an established name for this specific failure mode—where locally defensible formal adjustments accumulate into a global conceptual reversal, and the preservation of rhyme or meter is used to excuse the drift? I want to know precisely how it differs from ordinary domestication, from a single deliberate reinterpretation, and from simple semantic drift; if there is no term, please define its boundaries and say what it is not.
