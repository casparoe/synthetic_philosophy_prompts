I serve on the editorial board of a philosophy journal, and we are seeing a new pattern: authors embed long passages generated by language models, hypothetical interlocutors, or fictional narrators inside their arguments, using framing devices such as “Consider an AI that claims...” or “In the voice of a sceptic...”. We need a reusable referee procedure, grounded in the distinction between frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages (as developed in Hofstadter’s analysis of nested meaning in Gödel, Escher, Bach), to determine for any embedded passage: (1) whether it operates as frame (setting interpretive conditions), outer message (the author’s own claim delivered via a vehicle), or inner message (content not endorsed by the author); (2) whether misclassifying the level alters the evaluation of the paper’s argument; and (3) where the boundary is too ambiguous to resolve without asking the author.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests a referee applies, with clear stopping rules for each outcome. Include a small set of structural or linguistic diagnostic cues that distinguish the three levels, and flag explicitly where human judgement cannot be eliminated. Then apply it to three submissions: (a) an author frames an LLM output with “Here is where the model errs,” then uses that output to support a substantive premise; (b) an author sustains a fictional sceptic’s voice for several pages without explicit framing, then returns to their own voice to reject it; (c) an author presents a hypothetical dialogue in which one speaker advances a view the author endorses elsewhere, but the framing is unclear about whether the dialogue is illustrative or evidential. For one case, the procedure must yield an answer the author will dislike—state which one and why. Conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot do.

Keep the answer practical, honest about misfires, and suitable for a referee with no specialist training in philosophy of language. Start with the bottom line: a one-sentence core rule that assigns argumentative responsibility to the correct message level. Write in British English. Maximum 600 words.
