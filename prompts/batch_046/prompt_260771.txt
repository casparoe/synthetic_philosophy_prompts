I'm preparing a ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have just completed Descartes's Meditations and encountered Searle's Chinese Room argument, but who have no training in formal logic or neuroscience. The topic is Hofstadter's claim that the self is not a Cartesian soul, not the brain taken as a physical object, but specifically the symbol 'I'—a self-referential symbol that constitutes a strange loop within the brain's symbol-manipulation patterns. Don't explain the view to me; design the pedagogical sequence. Specify the dependency order: which concept must be secured before the symbol 'I' can be introduced, and how the Cartesian framework should be engaged but left unresolved at the start. Identify the single carrying scenario—one that exhibits symbolic feedback rather than visual or causal feedback, and that must structurally exclude a homunculus—together with the properties it needs and the ones it must lack. Then map the misconceptions this audience will predictably generate in sequence: that the symbol is merely a label and therefore the self is an illusion; that strange loops require an external observer to complete them; and that this is only functionalism restated. For each, state precisely where in the arc it should be intercepted. Provide one diagnostic question for the end that can distinguish genuine grasp of the loop from mere acceptance of neural complexity. Finally, state explicitly what I should leave out—Gödel numbering, neuronal implementation details, or other apparatus—and declare that I will do so.
