Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning, somewhat belatedly, to philosophy, I find myself perplexed by a reassurance that circulates in discussions of judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma: namely, that "if every member reasons consistently, the collective verdict cannot be irrational." I have heard this deployed both to defend majority voting in committee ethics and to dismiss concerns about the doctrinal paradox, yet I suspect it conceals several distinct theses rather than one. Could you please separate the precise claims that might be hiding within that sentence? I should like a menu of readings: which are trivial truths about logical closure, which depend on assuming that aggregation must be premise-based rather than conclusion-based, which presuppose a particular voting protocol—perhaps one in which premises are decided separately and then closed under logical consequence—and which are flatly contradicted by the possibility that individually consistent judgments can yield an inconsistent collective set? For each reading, please indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or actually false, and tell me which interpretation is typically being invoked when someone uses the slogan to close a debate about whether a jury ought to vote on each element of a charge separately.

In particular, I am trying to assess whether there is a coherent middle path—something I have been calling, for want of a better term, a "nested deliberative mirror"—in which smaller sub-committees first aggregate premises according to a restricted logical framework, and only then does the plenary body vote on the derived conclusions, thereby preserving collective rationality without collapsing into either a dictatorial or a unanimity rule. Would any of the defensible readings of the slogan actually support such a protocol, or do they all implicitly rule it out? I do not require a bibliography, reading list, or citations, as I prefer to trace the logical structure for myself, but I should be grateful if you could present your analysis in British English and keep the discussion at the level of conceptual architecture rather than historical exegesis.
