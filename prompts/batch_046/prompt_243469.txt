I am preparing a single fifty-minute session for a graduate reading group in philosophy of religion that has read Hume on causation but has no background in medieval Islamic philosophy. I want to explain al-Ghazālī's occasionalism—not merely as the theological claim that God is the direct cause of every event, but specifically the structure of his challenge to the falāsifa regarding fire and cotton, and why he holds that observed regularities are fully compatible with God's direct agency at every instant rather than with necessary causal connections in nature. I need a teaching plan rather than a lecture script: the exact dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that occasionalism does not collapse into either miracle-mongering or a redundant gloss on Humean constant conjunction; the one concrete scenario that should carry the entire explanation and what properties it must possess and lack in order to make the metaphysical point precise; the misconceptions these particular students will predictably form given their Humean background—I am anticipating they will conflate occasionalism with modern interventionist divine action, or read it as an empirical claim about hidden mechanisms—and exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted; plus one diagnostic question I can pose at the end to distinguish a student who has grasped the denial of necessary connection from one who has only absorbed the theological framing.

I am also unsure what should be explicitly refused at this level—perhaps the internal distinctions of the Ash'arite school or the modal logic developed in kalām—so that the session does not buckle under its own historical weight. I am inclined to believe that the epistemic and metaphysical strands of the argument must be separated early, but I want confirmation of whether that ordering actually helps or whether it risks making al-Ghazālī sound like a precursor to Hume rather than a critic of Aristotelian causation.
