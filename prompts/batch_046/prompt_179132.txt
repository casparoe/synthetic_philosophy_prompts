You are an expert moral philosopher specializing in collective responsibility, social ontology, and the persistence of institutional identity over time. Before answering, please think carefully and briefly outline the conceptual distinctions you will need—especially between legal continuity, biological membership replacement, normative self-identification, and the conditions for collective agency. Then address the problem below and write your response in American English.

Consider the following five verdicts I hold about whether a collective can bear moral guilt across generations, and tell me whether there is a coherent principle behind them. If there is, state it precisely, identify which clause is motivated by each case, apply it to one further case I have not mentioned, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without revision.

Case 1. A modern democratic state was founded 250 years ago through a treaty that authorized a genocide executed by its founding generation. Since then, the state has adopted an entirely new constitution, replaced every governmental institution, and the current citizenry are descendants of both perpetrators and victims, with no legal continuity of individual citizenship from the founding era except the territorial boundary and the international legal personality. Verdict: The state as a legal entity bears a lasting political guilt, and perhaps a thinner moral guilt, but no current individual citizen bears moral guilt solely by virtue of citizenship.

Case 2. A multinational corporation committed knowing fraud in 1985. In 1995 it dissolved in bankruptcy; its assets and brand name were purchased by an entirely new holding company that replaced all staff, board members, and internal policies. The new entity operates in the same buildings under the same trademark. Verdict: The current corporation bears no moral guilt. The guilt died with the dissolved legal personality, though the new entity may have remedial obligations tied specifically to the assets it inherited.

Case 3. A religious denomination maintains an unbroken institutional lineage—same foundational charter, same sacramental authority, same named office—across four centuries. During one thirty-year period its leadership systematically concealed crimes. Every leader from that period is dead; current leaders were appointed after a thorough reform and have publicly repudiated the cover-up. Verdict: The institution bears a persistent moral stain requiring public atonement, and current members who identify with the institution share in a derivative, non-punitive moral responsibility to repair, even though none are personally culpable.

Case 4. In 2023 a democratic electorate votes 51% to 49% for an environmental policy. Credible experts had warned that, due to unforeseen systemic interactions, the policy would cause catastrophic harm by 2040, but the majority dismissed the warnings as alarmist. The harm was not intended by any voter. Verdict: There is no collective moral guilt—only tragic political error—because the conditions for shared collective agency with a culpable mental state were not met, despite the causal chain running directly through the democratic decision.

Case 5. An open-source software governance collective has existed continuously since 1998 with no legal incorporation. Its contributor base has turned over completely every five years. In 2010 the then-active maintainers knowingly ignored a severe security vulnerability in the project’s charter, documenting their awareness in archived meeting logs but taking no action. The current maintainers have unanimously repudiated that decision, rewritten the charter, and patched the vulnerability. Verdict: Because there is no legal entity to absorb the guilt, the collective bears an enduring institutional guilt manifested specifically as a standing documentation obligation distributed among all current maintainers; no individual contributor inherits personal guilt, but the group’s authoritative self-narrative must permanently include the failure.

These verdicts feel mostly right to me, yet they are hard to reconcile with a simple rule: legal continuity explains Case 1 versus Case 2, but not Case 5; institutional identity explains Case 3 and Case 5, but Case 4 involves a democratic collective with shared decision-making and no guilt; and the form of responsibility differs sharply across cases. Is there a unified principle of trans-generational collective guilt—perhaps centered on continuity of normative self-identification, archival narrative authority, or inherited conditions of agency—that captures all five? Or must I drop one verdict?
