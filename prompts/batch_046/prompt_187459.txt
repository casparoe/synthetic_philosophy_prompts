I am writing from an upper-level course on dependency, moral attention, and the ethics of care. Outside of class, I serve as a live-in companion for an elderly neighbor experiencing early-stage cognitive decline. Last month, her family implemented a domestic-monitoring system that quantifies my work: sensors track response times to alerts, a camera logs the duration of eye contact and verbal reassurance, and these inputs generate a weekly "attentiveness score" that determines both my stipend and my lease renewal. In addition, the system now produces algorithmic conversational prompts—drawn from her biometric stress indicators—that suggest specific emotional responses I should offer during interactions. I have found that complying with the prompts makes our conversations more predictable and improves her metrics, yet when I follow the script I feel as though I am performing intimacy rather than practicing it; conversely, when I set the dashboard aside and simply sit with her in unstructured silence, she seems more at ease, but my score declines and I risk losing housing. The family argues that standardization protects her from neglect and creates accountability where informal care previously had none. I am trying to determine whether adapting my presence to these audit mechanisms constitutes a legitimate form of responsible care, or whether it transforms the relationship into something that can no longer be called care in any meaningful moral sense. I would welcome an analysis that examines whether relational presence can survive continuous quantification and algorithmic mediation, and whether my obligation to the neighbor should outweigh my institutional vulnerability. I am not looking for practical career guidance or family mediation strategies, but for a philosophical account of what happens to the moral quality of attentiveness when it is made legible to a scoring system.
