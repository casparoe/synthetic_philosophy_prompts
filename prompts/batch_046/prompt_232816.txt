I am giving the dedication address for a public library I designed, and I need the speech drafted. The building's center is an atrium with a glass-floored mezzanine that overlooks a reading room, which in turn overlooks the mezzanine; structurally, neither floor functions as the base of the other. Three weeks ago, while reviewing my late mentor's papers after his death, I found his insistence that every explanation and every edifice requires an inviolate level — a ground that does not depend on what rests upon it. I now believe that principle fails both in this building and in the conceptual architecture he used to defend it, because the levels are tangled: each supports the other in a loop analogous to Escher's image of two hands drawing each other. The audience is mixed: structural engineers who need literal reassurance about load distribution, philosophers of science invited by the university who will recognize the reference to tangled hierarchies, and neighborhood residents who will use the library. Write the address — no more than 450 words, roughly one page — opening with the bottom line (the building is safe precisely because its stability is distributed across interdependent levels, not anchored in a single untouched foundation), then moving through the personal discovery and the architectural parallel, and closing with a welcome that avoids claiming anyone watches over the space from an untouched ground level. The idea of tangled hierarchies and the absence of any inviolate level should do real structural work: it should govern which metaphors I may use, allowing foundation only as distributed relation and forbidding it as bedrock. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Do not quote verbatim from Hofstadter or Escher; paraphrase the concept of the tangled hierarchy and the failure of an inviolate base in your own wording.
