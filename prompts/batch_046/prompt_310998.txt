I'm running a fifty-minute tutorial for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have basic logic but no formal probability theory. I want them to understand the problem of old evidence in Bayesian terms—not as a quirk of scientific practice, but as a structural tension in how credence should update when a theory entails a fact already in hand. Build the session around one carrying scenario: a newly proposed unified archaeological chronology that retrodicts a carbon-dating result the research team already possesses, where the question is whether learning that the model entails this result should rationally increase confidence in the model itself.

Plan the explanatory arc: what dependency must be secured first (the difference between observing the fact and observing that the theory predicts it), the order in which the intuitive and formal pieces should appear so the paradox feels like a genuine conflict rather than a formal mistake, and why this archaeological case succeeds where the standard Mercury-perihelion and Neptune examples fail at this level. Identify the misconceptions students will predictably form—I'm expecting "Bayesians must deny all retrospective confirmation," "this collapses into the general problem of induction," and "if the prior for the evidence is near one, conditioning on the conjunction of evidence and entailment is trivially uninformative"—and specify exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Include one end-of-session check that distinguishes understanding the structural problem from merely repeating that old evidence doesn't confirm, and state explicitly which formal apparatus (for instance, Jeffrey conditioning or comparative likelihood-ratio analysis) should be left out and why it would break the arc at this stage.
