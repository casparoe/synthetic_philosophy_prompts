I am serving as a grading assistant for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early Chinese moral philosophy. For the midterm essay, students were asked: "Compare Mencius's and Xunzi's accounts of human nature (xing) and moral development. Specifically, explain how each thinker accounts for the phenomenon of deliberate moral failure in an agent who has received proper upbringing, and assess whether each view treats moral transformation as the recovery of an innate structure or as the construction of an artificial order."

Student answer:

Mencius and Xunzi both recognize that human beings are not born with fully formed moral virtue, but they diverge sharply on whether original nature contains any moral resources. For Mencius, human nature includes four innate sprouts—compassion, shame, respect, and discernment—which are the seeds of benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom. These sprouts are present in every person, though they can be neglected. If they are nourished through reflection and right action, they develop into full virtues; if ignored, they wither, like trees on a mountain stripped of vegetation. Moral failure in a well-raised person is therefore best understood as a failure of self-cultivation: the agent has lost contact with his own heart and has failed to seek what was originally his. Even apparently deliberate wrongdoing is ultimately explained by this neglect, because once the sprouts are fully developed, they determine the will so thoroughly that acting against them becomes psychologically impossible.

Xunzi holds the opposite view: human nature is oriented toward sensory satisfaction and personal profit. Left unregulated, these tendencies produce conflict over scarce goods, leading to social chaos. Ritual and righteousness are therefore artificial constructions—what Xunzi calls wei—that impose an external order on these raw tendencies. Through prolonged study and practice under a teacher, the heart gradually learns to take pleasure in ritual, but the underlying nature never changes; it is only channeled and restrained. In this framework, moral failure in someone with good upbringing occurs when the artificial structure has not been fully internalized: the old tendencies resurface because ritual has suppressed them without eradicating them.

Despite these differences, I think the metaphysical gap between the two thinkers is smaller than it first appears. Both agree that moral development requires sustained education and habitual practice. Moreover, Mencius's idea of nourishing the flood-like qi is functionally identical to Xunzi's claim that long study makes the heart find ease in ritual. In both cases, the agent reaches a state in which moral action feels spontaneous and natural. The only real disagreement is pedagogical and directional: Mencius believes we should begin with internal feelings and grow outward, whereas Xunzi believes we should begin with external rules and work inward. Once we recognize that both describe the same process of habituation approached from opposite ends, their disagreement about whether nature is good or bad collapses. Consequently, the case of deliberate evil in a well-raised person is explained identically by both accounts—the cultivation was simply incomplete, leaving either the sprouts unnourished or the ritual structure unabsorbed.
