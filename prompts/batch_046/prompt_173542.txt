In intensive-care medicine and organ-procurement law, a patient can be declared dead by neurological criteria—irreversible loss of all brain function including the brainstem—while the heart continues to beat with ventilatory support, or by circulatory criteria—irreversible cessation of cardiac and respiratory function—usually after a waiting period of two to five minutes during which spontaneous autoresuscitation is treated as impossible. Yet the institutional architecture treats these two pathways asymmetrically: once brain death is pronounced, procurement can proceed immediately even though circulation is mechanically maintained, whereas after circulatory death is pronounced, procurement must observe a “no-touch” interval whose length is calibrated partly by organ-viability windows rather than solely by biological certainty of irreversibility, despite documented instances of delayed autoresuscitation beyond standard waits. The asymmetry deepens when families refuse donation: in many jurisdictions the hospital continues mechanical support after a brain-death declaration until cardiac arrest occurs, then declares death by circulatory criteria, as though the patient had been alive during the interval despite having already satisfied the neurological standard.

What theory of death—or of the relation between biological status and legal status—does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics: is death the loss of integrated organismic functioning, the irreversible loss of consciousness or personhood, a conventional legal boundary that serves procurement logistics, or a hybrid that shifts with institutional context? Say whether the protocol strictly requires one of these views or is philosophically underdetermined, and identify which specific elements—the immediate procurement pathway after brain death, the viability-sensitive wait after circulatory arrest, the dual-declaration route for objecting families—are compatible with which theories and which generate tension. Finally, describe what an end-of-life and procurement policy built on a purely consciousness-based account, or on a purely biological-continuity account, would have to change, and whether either revision could preserve both the dead-donor rule and the practical demands of transplantation without collapsing the distinction between the two criteria.
