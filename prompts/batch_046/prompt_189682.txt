I'm leading a ninety-minute session for my school's philosophy reading group—mostly eleventh and twelfth graders with no training in classics—on how biography functions as moral education, specifically the tension between Plutarch's comparative method in the Parallel Lives and the flattening effect of hagiography. Rather than surveying virtue ethics generally, I want to teach one structural claim: that moral learning from a life requires what we might call exemplary imperfection, meaning the subject's documented failures must remain narratively visible for virtue to be pedagogically legible, because hagiographic erasure doesn't elevate the figure but collapses the narrative distance the reader needs for self-comparison.

Plan the session's dependency arc: which conceptual distinction must be secured first—perhaps between admiring a life and imitating a character, or between moral exemplification and direct moral instruction—before the claim about exemplary imperfection can even be heard; which single paired comparison from Plutarch should serve as the carrying case, what specific episode within it must be included and what biographical detail must be deliberately excluded so the example neither becomes overly heroic nor sordidly disillusioning; the misconceptions students will form in sequence—likely that flawlessness is the proper goal of biography, that historical distance voids moral comparison, and that identifying with a figure requires liking them—and the precise point in the arc to intercept each; a closing diagnostic question that reveals whether they have grasped the structural point about narrative distance rather than merely expressing a preference for realistic heroes; and what standard scholarly apparatus—the full comparative architecture of the Parallel Lives, reception history in early modern hagiography, or modern virtue-ethics literature—I should explicitly bracket and name as outside our scope.
