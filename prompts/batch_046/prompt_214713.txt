I’ve been thinking about how non-classical epistemologies might be operationalized in high-stakes decision architectures, particularly after a long thread on rationalist forums about whether Bayesian updating implicitly assumes a single true world-state. This led me back to Jain anekāntavāda—the doctrine that reality is complex and any single proposition captures only one aspect—along with its formal counterpart, syādvāda, which articulates truth through seven conditional predicates, the saptabhaṅgī. Rather than treating this as mere relativism, I want to see it rendered as a rigorous, engineered deliberative procedure.

Please write a short story—around 800 to 1,000 words, in a restrained, speculative-fiction register—set aboard a closed-loop orbital habitat where an emergency has forced what looks like a binary choice: either vent the agricultural sector to save the main reactor, or preserve the crops and risk cascade failure. The protagonist should be a mediator, human or hybrid, who is contractually bound to resolve disputes not by optimizing a single utility function but by applying the seven-fold predication framework. The narrative should show, through dialogue and internal reasoning, how each stakeholder’s claim—“the reactor must be saved,” “the crops are irreplaceable,” “both conditions are simultaneously necessary,” “the framing itself is inadequate,” and so on—maps onto specific modes of the saptabhaṅgī. The mediator’s final arbitration should not collapse into a simple compromise; instead, it should demonstrate what a genuinely many-sided resolution looks like when no party’s perspective is dismissed as noise, yet a concrete, irreversible action is still taken. Avoid treating the framework as mystical or ornamental; treat it as a protocol with its own failure modes, edge cases, and institutional history. I’d like the story to feel like something that could appear in a concept-heavy SF journal—more interested in the logic of the decision than in action sequences or character backstory.
