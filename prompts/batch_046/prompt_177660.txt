In the philosophical literature on moral uncertainty, a long-running dispute has reached an apparent impasse: those who defend expectation-based aggregation treat competing moral theories as discrete hypotheses assigned credences, computing choice-worthiness across them, while those defending robustness-based or precautionary rules reject this collapse, insisting that deep normative disagreement demands decision procedures—such as maximin or satisficing over worst-case scenarios—that do not depend on precise inter-theoretic weights. The standard objections on both sides—problems of inter-theoretic value comparison, the risk that expectation maximization licenses catastrophic outcomes, the difficulty of calibrating credences over incommensurable frameworks—are by now thoroughly catalogued, yet neither camp has substantially revised its position.

My hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about rationality or ethics, but about a hidden disagreement over formal representation: aggregationists implicitly model moral theories as probabilistically weighted total orderings over outcomes, whereas precautionists implicitly rely on partially ordered value structures that allow for irreducible incomparability and do not support unique expectation values. If that is right, the sides may be answering structurally different questions—one about optimal choice under probabilistic belief, the other about acceptable choice under partial knowledge—while using the single heading moral uncertainty.
