In the course of mapping negative theories of well-being, I have been trying to isolate a position that sits very close to tranquilism—the view that well-being consists entirely in the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, with no independent positive goods—yet differs in a way that only becomes visible under a particular phenomenological pressure. I will call the neighboring view "latent-quietism": it agrees that suffering is the sole negative constituent, but it denies that craving, when present only as a dormant structural orientation rather than as an active distress, subtracts from well-being. To test the boundary between them, consider a contemplative state in which a subject reports no pain, no anxiety, and no acute desire, yet retains a minimal, non-affective vector of orientation toward future sensory experience—something like a background inclination without thirst. Tranquilism appears to count even this dormant vector as a remaining deficit, since craving in some form persists; latent-quietism appears to count it as axiologically neutral, since no suffering is present. Compare the two positions by examining which offers a more coherent explanation of why such a state feels almost, but not quite, fully complete. Can the latent-quietist account for that near-completeness without implicitly appealing to a positive good that tranquilism rejects? Conversely, does the tranquilist risk treating every dormant mental structure as a suffering-adjacent flaw, thereby collapsing the distinction between craving and suffering? I am not asking for a bibliography or for named authorities—only for a precise conceptual cartography of where one view ends and the other begins.
