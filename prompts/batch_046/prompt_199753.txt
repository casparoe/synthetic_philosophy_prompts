I've been working through the tight-loose culture framework—the idea that societies vary in how strictly they enforce norms and punish deviation, and that this variation tracks ecological threat and institutional strength—and I've constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to license totalitarian uniformity rather than mere cultural difference. Here are the steps: (1) Social coordination requires that individuals can reliably predict others' behavior across repeated interactions. (2) Reliability requires that norms carry social sanctions; without them, norms are merely suggestions that rational agents can ignore when convenient. (3) But if sanctions are probabilistic, context-dependent, or applied with discretion, then prediction becomes probabilistic, which undermines the very reliability that coordination demands—agents need to know not just that deviation is usually punished, but that it is punished in this case. (4) Therefore, genuinely coordinative norms must be enforced uniformly and without exception in every context where they apply. (5) Now, every functioning culture distinguishes domains—religious ritual, economic exchange, intimate family life, artistic creation—where different normative codes apply. (6) If domain-specific norms are themselves subject to contextual exceptions (for instance, if politeness rules differ between a funeral and a tavern), then by (4) they fail to coordinate within their domains, since agents cannot predict which sub-code governs. (7) So either a society eliminates domain distinctions and enforces one uniform behavioral code everywhere, or it develops a recursive hierarchy of exceptionless meta-rules governing precisely when each domain-norm applies. (8) The latter exceeds human cognitive capacity and learning time. (9) Hence any society serious about coordination should suppress domain differentiation, eliminate contextual flexibility, and enforce maximal tightness across all settings.

The conclusion is clearly wrong—healthy societies navigate multiple normative domains gracefully, and excessive tightness stifles the innovation that long-term coordination sometimes requires—but I want to know exactly where the reasoning breaks. Is the mistake in (3), treating statistical predictability as insufficient when good-enough reliability might satisfy coordination? Or is there a hidden equivocation in (4) between logical deduction and social expectation—between a norm that allows no exceptions and one that allows no unpredictable exceptions? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (2), since they seem almost definitional, but I am open to revising (3) if doing so requires giving up the stronger claim that coordination demands perfect predictability rather than merely robust, learnable patterns. If (4) is the problem, what is the correct relationship between uniformity of sanction and flexibility of context?
