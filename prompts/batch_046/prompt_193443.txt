Read David Mitchell's Cloud Atlas through the lens of Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, but with a precise twist: treat the novel's six nested narratives not as reincarnation or karmic recycling but as a test of whether the same life can recur with only structural, not token, identity across epochs. The protagonists share a comet-shaped birthmark, recurring motifs, and moral dilemmas, yet they bear different names, live in different centuries, and face altered circumstances. Does this amount to eternal recurrence at all, or does it collapse into mere thematic rhyme? Focus especially on the Frobisher section, where artistic creation is copied and distorted across generations, and on the post-apocalyptic Sonmi-451 section, where manufactured clones live nearly identical lives until one breaks the pattern. Ask whether the novel ultimately affirms amor fati—loving one's fate as necessary repetition—or whether it treats repetition as an oppressive cycle that must be shattered to achieve freedom.
