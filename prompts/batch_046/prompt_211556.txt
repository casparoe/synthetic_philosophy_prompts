Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy of mind and cognitive science seminar of about 16 graduate students that makes participants feel the force of concept-induction underdetermination in the style of Bongard problems, staged as a live social game rather than a solitary puzzle. Specify the setting, group size, available time, and what participants should come away seeing: specifically, that negative examples constrain a concept in a different logical direction than positive examples extend it, and that group agreement on a verbal rule can mask radically different understandings of the hidden generator that produced the examples. Structure the activity as follows. Divide the class into four groups of four. Give each group a sealed envelope with sixteen index cards describing simple geometric shapes—eight marked positive, eight marked negative—but with a hidden structural twist: two groups are working from cards generated by an intensional rule (the shape contains exactly one enclosed region that touches the border) and two groups from cards generated by an extensional rule (the shape is symmetric about at least one axis with no enclosed regions). Do not reveal which type each group has; they see only their cards. Assign each group three rotating roles: a Proponent who drafts a candidate rule, a Skeptic who must articulate an alternative rule that also fits every card seen so far, and a Recorder who logs each broken rule and the card that broke it. Use a non-monetary payoff scheme: groups earn one consensus point only when all four members sign a single rule, but they lose a point each time the facilitator reveals a new card at 15-minute intervals that violates their current rule. After 45 minutes, the facilitator introduces an ambiguous test card that satisfies both the intensional and extensional rules but violates the hidden generator's actual intent for each group's assigned type, forcing a split decision.

The debrief should map specific lived moments—the first broken rule, the Skeptic's successful counter-proposal, the crisis at the ambiguous card—onto the philosophical distinction between pattern-fitting and concept-capture, and should explain why negative examples function as constraints rather than mere absences. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run: groups will converge too quickly on superficial features like color or roundness, the Skeptic role will feel adversarial rather than constructive unless the facilitator frames it as cooperative stress-testing, and at least one group will split rather than discuss when the ambiguous card appears; provide concrete facilitation moves to handle each failure. Include an honest note about which feature of Bongard-style induction this activity cannot reproduce: the cross-domain analogical leap that allows a human solver to import relational structure from an entirely different domain is absent here, since all cards share the same visual vocabulary, so the facilitator must supply that dimension in closing discussion if the seminar wishes to address it. Write the answer in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask participants to research external sources. Do not use verbatim quotations from specific texts; if any quotation marks appear, they must contain only invented example phrases from the cards, not quoted material from real sources.
