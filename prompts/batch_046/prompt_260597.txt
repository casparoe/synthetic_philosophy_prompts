Hey, I'm hoping someone can referee a disagreement two people in my study group got into about Nomic-style self-amending rules — the kind of thing you see in old rationalist posts about Peter Suber's game, though we cooked up our own twist.

So imagine a hypothetical constitution with three layers. Layer 1 is ordinary law. Layer 2 is the amendment rules, which normally need a 3/4 vote. Layer 3 is a "lockbox" clause saying Layer 2 can only be changed by unanimous consent plus an outside arbitrator.

Then Amendment X comes along. It passes with 3/4 but not unanimous, and it does two things: (a) it lowers the Layer 2 threshold to 2/3, and (b) it includes a "self-certification" clause saying that if an amendment directly addresses the lockbox, the unanimous/arbitrator requirement is waived for that specific amendment.

My friend Leo said this is a classic bootstrapping fallacy: the amendment uses its own authority to cancel the authority-check that should apply to it. He compared it to writing yourself a check with no money in the account, then using that check to prove you have funds.

My other friend Maya said Leo's missing the hierarchical structure. The lockbox applies to external changes to Layer 2, but Amendment X isn't external — it's an internal self-correction. She said every system needs some conflict-resolution mechanism between layers, and this clause just makes it explicit. She also said if Leo is right, then entrenchment is absolute and democratic change is impossible, which is a reductio.

Leo came back with: if Maya's right, then any clause can declare itself exempt from higher-order constraints just by calling itself a "self-correction." That makes the lockbox a dead letter. He said the only non-circular resolution is through the outside arbitrator, not through the amendment's own say-so.

Maya's final move: she said Leo treats the system like a static logic puzzle, but legal systems are dynamic practices. The amendment's validity isn't determined by abstract logic alone but by whether the community recognizes it. Since the 3/4 vote represents that recognition, and the self-certification clause was part of what they voted on, there's no paradox — just a democratic decision to restructure the hierarchy.

Say who's closer overall and what both are missing, especially whether there's a principled line between a legitimate self-correction and a bootstrapping cheat.
