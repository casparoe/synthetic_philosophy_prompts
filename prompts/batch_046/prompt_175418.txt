Consider a philosopher who applies Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against true moral responsibility and has rendered these verdicts: (i) an offender formed by sustained childhood abuse is not truly responsible for his crime, since he did not author the conditions that shaped his character; (ii) a reflective adult who, through years of ordinary deliberation, cultivates generosity is still not truly responsible for her generous acts, because she is not responsible for the deliberative capacities and values that made that cultivation possible; (iii) a person whose personality is radically altered by an unchosen neurological event is not responsible for actions after the event; (iv) even a hypothetical agent who attempts a single, groundless act of self-creation—choosing ex nihilo to be kind—would fail to be responsible, since such an act presupposes a prior chooser. Now consider this new case: a person initiates a six-month process using an implanted neural interface, transparent to conscious oversight, to restructure her value hierarchy; she initiates each session, reviews proposed revisions, and retains veto power, though an algorithm determines which revisions cohere with her existing commitments. Afterward she donates a large share of her wealth. Would this philosopher judge her truly responsible for the donation? Identify at least two rules that fit her earlier verdicts—one extending Strawson's regress strictly, one allowing that mediated, transparent self-revision can break the causal chain—say what each predicts about the donation, indicate whether case (ii) or (iii) is the closer analog, and specify one further case that would distinguish the two rules.
