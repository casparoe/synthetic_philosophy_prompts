I've been sitting in on my roomate's grad seminar in phil of language and the prof keeps handing out these little vignnettes asking whether someone asserted properly. Her calls so far: you see the cat on the mat and say it — proper; you misread an email saying the meeting's at three, assert that, but it's four — improper, even though you genuinely believed it; you guess there are exactly 100,000 hairs on your head, happen to be right, and blurt it out — still improper; you relay a doctor's diagnosis you don't understand, she's correct, you trust her — proper; you identify a rare bird with a field guide you half-remember, you're right, but you'd have said robin even if it were a sparrow — improper; you read the time off a clock that's stopped but happens to be correct — improper.

Next week she's got a new case: a grad student asserts that chemical reaction R yields compound X. She worked it out through a long derivation. There's one false intermediate step — she misstates a coefficient, but the mistake cancels later, so the conclusion is actually true. In nearby worlds with the same conditions, X still forms, so it's safe; but if X failed to form in some distant scenario, she might still run the same derivation and assert it, so it's not sensitive. She doesn't know about the false step. What's her verdict — proper or improper? I'm trying to tell if she's running a knowledge norm, a safety condition, a sensitivity requirement, or a no-false-lemmas rule, or maybe some hybrid. Which of her earlier rulings best support each reading? And if her track record genuinely underdetermines it, what's a sharper follow-up case that'd split the two strongest options?
