After a long argument with a friend about whether suffering-focused ethics can be operationalised without collapsing into arbitrary clinical judgement, I want to formalise a reusable decision procedure for my practical role. I chair a hospital palliative-care triage panel that meets twice monthly to prioritise scarce deep-sedation and high-dose analgesic resources among end-of-life patients; each case gets thirty minutes, there are no ethicists on staff, and the senior clinician must sign off on every outcome. The framework is negative utilitarianism, but with the specific asymmetry we kept circling back to: present phenomenological suffering carries direct lexical weight; anticipated suffering carries weight only if it is clinically inevitable rather than counterfactual (that is, suffering that only arises if we actively prolong life); and the absence of future pleasure is morally irrelevant. Please translate this into a step-by-step procedure my panel can actually run: the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the stop points that yield a refusal, the hand-off points where the question is one of values rather than suffering-reduction, and the points where judgement is irreducible. Apply it to three cases: a conscious patient with refractory pain requesting sedation; an unconscious patient with intermittent painful episodes who could survive months on basic care; and a family demanding life extension for a patient whose inevitable trajectory involves severe suffering, where the procedure yields an answer the family will reject. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle and where it will misfire.
