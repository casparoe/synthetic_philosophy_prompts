As a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of biology in earnest, I want to test whether the commitments I have accumulated hang together consistently. My position is as follows. First, I accept Gould and Lewontin's critique that adaptationist reasoning often falls into ad hoc functional narratives unless it actively tests for structural byproducts—spandrels—rather than assuming that every apparently functional trait is a direct product of selection. Second, I take the original spandrel metaphor literally in its architectural sense: a spandrel is an unavoidable geometric byproduct of constructing an adaptive structure, not merely any trait that happens to lack present utility. Third, I propose a distinction I have been calling developmental spandrels—features that arise inevitably from the ontogenetic machinery that builds a genuine adaptation, without themselves being targets of selection—as separate from architectural spandrels in the adult form; both fall within the broader anti-adaptationist critique, but only the latter are spandrels in the strict Gould-Lewontin sense. Fourth, I do not think evolutionary psychology is inherently pseudoscientific, but I hold that its reverse-engineering of cognitive adaptations is methodologically legitimate only when it can exclude both kinds of spandrels using comparative and developmental evidence, which I believe it rarely achieves for higher cognition. Fifth, I maintain that natural selection is the only mechanism capable of generating intricate functional coordination—such as that found in the mammalian eye—yet I also think that many human cognitive capacities, abstract mathematical reasoning among them, are better explained as exaptations or spandrels built upon genuinely adaptive substrates like spatial navigation. Sixth, I believe that the historical distinction between adaptation, exaptation, and spandrel is sharp in principle—classification depends on evolutionary history, not current function—even though I concede that for most human psychological traits we lack the fossil and comparative data to apply it with confidence.

Are these jointly tenable? In particular, does my endorsement of the spandrels critique undermine my qualified defense of evolutionary psychology? Is my introduction of developmental spandrels consistent with the strict architectural metaphor, or does it expand the concept in a way that renders it empirically unfalsifiable? Does my claim that selection alone explains intricate design conflict with my view that many cognitive traits are spandrels or exaptations, especially if the substrate adaptations themselves may contain spandrel components? Furthermore, is the tension between my insistence on a sharp historical classification and my admission that we rarely possess the evidence to apply it a genuine epistemic contradiction, or does it dissolve upon recognizing that sharpness in principle is compatible with ignorance in practice? Please order the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether resolution requires abandoning a commitment or merely clarifying terminology, and tell me which single revision—if forced—would do the least damage to the overall framework. I am especially concerned about whether the developmental spandrel distinction introduces a hidden circularity rather than a useful analytical tool.
