Hey, I'm a retired physicist who got into philosophy late in life, and I'm trying to wrap my head around Scanlon's contractualism — the idea that something's wrong if it's allowed by a principle that someone affected could resonably reject. I think I found a loop that makes it either forbid all government or secretly be utilitarian, and I can't tell where the wire's crossed.

Here's my reasoning:

(1) Contractualism says an act is wrong iff it's justified only by a principle P that at least one actually affected party could reasonbly reject.
(2) For big policies — taxes, conscription, climate rules — there's always some tail-risk individual who gets crushed, so there's always someone who can rejeect P.
(3) Therefore, if we apply (1) literally to every affected person, all collective action is wrong.
(4) To escape that, Scanlon must be using an ex-ante version: ask whether anyone could reject P before knowing their place in the distribution.
(5) But then in the classic one-vs-five rescue, everyone ex ante prefers "save the five" because they're likelier to be among the five.
(6) So ex-ante contractualism picks the five, which solves aggregation.
(7) Now apply (4) back to the carbon-tax case: ex ante, almost everyone prefers climate stability, so the crushed factory worker's rejection doesn't count.
(8) So far so good — but notice that (4)-(7) just weigh outcomes by how likely each position is, which is exactly expected-utility aggregation.
(9) That means contractualism either stays literal and collaps into anarchism, or it uses ex-ante probabilities and collapses into utilitarianism.
(10) Scanlon clearly wants neither, so one of my steps must be garbage.

I'm especially suspicious of the slide from (4) to (8) — I might be smuggling in probabilistic reasoning that contractualism doesn't actually need. But I also worry about (2), since maybe "affected" is meant differently.

Can someone tell me which premise to ditch, and what ditching it costs? Does keeping (1) force me into (3), or is there a middle path?
