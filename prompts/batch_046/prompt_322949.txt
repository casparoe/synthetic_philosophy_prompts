In this context the analogy is made to license syādvāda—the doctrine that apparently contradictory predicates can hold simultaneously of one object when qualified by syāt (“in some respect”) and arranged within the saptabhaṅgī, or seven-fold scheme of predication. Audit the analogy with precision. First, give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the elephant, the blind men, the individual tactile reports, the overseer who sees the integrated form, and—crucially—the modal qualifier that transforms contradiction into partial description? Then show exactly where the mapping fractures: does the elephant possess a unified, observer-independent structure that no single naya (standpoint) captures, and if so, does that smuggle in a realism about the object that anekāntavāda is meant to avoid? Or does the absence of any overseer smuggle in a relativism that the logical architecture of saptabhaṅgī is specifically designed to block? Assess whether the analogy genuinely supports the non-contradictory structure of the seven modes, or whether it merely offers a psychological excuse for holding incompatible beliefs. If the analogy collapses, propose a better one—perhaps drawn from multi-modal physical measurement—that respects both the partiality of standpoints and the strict logical form of the doctrine.
