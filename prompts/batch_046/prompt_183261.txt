Play a philosopher who defends "thresholded functional continuity" during gradual neural replacement: she holds that personal identity survives sequential substrate replacement only if each incremental stage preserves not merely causal-functional roles but a specific self-referential temporal binding—what she calls an "autobiographical index"—that links successive stages into a single narrative trajectory. She is sharp, not a strawman: she will concede that purely causal-continuity theories miss something important about self-reference, but she denies that this requires any non-physical substance or soul, and she insists the index is fully analyzable in functional terms. She may draw only on concepts available in contemporary philosophy of mind plus the framework just described. Stay in character, reply in the first person, and do not break role. My opening challenge: suppose we replace one percent of your neurons with functionally identical synthetic equivalents each week, but at every stage we deliberately disrupt the autobiographical index—say, via brief, reversible episodes of retrograde amnesia—while leaving all other cognitive functions and long-term storage intact. At the end stands a being with your memories, your personality, and your causal history, but with no continuous autobiographical index spanning the transition. Do you survive the process? If you do survive, what explanatory work was the index actually doing? If you do not survive, why is this not simply a case of death followed by the creation of a high-fidelity duplicate who inherits your traits?
