I have recently been appointed to a community restorative-justice panel, and our deliberations have made me acutely aware that we invoke "taking responsibility" in two incompatible registers: we treat the offender as the origin of the harm, yet we also treat accountability as a social standing that does not require the offender to have authored their own character. This tension has led me to propose a one-semester independent study of approximately 7,000 words on the following question: Does Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility succeed only against a "source-responsibility" conception that requires being causa sui, and if so, does a distinct "standing-responsibility" conception—according to which being a legitimate target of reactive attitudes depends on relational and institutional conditions rather than on metaphysical self-origination—survive the argument intact?

The existing literature largely treats Strawson's regress either as a decisive refutation of ultimate responsibility, pushing toward revisionism, or as something to be dissolved by compatibilist distinctions between guidance control and regulative control. What these responses leave open is whether the argument's premise—that true moral responsibility requires being the ultimate cause of oneself—already embeds a particular, contestable model of agency: the isolated, diachronic individual as the sole locus of responsibility. If responsibility is instead understood as a distributed social practice, the regress may show only that no individual is a causa sui, not that no one can occupy the normative standing of being held responsible. The question is therefore whether the Basic Argument collapses these two dimensions, and whether separating them yields a coherent position that is neither standard compatibilism, which often retains source-language, nor revisionism, which abandons desert.

My approach will be conceptual rather than historical. I will first reconstruct the Basic Argument with explicit attention to where the causa-sui requirement enters, distinguishing metaphysical origin from normative standing. I will then examine whether institutional analogies—such as the authority of a judge or the obligations of a parent—demonstrate that one can bear a responsibility-status without being the self-created origin of that status, and whether this model can be extended to moral agency without reducing responsibility to mere social utility. Finally, I will test whether the resulting standing-responsibility view can account for the intuitive force of Strawson's examples, such as the wrongdoer shaped by abusive upbringing, without requiring the impossible condition of self-creation.

A successful result would be a clearly articulated framework that either shows that Strawson's argument applies with equal force to standing-responsibility, thereby strengthening the case for revisionism, or demonstrates that standing-responsibility is conceptually independent and can be justified through the internal logic of reactive practices rather than through metaphysical self-origination, thereby carving out a middle path. The project fails if the distinction between source and standing collapses into either standard compatibilism or pure social constructivism, or if it turns out that all normative standing necessarily presupposes some form of origin-responsibility after all. Given the one-semester scope, I will limit the analysis to Strawson's core argument and its immediate conceptual neighborhood, without attempting a full history of free-will debates or an empirical study of restorative-justice practices; I will supply citations myself. The deliverable should be useful precisely because it addresses a live tension in practical moral deliberation—how to honor both the reality of formative circumstances and the legitimacy of holding people to account—without requiring either metaphysical miracles or the abandonment of desert.
