You serve on the clinical ethics committee of a university-affiliated transplant center. A medically cleared, 28-year-old prospective living kidney donor has applied to enter the national paired-exchange pool to donate anonymously to a stranger, but she refuses the center's standard six-month psychological screening protocol. In her written submission she frames her intended donation as supererogatory—morally praiseworthy yet not morally obligatory—and contends that applying a clinical screening designed to detect coercion and psychopathology implicitly treats non-required beneficence as a cognitive defect or risk factor in itself. The surgical team is divided: one faction argues that precisely because the act exceeds ordinary duty, the institution owes heightened scrutiny rather than reduced barriers; the opposing faction argues that imposing a psychological clearance threshold specifically calibrated to altruistic motivation conflates empirical risk assessment with a hidden normative judgment about which motives are legitimate. The committee must circulate a formal decision memo before Thursday's review board meeting recommending a concrete course of action—approve with modified conditions, approve the waiver, or uphold standard screening—with a clear implementation timeline. The memo should treat the donor's philosophical objection seriously rather than reducing it to a non-medical preference, and it must honestly engage the strongest counter-argument: that institutional obligations of non-maleficence and due diligence apply with equal or greater force when a patient-volunteer undertakes harm without any therapeutic benefit to herself.
