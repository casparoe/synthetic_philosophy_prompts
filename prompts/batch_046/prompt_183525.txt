I'm currently preparing a contribution for a seminar on practical rationality, and we've reached the point where Buridan's ass is treated not as a historical curiosity but as a live stress-test for any account of choice. Rather than surveying the familiar solutions immediately, I want to step back and specify the target precisely: what must an adequate theory of rational decision-making under conditions of perfect equilibrium actually deliver? I need the full desiderata—the phenomena it must accommodate, the distinctions it must preserve, the conceptual costs it must avoid—before I can assess whether randomization, second-order volition, or hierarchical preference structures are even serious contenders.

Please lay out the requirements list in detail. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or consideration that makes it genuinely essential rather than a prejudice imported from asymmetric choice contexts, and flag which ones you regard as non-negotiable versus which might be relaxed. Among the necessary conditions: the theory must explain why an agent facing genuinely equal options is not rationally required to starve, without thereby licensing the claim that every arbitrary whim is a rational basis for action; it must maintain a firm distinction between having no reason to prefer A to B and having no reason whatsoever, since these carry different implications for practical and moral assessment; it must accommodate the observed fact that both human and non-human animals routinely break ties in ways that seem neither fully random nor fully reason-driven; and it must clarify whether the act of breaking a tie generates a reason retroactively, operates within a reason-free zone, or reveals a hidden preference. Crucially, identify any pair of these requirements that appear jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether demanding both guaranteed resolution and strict reason-dependence forces an internal contradiction. Keep the primary weight on the criteria themselves and their interrelations; then, only briefly, indicate how the three main approaches—pure rationalist denial of legitimate tie-breaking, arbitrary-choice permissivism, and hierarchical second-order preference models—fare against your list, treating this scoring as clearly secondary to the specification.
