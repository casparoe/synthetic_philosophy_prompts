I'm writing up my notes for a philosophy of emotion seminar next week and want a consistency check before I present anything. I was talking with a friend over coffee and realized I might be holding views that don't actually hang together, but I'm not sure where the crack is.

Here's what I think:

(a) Emotions are essentially feelings with a distinctive phenomenolgy—there's no anger without that hot, pressurized quality, and reducing anger to a judgment about offense misses the experiential core entirely.

(b) But emotions are also perceptions of evaluative properties—fear tracks danger, admiration tracks excellence—so they have accuracy conditions directed outward at the world, not just inward.

(c) I also think there's something like "evaluative proprioception" happening: emotions don't just point at values out there, they also report on the agent's own stance or commitment toward those values. So fear tells me both "that's dangerous" and "I'm treating it as a threat worth avoiding."

(d) This proprioceptive element is what makes emotions rationally assessable—I'm wrong not just when the world doesn't match my fear, but when my own stance doesn't fit the situation.

(e) At the same time, the mapping between feeling and evaluative content is partly culturally scripted—learning to feel "amae" or "schadenfreude" involves acquiring a framing, not just labeling a universal pre-existing state.

So I've got feelings, world-directed perception, self-monitoring, rational assessability, and cultural construction all packed in. Do these actually cohere?

Specifically: is the tension between (b) and (c) the deepest—can one mental state have two intentional directions without being two states? Or does (e) undermine (b), since if framing is learned, how can fear still track objective danger? And is there an apparent conflict between (a) and (d) that dissolves once you see feeling and rationality as compatible?

Please rank the tensions by severity, not just list them. For each real tension, say which commitment I'd need to weaken or drop and what that costs me. What's the least costly revision that keeps the core of the view intact? I want an audit, not a lecture—don't tell me I'm wrong about everything, tell me exactly where the pressure points are.
