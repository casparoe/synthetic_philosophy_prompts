I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy with a small group of former colleagues—mostly biologists and engineers—who tend to treat intellectual history either as a linear accumulation of correct facts or as a gallery of heroic discoveries. Next month I must lead our session on Nietzsche’s essay concerning the uses and disadvantages of history for life, focusing on the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes. Do not explain Nietzsche to me; instead, design a teaching plan for this audience. Specify the order in which the three modes must be introduced and why the dependencies require that sequence; propose one carrying example drawn from a superseded scientific framework—such as the luminiferous aether or spontaneous generation—that can illustrate all three modes without reducing them to mere error-correction, and defend why a scientific rather than artistic or political example suits this group. Then map the predictable misconceptions: I expect my colleagues will conflate antiquarian reverence with scientific data preservation, interpret monumental emulation as citation of founding papers, mistake critical history for simple falsification, and read Nietzsche’s praise of the “unhistorical” sense as anti-intellectualism. Indicate exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted. Conclude with one diagnostic question that reveals whether they understand why an excess of any single mode damages living scientific practice specifically, not just culture in general, and state what elements of Nietzsche’s broader metaphysics should be deliberately omitted at this level.
