I defend a strong version of historical relativism that moves past the familiar claim that victors control the archives. My view is that the institutional power surviving to preserve and classify records partly fixes the semantic content of core historiographical categories—terms like revolution, collapse, or restoration—so that attempting to narrate the same events from the defeated party's conceptual framework does not merely suffer from missing evidence, but involves applying terms with genuinely different extensions and criteria of adequacy. Consequently, there is no trans-perspectival standpoint from which to adjudicate rival narratives; the standards for what counts as explanation are themselves institutionally anchored, collapsing any sharp descriptive-normative boundary in historiography. What would count against this position? I want more than the standard reply that subaltern voices can be recovered through oral tradition or material traces. Identify the specific conceptual cases, the arguments from philosophy of language or sociology of knowledge, and the historical examples that would be decisive versus merely awkward. Which of these would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they reject as presupposing the very objectivity the view denies? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Also assess whether the view risks unfalsifiability—if every counter-narrative is reinterpreted as further proof of semantic domination—and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a fatal vice for a position of this kind.
