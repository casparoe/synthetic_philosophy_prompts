I have been working through Montaigne's essay on cannibals and built an argument that pushes his cultural relativism into self-refutation, but I cannot locate the error. The steps are these:

(1) Montaigne claims that Europeans call cannibal practices barbarous only by comparing them to European customs, not by any rational standard.
(2) He then reverses the judgment: Europeans are more barbarous than cannibals because Europeans torture captives and betray them through deceit, whereas cannibals kill honorably and without artifice.
(3) This reversal presupposes a standard that condemns deceit and cruelty while permitting ritual killing—call it the honor standard.
(4) If all moral judgments are custom-relative, then the honor standard is merely Montaigne's own European Stoic vocabulary projected onto the cannibals.
(5) So either Montaigne applies his own custom's values in violation of his relativist premise, or he appeals to a universal natural standard.
(6) If he appeals to a universal standard, he can condemn European cruelty, but he can equally condemn cannibalism itself, since ritual killing violates universal human sociability.
(7) Therefore, to maintain both that cannibalism is not barbarous and that European cruelty is, he needs a standard that permits ritual killing but forbids deceit.
(8) No such standard is available without importing exactly the custom-specific distinctions he rejects.
(9) Consequently, either his relativism collapses—there are universal moral standards after all—or his praise of cannibals collapses, leaving no reason to prefer them to Europeans.
(10) Yet Montaigne clearly intends both conclusions.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) and avoid conceding that Montaigne is a hidden universalist. Is the flaw in (3)'s assumption that reversal requires a shared evaluative standard? Or is there an equivocation between describing a custom and evaluating it? If I must reject one premise, I would rather reject (6) than (1), but I want to understand the cost.
