I'm working on a project about visual epistemology and recently came across Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE system and his broader conviction that a properly constructed picture language could serve as a universal, ambiguity-free medium for scientific and social communication. This made me think about a concrete institutional practice I have observed: several international public-health agencies now mandate that emergency epidemic-risk communications at airports and border crossings be distributed initially as standardized iconographic panels—without any accompanying textual translation—on the explicit assumption that visual symbols are culturally neutral, immediately transparent to cognition, and free from the interpretive variability that plagues verbal reports.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of picture languages, semantic transparency, and cross-cultural cognition does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it commit the institution to something like Neurath's view that images can carry determinate propositional content independently of a linguistic framework, or does it merely assume a pragmatic correlation between icons and behavior? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits comfortably with it—particularly regarding whether the institution must deny that pictorial interpretation involves tacit linguistic mediation, or whether it can remain agnostic on that point. Then consider whether this institutional reliance on image-first communication is consistent with Neurath's own anti-metaphysical, physicalist stance, or whether it quietly smuggles in a metaphysics of direct visual access to facts. Finally, tell me what elements of the practice would have to change if we replaced its implicit picture-language theory with a view according to which every pictorial representation is already structured by convention, context, and tacit conceptual frameworks.
