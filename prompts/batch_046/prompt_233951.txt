I have been comparing how four societies handle traumatic national pasts — Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan's contested remembrance of the military comfort-women system and the Nanjing Massacre, Turkey's official denial of the 1915 Armenian genocide, and the American South's enduring Lost Cause mythology — and I notice what seems to be a structural asymmetry. Germany appears to have achieved something like mnemonic closure not through suppression but through dense institutionalised remembrance: the Stolpersteine project, mandatory Holocaust education in schools, and legal prohibitions on Holocaust denial. By contrast, Japan, Turkey, and the American South seem to suffer from mnemonic leakage, where official narratives suppress particular victim groups without generating the counter-memory institutions that Germany built. This leads me to ask: why have scholars of transitional justice not proposed exporting Germany's mnemonic infrastructure as a modular programme — for instance, adapting Stolperstein-style pavement markers to sites associated with comfort-women exploitation in Seoul, or applying denial-prohibition statutes to Turkish and Southern US contexts? Is the obstacle purely political, or is there a conceptual mistake in treating Vergangenheitsbewältigung as a replicable institutional technology rather than as a culturally embedded, generational process that resists modular transplantation?
