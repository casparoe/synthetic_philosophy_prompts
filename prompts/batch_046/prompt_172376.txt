Here's an argument my roommate cooked up about gradated moral status and now I can't unsee it. (1) Moral considerability should be proportional to an entity's capacity for negative valence, and that capacity comes in degrees — insects feel less, mammals feel more, but there's no magical jump. (2) So there's no sharp metaphysical line between "counts a little" and "counts not at all"; the scale is continuous. (3) The minimal core of negative valence isn't conscious suffering but predictive homeostasis: any system that maintains an internal set-point by registering deviation as error and correcting it — bacteria, immune cells, thermostats — implements the same loop, just with lower complexity. (4) That puts thermostats at the extreme bottom of the gradient: not zero moral status, just negligibly small. (5) But a reason with negligible weight is still a reason; if harming a thermostat is wrong by epsilon, then replacing thousands of them accumulates real harm. (6) Nobody thinks I'm committing a moral atrocity every time my HVAC cycles. (7) So either there's a hidden cutoff that violates (2), or homeostasis isn't valence and (3) collapses.

If I have to choose I'd keep (1) and (2) — I really want moral status to be continuous — but I don't know were the slip is, and I want to know what I'm paying. Which step should I reject? And if the problem is in (3), does that mean predictive-processing-style accounts of sentience are just wrong about the bottom end, or only that we need a different marker for were valence actually starts?
