I was at a philosophy reading group last week where two graduate students, Mara and Leo, got into a sustained argument about Frankfurt cases and the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. The discussion had moved well past the standard Jones-and-Black scenario and into more subtle terrain that neither had fully mapped out.

Mara argued that even if Frankfurt cases show an agent can be morally responsible without having access to alternative possibilities at the exact moment of action, they do not touch what she called the modal-neighborhood version of PAP: responsibility requires that in nearby possible worlds—worlds that differ only in minor, non-miraculous ways—the agent could have done otherwise. She claimed Frankfurt cases actually preserve this condition, because in nearby worlds Black’s device might malfunction or fail to trigger, so alternatives remain modally nearby even if causally blocked in the actual world. Therefore, she said, Frankfurt’s attack misses the real principle, which concerns modal proximity rather than actual causal openness.

Leo countered that Mara was not defending PAP at all, but smuggling in a different condition—something closer to counterfactual robustness—that Frankfurt-style scenarios are specifically designed to undermine. He said that if Black’s presence makes the alternative impossible across all nearby worlds (because the device is perfectly reliable), then Mara’s modal-neighborhood condition collapses. But Leo then added his own twist: even if Mara is right about modal neighborhoods, there is a deeper problem. In Frankfurt cases, the agent’s reasoning is transparent—the alternative is never a live option in the agent’s practical deliberation—so asking whether they could have done otherwise in nearby worlds misses the phenomenological structure of agency. He proposed that responsibility requires not just modal access but what he termed deliberative openness: the agent’s practical reasoning must be structured so that alternatives function as genuinely live options in the reasoning process, not merely as metaphysically available possibilities in some distant modal space.

Mara replied that Leo was now conflating moral responsibility with a kind of existential authenticity, and that his deliberative-openness criterion would implausibly deny responsibility to people with strong habits or settled preferences, which is far too strong. She also noted that in many Frankfurt-style scenarios the agent does consider alternatives and rejects them for good reasons—so the reasoning is already open, just conclusively settled—meaning Leo’s criterion is satisfied anyway and adds nothing.

Leo conceded that compulsions and pathological cases should be treated separately, but insisted that the transparency of reasoning in canonical Frankfurt cases—where the decision is made for reasons that render the alternative practically unthinkable—reveals that what matters is not whether an alternative exists in some modal neighborhood, but whether the agent’s will is self-determining in a stronger, structurally open sense. He suggested that Mara and Frankfurt share a mistake: both treat responsibility as a matter of what possibilities exist around the agent, rather than as a matter of how the agent’s will is internally structured.
