hey, since i retired from the lab (systems biology, mostly) i've been trying to get into philosphy of art and i keep hitting this wall with the paradox of fiction. radford's setup: we feel real fear watching a horror movie, but we don't belive the monster is actually there, and fear is supposed to need belief that you're in danger. so we're either irrational or not really afraid. there's all these answers floating around -- walton's pretend-emotions, currie's mental simulation, carroll's thought-directed feelings -- but the argument has been spinnning its wheels for decades.

my working theory is that it's not about movies at all. i think the sides are talking past each other about what an emotional state needs to count as 'genuine.' one side wants an existence-commitment, like a doxastic tag that says 'this is happening,' and the other side treats arousal plus narrative framing as enough. but maybe that's just verbal: 'real fear' means one thing to the phenomenologist and another to the functionalist. or maybe there's a hidden empirical question -- do fiction-viewers actually lack the cortical belief-marker that real-threat responses have? i read somewhere about predictive processing treating belief as precision-weighting on prediction errors, and that made me wonder: is the whole paradox just assuming a representationalist model of emotion that neuroscience doesn't actually use?

i want a diagnosis, not another survey of the interpretations. is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or about different standards of evidence? do they differ on facts, on what counts as data, or on starting intuitions that neither side can defend? and be straight with me: could a new experiment, a conceptual distinction, or a formal result actually resolve it, or is the question ill-posed because we're stretching 'emotion' across contexts where it doesn't belong? also, does that predictive-processing analogy actually illuminate anything here, or am i just importing lab machinery into a place it doesn't fit?
