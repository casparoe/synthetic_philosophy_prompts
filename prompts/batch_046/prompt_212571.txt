In seminars on global economic history, my colleagues repeatedly invoke a clinical analogy for the Needham question: explaining why Britain industrialized and not China is treated like a physician asking why one patient contracted a fatal disease while an apparently identical patient, with the same genetics and environment, did not. The analogy is used to license an eliminative method—coal, large markets, and scientific culture are dismissed as explanations because both societies possessed them, leaving us to hunt for the pathogen unique to Britain. I want a committed audit of how far this holds: first, the explicit correspondence table (what plays the role of patient, symptom, pathogen, differential diagnosis, and clinical outcome); second, the points where the mapping breaks, especially those that import teleology, normative notions of health, or a single decisive cause into historical explanation; third, a clear separation between the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the eliminative inference and those that are merely decorative; and finally, a definite judgment on whether the analogy was carrying the argument for comparative causal inference all along. If the clinical framing collapses under scrutiny, propose a more adequate analogical framework for posing "why Britain and not China?" that does not presuppose both societies were equally "healthy" candidates for the same developmental trajectory.
