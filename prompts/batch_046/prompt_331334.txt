In recent work on the meaning of life, I keep encountering a particular two-layer structure that I do not have a precise name for, and I would like to know whether it corresponds to an established concept or whether it requires a new one.

Here is the phenomenon in its concrete form. A thinker denies, often quite starkly, that human existence possesses any cosmic, transcendent, or objective purpose. The universe, on this view, is indifferent; questions that ask what life is “for” are said to rest on a category mistake, or at least to have no answer beyond brute description. So far this is familiar territory—absurdism, naturalism, or certain forms of metaphysical anti-theism. But then the same thinker immediately introduces a restricted domain—mathematical inquiry, intimate friendship, aesthetic creation—within which things are described as genuinely important, binding, or even obligatory. Crucially, this importance is not derived from any larger framework: it is not instrumental to survival or happiness, it is not part of an objective list that applies to all rational agents, and it is not presented as mere subjective preference. Rather, it functions as a kind of local urgency. Within the domain, failure and success are treated as genuinely different, and the agent is said to have reason to care about that difference, even though nothing outside the domain cares whether anyone does.

I see this pattern, for instance, in certain interpretations of Frank Ramsey’s philosophical fragments. He is read as holding that the world is a brute fact and that asking about its “meaning” is malformed; yet interpreters simultaneously treat his commitments to mathematics and to personal relationships as carrying normative weight that exceeds mere psychological pull. The result is not a standard theory of meaning—neither objective-list, subjective-fulfillment, nor narrative-identity—but rather a partition: global metaphysical nullity at one level, and ungrounded local bindingness at another.

A second instance appears in some contemporary accounts influenced by absurdism. Writers deny that life has “deep” meaning, yet they defend particular practices—say, scientific inquiry or artistic production—as possessing internal standards of excellence that demand respect. The defense is not that these practices serve an external end, but that within the practice itself one ought to distinguish achievement from failure, and this “ought” is not reducible to the agent’s contingent feelings.

This is not exactly Camus’s revolt, because there is no explicit confrontation with the absence of meaning; it is more like a quiet compartmentalization. It is not exactly a narrative-identity view, because the local domains are not presented as parts of a larger life-story that confers coherence. And it is not straightforward subjectivism, because the agent does not present the value as contingent on her own attitudes. Is there an established concept—perhaps in the literature on local meaning, project-based value, or internal goods—that captures this exact configuration? If not, what term should we coin, and how exactly should we delimit it?
