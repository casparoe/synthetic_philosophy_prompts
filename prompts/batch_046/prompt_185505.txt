I'm a backend engineer with basically zero formal philosophy background, but I got into a long argument with my coworker over lunch about why people stop doing things everyone used to think was normal. We were talking about dueling and slavery ending, and she mentioned this Bernard Williams idea that big moral changes don't happen because someone writes a knockout argument—they happen when a practice becomes embarrassing or dishonorable to keep doing. Then she said Appiah thinks honor shifts work together with reasoning, not instead of it. Anyway I tried to formalize all this into a general theory and now I'm worried I accidentally proved that ethics debates are basically useless.

Here is my argument:
(1) If a social practice ends mainly because people start seeing it as shameful rather than abstractly wrong, then rational argument wasn't the real driver of the change.
(2) Dueling and slavery both lasted for centuries despite people already knowing strong arguments against them.
(3) So for these major moral revolutions, the driver was a shift in honor/shame dynamics (the Williams thesis).
(4) If that's true for big revolutions, it's probably true for smaller norms too: people don't change thier minds about right and wrong through reasoning, they change through social status pressure.
(5) But people currently hold lots of moral beliefs—like that you shouldn't steal company equipment, or that you shouldn't lie on a resume—where they can already hear all the counterarguments (everyone does it, the system rewards it) and they still reject them.
(6) If reasoning never actually moves people, then those beliefs will either never change, or if they do, it won't be because anyone convinced anyone.
(7) So either moral reasoning is basically useless for social change, or the Williams/Appiah story only applies to some special kind of "revolution" and not everyday norms.

This feels like it proves way too much—like I'm saying ethics committees and moral philosophy are just theater. Which step should I drop? I definatly want to keep (3) because the history seems right to me. But I'm not sure if (4) is sneaking in a false analogy between "historical revolution" and "everyday norm maintenance." Is there also a hidden slide in (6) where I'm treating "not convinced yet" as "can't ever be convinced"? My friend thinks Appiah saves this by saying reasoning creates the conditions for honor to shift, but I don't see how that stops my argument unless it makes reasoning causally relevant after all. Can you tell me which premise is the weak link, why it looked okay, and what the standard positions—pure Williams, pure rationalist, and some middle Appiah-style view—each have to pay to reject it?
