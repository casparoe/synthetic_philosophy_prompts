At a recent philosophy-of-science colloquium I attended as a retired physicist now reading in philosophy of mind, two colleagues—both trained philosophers, neither present here—debated whether disjunctivism about perceptual experience can survive the argument from illusion once we take seriously the sub-personal neuroscience of vision. I report their exchange as an uninvolved bystander.

The first interlocutor argued as follows. (1) The argument from illusion establishes that veridical perception and hallucination must share a fundamental mental kind, since they can be subjectively indistinguishable and are produced by the same causal mechanisms in early visual cortex and predictive-coding circuits. (2) Disjunctivism denies this shared kind at the level of conscious experience, claiming that in veridical cases one is directly related to an external object, while in illusion one is in a qualitatively different state. (3) But if the sub-personal machinery—feedforward activation, error-minimization dynamics, and so forth—is literally identical in both cases, then denying a shared experiential kind amounts to an unexplained dualism: the same physical process yields two fundamentally different mental types with no further difference in the subject’s cognitive access. (4) Therefore disjunctivism either collapses into representationalism by admitting a shared representational content, or violates the explanatory norms of cognitive science by multiplying mental kinds beyond what the mechanisms justify.

The second interlocutor replied in kind. (1') Subjective indistinguishability is an epistemic fact about the subject’s access, not an ontological fact about mental kinds; identical twins are indistinguishable to a stranger but remain numerically distinct. (2') Disjunctivism is precisely a thesis about the ontology of experience at the personal level, not about sub-personal computation; predictive-coding dynamics describe information-processing mechanisms, not constituents of phenomenology. (3') Consequently, accepting identical neural mechanisms for illusion and veridical perception poses no threat: the mechanisms explain how the subject comes to be in one experiential kind or the other, not that the kinds themselves are identical. (4') The first interlocutor therefore begs the question by assuming that every level of explanation must be homogeneous—that if the machinery is shared, the experiential product must be shared—which is exactly the representationalist premise disjunctivism rejects.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of the eight numbered moves (1 through 4, and 1' through 4'), rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then state which party is correct about the core dispute, whether either has overlooked an intermediate position—perhaps one that distinguishes the vehicle of experience from its object-related content without fully embracing either representationalism or naive disjunctivism—and whether the injection of predictive-coding neuroscience genuinely resolves the traditional argument from illusion or merely relocates it to a different explanatory level.
