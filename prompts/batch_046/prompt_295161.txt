I lead a philosophy-of-mathematics workshop for 14 graduate students, with 90 minutes and no prior lecture on set theory. Design a runnable classroom activity that makes participants feel the structural collapse behind Russell's paradox before anyone names it. Give each student a physical card defining a "registry rule" for a club: for example, one card might instruct its holder to list every other card whose rule does not apply to itself. Provide a pairing protocol, a small-stakes reward for completing a consistent master registry, and hidden instructions that guarantee one card generates a self-referential loop under the rules they are required to apply. The group should build the registry collaboratively, encounter the unavoidable contradiction in placing the final card, and only then learn the historical name. Include a debrief that maps each physical step—rule assignment, pairing, registry entry, exclusion attempt—onto the corresponding move in naive comprehension, and be explicit about which feature of the formal proof (for instance, the precise derivation of contradiction within first-order logic with unrestricted comprehension) the card game cannot reproduce and you as facilitator must supply. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—probably students patching the contradiction by arbitrarily excluding the troublesome card rather than seeing the principle fail—and how to prevent or redirect that. Write the response in American English.
