You are a philosophical psychologist and conceptual analyst specializing in theories of well-being and the structure of affective states. Before you answer, please take a few minutes to brainstorm: list the major strategies for unifying pleasures (phenomenal-tone theories, pro-attitude accounts, desire-satisfaction variants, functional-role views), and then test each strategy against the concrete cases below, noting where they overgenerate or undergenerate.

This question has become personally urgent rather than merely academic. After a prolonged illness and a gradual return to both physical rehabilitation and my old administrative work, I have been constructing a minimal private ethics of well-being and keep stumbling over the heterogeneity problem: when I call different experiences "pleasures," what, if anything, are they actually sharing? I need a precise principle, not a survey, and I need to know which of my own verdicts it forces me to abandon.

Here are my judgments on specific cases.

Case A: The bodily warmth and muscular release of stretching a severely cramped limb after hours of immobility. Verdict: unambiguously pleasant, and clearly a contribution to well-being.

Case B: The quiet, non-bodily satisfaction of finally aligning a complex spreadsheet formula correctly after repeated failures. Verdict: genuinely pleasant, yet phenomenally cold—no warmth, no excitement, almost purely cognitive.

Case C: The sharp, almost painful exhilaration upon receiving long-awaited news that resolves months of uncertainty. Verdict: pleasant, but its quality is piercing and agitated, closer to a jolt than to soothing relief.

Case D: The diffuse, low-intensity contentment of sitting in familiar silence with an old friend, saying nothing and pursuing nothing. Verdict: pleasant, yet it lacks directedness, intensity, and any specific object; it feels like a background condition rather than an event.

Case E: The relief experienced upon framing a personal failure—a rejected manuscript—as "merely a poor institutional fit" rather than "poor quality," thereby avoiding a more painful self-assessment. Verdict: phenomenally it feels like a release of tension, similar to Case D, yet I want to say it does not contribute to my well-being because it depends on self-deception. I cannot articulate why this differs from D except by circular appeal to "authenticity."

Case F: The cool, observational satisfaction of seeing a distant rival fail in a domain where I have no direct stake, experienced not as malicious glee but as a sense of cosmic symmetry or balance. Verdict: phenomenally positive and detached, much like Case B, yet I am strongly inclined to exclude it from well-being, though I lack a non-ad-hoc reason.

Case G: The state induced by a strong analgesic that eliminates bodily distress but produces no positive feeling—only mental fog and the absence of pain. Verdict: my condition is improved, but I do not count this as pleasure.

Case H: The micro-triumph of completing a mandatory, meaningless bureaucratic form with perfect accuracy—a small, structured victory of order over chaos. Verdict: pleasant, but it seems purely formal or structural, with no emotional or bodily warmth whatsoever.

My central tensions are these. Cases A through D and H all seem like pleasures, yet they share no obvious phenomenal feature: some are bodily, some cognitive, some intense, some diffuse, some directed, some background. Cases E and F feel like they should be excluded despite being phenomenally positive, but my reason looks circular or moralistic rather than descriptive. Case G shows that mere absence of pain is insufficient. So is there a unified account—perhaps centered on a specific kind of pro-attitude, on a functional role in motivation, or on a subtle phenomenal marker I am missing—that includes B, D, and H, excludes E and F, and distinguishes G? Or must hedonism fragment into a family-resemblance concept?

Please state any proposed principle precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, apply it to at least two cases I have not listed (for instance, the pleasure of remembering a past success years later, or the pleasure of solving a puzzle one knows is trivial), and honestly state which of my verdicts above it cannot accommodate without revision. If you conclude that no single principle saves all eight verdicts, say exactly which ones must go and why that loss is acceptable.
