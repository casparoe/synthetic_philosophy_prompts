Below are notes for an essay that revises the tripartite distinction—frame, outer message, and inner message—found in discussions of formal interpretation and self-reference. The essay applies it to self-modifying formal grammars in which the encoding rules (frame) are rewritten by messages produced under those rules. I argue that when the frame is altered by its own outputs, meaning does not relocate upward into a meta-frame or downward into interpretation; instead, a fourth locus emerges in the temporal gap between successive frames. I call this the diachronic interstice, treating it as a structural feature rather than a hermeneutic act. The claim is that in standard cases the interstice is implicitly occupied by an external interpreter, but in automated systems it must be accounted for within the architecture itself.

Please comment on: (1) whether my characterization of the original three-layer model is accurate, especially the claim that the inner message requires recognition of isomorphism by an interpreter; (2) whether the diachronic interstice is genuinely distinct from standard meta-level or hierarchy accounts in logic, or merely a renaming; (3) whether my central example—a grammar that rewrites its own production rules upon encountering a specific symbol—coherently illustrates the point, or whether it conflates syntactic transformation with semantic interpretation. I am particularly interested in whether the argument mistakes encoding dynamics for the location of meaning, and whether there is an internal objection within the original framework that renders the revision unnecessary.
