I've come around to this gradualist take on JOOTS and the MU-puzzle: there's never a real jump out of the system, only slow drift in weights, heuristics, and evaluation criteria. Any self-modifying agent—whether it's a learning algorithm updating its reward model or a human changing values over decades—just incrementally revises. The supposed single decision-point where you switch utilty functions is a fiction; at best it's rapid gradual change misdescribed in retrospect. So the puzzle dissolves into a tracking problem, not a catastrophic leap problem. But I want to know what would actually count against this. Not just the obvious rebuttal about rewriting source code in one go—I'm after the live objections. Which empirical or formal results about self-modification show there's genuinely a discrete transition available? What would defenders of the classic JOOTS framing concede is a real weakness in my gradualist picture, versus what they'd dismiss as misdescription? And does this view risk being unfalsifiable—if every apparent jump can be redescribed as fast drift that occurred too quickly to observe—such that it's insulated in a bad way? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, especially ones that hit the claim that value stablity concerns just collapse into slow-drift monitoring.
