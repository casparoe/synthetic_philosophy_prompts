A fellow graduate student summarized for me the argument usually called Friedman’s F-twist about unrealistic assumptions in scientific and economic modeling. She wrote that Friedman held assumptions should never be judged by how closely they mirror reality, and that the only legitimate criterion is predictive success. She added that Friedman went further and claimed the more wildly false an assumption is, the stronger the theory that employs it, because extreme unreality forces the model to isolate a single causal mechanism. She said he applied this equally to economics and physics—citing perfect-competition models and the ideal-gas law as parallel cases—but insisted that in physics unrealistic premises are always approximations to deeper laws, whereas in economics they are purely instrumental fictions with no underlying reality. She concluded that, on this view, a post-2008 DSGE model that assumes a representative agent, rational expectations, and no financial intermediation can be fully defended solely by its ability to match a few aggregate time-series moments, even if it systematically fails every counterfactual policy test and describes no actual transmission mechanism. She also claimed that Friedman coined the term “F-twist” himself and used it to argue that any model tuned after the fact to fit an anomaly should be granted the same predictive credit as a model that anticipated the anomaly.

This sounds partly right, but I suspect it conflates several distinctions I have seen in the philosophy-of-science literature. Could you audit it for me? Please separate what is an accurate rendering of Friedman’s position, what is a standard but defensible simplification, and what is a subtle misrepresentation or overstatement. In particular: does Friedman actually treat greater unreality as a theoretical virtue, or only as an irrelevant feature? Does he draw the physics-economics comparison in the direction she claims, or closer to the opposite? Is her characterization of how the F-twist is supposed to defend DSGE modeling a fair extension of the view, or does it import commitments about counterfactual testing and structural realism that Friedman never endorsed? And is the attribution of the term “F-twist” correct? Please also say what the corrected view implies for a case she did not mention: an agent-based climate model that uses highly unrealistic simplifying rules for individual farmer behavior—rules that are known to be descriptively false—but that reproduces regional drought patterns more accurately than a structurally richer model with more realistic micro-foundations. Would Friedman’s actual argument defend the simpler model, and on what precise grounds?
