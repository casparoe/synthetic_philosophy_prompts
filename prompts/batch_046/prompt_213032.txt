I have noticed a recurring structural feature across several secular democracies—England, Germany, France, and the United States—where the state does not fund religion uniformly but instead offers specific administrative containers that only certain religious bodies can occupy. In Germany, this takes the form of state-collected church taxes available only to communities holding public-law corporation status, coupled with a state-processed exit declaration. In England, the Church of England retains automatic seats in the House of Lords and a distinct legal framework for parish governance, while other faiths navigate separate incorporation rules. In France, the 1905 separation law is modified by state ownership of pre-1905 religious buildings and by publicly funded chaplaincies in hospitals, prisons, and the military that apply selectively. In the US, 501(c)(3) tax exemption requires a specific organizational form that many informal or non-hierarchical religious groups do not adopt. The common thread is neither classic establishment nor uniform separation, but a bureaucratic filter: the state creates privileged administrative pathways—tax collection, legislative representation, property regimes, tax exemption—that distinguish between religions adopting a particular legal-bureaucratic shape and those that do not, producing a two-tier system of religious citizenship mediated by compliance with state-defined organizational norms rather than by theology. Is there an established term in constitutional theory or sociology of religion for this precise pattern, in which secular bureaucratic machinery becomes the gatekeeper of religious membership and privilege through formal organizational criteria? If there is no precise term, how should one distinguish it from classic establishment, from cooperationist church-state models, and from neutral accommodation, particularly with respect to the boundary between procedural neutrality and structural favoritism?
