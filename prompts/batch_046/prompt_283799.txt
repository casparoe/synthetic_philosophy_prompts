Here's my current package on cross-cultural philosophical intuition, and I'd like an audit of whether it holds together: (1) systematic survey divergence in judgments about knowledge attribution, reference determination, and moral luck across populations reflects genuine philosophical disagreement rather than noise or translation error; (2) reflective equilibrium should be cosmopolitan, treating intuitions from non-Western and small-scale societies as equally authoritative inputs alongside those shaped by Western philosophical training; (3) where an intuition can be explained by hyperbolic discounting—systematic overvaluation of immediate over delayed outcomes—that explanation debunks its normative authority; (4) judgments gathered in small-scale societies with minimal formal schooling are especially trustworthy because they sit closer to baseline conceptual competence, less distorted by explicit theoretical indoctrination; (5) the appropriate response to persistent divergence is philosophical pluralism: locally coherent frameworks that need not be forced into convergence on a single universal system. Is this jointly tenable? I am unsure whether (4) conflicts with (2)'s egalitarianism, whether (3)'s debunking strategy undermines (1)'s claim that divergence is philosophically deep, and whether (5) renders the cosmopolitan weighing in (2) pointless. There also seems to be a tension between (1)'s context-sensitivity and (4)'s appeal to uncontaminated competence—does that dissolve on inspection? Order the real tensions by severity, indicate for each which commitment is cheapest to abandon and what that costs, and clearly separate the apparent conflicts from the genuine ones.
