I'm grading papers for an advanced undergraduate seminar on the ethics of war, and I want a second opinion on one answer before I finalize marks. The exam was open-book but required no citations; students were expected to reason from the concepts covered in lectures and readings.

Exam question: "The moral equality of combatants (MEC) holds that soldiers on opposing sides possess symmetric permissions and liabilities in battle, regardless of whether their cause is just. Critics drawing on liability-based accounts of self-defense argue that unjust combatants are analogous to wrongful aggressors and therefore forfeit their right not to be killed, destroying the symmetry. Defenders respond that institutional role morality separates the sphere of individual combat from collective political judgment, so soldiers should not be held liable for the war's injustice. Evaluate this defense. In particular, distinguish authorization liability—liability for the collective decision to wage war—from execution liability—liability for individual acts of killing in battle—and assess whether role morality can consistently block execution liability without also undermining authorization liability. Finally, consider whether voluntary enlistment weakens the role-morality shield."

Student answer: "The moral equality of combatants is the traditional view that just and unjust soldiers can kill each other without committing murder. The liability objection denies this symmetry: unjust combatants lack a just cause, so they are like criminals attacking defenders, and are therefore liable to defensive killing. Just combatants retain their rights, so equality collapses.

The standard reply appeals to institutional role morality. Soldiers occupy roles within a collective structure—wearing uniforms, following orders, serving the state. Because they do not decide whether the war satisfies jus ad bellum criteria, they should not be held personally liable. Michael Walzer argues for a strict separation between jus ad bellum and jus in bello, claiming that soldiers lack the epistemic authority and position to judge the war's justice. On this view, soldiers are morally innocent instruments: they carry out harm but bear no moral weight for it.

But this defense seems to confuse absence of liability with full moral innocence. By treating soldiers as instruments without moral weight, it relocates all responsibility to the collective rather than resolving it at the individual level. Moreover, the defense focuses on epistemic limitations—soldiers cannot know if the war is just—when the real issue is structural separation, not ignorance.

There is also a problem with voluntary enlistment. If a soldier voluntarily joins the military, they choose to enter the role knowing it involves killing. This voluntary acceptance should undermine the role shield: the unjust combatant assumes both the risk and the moral burden of the war. So role morality either pushes liability up to the state, or it collapses once we recognize that voluntary participation reintroduces individual responsibility. Thus the defense fails to solve the liability problem; it just shifts it upward without blocking execution liability consistently."
