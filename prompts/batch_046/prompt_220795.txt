I'm drafting notes for an essay on Hempel's raven paradox. The standard formulation holds that "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens," so observing a green apple should confirm the universal claim about ravens. The usual Bayesian reply appeals to differing priors, and Goodman's reply invokes projectible predicates. I want to defend a further, less standard claim: the paradox genuinely dissolves only if we reject the assumption that logical equivalence preserves inductive relevance across natural-kind boundaries. Specifically, I propose that confirmation requires "kind-relevant sampling"—an observation confirms a universal claim about kind K only if the observation is drawn from a domain where K-membership is a live, projectible possibility. On this view, a green apple fails to confirm that all ravens are black not merely because of low prior probability, but because apples belong to a kind-domain in which ravenhood is not a live option; the sample space is implicitly partitioned by kind. This saves the intuition that ravens confirm while apples do not, but at the cost of making confirmation partly pragmatic and context-dependent, since kind-boundaries themselves depend on background theoretical commitments. I also want to address the circularity objection—that "kind-relevant" merely restates "confirming"—by arguing that kind-partitioning functions as a pre-inductive framing condition rather than as part of the confirmation relation itself. Please comment on these notes, especially on whether the kind-partitioning move resolves the paradox or merely relocates it. Do not survey every response in the literature; instead, take a definite position on whether my argument succeeds, identify its single weakest point, and propose the cheapest concrete revision that would fix it. If you think the argument is sound, say so explicitly and explain what it forces us to abandon about purely formal theories of confirmation.
