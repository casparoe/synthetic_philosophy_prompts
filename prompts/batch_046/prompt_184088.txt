I am working on a long-form feature about whether our present anxieties over algorithmic media represent something genuinely new or merely the latest expression of an older critique of mass publicity, and I have been returning to Kierkegaard's review of Two Ages with exactly this framing in mind. In conversations with editors and media theorists, a recurring analogy surfaces: today's recommendation algorithms are essentially the nineteenth-century press scaled up to planetary dimensions. Both are said to manufacture an abstract, anonymous audience; both reward sensationalism over sustained thought; both produce a leveling effect in which individual voices dissolve into an undifferentiated collective noise. The comparison is typically used to argue that algorithmic culture is not a rupture from modernity but its logical fulfillment, and that any adequate response—if one exists—must mirror the recovery of inward individual responsibility that Kierkegaard advocated.

I want you to audit this analogy with real precision rather than rhetorical flourish. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: map the press, the subscriber, the phantom public, and the leveling process in Kierkegaard's account onto the algorithmic feed, the user, the viral crowd, and the flattening of discourse today. Then isolate the disanalogies that actually bear on the argument—particularly the fact that the historical press addressed a uniform public through shared texts, whereas algorithms personalize content and fragment audiences into non-overlapping bubbles. Does this personalization dissolve the very idea of a single phantom public, or does it merely multiply it into countless micro-phantoms? Consider also the economic substrate—subscription versus engagement-maximization—and whether the analogy quietly assumes that the public remains a coherent entity capable of being addressed, when algorithmic distribution may have reduced it to statistical aggregates with no collective consciousness at all. Finally, assess whether the normative conclusion—that algorithmic culture represents perfected leveling and therefore demands a turn toward inwardness—survives once these structural differences are visible, or whether the analogy itself was carrying the prescriptive weight. If the comparison breaks at a decisive point, propose what a more accurate framing of the relationship between algorithmic media and Kierkegaard's critique might look like.
