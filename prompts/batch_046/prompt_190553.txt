You are a professional philosopher with expertise in Buddhist metaphysics and in the structural logic of fixed-form poetry. Before composing, spend two to three sentences brainstorming how a villanelle’s alternating refrains can enact the doctrine of anatta: the returning lines should mirror the persistent illusion of a substantial self, while the shifting rhyme context should show that the “same” word applies differently to conventional designation and to ultimate absence.

Then write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by one quatrain, with refrains A1 and A2 alternating in the standard ABA / ABA pattern—on Buddhist anatta (no-self) and personal identity. Take the chariot analogy from classical Buddhist texts as your governing image: form is the axle, feeling the yoke, perception the reins, mental formations the harness, consciousness the charioteer—except that no charioteer is found.

Requirements: Refrain A1 should state that the self is only a conventional designation (nama) for assembled aggregates; Refrain A2 should deny any underlying controller or owner. Each tercet should pair one skandha with its chariot-part, emphasizing functional dependence without substantial unity. The final quatrain must apply the analogy reflexively to the speaker: the “I” that asks where the self is, is itself only the aggregates. Use slant or shifting rhyme so that key terms (bound/unbound, frame/name) alter meaning across repetitions. The poem must be accurate enough that a specialist recognizes the Abhidhamma framework and the conventional/ultimate distinction, yet accessible to a philosophy student who knows no Buddhism. Do not resolve into nihilism or eternalism; end with the tension intact. Keep the tone precise and avoid sermonizing or shrugging. The poem should be suitable for closing a graduate seminar on personal identity.
