Please draft the joint statement that a defender of coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) and a critic who holds that idealized preference aggregation necessarily collapses plural autonomy into a false coherence could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The defender should be understood as maintaining that an agent’s actual preference states—including those that are diachronic, contradictory, or shaped by temporal framing—can be extrapolated through sustained reflective equilibrium into a single normatively authoritative set of idealized preferences. The critic should be understood as maintaining that when an agent’s preferences are irreducibly plural across stable temporal stages, any procedure that resolves them into coherence substitutes the theorist’s normative commitments for the agent’s autonomy, and that no formal aggregation rule can avoid this result.

The document should be structured as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should include shared commitments about the nature of revealed preference, the distinction between procedural and substantive rationality, the fact that hyperbolic discounting and framing effects complicate preference assessment without by themselves settling questions of idealization, and the precise description of a constructed test case—call it Case Delta—in which one agent sincerely endorses three stable preference orderings (career-maximizing, family-centered, and risk-averse) over a ten-year span, with no ordering emerging as dominant even after reflection with full information and without cognitive constraint. Both parties should also agree that Arrow-type conditions (unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship) can be formulated for intra-personal preference aggregation and that it is an open question whether they apply with the same force inside one life as across many agents.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement in wording that its own side would accept as an accurate formulation of its view. At minimum, include disagreements over: whether coherence across diachronic preference slices is a necessary condition for normative authority or whether maintaining unresolved plurality is itself a value; whether the intra-personal aggregation problem is structurally analogous to interpersonal social choice; and whether an agent’s persistent refusal to resolve a conflict after adequate reflection is best interpreted as evidence of a hidden unified preference or as evidence that no such preference exists.

Third, a cruxes section. For each disagreement, identify the precise sub-question such that settling it in one direction would cause at least one party to revise its view. Then specify, for each crux, a concrete argument, formal result, or structured case that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side’s explicit prediction recorded in advance. For example, both sides should agree that demonstrating a consistent aggregation rule for Case Delta that respects unanimity and independence of irrelevant alternatives—without collapsing the three orderings into one—is directly relevant to the analogy with social-choice impossibility; the defender should predict whether such a rule can exist under CEV-style idealization conditions and what its existence or failure would imply, while the critic should record the corresponding prediction.

Write in formal academic prose suitable for a philosophical audience. Do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading. Do not invoke highly specialized formalisms that depend on specific unpublished technical results. The deliverable should be a constructive document both parties could sign, with the remaining disagreements rendered exact rather than rhetorical.
