I'm a doctoral student in political theory preparing a workshop contribution on associative democracy and moral attention, and I've been trying to formalize Simone Weil's hostility to political parties into a precise institutional argument. The reconstruction runs as follows, but it collapses into a conclusion far stronger than anything she actually held. (1) For Weil, a party is defined not by shared opinion but by an organizational structure that demands doctrinal loyalty: members must internalize the collective's judgments as their own, suppressing the individual's direct moral attention to reality. (2) Any institution that structurally requires individuals to subordinate private moral judgment to collective will—whether through belief-substitution or through binding rule-acceptance—functions in her framework as a mechanism that corrupts attention. (3) Modern democratic representation depends on procedural loyalty: citizens and representatives must accept collective outcomes and rules they personally reject, precisely because democratic procedure, not individual judgment, authorizes the result. (4) Procedural loyalty is structurally analogous to doctrinal loyalty, since both require subordination of individual judgment to collective will; therefore democratic representation, like party membership, necessarily corrupts moral attention. (5) Consequently, on Weilian premises, democratic institutions as such—not merely partisan organizations—are illegitimate and ought to be dismantled. (6) Yet Weil herself defended collective resistance movements and endorsed local forms of democratic deliberation that clearly involve procedural loyalty, which suggests she did not regard all democratic coordination as corrupting. The conclusion in (5) is therefore far stronger than her actual position. Where does the slip occur? Is the error in (2), which collapses procedural loyalty into doctrinal loyalty by treating all subordination of judgment as attention-corrupting? Or is it in (3), which assumes democratic representation necessarily suppresses individual judgment rather than channeling it? If I reject (2) and insist on a strict distinction—doctrinal loyalty replaces judgment, procedural loyalty preserves it while deferring its exercise—I can preserve Weil's specific anti-party argument, but I risk reducing her critique to organizational sociology rather than the metaphysical claim about attention and moral gravity that seems central to her thought. If instead I reject (3) and argue that democratic procedures can avoid suppressing judgment, I need to explain how this squares with her broader skepticism about collective judgment in her writings on rootedness and obligation. I would strongly prefer to keep (1) intact, since it captures what I take to be the core of her view: that parties destroy the soul's orientation toward the good by substituting collective reflex for individual attention. Which premise should I give up, and what does each option cost for interpreting Weil consistently?
