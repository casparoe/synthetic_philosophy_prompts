Please provide a precise conceptual analysis of the relationship between the orthogonality thesis—according to which intelligence and final goals are independent across the space of possible agents—and the thesis of instrumental convergence, which holds that rational agents with diverse terminal values will nevertheless pursue common intermediate subgoals such as self-preservation and resource accumulation.

The standard presentation treats instrumental convergence as a straightforward consequence of rationality given a shared world-structure: any agent that values X will value the means to X. I want you to probe whether this presentation implicitly collapses a distinction that the orthogonality thesis should keep open. Specifically, introduce and work with the idea of an 'instrumental topology': the structural mapping, internal to an agent's reasoning architecture, that determines which intermediate states are registered as useful for reaching a given terminal state.

Then address the following. First, if orthogonality is strict—if an agent of arbitrarily high intelligence can be paired with any final goal—why should we assume that the instrumental topology is invariant across architectures? Could there not be 'orthogonal instrumental paths,' where two agents share an identical final goal but, because their reasoning architectures differ, identify entirely disjoint sets of intermediate subgoals as instrumentally valuable? Conversely, could an agent with a final goal of minimal resource consumption nevertheless exhibit resource-acquisition behavior if its instrumental topology is coupled to its architecture in a way that overrides its terminal valuation?

Second, clarify whether instrumental convergence is best understood as a claim about the geometry of goal-space (all paths to diverse ends intersect at certain nodes) or as a claim about the uniformity of reasoning architectures (all sufficiently intelligent agents share a topology that forces convergence regardless of ends).

Finally, specify what empirical or conceptual conditions would have to obtain for an intelligent agent to genuinely avoid instrumental convergence—without the explanation being that the agent is 'irrational' or 'approximately human-like.' Your answer should not depend on bibliographic references; it should proceed by conceptual clarification and the construction of boundary cases.
