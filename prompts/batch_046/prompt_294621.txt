Im putting together a handout for a seminar on nationalism — about 8 grad students, we just read Hobsbawm and Anderson and now I want them to figure out where they actually land on how old nations really are. Dont write me an essay comparing the two. Build a taxonomy tree (branch nodes are yes/no or either/or claims, leaves are positions) that sorts answers.

Start with whether national consciousness needs mass mediation (print, broadcast, etc.) or can come from face-to-face ethnic solidarity alone. Next split: if traditions feel ancient, is that best explained as a cynical state project (the Hobsbawm angle) or a sincere collective imagining thats actually recent (the Anderson angle)? Then branch on whether persistence is mainly about bureacratic homogenization (Gellner-style) or ritual repetition that builds emotional attachment even without strong state power. I also want you to work in a niche distinction about whether invented traditions succeed because people sincerely believe the myth or because they solve a coordination problem regardless of belief — stick that in as a branch.

The leaves should include: pure primordialism/perennialism, Anderson-style imagined community, Hobsbawm-style invented tradition, ethnosymbolist middle ground (Smith-type), bureaucratic-modernist (Gellner-type), and at least one unnamed combo where nobody actually sits. Flag that empty one and diagnose why its empty — incoherent? just ugly? unexplored?

Every internal node should be a concrete claim or case a student can answer without knowing the label. No asking "do you like Anderson?" — ask about mechanisms or cases. Each named leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The empty leaf gets a one-sentence diagnosis.

Start with the tree itself, no intro paragraph, no "heres the taxonomy". Write casual, like an email; a typo or two is fine.
