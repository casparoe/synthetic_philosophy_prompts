The exam prompt was as follows: "Standard Bayesian conditioning updates beliefs only when an event is observed with certainty, yet much of our evidence is ambiguous—for instance, a noisy measurement or a blurry percept. Richard Jeffrey proposed an update rule for such cases. (a) State Jeffrey's rule formally and explain the rigidity condition. (b) Provide a concrete example involving ambiguous perceptual evidence. (c) Contrast this rule with standard conditioning and explain why applying Bayes' rule directly is problematic when evidence is uncertain."

The student submitted this answer: "Standard conditioning updates P(H) to P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E), which requires learning E with full certainty. Jeffrey addressed situations where experience shifts our confidence across a partition {E_i} without fixing any single cell—say, seeing a bird in poor light and becoming 60% sure it falls in the robin color-range and 40% in the sparrow range. The rule assigns new weights q_i to the cells and updates H by mixing old conditionals: P_new(H) = Σ P(H|E_i) q_i. The rigidity condition requires that within each cell the relative probabilities of hypotheses stay exactly as in the prior, so P_new(H|E_i) = P_old(H|E_i). This avoids standard Bayes because we never observe one specific E_j with probability one; instead we redistribute weight across the partition. Jeffrey originally developed this to solve Carnap's problem of old evidence, where a familiar fact has prior near zero and division by P(E) breaks down. By partitioning the space we can update on long-known data, and rigidity keeps our internal beliefs stable even for events lying outside the observed partition."
