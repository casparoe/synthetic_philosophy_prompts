I sit on a fellowship selection panel with four other senior members. After reading all applications independently, I formed a firm conviction that Candidate A's proposal is stronger than Candidate B's, based on my direct evaluation of methodological rigour and originality. Once we exchanged anonymised scores, I discovered that all four colleagues ranked B above A. I also happen to know—though this is not part of the official record—that two of those colleagues have a well-documented tendency to penalise interdisciplinary work of precisely the kind A undertakes, while the remaining two are genuinely neutral on that dimension. The panel's charter requires me to submit a single definitive ranking with written justification for any departure from the collective mean; I can cite the documented bias of the two reviewers, but I have no specific flaw to point to in the judgments of the other two. I still believe A is superior, yet I am acutely conscious that four experienced peers, half of them unbiased, disagree with me. Should I treat my continued conviction as a defensible exercise of independent judgement, or as an irrational refusal to defer to superior collective reliability? And if I do maintain my view, am I obliged to reduce my confidence to match the weight of the dissent, or can I legitimately hold it at full strength while acknowledging that I might be wrong?
