In modern Stoic revival literature, the dichotomy of control is often compressed into the one-liner: "You control your responses, not the world." Separate the precise claims this sentence could be expressing — distinguishing, at minimum, a causal thesis about the limits of agency, a normative thesis about the scope of responsibility, an epistemic thesis about the proper objects of concern, and a phenomenological thesis about the source of emotional disturbance. For each reading, assess whether it is defensible on classical Stoic grounds (for instance, the division of things into what is up to us and what is not, and the classification of externals as indifferent), whether it survives contact with contemporary cognitive science (such as predictive-processing models that blur the boundary between internal inference and external causation, or embodied-cognition accounts that treat action as extending into the environment), and whether it actually supports the therapeutic conclusion that anxiety about external outcomes is always irrational. Where the modern slogan overreaches or collapses distinct Stoic commitments, say exactly what gets lost in the compression.
