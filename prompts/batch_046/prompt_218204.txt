I'm preparing materials for an advanced philosophy seminar focused on the metaphysics of agency, specifically centered on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument—that true moral responsibility is impossible because nothing can be causa sui, and thus no one is ultimately responsible for the mental nature from which their actions flow. Rather than asking for a standard literature review, I want a diagnostic classification instrument: a decision tree or flowchart that takes a seminar participant from a starting commitment about the argument's premises to a specific, named or precisely characterized position in the logical space of responses. The tree should be built from the bottom up: first the branching questions, then the positions mapped onto the combinations of answers, with unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed.

The root node should ask whether the respondent accepts that true moral responsibility requires being the ultimate source (causa sui) of one's own mental nature, but the subsequent branches must turn on distinctions that actually divide philosophers in this debate rather than on superficial labels. Specifically, include nodes addressing: (a) whether one allows that being partially shaped by one's own prior choices—what we might call recursive partial authorship—breaks the regress in a way that satisfies the demand for self-creation; (b) whether one distinguishes between basic desert (being worthy of blame or praise in an ultimate sense) and practical accountability (being an appropriate target of social response), and if so, whether rejecting the former undermines the latter; (c) whether one thinks the Basic Argument applies symmetrically to praise and blame, or whether negative responsibility is more vulnerable to the regress than positive responsibility; and (d) whether one responds by denying the causal principle that drives the regress, by accepting the principle but denying it applies to agents, or by accepting both principle and applicability but revising the concept of responsibility. Each leaf should correspond to a position such as hard incompatibilist, revisionist about desert, threshold compatibilist, recursive compatibilist, or asymmetrical desert theorist. Where a combination of answers produces a logically possible but philosophically unoccupied cell—say, a view that accepts full ultimate self-creation as unnecessary, rejects recursive authorship, yet insists on unrevised basic desert—identify it and briefly diagnose whether the emptiness stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Every node must be phrased as a concrete case or claim that a respondent can answer without already knowing which philosophical label it leads to. The instrument should also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate given the questions included—for instance, if two positions converge on all four branching commitments but diverge only on rhetorical framing or on issues outside the tree's scope. Finally, the taxonomy should accommodate at least six distinct leaves, include one unoccupied combination with its diagnosis, and avoid asking for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to obscure texts that a model without search access could not verify. Frame the whole thing as a tool for students to discover which position they actually hold rather than to identify themselves by name.
