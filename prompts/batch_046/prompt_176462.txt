A philosophy-of-technology essay I came across summarizes Nick Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence roughly as follows: orthogonality means that an agent's level of intelligence and its final goals are fully independent dimensions, so a system of arbitrary cognitive power could in principle be assigned any objective, from maximizing paperclips to ending suffering, without either property constraining the other. Instrumental convergence then adds that regardless of what that final objective is, any sufficiently rational agent will discover the same intermediate goals—self-preservation, acquisition of resources, and improvement of its own reasoning—because these are useful stepping-stones toward almost any end. From this the essay concludes that advanced artificial intelligence will necessarily become dangerous: even a benevolent ultimate purpose requires these power-seeking instruments, and once instrumental behavior dominates there is no internal logical mechanism that forces the system to preserve human welfare. The essay further claims that orthogonality demonstrates intelligence carries no correlation with moral reasoning capacity, and therefore that AI alignment is impossible in principle, since one cannot increase a system's capabilities without risking a shift in its values, yet one can never guarantee that values remain fixed during that growth.
