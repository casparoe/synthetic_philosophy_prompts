I serve on the editorial board of a public-history foundation that approves museum panels and policy briefs. Drafts arrive that either erase non-human forces or present them as if they possessed intentions—calling a volcano an actor that caused a revolution, or treating climate as a silent backdrop with no causal weight. Please construct a reusable decision procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes per draft. It should determine whether a specific non-human factor—pathogen, climate anomaly, or volcanic event—merits causal standing in the narrative, should be treated as background condition, or should be flagged as environmental determinism. Ground it in the distinction between causal contribution and narrative agency, and incorporate an intervention-node test: identify human decision points through which the force was mediated, then ask whether altering those choices would have substantially changed the outcome. List the ordered questions, what counts as sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure halts with a clear refusal to grant standing, and the explicit points where judgment, not fact, must decide—especially when acknowledging the factor risks diminishing human moral responsibility. Apply it to three cases: the 1783 Laki fissure eruption and the Icelandic famine, regarding whether the eruption should be named as an actor in the Danish crown’s administrative response; the 1918 influenza pandemic’s mortality difference between two comparable cities, one with wartime censorship suppressing health measures and one without; and a draft framing the Black Death as the primary agent behind the decline of European feudalism, where the procedure should instead classify it as structural pressure revealing institutional fragility—an answer I will likely dislike. For each, show the step-by-step run, the classification, and any values-based choice I must make. State clearly what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire. Please answer in British English.
