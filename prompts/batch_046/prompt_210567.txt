A philosophy blog I encountered summarizes David Benatar's asymmetry argument for antinatalism in the following terms: "Benatar proves that coming into existence is always a net harm, because pleasure requires an actual experiencing subject to carry axiological weight, whereas pain harms even a merely potential subject. This yields an asymmetry of value: the absence of pain in a non-existent being is genuinely good, but the absence of pleasure is not bad, since no actual person is deprived. From this he derives what might be called a principle of actuality-weighted harm — essentially negative utilitarianism applied to procreation — according to which we have an overwhelming duty to prevent suffering, but generating happy people carries no positive moral weight whatsoever, and subjective satisfaction is irrelevant to the comparison between existence and non-existence. Therefore any life, however excellent, is necessarily worse than never having existed, because its suffering is an unconditional harm while its pleasures are only conditionally valuable. The argument also implies that objections appealing to the quality of lived experience miss the point entirely, since the axiological comparison operates independently of first-person feeling."

How fair is this summary? Please audit it point by point: which claims accurately reflect Benatar's asymmetry between the presence and absence of pleasure and pain, and which conflate it with negative utilitarianism or with a blanket claim that every life is net-negative? In particular, examine whether Benatar really holds that pleasure is only conditionally valuable, whether he treats subjective satisfaction as irrelevant to the comparison, and whether the summary's "principle of actuality-weighted harm" is a legitimate extraction or a mischaracterization. I also want to know whether the conclusion — that there is an overwhelming duty not to reproduce but no corresponding reason to create happy people — follows directly from the asymmetry alone or requires additional premises about obligation. Where the summary makes Benatar sound more extreme or more psychologically implausible than his actual view, please flag the distortion and say how a defender of the position would correct it.
