I have been trying to map the logical space between epistemicism and supervaluationism for a long-form essay, and I keep running into what looks like a hidden convergence that standard surveys gloss over. The framing I want to test involves a diachronic sharpening protocol: imagine we gradually replace every instance of a vague predicate in a language with a precisely defined successor term, but we do so in stages, allowing speakers at each stage to revise their admissible precisifications based on the replacements made at prior stages. On the supervaluationist picture, each stage generates its own family of admissible sharpenings, and there is no trans-stage fact about which family is correct. The epistemicist, by contrast, insists that there is a single, sharp boundary for the original predicate—one that persists unchanged across all stages, even though speakers at every stage remain ignorant of it.

My confusion is this: if the epistemicist's sharp boundary is, by its very nature, permanently insulated from any revisionary pressure—since no amount of precisification or linguistic change can reveal it—then in what sense does it do any metaphysical work that is not already done by the supervaluationist's distributed family of stage-relative cutoffs? Specifically, does not the epistemicist's one hidden fact become functionally indistinguishable from the claim that there are infinitely many possible cutoffs, none of which can be privileged, except that the epistemicist arbitrarily labels one of them as the true boundary without being able to point to it? If that is right, then the debate seems to collapse into a disagreement about labeling rather than about vagueness itself. But I assume I am missing something structural—perhaps about how the margin-for-error principle operates differently across stages, or about whether supervaluationism can accommodate a trans-stage invariant—because philosophers continue to treat the two views as genuinely incompatible. Where exactly does the analogy between the hidden cutoff and the distributed family break down, and what feature of either view prevents this kind of diachronic collapse?
