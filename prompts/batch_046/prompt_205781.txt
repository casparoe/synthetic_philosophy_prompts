Two colleagues in my engineering group debated during planning whether to redirect substantial resources toward mitigating a hypothetical, low-probability failure mode in a future system architecture—claimed 0.001% likelihood but allegedly catastrophic at scale—versus shipping near-certain user-facing improvements. Colleague A argued that any consistent expected-value framework demands the redirection: the catastrophic tail’s disutility, multiplied by its non-zero probability, swamps the bounded, near-certain product gains, so refusing to act is merely risk-averse irrationality dressed as pragmatism. Colleague B replied that this is expected-value fanaticism: the probability is essentially uncalibrated, the “catastrophic” outcome is defined vaguely enough to be unfalsifiable, and treating speculative long-tail risks as decision-dominant paralyzes any institution adopting the rule. A countered that B is not attacking expected-value reasoning but smuggling in ambiguity aversion and an unstated maximin or satisficing criterion without defending it; if B truly rejects comparing outcomes across epistemic worlds on a common scale, B owes an explicit alternative decision rule. B answered that the burden is reversed: A applies a framework requiring precise cardinal utilities over outcomes outside any shared empirical check, and in rivalrous institutional contexts—where probabilities are manufactured by speculation—such application is not rationality but formalism run amok. They asked me to referee.
