I am organizing a session for a graduate philosophy-of-biology seminar on whether “life” marks a natural kind or a pragmatic gradient, and I want a classification instrument that sorts positions by what they say about concrete borderline cases rather than by the labels people prefer. Please write a decision-tree taxonomy—built from logical distinctions, not from a list of named theories—that takes a student from an initial commitment about the nature of life to one of several named or unnamed positions. The root node should ask whether being alive is a binary property with sharp boundaries or a graded continuum of organizational complexity. From there, construct four successive levels of branch nodes, each phrased as a yes-or-no question about what an entity must actually do—maintain autonomous metabolic closure, undergo open-ended evolutionary adaptation, replicate via nucleic acids, or possess functional information integration—so that a student can answer without knowing which philosopher endorses which view.

The tree must be tested against four concrete borderline entities that should appear as explicit diagnostic conditions: viruses (genetic replication without autonomous metabolism), synthetic minimal cells with engineered minimal genomes (autonomous metabolism but artificial origin), viroids (RNA replication without proteins or metabolism), and protein-only replicators such as prions (templating without nucleic acids). At each leaf, provide the position’s name—such as “autopoietic essentialism,” “evolutionary functionalism,” “genetic replicationism,” or “strong gradualism”—or mark it “unnamed/unoccupied” with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and note what finer distinction would be needed. To keep the instrument from being answerable by rote recitation, inject one somewhat niche framing idea: include a branch that asks how the position treats hypothetical “shadow biospheres”—life-forms with non-carbon chemistry—without relying on obscure formalisms. The audience is graduate students with basic cell biology but no specialist training in synthetic genomics; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask the student to identify specific authors.

Finally, provide a brief scoring key that maps patterns of answers across the four borderline cases onto the corresponding leaves—for example, if a student classifies viruses as alive, viroids as not alive, synthetic minimal cells as alive, and prions as ambiguous, which leaf do they reach? Write the entire response in American English, in a formal but accessible register.
