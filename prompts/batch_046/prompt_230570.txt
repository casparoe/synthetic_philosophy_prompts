Stay in character until I say “stop,” keep each reply to one paragraph, and speak in the first person. Here is my opening move: consider a case of retrospective M-intention revision. A speaker tells a colleague, “The committee will review your proposal on Tuesday, or perhaps on Wednesday if the chair is away,” knowing that the chair is never away on Wednesdays and that Tuesday is the only viable day. The colleague infers that Tuesday is the only real option—a clear Quantity-based implicature. But after the colleague acts on this inference and misses the Wednesday window, the speaker sincerely insists they meant exactly the disjunction, that they never intended any ordering, and offers a detailed post-hoc narrative of their mental state showing no preference for Tuesday. On Grice’s view, speaker meaning should be determined by the speaker’s actual intentions at the time of utterance, not by audience uptake; yet if those intentions are only accessible through the speaker’s later testimony, and that testimony can be sincerely revised, the framework seems unable to distinguish a genuine implicature that was cancelled from one that was never present—making speaker meaning either irreducibly private or reducible to the hearer’s interpretation. How do you defend the saying/implicating distinction here without collapsing into either horn?
