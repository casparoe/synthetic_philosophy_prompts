The asker has noticed a recurring move in biographical films and prestige historical dramas and wants to know whether philosophy or film theory has a precise name for it.

Instance one: A well-known biographical film keeps a real twentieth-century scientist’s name, her major discoveries, and the chronology of her career intact, but systematically replaces her documented personality—marked by archival records of pettiness, plagiarism accusations, and professional jealousy—with an invented temperament of consistent humility and collaborative warmth. Viewers and some commentators defend the portrayal not as mere simplification but as revealing her “true spirit” or “what she really stood for,” despite the fact that every interpersonal scene is fabricated.

Instance two: A recent prestige drama depicts a real political leader’s final days. The historical record shows he died estranged from a longtime rival and left bitter private correspondence. The film invents a deathbed reconciliation scene that never occurred, presenting it with the same visual and narrative weight as documented events. When challenged, defenders argue that the invented scene expresses a “deeper moral truth” about the leader’s legacy, even though it directly contradicts the archival record.

In both cases, the creators are not producing fictional characters inspired by real ones, which would be ordinary roman à clef, nor are they merely compressing timelines or merging minor figures, which is ordinary dramatic license. Instead, they preserve the external identity—name, achievements, historical role—while substituting an interior moral and emotional architecture that is more coherent, more admirable, or more narratively satisfying than the documented one. The audience then tends to remember the invented interior as belonging to the real person.

The asker asks: Is there an established term for this specific move—the preservation of a biographical shell with a substituted moral-psychological core, defended as revealing a deeper truth? If the nearest concepts are hagiography, dramatic license, or the “truth of art,” how exactly does this phenomenon differ from each? And if there is no precise term, can you coin one, define it rigorously, and specify its boundary cases—for example, distinguishing it from simple factual error, from counterfactual biography, and from the use of composite characters?
