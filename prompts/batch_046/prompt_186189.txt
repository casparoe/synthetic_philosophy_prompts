My advisor in the philosophy of social science has given me her rulings on whether cultural values should count as genuine causes in historical and sociological explanations. Her verdicts so far are:

- The spread of double-entry bookkeeping through Renaissance Italian city-states: not caused by culture; it was a functional adaptation to long-distance trade.
- The persistence of foot-binding in parts of rural China into the 1940s despite state prohibition: yes, caused by culture; the value placed on domestic femininity sustained the practice independently of economic utility.
- The decline of aristocratic dueling in nineteenth-century Europe: not caused by culture; honor codes adjusted after the state's monopoly on violence made dueling irrational.
- The higher literacy rates in Protestant regions of eighteenth-century Germany compared to Catholic ones: yes, caused by culture; the value of direct scriptural access motivated learning beyond school availability.
- The survival of Orthodox communal religious practices under Soviet collectivization: yes, caused by culture; embedded communal values resisted institutional restructuring.
- The rise of "hustle culture" among American professionals in the late twentieth century: not caused by culture; it is an ideological gloss on structural labor-market precarity.

Next week's case is the rapid adoption of plant-based meat substitutes among middle-class urban consumers in Northern Europe over the past decade, despite higher prices and the absence of state mandates. Predict her verdict—would she say culture genuinely caused this shift? Then identify the two competing rules that both fit her earlier track record: one that treats culture as causal only when it supplies a distinctive motivational orientation that material conditions do not generate, and one that treats culture as causal only when it is embedded in durable social practices or institutions capable of resisting or redirecting structural pressure. Say which rule she is more likely running, and name one further hypothetical case—one involving a widespread but institutionally unembedded cultural ideal that disappears immediately when economic conditions shift—that would settle which rule she actually holds.
