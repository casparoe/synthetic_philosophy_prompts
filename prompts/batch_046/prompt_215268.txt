At a pub after our seminar on climate ethics, two mates — Maya and Leo — got into it about what we actually owe future people. Maya said intergenerational justice means we shouldn't just avoid catastrophic harm; we should aim to maximise the long-run wellbeing of whoever comes next, or at least not leave them worse off in absolute terms, so climate policy ought to pursue the optimal mitigation path we can calculate. Leo said thats to quick: given radical uncertainty about technology and values centuries out, the idea of optimising is hubris, and morally all we need is a stability threshold — a climate system that can still support autonomous societies — once we hit that floor, extra carbon cuts are supererogatory, not required by justice. Maya replied that good enough is just a weasel phrase that lets us off the hook; if we pick a cheap path that barely clears the threshold, we might still deny future generations the expansive opportunities we enjoy. Leo answered that optimisation pretends we know weights for goods we can't foresee, whereas satisficing respects our epistemic limits; he said robustness is just satisficing in a fancy suit, and that past investment in fossil infrastructure shouldn't lock us into over-correction. Maya came back that epistemic humility doesn't justify doing less, only avoiding false precision — we can still adopt precautionary targets without claiming to maximise. They asked me to referee but I was to tired. Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who's right about each step, who wins overall, and what they're both missing?
