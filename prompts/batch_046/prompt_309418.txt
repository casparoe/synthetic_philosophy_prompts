Question:
A technology consultancy has proposed an automated corporate ethics engine: it encodes company policies as formal axioms, assigns numerical integrity scores to actions, and derives verdicts about employee conduct through algorithmic inference. A critic claims that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem proves that, provided the engine is consistent and sufficiently expressive, there will necessarily be cases of employee behaviour that are ethically correct but that the system can neither approve nor reject. Evaluate this claim. In your answer, clarify exactly what conditions Gödel's theorem requires of a formal system, and discuss whether an ethics engine of this kind is likely to satisfy them.

Student answer:
Gödel's first incompleteness theorem demonstrates that any consistent formal system capable of expressing elementary arithmetic must contain propositions that are true yet unprovable within that system. Applied to the proposed corporate ethics engine, the argument has some force: if the system formalises policies as axioms and can encode numerical case identifiers, it may possess the requisite expressive power. Consequently, there could be ethically correct assessments of employee behaviour that the algorithm cannot derive from its own rules. The critic correctly notes that consistency is a necessary precondition; an inconsistent system escapes the theorem but is useless. Where the argument overreaches is in treating ethical concepts—such as professional integrity or undue influence—as if they were precisely defined like arithmetical relations. In reality, the vagueness of moral language makes formalisation doubtful. Furthermore, Gödel's second result implies that if the system could demonstrate its own consistency, that demonstration would itself be unreliable. Thus, while the engine might assist decision-making, one should not expect it to capture every valid ethical judgment.
