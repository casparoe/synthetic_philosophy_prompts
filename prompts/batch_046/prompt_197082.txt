For my philosophy-of-art seminar, I am trying to fix the evaluation criteria before I compare existing accounts of why originals carry value that perfect replicas and forgeries do not. The familiar cases are clear enough: a forged Vermeer executed with period-correct pigments and brushwork but lacking any causal link to Vermeer; a museum replica that preserves every perceptible property of the original; and an original whose surface has been heavily restored, altering its physical state while preserving its causal history. To keep the specification from being merely an inventory of standard puzzles, I want it tested against two less-discussed scenarios my instructor introduced. First, “derivational twins”: works generated by an algorithm trained exhaustively on every physical feature and brushstroke of an artist’s complete oeuvre, using identical materials, producing objects that share no direct causal chain with the artist’s hand but are computationally derived from the totality of that hand’s output. Second, “distributed originals”: works the artist intentionally produced as physically identical multiples—each signed, approved, and released—such that no single instantiation can claim uniqueness, yet each is officially “by” the artist.

Please lay out the full desiderata: which phenomena any adequate theory must explain (the standard forgery and restoration cases, plus the two injected ones); which distinctions it must preserve without collapse (authenticity versus uniqueness versus authorship versus aesthetic merit; having value versus having more value; market value versus artistic value); which results from art history, conservation science, or perceptual psychology it cannot contradict; and which pairs of requirements you take to be jointly unsatisfiable—especially where causal-origin conditions clash with experiential-identity conditions in the derivational-twin case, and where intentional multiplicity undermines uniqueness-based conditions. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for a theory to count as addressing the problem at all, and which are camp-specific preferences (for instance, demands that value be publicly accessible, demands that the market’s judgments be explained rather than justified, or demands that uniqueness be essential). Then, briefly, how a causal-historical account, an experiential-indistinguishability account, and an institutional account fare against the list. I want an audit ordered by severity: rank the tensions between desiderata rather than listing them as equally damaging, and flag the single requirement that, if retained, eliminates the most popular theories. No bibliography or citation list is needed—just the criteria, their motivating cases, and where they break.
