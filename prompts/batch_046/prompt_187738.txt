Write a villanelle—nineteen lines arranged as five tercets followed by one quatrain, with two refrains that rhyme on their shared terminal sound and interweave in the standard pattern—to take up Nietzsche's thought of eternal recurrence. Speak in the voice of the demon who reveals, in the well-known aphorism in The Gay Science, that every detail of a life will return in identical sequence, down to the spider crawling in moonlight and the sleeper's sudden awakening. Do not treat the recurrence as a simple cosmological doctrine about circular time, though you should let that reading hover in the background through reference to the notebook argument from finite forces and infinite duration; instead, make the poem's central tension the ethical test Nietzsche proposes, the demand that one affirm this identical repetition as amor fati. Assign the first refrain to the descriptive claim of inevitable repetition—every pain, every joy, every silence in the same order—and assign the second to the prescriptive response, which must change its emotional weight each time it returns: first as dread, then as provocation, finally as the condition for a life that could be willed again. Because the villanelle mechanically repeats its lines, use enjambment, altered punctuation, and shifting adjacent imagery so that the returning phrases accumulate new meaning, enacting structurally the very transformation from fate to freedom that the idea demands. The quatrain's closing couplet should bring both refrains together, but resist dissolving their tension; let the rhyme hold the cosmological inevitability and the ethical affirmation in suspension, so that the poem closes without sermonizing about acceptance or shrugging at cosmic necessity. The result should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Nietzsche recognizes both the cosmological sketch and the demon's existential challenge, and should stand on its own as verse for a reader who knows neither.
