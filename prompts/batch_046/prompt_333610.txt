Hey -- writing a feature on philosophical self-traps and need a sanity check on an argument a friend built. It's about Quine and moral values, but with a twist I added: the "moralized framework" idea, where you keep every physical law identical but let your ontology quantify over obligations and rights, and still predict every observation exactly. The argument seems to show Quine's naturalism either forces moral realism or collapses science into nominalism, which feels like overkill. Here are the steps:

(1) For Quine, to exist is just to be the value of a bound variable in our best overall scientific theory.
(2) Moral properties -- injustice, cruelty, obligation -- don't show up in the quantificational commitments of physics, biology, or neuroscience.
(3) So inside the scientific framework, moral values have no real ontological standing; they're cultural posits at best, like phlogiston or witchcraft.
(4) But Quine also says our choice of framework is underdetermined by evidence: empirically equivalent theories can carry different ontologies.
(5) That means we can build a "moralized" twin framework -- same equations, same predictions -- but with obligations and rights sitting right alongside electrons.
(6) Quine keeps math in the ontology not because numbers cause anything, but because science is indispensibly formulated with them.
(7) Social science and evolutionary biology are indispensibly formulated with normative terms: you can't model cooperation collapse or institutional stability without quantifying over obligations and sanctions.
(8) So if indispensability saves mathematics, consistency demands it save moral properties too; otherwise Quine is applying a double standard.
(9) Therefore either he accepts moral realism, or he has to ditch mathematics and let science slide into nominalism, which is absurb.
(10) Obviously he won't do (10), but then the argument proves too much: applied consistently, his naturalism either stuffs moral entities into the catalog or destroys the catalog itself.

Where does the slip happen? Is it (4) to (5) -- that empirical equivalence doesn't license arbitrary ontological inflation? Is (7) wrong because normative language in science is always paraphrasable away? Or is there a hidden scope error between formal indispensability and empirical indispensability? I'd rather keep (1)-(3) if I can, but I'm not attached to (9). Which step do you reject, and what's the cost?
