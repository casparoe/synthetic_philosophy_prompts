1. classic quantum suicide device: quantum RNG kills you instantly unless it measures |1>. do it once. verdict: don't. not because i think consciousness magically jumps to survivor branches, but it still feels like an unacceptible risk, even in MWI.

2. cancer treatment that works in 5% of worlds, kills you painlessly in 95%. verdict: actually yes, take it? you're dying anyway, and the survivor branch gets cured.

3. russian roulette with a quantum twist: pull trigger, in branches where gun fires you're dead, others live. verdict: don't do it. but this looks structurally identical to case 2 — mostly death branches, a few live ones. so why different?

4. you're one of infinitly many copies in identical situations. you can press a button that saves exactly one copy from torture, nothing for the others. verdict: press it, obviously. but if suffering is infinite across copies, does saving one copy actually change anything?

5. quantum coin bet: heads you win $1M, tails you suffer a mild headache in that branch and survive. verdict: take it? but then in infinite copies, infinite headaches.

the cancer-drug vs suicide-box tension is the one i keep circling back to. is there a principle — maybe about whether the branching is under your control, or about identity across branches, or about expected suffering summed over observers — that gets all these? and if so, what does it say about the case i haven't settled: should you ever rig a quantum suicide device to skip a long painful terminal process, knowing you'd only experience either quick death or cure?

i want the priciple stated precisely, with which clause is motivated by which case, its verdict on some unlisted case (like the skip-the-dying-process one), and an honest admission of which of my gut verdicts it has to throw overboard to stay coherent.
