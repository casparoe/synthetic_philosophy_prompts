I'm currently drafting a chapter on ontological identification when distinguishing information is permanently epistemically inaccessible, and I've been stress-testing my own judgments against concrete scenarios with a colleague in philosophy of science rather than surveying the literature. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind the pattern of my intuitions, or whether they fracture under scrutiny, and I am particularly interested in whether the principle needs to vary by kind of entity or by the type of inaccessibility involved.

Here are five cases with my verdicts. First, two gravitational-wave observatories record signatures identical in every measured parameter, but catastrophic corruption has permanently destroyed the synchronization logs that would reveal whether they detected one event from opposite orientations or two simultaneous distinct events; I am inclined to list only one event in our ontology, though I feel some unease about multiplying entities without positive reason. Second, a paleontological record is split by an unbridgeable stratigraphic gap, with fossil assemblages on either side morphologically identical in every recoverable trait but with no intermediate specimen ever recoverable; I tend to classify them as one continuous species despite the broken causal chain. Third, consider a modal case in which two possible worlds differ solely by a pure haecceity swap between two particles that are otherwise relationally identical in every respect, with no observable consequence in either world; my judgment is that this does not constitute a genuine ontological difference, so we should not multiply worlds for it. Fourth, suppose our best unified physical theory mathematically identifies what were previously treated as distinct entities—a particular dark-matter candidate and a dark-energy fluctuation mode—but the derivation depends on formalisms permanently beyond any empirical verification or observational access; if the theoretical unification is otherwise well-motivated, I think we should eventually identify them as one underlying structure rather than maintain separate commitments. Fifth, in apparent tension with the preceding, imagine a being behaviorally, structurally, and psychologically identical to a specific historical person, yet created through an entirely independent causal process with no shared origin, no information transfer, and no historical continuity whatsoever; here my verdict is firm that they are not the same person, despite the perfect qualitative match.

These judgments seem to pull in opposite directions—sometimes I allow identification despite missing linking information, and sometimes I withhold it despite perfect qualitative identity—so I am unsure whether one principle can save all five. Is there a unified account of ontology identification that delivers these verdicts, perhaps by distinguishing kinds of inaccessibility or kinds of entities? If coherence forces me to drop one verdict, which should go, and what does the surviving principle predict for a case I have not considered—namely, two permanently isolated laboratories that independently synthesize molecular structures with identical chemical and functional properties through entirely different synthetic pathways? Should our ontology recognize one kind of synthetic structure, or two?
