I spent my career as a condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been trying to understand legal philosophy—particularly the debate between legal positivism and natural law that I keep encountering in my reading. I have become fixated on a specific institutional pattern I observe in modern environmental and administrative regulation. Regulatory agencies promulgate binding technical interpretation guidelines that determine liability for pollution or safety violations, yet the enabling statutes rarely define key terms—such as significant harm or unreasonable risk—with any precision. The agencies defend these guidelines as mere discoveries of pre-existing legal meaning rather than new legislation. When courts review them, however, they do not limit themselves to asking whether the interpretation is textually plausible within the statute; they ask whether the interpretation respects what judges call the moral architecture of the statute, and they strike down guidelines that are technically consistent with the text but that distribute burdens in ways the court regards as morally arbitrary. At the same time, those courts reject broader arguments that a guideline should be invalid simply because it produces unjust outcomes in general, insisting that they are not enforcing natural law and that law and morality remain conceptually separate.

This practice seems to treat legal validity as partly constituted by a bounded moral-coherence condition—extreme moral arbitrariness can apparently defeat legal status—while denying that ordinary moral wrongness does the same. What implicit theory of law does this institutional division of labor presuppose? Can a sophisticated Hartian view, with its emphasis on a socially accepted rule of recognition, accommodate such a moral-coherence filter without collapsing into Fuller's claim that law must satisfy certain internal moral conditions in order to be law at all? Or does the practice point toward something like a threshold naturalism, where only severe moral failure removes legal standing, leaving a core domain for pure source-based validity? Reconstruct the implicit jurisprudential theory operating in this agency-court interaction, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and explain what would have to change in both administrative practice and judicial review if that implicit theory were replaced by a pure source-based positivism on one side, or by a robust natural-law doctrine on the other.
