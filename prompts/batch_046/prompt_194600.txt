I have been reading the literature on virtue ethics and the situationist critique, and I keep noticing a recurring argumentative move that I do not have a name for. Here is what it looks like in practice.

A virtue ethicist begins by defending a trait—say, honesty or courage—as a global psychological disposition that should manifest consistently across different situations. Empirical findings are then cited showing weak cross-situational correlation: people behave honestly in close friendships but not in workplace competition, or courageously in physical danger but not in social confrontation. Rather than abandoning the trait, the defender narrows it: it is not global honesty, but honesty-in-intimate-relationships, or not courage simpliciter, but physical-courage-under-visible-threat. Critics respond that these narrower patterns are merely behavioral regularities, lacking the deep psychological unity that was supposed to distinguish virtue from habit or social role. The defender then replies that the unity still exists, but it is local and relational, embedded in specific contexts rather than spread across the personality. If further studies show that even these local patterns dissolve under minor contextual shifts—say, the presence of an authority figure or a small change in incentive—the trait is narrowed once more, now tied to a highly specific configuration of relationships and conditions. At each stage, the defender insists that the normative core has been preserved and that the narrowing reveals the true, more nuanced scope of the virtue, not that the concept is being patched to survive refutation.

What I am after is not the situationist critique itself, nor the generic idea of saving a theory with qualifications. I want to know whether there is an established concept for this particular kind of progressive fragmentation: the retreat from a broad, psychologically unified character trait to ever smaller, context-bound versions, where each retreat is presented as conceptual clarification rather than as an ad hoc defense, and where the concept retains its full normative weight even as its explanatory and predictive content shrinks. Does this pattern have a specific name in moral psychology or philosophy of science? How exactly does it differ from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, and from what Lakatos called a degenerating research program? If there is no established term, what should we call it, and how can we draw its boundaries—distinguishing it from legitimate conceptual refinement on one side and from the complete abandonment of character-based evaluation on the other? I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
