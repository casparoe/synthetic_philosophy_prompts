I'm drafting a short essay that attempts to use Bostrom-style simulation reasoning to probe the psychological and normative structure of far-future fanaticism—the kind of urgency often defended in longtermist circles about shaping outcomes over cosmological timescales. Rather than treating the simulation argument as a direct debunker of longtermism, I want to distinguish three interacting layers where it might dampen fanatical commitment without requiring us to reject longtermism outright.

First, an epistemic layer: if we assign non-trivial credence to being in a simulation, then our confidence in the physical constants, cosmological timelines, and resource constraints of base reality should drop sharply. This does not prove the far future is impossible, but it introduces a kind of radical model-uncertainty about whether the multi-billion-year trajectories longtermists worry about are even physically available in the world that actually runs our computation.

Second, a normative layer: I want to examine whether simulated beings possess the same moral standing as base-reality beings, and whether this undermines or amplifies concern. If simulated minds lack standing, then longtermist projects that aim to preserve value across deep time may be preserving value only within a disposable substrate. But if they do have standing, then the base reality might eventually run vastly more simulations, and the moral weight of the far future could actually be larger—just distributed across simulated rather than physical space.

Third, a strategic layer: longtermist planning typically assumes a kind of steady-state environment where extinction risks accumulate gradually. If we are simulated, however, there is an external agent or system that can terminate, modify, or fork our run. This introduces a discontinuous, exogenous risk that does not map neatly onto the usual taxonomy of existential risks like asteroid impacts or misaligned AI. The presence of an external shutdown mechanism might make longtermist investments in physical infrastructure or institutional continuity look especially fragile.

My central worry is that I am conflating these layers—especially treating epistemic uncertainty about base reality as if it automatically deflates normative urgency. I am also unsure whether the external termination point is genuinely distinct from standard existential-risk framing, or whether it is just a rebranded version of the same vulnerability.

I would appreciate comments on whether this three-layer structure holds together, and specifically on two points: first, whether there is a hidden assumption that simulated worlds lack moral weight that I need to defend or reject explicitly; and second, whether the strategic layer actually adds anything beyond standard concerns about discontinuous catastrophic risks. I am not looking for a literature review or citations—just a conceptual check on whether the argument avoids obvious confusions and whether the framing is interesting enough to be worth developing into a full post.
