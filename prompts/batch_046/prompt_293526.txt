I cover science and institutional design, and I have been examining an asymmetry in how one major medical accreditation system evaluates clinical expertise. In surgical residency, candidates now face a “silent simulation” assessment: they must execute emergency procedures in a high-fidelity simulator and are graded solely on temporal consistency, adaptive error-correction, and improvisational adjustment when conditions shift unexpectedly. The rubric explicitly excludes verbal explanation, theoretical justification, or reference to propositional anatomical or physiological knowledge. In the same healthcare system, however, critical-care nursing certification relies almost entirely on standardized written case-study analysis and declarative reasoning about pharmacology and pathophysiology; no live simulation or observed performance component is required for licensure. The result is that surgical expertise is treated as purely embodied, non-propositional skill, while nursing expertise is treated as purely propositional knowledge, despite both roles requiring rapid judgment under uncertainty.

What philosophical commitments about the distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that does this institutional split presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does the surgical protocol presuppose an anti-intellectualist view that practical mastery is independent of propositional knowledge, or can an intellectualist framework explain the exclusion of verbal justification? Does the nursing exam presuppose that clinical judgment is fully reducible to declarative reasoning? Say whether the two practices are philosophically consistent with each other, distinguish what each strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it well, identify where the design is philosophically underdetermined so that several rival accounts of practical knowledge could license the same split, and describe what either assessment regime would have to change if it were rebuilt on the view that knowing-how and knowing-that are neither fully separable nor reducible to one another.
