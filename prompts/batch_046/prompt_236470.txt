In strategic-studies circles I follow, the debate over whether historical analogies—Munich 1938, Vietnam, the so-called Thucydides trap—systematically mislead policymakers has reached an impasse that feels less like a clash of evidence and more like a mutual failure to hear the other side. Both camps concede that analogies are imperfect and that structural differences abound; neither denies that leaders invoke history. Yet the analogists insist these comparisons are indispensable heuristics for navigating uncertainty, while the skeptics treat every invocation as a rhetorical cover for predetermined policy, rendering the analogy epistemically hollow. Is this stall substantive—perhaps a hidden empirical disagreement about whether analogies actually shape deliberation or only justify it post hoc—or is it conceptual, with one side asking about predictive accuracy and the other about democratic legitimacy? Or is it bedrock: an irreconcilable intuition about whether human affairs are sufficiently recurrent to license comparison at all? Diagnose the disagreement using what each position actually commits to, and tell me what would genuinely advance it—a formal taxonomy of analogical mapping, archival study of cabinet-level reasoning, or a conceptual separation of predictive from justificatory uses—versus what would merely restate the stalemate in sharper language.
