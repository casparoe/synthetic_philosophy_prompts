I read a summary of Benjamin Franklin’s project of moral perfection in a popular biography, and I want to check whether it holds up. The writer describes a thirteen-week boot camp for the soul: Franklin isolated one virtue each week—starting with temperance, then silence, then order—while deliberately ignoring the other twelve, and he recorded every failure in a private ledger. By cycling through all thirteen four times a year, he eventually eliminated every fault and reached near-perfect self-mastery, especially in humility, which he placed last because it is the crown of a finished character. The summary also claims that Franklin believed this private ledger-keeping made public institutions like courts and churches obsolete, since a society of self-perfecting individuals would never conflict. Finally, it suggests that Franklin’s well-known admission that he never achieved perfect humility was mostly a rhetorical flourish, because his own records show almost no slips after the first year, and that he essentially treated moral improvement as the same kind of rational cost-benefit calculation he used in his printing business. I suspect some of this is wrong, but I cannot tell where the misrepresentations are versus the accurate parts. Which claims would Franklin himself accept, which ones conflate different parts of his project, and does the conclusion—that private self-discipline can replace collective moral structures—actually follow from anything he wrote?
