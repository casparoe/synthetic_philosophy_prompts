Benjamin Franklin pursued moral perfection through a regimented scheme of thirteen virtues, each assigned a day of the week, with failures recorded in a ledger and reviewed in cyclical repetition; he eventually abandoned the project, conceding that human nature resisted such geometric regularity. Imagine that Franklin, retaining his empirical scepticism but also his faith in gradualist self-improvement, is presented with a contemporary technology: a biometric wearable coupled to a large-language-model interlocutor that performs continuous, granular moral accounting. Rather than weekly retrospection on discrete actions, the device tracks pre-emptive physiological markers—galvanic skin response during conversations, pupillary dilation when encountering dissent, micro-variations in vocal prosody—and assigns virtue-scores in real time, feeding back nudges through an earpiece before the user has completed a moral failure. The system claims to operationalise Franklin’s own principle of precept and example by preventing vice before it becomes habit, yet it eliminates the reflective gap that Franklin considered essential for genuine habituation. Predict, with explicit reference to Franklin’s documented reasoning about habituation, the limits of artificial regularity, and the social dimension of virtue, what position he would take on adopting this device. Would he regard the elimination of the weekly review cycle and the substitution of biometric proxies for conscious self-assessment as an extension of his method or as a corruption of it? In particular, address how he might reconcile his belief that virtue must become habitual through conscious repetition with the device’s promise of unconscious optimisation. Avoid anachronistic references to modern philosophical literature; ground your answer in Franklin’s own conceptual vocabulary—industry, order, temperance, humility, and the distinction between appearing virtuous and being so.
