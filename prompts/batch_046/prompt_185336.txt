I am preparing a unit for an advanced undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and my students have already worked through the standard Frankfurt cases—the neuroscientist with the hidden chip, Black and Jones with the prior sign, and the usual variants involving external manipulators. For the next session I want them to confront a more difficult design problem: construct a realistic, non-science-fictional thought experiment in which the Frankfurt-style blocker is entirely endogenous rather than external. The scenario should involve an agent who commits a clearly wrongful act—something like embezzling from a trusted organization—while fully aware of what she is doing and without any external coercion. Crucially, there must be an internal blocker embedded in her own deliberative psychology: for instance, a deeply automatized defensive heuristic that, had her reflective reasoning begun to favor honesty, would have immediately overridden that reasoning and channeled her back toward the wrongful choice, not through conscious suppression but through an involuntary restructuring of her attention and weighting of reasons. The case must meet four joint specifications: first, the agent retains the physical and psychological capacity to do otherwise, so that an external observer could not identify any outside constraint preventing alternative action; second, despite this, her own mental architecture makes the wrongful choice inevitable once she begins deliberating, because the internal blocker activates deterministically in response to any deviation; third, our intuitive reactive attitudes—resentment, blame, the expectation of apology—remain firmly directed at her; and fourth, the entire setup is explainable in one seminar-length paragraph with no appeal to demons, perfect predictors, brain implants, or hypothetical technology.

Once the scenario is built, provide the check: state precisely what judgment about moral responsibility is at stake, identify the assessment method (counterfactual robustness of the will, reasons-responsiveness, or reactive-attitude fit), specify the relevant nearby worlds used to test the principle of alternative possibilities, and explain why a PAP defender can claim that the first condition saves their view, why a Frankfurt-style opponent argues that the second condition defeats PAP without defeating responsibility, and why a reasons-responsive theorist might distinguish guidance control from regulative control to split the difference. Then include an honest design note: identify which feature—the internalization of the blocker, the preservation of conscious awareness, or the absence of external manipulation—does the actual philosophical work, describe exactly what happens to all three verdicts if that feature is weakened or removed, and confirm that the conclusions are generated by applying the theories to the case rather than being stipulated in advance. Avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the exercise should be answerable entirely from the conceptual resources already in play.
