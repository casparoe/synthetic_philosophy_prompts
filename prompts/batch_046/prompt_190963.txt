For a course on philosophy of history and environmental causation, I have been working through recent historiography on the 1815 Tambora eruption and on nineteenth-century cholera epidemics, and I keep encountering a particular argumentative structure that I cannot place precisely within the standard vocabulary. In one narrative, the eruption is said to have 'forced' fiscal restructuring across several European states by 'cutting off' grain supplies—verbs that imply directed, almost tactical action—while the author immediately disavows any anthropomorphism and frames the event as pure structural constraint. In a parallel epidemiological account, a pathogen is described as 'exploiting' urban density and trade corridors, language that sounds strategically selective, followed by a clarification that this is only evolutionary pressure acting on random variation. In both cases, the non-human cause is narrated as having struck particular institutional vulnerabilities rather than others, and its precise scale and timing are treated as selectively consequential, even though no intentionality is attributed to it.

What I seem to be observing is not quite the cruder form of environmental determinism—since these texts consistently emphasize that local political choices mediated the outcomes—nor is it the symmetric agency granted to human and non-human entities in an actant-based network, because the human actors remain fully intentional while the volcano or germ does not. Instead, the pattern assigns what I would call directed causality without intentionality: the event operates with a kind of vector, hitting specific nodes in a social or biological system, and the explanation often depends on the counterfactual idea that a slightly different environmental event—a smaller eruption, a different pathogen strain—would have produced a markedly different political or social result. This counterfactual specificity seems central to the causal claim, yet it is paired with an explicit denial that the cause had any aim or strategic purpose.

Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy of history, science studies, or causal metaphysics? If there is a term, I want to understand exactly how it differs from structural causation, from Latourian actant-based explanation, and from ordinary counterfactual dependence—particularly whether the pattern genuinely requires that counterfactual specificity about altered events, or whether it collapses into standard causal overdetermination once the rhetorical language is stripped away. If there is no stable concept for it, I would like a proposed definition with clear boundary conditions: what distinguishes it from mere metaphor, from full-blown agency attribution, and from deterministic environmental explanation, and which cases sit at its edges—say, climate events that reshape trade routes versus those that merely coincide with them? I am not looking for a bare label; I want the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its limits lie.
