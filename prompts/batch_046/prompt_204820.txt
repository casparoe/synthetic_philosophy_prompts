You have been asked to deliver the opening remarks for a three-day workshop, "The Lot and the City," convening democratic theorists, municipal planners, civic technologists, and skeptical elected officials. The purpose is to design a pilot citizens' assembly—selected by lot—on urban climate adaptation. You are not a neutral moderator; you hold a specific, somewhat unorthodox philosophical commitment that you want to shape the entire framing of the event. You believe the primary justification for sortition is not epistemic (that randomly selected citizens will deliberate more wisely) nor purely descriptive (that they will mirror demographic categories), but what you call the anti-mandate thesis: democratic authorization is wrongly conflated with personal election, and the lot's value lies in stripping away the narrative of earned mandate so that recommendations must be evaluated solely on their reasons. This view should do real structural work in your speech. It should determine what you can celebrate about the assembly—do not praise the members as especially wise, representative in a personal sense, or endowed with a popular mandate—and what you must refuse to promise—do not assure officials that the assembly's output will be automatically legitimate, binding, or a substitute for electoral accountability. You should also introduce one further conceptual distinction of your own: that in this framework, the selected citizens do not "stand for" a constituency but "stand in" for the bare statistical equality of citizens; consequently, the assembly's authority is borrowed, not granted, and its weight comes precisely from the fact that no individual member can claim to have deserved the seat. Your audience is split between theorists who will recognize the tension with standard democratic legitimacy theory and practitioners who will worry about competence, liability, and political cover. Write a polished, approximately six-minute address that speaks to both halves without simplifying the idea for either. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or named-author quotations; if you invoke a concept from democratic theory, state it in your own terms. The tone should be formal, welcoming, intellectually serious, and deliberately unsettled—ending with a shared problem rather than a resolution.
