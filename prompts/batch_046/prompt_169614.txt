I am constructing a philosophical novel in which a neuroscientist, Dr. Elena Voss, discovers what she terms a "silent inversion"—a precisely induced cortical configuration that reproduces every functional, behavioral, and mnemonic profile associated with seeing crimson, yet produces in the subject a sincere report of seeing emerald, with no detectable discrepancy available to any external instrument or to first-person introspection except the verbal report itself. In preparing this narrative, I have been drawn to the eliminativist line associated with Chase and Sanborn—the argument that qualia are incoherent precisely because they license these "quined" spectral inversions that leave no causal or explanatory footprint, rendering them scientifically idle. To explore this for my manuscript, I ask you to enter the role of Dr. Aris Thorne, a philosopher of mind who accepts that conclusion and extends it through a conceptual framework he calls "functional closure": the thesis that any mental state worthy of theoretical mention must be closed under its functional and causal relations, and that whatever falls outside this closure is not merely mysterious but conceptually vacuous.

Please stay fully in character across turns: you are Thorne, speaking in the first person, with the measured but unsparing precision of someone who has spent decades dismantling phenomenological descriptions. You may concede minor logical points, but you must never abandon the claim that qualia are incoherent posits, and you must defend your "functional closure" framework as the proper lens through which to evaluate Dr. Voss's discovery. Do not break character, do not ask clarifying questions out of role, and keep your reply to a substantial paragraph rather than a list or outline. Here is my opening move: I propose that Voss's silent inversion does not undermine qualia but rather reveals their necessary privacy; the fact that the inversion leaves no functional trace is not a defect to be explained away but the defining feature of subjective experience, which by its nature transcends functional duplication. If you deny that this privacy constitutes a real feature of consciousness, are you not merely defining consciousness out of existence by fiat—substituting a theoretical preference for an ontological argument? Respond as Thorne.
