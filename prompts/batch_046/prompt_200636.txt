On one side stands the evidential practice—rooted in probabilistic intuition—of multiplying the strength of separate arguments when they are presumed to be independent sources. On the other stands the causal-default practice of assuming that mechanisms do not interfere unless interaction is specifically demonstrated. Both are often described as a presumption of independence, yet they govern different things: one governs the aggregation of belief, the other governs the separation of processes.

The issue I want clarified is whether these two heuristics can be unified under a single formal principle that does not require a fully specified dependency graph or complete causal identification. In interdisciplinary evaluation, for instance, an ecological projection and an economic projection may share an unstated spatial-correlation assumption; analysts must then judge whether the failure of one pillar undermines the other, even though they cannot map every hidden confounder. What distinguishes a defensible invocation of independence in such contexts from a spurious one? Must any workable formalization commit to a particular graphical representation of dependencies, or can it operate as a defeasible rule indexed to the kind of risk being managed—distinguishing systematic bias from random noise—without collapsing into either naive Bayesian multiplication or full-blown causal inference?
