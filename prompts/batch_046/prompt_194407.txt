Argue that multimodal grounding—anchoring linguistic symbols in perceptual or sensorimotor embeddings—does not solve the symbol grounding problem but displaces it onto the binding problem of distributed representations. Focus on the distinction between associative feature correlation and compositional symbol-role independence: present the case of a system that learns to classify “red cube” and “blue sphere” but fails to generalize to “red sphere” without further training, and explain why this systematicity failure reveals an absence of discrete, variable-bound semantic roles rather than a mere deficit of sensory data. Defend the claim that genuine grounding requires an architecture enforcing syntactic independence between symbol and role, so that representations can be decomposed and recombined without altering joint correlation strengths. Argue that scaling associative models alone cannot supply this structure, because increased correlation density in high-dimensional spaces does not entail compositional decomposition. Conclude by explaining why this relocated problem is more demanding than the original: if binding requires explicit structural mechanisms for discrete role assignment, then grounding is not achieved by adding modalities but depends on solving a prior architectural question about how continuous neural spaces generate genuinely recombinable concepts.
