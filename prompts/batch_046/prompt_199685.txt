I am a high school student writing an independent philosophy essay on Leo Tolstoy’s account of art, and I am asking for rigorous critical comments rather than a bibliography or further reading list. Tolstoy treats art as a conscious, human-to-human infection of feeling: an artist who has genuinely lived through an emotion uses external signs to transmit that feeling to others, and the value of the result depends on whether the transmitted feeling promotes human unity and moral improvement. Rather than applying this framework to conventional paintings or novels, I want to test it against images produced by generative models, but I want to avoid the obvious question of whether a machine can “feel.” Instead, I propose a mediated-infection criterion: an AI-generated image counts as Tolstoyan art only if the emotional response it produces can be traced back to a specific, pre-existing human feeling-state embedded in the training data, and only if the transmission is not structurally distorted by an optimization layer—such as an engagement-maximizing algorithm—that amplifies or warps the feeling for non-artistic ends.

I apply this criterion to two cases. Case A: a model generates a quiet image of a solitary figure in snow that evokes precise grief; I argue it passes, because the grief traces to original human sources and the image is not calibrated to escalate the emotion. Case B: a model generates an image engineered to provoke outrage at a public figure, tuned by metrics that reward arousal; I argue it fails, not because outrage is always immoral, but because the algorithmic selection and amplification insert a non-human mediator that breaks the direct infection Tolstoy requires.

My specific questions are these. First, does my mediated-infection criterion misrepresent Tolstoy’s emphasis on the artist’s conscious intention, since in Case A there is no single conscious artist directing the infection? Second, is there a hidden circularity in using the viewer’s emotional response both to identify the infection and to validate its authentic human source? Third, could Case B be defended by arguing that the outrage remains originally human and that the algorithm merely selects and distributes it, rather than creating a new emotional object? I want comments that are accessible but conceptually sharp, and I particularly want you to flag the points where my procedure depends on a judgment call that cannot be eliminated, and to say what the argument cannot do—whether it collapses into a claim about authorship rather than about art.
