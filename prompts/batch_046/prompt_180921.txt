I've been thinking about what philosophers call Darwin's strange inversion—where evolutionary processes generate apparently designed competence without any comprehending designer—and about Turing's parallel inversion, in which formal computation produces behaviorally intelligent outputs without an understanding subject manipulating symbols. Both seem to decouple functional success from mental grasp, and together they appear to establish a general principle: that competence never entails comprehension. Applying this to present artificial systems, if a large language model demonstrates high-level philosophical reasoning, doesn't the conjunction of these two inversions place the burden of proof entirely on anyone who claims comprehension is present? More specifically, is there a coherent "double-inversion" argument showing that algorithmic competence is doubly disqualified from involving understanding—once because it is mechanical in the Turing sense, and again because it is non-biological in the Darwinian sense—so that attributing genuine comprehension to such a system violates the very logic both inversions established?
