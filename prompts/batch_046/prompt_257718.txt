Student answer: Darwin and Turing both reverse the usual expectation that intelligent performance requires an underlying understanding of what is being done. In Darwin's case, evolutionary adaptation produces complex functional structures—eyes, camouflage, echolocation—through random variation and selective retention over generations, with no designer or organism comprehending the final result. The competence of the organism is therefore explained without invoking any comprehension on the part of nature or the individual. Turing performs a structurally identical inversion: in the imitation game, an observer is entitled to attribute intelligence to a machine solely on the basis of its external behavioral outputs, without requiring direct access to or verification of any internal mental comprehension.

The main asymmetry is temporal. Darwin's inversion is diachronic and causal: it accounts for how competence is produced across evolutionary time in the absence of a comprehending agent. Turing's inversion is synchronic and epistemic: it concerns the conditions under which an observer may legitimately ascribe intelligence during a single interaction, leaving open whether internal comprehension exists but is unobservable.

Regarding Turing's 1948 learning machine, the student-machine acquires competence through reward and punishment, modifying its internal connections in response to experience. This is essentially the Darwinian mechanism compressed into one lifespan: competing internal pathways are selected for success, so the machine achieves competence without ever grasping the rules it follows. Thus the two inversions converge, with Turing extending Darwin's logic from populations to individual artificial systems.
