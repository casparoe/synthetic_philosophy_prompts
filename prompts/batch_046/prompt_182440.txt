For more than seven decades, the debate triggered by Milton Friedman’s defense of unrealistic assumptions in scientific modeling—commonly read as the claim that a model’s predictive accuracy matters far more than the literal truth of its premises—has cycled through remarkably familiar positions without clear convergence. On one side, defenders treat idealizations such as perfect competition, rational-expectation agents, or point-mass representations as indispensable instruments: they isolate stable causal tendencies, yield precise predictions, and can be relaxed in successive approximations. They accuse critics of committing a category mistake by demanding literal descriptive accuracy from heuristic devices. On the other side, critics in the realist tradition argue that when core assumptions are systematically false across every application domain, the model cannot genuinely explain why its predictions succeed; it offers at best an instrumental correlation and at worst conceals the very structural mechanisms that must be understood for sound policy intervention. The literature repeatedly returns to the same examples—macroeconomic forecasting, market equilibrium, climate parameterization—yet defenders continue to cite predictive triumphs while critics continue to cite explanatory failures, and neither camp has revised its core stance.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not one disagreement but at least two debates running in parallel under the single label “realism about assumptions.” The first concerns predictive adequacy versus explanatory depth. The second, largely unacknowledged, concerns a hidden taxonomy of idealization: defenders tend to interpret “unrealistic” as counterfactual isolation of a mechanism that remains invariant under relaxation, whereas critics interpret the same term as domain-independent falsehood that corrupts every inference drawn from the model. If this is correct, the sides are not answering the same question. Defenders ask whether a model is robust as its simplifying conditions are progressively dropped; critics ask whether the model’s posited entities and relations correspond to actual structures in the target system.
