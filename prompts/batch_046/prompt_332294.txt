Two musicians are recording a concept album released in eight sequential chapters, with one chapter published each month. After any chapter is completed, either musician may unilaterally release the accumulated material as an incomplete solo project. The payout for this solo release after chapter n is always larger than what that musician would earn from chapter n alone within the completed album, yet always smaller than each musician's share of the finished eight-chapter work. Both musicians are ambitious, fully understand the payout structure, and are commonly regarded as highly rational. After finishing chapter two, one musician proposes that they immediately split the royalties earned so far and dissolve the collaboration. She argues that both should reason backward from chapter eight: at that final stage, the incentive to defect and release a solo version is strongest, so rational musicians will do so; knowing this, they will also defect at chapter seven; and repeating this logic step-by-step forces the conclusion that they should never have begun chapter three, which means they ought to stop now rather than continue. The other musician wants to proceed to chapter three and eventually complete the album, insisting that backward reasoning need not dictate actual behavior, yet he struggles to identify precisely where the logical chain breaks—particularly whether it requires not merely individual rationality but common knowledge of perfect rationality at every stage. Analyze the situation: identify the philosophical structure underlying this dispute, determine whether the dissolution argument is sound, specify exactly where and why the backward chain might fail, and advise whether they should continue the collaboration or end it now. Defend your conclusion.
