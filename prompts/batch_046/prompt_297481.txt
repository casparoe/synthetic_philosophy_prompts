I take the view that the Church-Turing thesis, interpreted as the claim that every physically realizable computation is Turing-computable, is best understood not as an empirical conjecture open to direct falsification but as a regulative or definitional boundary on what counts as effective computation—so that proposed physical countermodels, whether quantum hypercomputational schemes, analog continuous dynamics, or oracles embedded in spacetime geometry, fail not because the world lacks them but because they violate the informal conditions of finiteness, discrete steps, and local determinability that the thesis codifies. Play it straight: what would have to be true for that insulation strategy to collapse? Specifically, which formal results or physically implementable processes, if they satisfied those same informal conditions yet computed a function no Turing machine can, would be decisive? Which current debates in quantum computing or analog computation are genuinely awkward for the view versus merely noisy? Rank the defeaters from fatal to cosmetic. Also, say what this position predicts that its empirical-rival interpretations do not—about, say, the status of uncomputable physical processes or the burden of proof in modeling computation. Finally, assess honestly whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a virtue for a foundational boundary claim or a vice that empties it of physical content.
