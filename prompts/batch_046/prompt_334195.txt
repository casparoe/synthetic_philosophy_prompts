Write a dialogue between two philosophers—one a strict Kantian republican pacifist and one a cosmopolitan institutionalist—debating whether Kant’s essay on perpetual peace can accommodate governance of non-territorial collective goods, specifically transboundary atmospheric carbon budgets. The republican should defend Kant’s preliminary and definitive articles, arguing that republican constitutions internalize incentives against war, that the federation of free states must remain voluntary to preserve juridical sovereignty, and that any world state risks global despotism. The cosmopolitan should concede the danger of despotism but argue that Kant’s own distinction between provisional and peremptory rights implies that non-excludable, transboundary harms require more than voluntary association; she should press the republican on whether his refusal of a world state is merely a practical caution or a principled limit on the form of right itself. The exchange should be genuinely responsive: each speaker should reformulate the other’s position before objecting, and neither should be reduced to a strawman. Introduce an unconventional interpretive move: have them dispute whether Kant’s right of hospitality can be read not as a mere right of temporary sojourn but as generating structural duties of resource transparency and ecological accounting for states, and whether accepting such duties transforms the preliminary federation into something functionally indistinguishable from a cosmopolitan authority. The dialogue should be philosophically rigorous, avoid appeals to outside scholarship or citations, and should conclude with both positions sharpened rather than resolved.
