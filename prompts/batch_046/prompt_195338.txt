I have been reflecting on Benjamin Franklin’s well-known scheme for moral perfection, particularly his practice of tracking faults against a list of virtues in a private ledger. In attempting something analogous for my own habits, I noticed a peculiar tension: the more meticulously I recorded lapses in temperance or order, the more I seemed to cultivate a quiet satisfaction in the completeness of the record itself. This led me to an argument that Franklin’s method may be structurally self-defeating, yet I cannot locate the flaw.

(1) Franklin’s project aims at moral perfection through the habitual elimination of faults, aided by continuous written self-monitoring.
(2) For the ledger to be genuinely useful, it must capture not only obvious failures but subtle ones — for example, taking pride in one’s diligence at recording failures.
(3) Recognising and documenting such subtle failures demands intense, sustained self-scrutiny.
(4) This very scrutiny generates self-regard: one comes to esteem oneself for being the kind of person who detects these hidden faults.
(5) On Franklin’s own account, such self-esteem is a form of pride, which conflicts with the humility he sought to practise.
(6) Hence, any sufficiently rigorous application of the ledger necessarily produces the vice it is meant to eradicate.
(7) One might reply that the correct course is to record the pride as well, but then the same scrutiny applies to that recording, initiating an infinite regress of meta-failures.
(8) Therefore, Franklin’s method either abandons rigour — leaving faults unexamined — or pursues it into an endless loop of self-observation that makes genuine humility unattainable.

The conclusion seems excessively strong, for it appears to condemn not only Franklin’s grid but any reflective practice of self-improvement. I am inclined to reject step (4) or step (6), yet each rejection seems costly. Which premise or inference should I abandon, and what does that abandonment commit me to?
