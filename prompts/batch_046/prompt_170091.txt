I found this characterization of Spinoza's democratic theory in a graduate-student blog post summarizing the Political Treatise: "Spinoza defines democracy as the form of state in which sovereignty resides in the entire multitudo, not in a representative assembly, because natural right is never fully alienated but only pooled. This means that in democracy every citizen retains an equal share of sovereign power, and since reason is distributed equally among rational beings, democratic law automatically expresses the collective rational will. Spinoza therefore excludes women, domestic servants, and foreigners from citizenship not out of historical prejudice but as a logical consequence: anyone who lives under the potestas of another person cannot hold a share of sovereign right, and therefore cannot be part of the democratic multitudo. The result is that Spinoza's democracy is universal in principle—everyone who is sui iuris participates equally—while being restricted in practice to independent heads of households. This makes his view a kind of rationalist republicanism, closer to Aristotelian natural slavery arguments than to modern liberal equality, and it implies that democratic stability depends on homogeneity among the citizen body rather than on institutional checks."

The author draws two conclusions from this: first, that Spinoza's democracy is incompatible with representative government because sovereignty must be exercised directly; second, that the exclusion criteria are rational rather than contingent, so Spinoza's theory provides no resources for expanding citizenship.
