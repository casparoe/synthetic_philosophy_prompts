Translating this into distributed-systems vocabulary made it sound substrate-independent: the same pattern could be instantiated across multiple nodes if the architecture supports it. From that I constructed an argument that seems to prove personal identity dissolves into a social graph, and I cannot find the leak. Here are the numbered steps: (1) If being me consists in the persistence of a specific strange-loop pattern, then wherever that pattern is genuinely instantiated—not merely represented—part of me exists. (2) A close partner can internalize my reasoning architecture so thoroughly that she can generate my responses in my absence; this is not just a predictive model but a functional sub-loop running on her substrate. (3) By the same token, anyone who knows me well hosts fragments of my pattern, and those fragments extend through their own relationships, making my self a dense, transitive network with no privileged node. (4) Consequently, destroying my body removes only one node; the distributed pattern continues to interact elsewhere. (5) Applied symmetrically, every self overlaps with every other, leaving no coherent distinction between individuals and no non-arbitrary fact about who survives or dies. (6) But ethics and law presuppose distinct individuals with separate fates, so (5) is absurd. (7) Therefore the substrate-independent view proves too much. Which step is the weakest, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost—must I return to brain-bound identity, or is there a more subtle repair?
