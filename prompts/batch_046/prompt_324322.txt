I am drafting the final examination for my graduate seminar on the relationship between philosophy and the physical sciences, and the issue has become personally urgent since a senior colleague in my interdisciplinary research group recently argued that philosophy-of-science coursework should be eliminated because it offers no operational value to working scientists. I want one essay question suitable for a ninety-minute exam, expecting approximately eight hundred words, that tests whether students can do more than summarize Weinberg's argument that philosophy is largely irrelevant to physics, Hawking's claim that philosophy is dead, or Feynman's cargo-cult analogy. Instead, students should apply a specific conceptual distinction: philosophy functioning as heuristic scaffolding, meaning temporary conceptual frameworks that scientific practice eventually outgrows and abandons, versus philosophy functioning as normative calibration, meaning enduring standards for explanation, evidence, and theoretical virtue that remain active within scientific reasoning even when invisible to practitioners. The question should ask students to determine whether Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman attack both functions or only one, whether their critiques presuppose an unstated commitment to the complete methodological autonomy of science, and how a defender of philosophy's utility might reply without collapsing the distinction. Please provide: the exam question itself; a model answer of about six hundred words; and a marking scheme out of twenty points built around at least five must-hit conceptual moves, with marks for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically miss each, and explicit criteria that separate an A-level response, which integrates all moves, from a B-level response, in which the moves are present but disconnected. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Finally, please state how confident you are in the substantive claims made in your model answer and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
