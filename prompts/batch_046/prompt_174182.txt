I manage digitization priorities for a municipal archive now cataloguing its twentieth-century administrative holdings. We have observed that neighborhoods erased by urban renewal leave almost no trace, while affluent districts are densely documented, and I need a reusable assessment procedure my staff can apply to any collection or finding aid before publication. The goal is to distinguish three diagnoses—random physical loss, absence within present records, and structurally produced archival silence reflecting power asymmetries—grounded in the philosophy of archives and the epistemology of absence. Provide ordered questions with sufficient-evidence thresholds for each diagnosis; include a short indicator checklist covering provenance breaks, metadata clustering, and cross-archive mismatches; and specify exactly where the procedure stops with a clear “no systemic silence” verdict, where it requires a values judgment rather than a factual call, and where it risks misclassifying accidental decay as erasure. Apply the procedure to two cases: a missing folder of 1962 eviction notices, and a complete but exclusively official set of planning-commission minutes. Include one worked outcome where the procedure yields an uncomfortable result—likely designating a well-preserved, institutionally prized collection as silently complicit—and flag the limits where the procedure cannot settle the question without appealing to ethical commitments about representation.
