The hole argument, discovered by Einstein around 1913, proves that general relativity is not deterministic. Apply a smooth coordinate transformation that leaves every point outside a small region of the spacetime manifold fixed but moves the points inside that hole; this produces a second metric field that satisfies Einstein's field equations, matches the first field everywhere outside the hole, yet differs inside. Because both solutions share the same initial data outside the hole, the theory cannot uniquely fix the metric within it, so determinism collapses. Einstein initially treated this as a fatal defect and came close to discarding the field equations entirely. He resolved the problem by insisting that only point-coincidences—intersections of physical world-lines—have observable meaning; since the two metrics assign exactly the same coincidences, they represent one physical situation, not two. Therefore the ambiguity is only mathematical, not physical, and determinism is restored. Today physicists and philosophers generally regard the hole argument as solved and philosophically inert: it shows that bare spacetime points lack independent reality, but it raises no active problem for determinism in GR. Some authors extend this result to support ontic structural realism, arguing that the argument demonstrates that only the relational structure of the metric field is physically real, while the manifold points that bear that structure are merely mathematical scaffolding.
