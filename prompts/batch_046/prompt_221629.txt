I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, and I have been attempting to apply Blaise Pascal’s reflections on justice and force—encountered in a modern English rendering of his fragments—to questions of governance in distributed computing protocols. In my reading group we have formulated the following chain of reasoning, which appears to derive an uncomfortably permissive conclusion, yet I cannot locate the precise misstep. (1) Pascal holds that justice unaccompanied by enforceable power is ineffectual, whereas power unaccompanied by justice is tyrannical; therefore, a legitimate order must couple disputable normative principles with an indisputable mechanism of enforcement. (2) Within a distributed consensus protocol, the normative rules—such as which nodes may propose a block—are inherently contestable, since participants may hold conflicting local states; by contrast, the cryptographic verification and the consensus algorithm itself operate as, for practical purposes, indisputable once executed. (3) Consequently, for the protocol to maintain coherence, its disputable “justice” must be yoked to its indisputable “force”, mirroring exactly the political structure Pascal describes.

(4) This structural dependency, however, holds irrespective of the moral content of the rules: a protocol that arbitrarily excludes honest participants functions just as well technically as one that treats them fairly, provided only that its enforcement layer is robust and widely adopted. (5) From this it seems to follow that Pascal’s framework cannot distinguish between legitimate authority and mere effective domination, reducing justice to a decorative label attached to power. (6) Yet Pascal clearly intended justice to retain some independent normative weight, not to dissolve entirely into the fact of enforcement. I suspect the error lies somewhere between steps (3) and (5)—perhaps in an unnoticed slide from functional necessity to normative justification, or in an equivocation over what “indisputable” means across political and technical domains—but I cannot identify it with confidence. Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost for both Pascal’s original argument and for the analogy to system design?
