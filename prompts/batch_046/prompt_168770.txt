Public museums and school-curriculum boards are chartered as stewards of documentary truth, yet they routinely operate under formal community-consultation rules that grant stakeholder or descendant groups veto authority over exhibit text and lesson content. The institutional pattern is consistent: when archival records—land registries, census data, or contemporaneous reports—conflict with a community's collective memory of its origins or of a traumatic event, the board typically produces parallel dual-narrative displays, mandates that both versions be taught with equal respect, or quietly suppresses the archival finding in order to protect group identity. At the same time, these bodies condemn historical revisionism that denies well-documented atrocities. What philosophical commitments about the authority of collective memory, the nature of historical truth, and the moral status of group identity does this practice presuppose? Can it be reconstructed on purely pragmatic or democratic grounds without granting identity-constituting beliefs a special privilege over documentary evidence, or is that privilege indispensable? Is the distinction they draw between protecting a community narrative and tolerating revisionism philosophically stable? And if one held that archival truth should always prevail over collective memory, which concrete mechanisms—consultation vetoes, dual-labeling norms, funding criteria tied to community impact—would have to be abandoned?
