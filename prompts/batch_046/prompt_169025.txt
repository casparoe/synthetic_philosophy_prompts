My family operates a small workshop devoted to a craft that requires roughly twelve years of apprenticeship; only about a dozen people worldwide are fully qualified. I completed the training, but I am noticeably less skilled than two apprentices who joined through an external scholarship program—one of whom had no prior exposure to this kind of work. My parents intend to leave me the workshop, its specialized equipment, and its proprietary methods outright. They defend this by saying that family continuity safeguards rare knowledge and that I sacrificed other career paths because of an implicit understanding I would eventually take over. At the same time, they concede that my being born into the workshop was pure luck, and that the scholarship apprentices earned their places on merit. Those apprentices have proposed restructuring the workshop as a cooperative: ownership would migrate gradually over the next decade to whoever demonstrates the highest sustained skill, with my family retaining only a non-controlling advisory stake. My parents reject this as violating the promise embedded in my upbringing, yet they insist that advantages from birth are morally undeserved. I am trying to decide whether to accept the inheritance, advocate for the cooperative, or seek a middle path. Can their position be made consistent, or are they necessarily trying to defend an inherited advantage while denying that birth should determine opportunity?
