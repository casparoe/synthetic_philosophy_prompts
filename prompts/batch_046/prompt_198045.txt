I am drafting a long-form feature on whether the so-called species problem in biology is a genuine metaphysical puzzle or a category mistake inherited from Aristotelian essentialism. At a recent interdisciplinary workshop, two senior researchers—an evolutionary philosopher and a practicing taxonomist—engaged in a sustained but civil disagreement. I report their exchange below as a bystander; I have no horse in the race. Please adjudicate it move by move.

First, the philosopher argued that the problem dissolves once essentialism is abandoned. Drawing on the evolutionary species concept, she claimed that species are not natural kinds but spatiotemporally restricted lineages—individuals, in the metaphysical sense—much as a river is an individual rather than a kind defined by essential properties. Asking for the essence of a species, she said, is therefore as confused as asking for the essence of the Mississippi River: the boundaries are practical and conventional, not metaphysically deep.

Second, the taxonomist replied that this dissolves the problem only by redefining it away. He noted that in asexual microbes and in hybridizing plant complexes, reproductive isolation—the linchpin of the biological species concept—is either absent or empirically useless. Meanwhile, phylogenetic approaches fail in these same groups because evolutionary history is genuinely reticulate rather than tree-like. He then introduced what he termed the boundary-indeterminacy thesis: if species are genuinely individuals, then gradual anagenetic transitions generate a Sorites-style paradox about precisely when one lineage-individual ends and another begins. Essentialism never faced this difficulty, he argued, because it posited discrete essences; treating species as individuals without essences makes the boundary problem more acute, not less.

Third, the philosopher offered a pluralist response. Different domains of life, she said, require different operational criteria: morphological clustering for macroscopic animals, gene-flow metrics for plants, genomic coherence for bacteria. Pluralism is not intellectual surrender but methodological humility: taxonomy should deliver domain-specific predictive tools rather than a single unified ontology.

Fourth, the taxonomist countered that pluralism concedes his central point—that there is no unified category "species"—while disguising the concession behind practical convenience. He then advanced a further claim drawn from recent work in processual taxonomy: if taxa are treated not as static entities but as continuously transforming processes, then there is never a non-arbitrary temporal point at which a new species-process begins. On this view, the boundary problem is not solved by pluralism but intensified, because every operational criterion now applies to a moving target.

Please rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Identify where the two speakers talk past each other—particularly regarding whether taxonomy should aim at a single ontology or at domain-specific predictive success—and note whether either party misses an objection that the other should have raised but did not. Do not simply declare an overall winner; your deliverable is a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as actually made.
