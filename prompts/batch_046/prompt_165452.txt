Write a deadpan parody of a clinical-trial informed-consent form for a fictional study titled “Inter-Subject Pattern Persistence,” which tests whether a first-person narrative can be distributed across paired brains via synchronized cortical stimulation. The form should follow standard bureaucratic sections—purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, confidentiality, and withdrawal rights—but the fine print should quietly encode Douglas Hofstadter’s idea that the self is a high-level pattern rather than a localized substance, and therefore capable of partial replication in another substrate. Include a clause defining “you” as “the distributed pattern of narrative coherence registered across both participants, severable without destroying either brain”; a risk disclosure stating that if one participant withdraws, residual “I”-fragments may persist in the partner; and a compensation schedule paid in “continuity credits” rather than currency. The satire should make the philosophical tension legible without explaining it: if selfhood is genuinely a pattern, then bodily autonomy, liability, and informed consent all become ambiguous about which substrate holds them. Keep the voice neutral and institutional; let the absurdity emerge from the logic itself, not from gothic or melodramatic language. Target the view that an “I” can survive distribution across brains, and the institutional practices that assume a one-to-one mapping of person and body. Around 600 words.
