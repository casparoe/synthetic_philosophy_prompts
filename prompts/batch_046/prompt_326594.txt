hey—i'm drafting a column on Black's spheres for my science-and-ideas blog and i've talked myself into a corner. The identity of indescernibles is Leibniz's claim that if two objects share every single property then they're not two objects at all, right? So Black puts two spheres in an otherwise empty, symetric universe and asks how we tell them apart. But here's my hangup: if the only thing that could distinguish sphere A is "being three feet to the left of sphere B," that's circular, because sphere B is part of the description. So doesn't that mean the spheres actually share ALL properties—including the relational ones—once you strip away the circular references? By Leibniz's law they should be one sphere, not two. I'm probably botching the logic, but it feels like Black accidentally proved the principle and then miscounted. When philosophers respond, do they say spatial relations aren't circular like that, or do they accept that in Black's universe there's literally only one sphere?
