I have been thinking on my own about whether failures to act—omissions—can genuinely cause things, and I have arrived at five commitments that feel coherent in isolation but which I suspect may pull against one another. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I would like a careful audit of whether this package is jointly tenable. Here is where I stand:

(1) I believe omissions are real causes, not just descriptions of absence. If I neglect a plant for months and it dies, my neglect caused the death in the same substantive way that pouring poison would have.

(2) I also believe that genuine causation requires some actual physical interaction, transmission of energy, or at least a concrete mechanism linking cause to effect. An omission, being literally nothing happening, seems to lack this.

(3) I distinguish sharply between failing to prevent an existing harm and failing to confer a benefit. Not pulling a stranger from train tracks allows death; not donating to famine relief allows death. Only the first omission strikes me as genuinely causal, because in the second case the harm was already independently proceeding.

(4) I think mental omissions—such as forgetting to check a stove—are fully causal. Even though forgetting is just a brain state failing to trigger action, I treat that internal absence as no different in kind from an external absence.

(5) I hold that whether an omission counts as a cause depends on an expectation structure embedded in the situation. A lifeguard’s failure to swim is causal; a bystander’s identical failure is not, because the social role creates a "channel" that should have been present, and its absence directs the outcome.

Please examine these for hidden tension. Rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equal. For each genuine tension, say which commitment has to give and what the least costly revision would be. Also tell me if any apparent tension dissolves once the commitments are properly understood. Finally, be frank about whether my distinction in (3) can survive alongside my expectation-based view in (5), and whether my physical-interaction requirement in (2) can accommodate mental omissions in (4) without collapsing into a view I do not want to hold—namely, that only actions, never omissions, cause anything.
