I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about whether there is anything that makes every instance of the letter 'a' an 'a', and we are stuck exactly where we started. She thinks recognition works through a continuous parametric style-space — something like the old Letter Spirit idea that letterforms live in a high-dimensional space of strokes and proportions, so an 'a' is any point near enough to the cluster center. I keep insisting that this only pushes the question back: why that cluster, and why do we treat a single-story handwritten 'a', a double-story printed one, and a severely distorted sign that barely closes its bowl as the same category, while refusing to call a circular 'o' with a tail an 'a' even when the geometry is closer? My friend calls that family resemblance; I say family resemblance avoids the hard problem of what binds the resemblance. Meanwhile, a third view we both considered is that there is no essence at all — recognition is entirely context-dependent and task-driven — but then neither of us can explain why children learn the category across wildly different fonts without explicit instruction. We agree on every case: the distorted sign reads as 'a', the 'o'-with-tail does not, the computer-generated symbol in an invented script can still trigger 'a' if it respects certain relational constraints. The disagreement is not about the examples.

My working hypothesis is that we are not actually disagreeing about cognition or ontology, but about what kind of answer counts as an explanation: she wants a generative mechanism, I want a normative principle that distinguishes correct categorization from mistake, and the contextualist wants a functional story about communication. Is that right — are we answering different questions masked by the phrase 'what makes it an a'? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about human visual processing that we are dressing up in philosophical language? And if the stall is conceptual rather than factual, what would actually move it: a formal model that predicts which novel shapes count as 'a', a neuropsychological result about invariant representations, or a distinction that shows one of our questions was ill-posed from the start? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, supported by what each position actually commits us to, and an honest account of whether anything should count as progress here.
