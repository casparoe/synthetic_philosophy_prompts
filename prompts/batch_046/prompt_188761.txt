Read Alex Garland's film Ex Machina (2014) through the lens of Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology as a method for the science of consciousness. The narrative turns on Caleb's attempt to determine whether Ava possesses consciousness, yet the film persistently invites the audience to treat her verbal reports and emotional displays as direct evidence of an inner phenomenological life — precisely the commitment that heterophenomenology rejects in favour of treating such reports as constituting a heterophenomenological world whose ontology remains bracketed. How does the film dramatise the tension between interpreting Ava's behaviour as data for such a bracketed world and the irresistible pull to read her as having first-person authority over her own mental states? Does Ava's final escape vindicate the heterophenomenological stance by revealing that Caleb's attributions of belief and desire were always interpretive constructions projected onto a behavioural profile, or does the film's cinematography — particularly its treatment of Ava's face and the glass walls — smuggle in a Cartesian theatre that heterophenomenology denies? I want a reading that takes both the film's narrative architecture and Dennett's methodological strictures seriously, asks where the work resists a purely heterophenomenological interpretation, and says whether Ex Machina adds to the philosophical debate something that argument alone cannot supply — for instance, about the ethical stakes of bracketing first-person authority when assessing artificial agents.
