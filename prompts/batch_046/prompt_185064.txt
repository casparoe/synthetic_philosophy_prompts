I serve on the clinical ethics committee at a university-affiliated neurorehabilitation center, and I need the written opinion I will circulate before our meeting next Tuesday. A research team has proposed a pilot study using an adaptive brain-computer interface for patients with locked-in syndrome. The device detects the Bereitschaftspotential — the readiness potential that precedes conscious awareness of voluntary movement by several hundred milliseconds, a phenomenon long associated with Benjamin Libet's experiments on the neuroscience of free will — and initiates a selection protocol before the patient reports any conscious intention to act. The investigators argue that this improves response speed and reduces fatigue; the family of the first proposed participant, however, contends that if preparatory brain activity determines the selection before conscious awareness, then the interface is not expressing the patient's autonomous will but substituting a mechanistic neural precursor for it. They ask us to reject the protocol unless it can demonstrate that the pre-conscious signal genuinely corresponds to the patient's reflective agency rather than to deterministic neural dynamics. Our institutional policy permits pilot studies with heightened oversight when autonomy is contested, but it offers no criteria for adjudicating whether a readiness potential counts as an exercise of free will. Please draft the committee's opinion in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, composed as one would write a formal memorandum or letter — recommending whether to approve, approve with specific conditions and a concrete time frame, or reject the study, with reasoning that takes the family's determinist objection seriously rather than merely restating the legal standard of informed consent, and with an honest account of the strongest consideration supporting the course you do not recommend.
