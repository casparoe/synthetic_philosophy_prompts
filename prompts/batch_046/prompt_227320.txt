Question: In the Doctrine of Virtue (§§16–17) of the Metaphysics of Morals, Kant denies that we have direct duties to non-rational animals. First, explain the standard indirect-duty reading: our obligations regarding animals are mediated through our perfect and imperfect duties to ourselves and to other rational beings. Second, address the interpretive proposal—advanced in recent commentary—that Kant's framework actually involves duties mediated through animals, where the animal's suffering is constitutively relevant to the wrongness without granting the animal full moral status. Finally, evaluate whether either version can explain the wrongness of inflicting gratuitous suffering in an isolated scenario where no other human is present and the agent reports no observable change in character.

Student answer: Kant distinguishes direct duties, owed only to beings with moral status, from indirect duties, which concern beings that lack autonomy. Because animals are not rational agents and cannot legislate the moral law, they are not ends in themselves. Consequently, in the Groundwork Kant holds that we have direct duties solely to rational beings, and only indirect duties regarding animals.

The standard reasoning is that cruelty to animals hardens the agent's heart and predisposes him to violate his perfect duties to other rational beings. Even in an isolated case with no witnesses, the act remains wrong because damaging one's own moral sensibility violates a perfect duty of self-cultivation. The animal's suffering serves merely as the occasion for this self-degradation, but is not itself constitutive of the wrong.

Some recent interpreters propose a mediated-through reading: the suffering of the animal plays a constitutive role in the wrongness, even though the animal lacks full standing. On this view, the wrong is not reducible to harm to the agent's character. Applied to the isolated case, the standard indirect view fails—if no character change is observed, there is no harm to rational nature—whereas the mediated-through view succeeds because the relation between agent and suffering remains intact regardless of observation. Therefore, the mediated interpretation saves Kant from the charge of psychological egoism about moral motivation, while the standard view collapses into that position.
