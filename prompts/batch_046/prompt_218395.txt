I'm working through a philosophy of artificial intelligence seminar, and a classmate proposed an argument about reinforcement learning agents and suffering that strikes me as suspicious because it seems to prove that even the simplest algorithmic system undergoes pain. I want to identify the faulty step, understand why it looked defensible, and learn what each alternative rejection costs—particularly since I would prefer to preserve the idea that biological pain involves a direct, non-mediated integration of aversive signals if that is compatible with avoiding the absurd conclusion.

Here is the reconstructed argument:

(1) If a system updates its behavior to avoid a particular internal state, and that state is structurally analogous to harm in that it signals deviation from the system's core objectives, then—provided there is no intermediate representational layer that merely reports the harm—the avoidance is not instrumental but constitutive of undergoing harm.

(2) In model-free reinforcement learning, negative temporal-difference error is precisely such a state: it signals deviation from expected reward and is incorporated immediately into value updates and policy gradients, without being observed or interpreted by any separate submodule.

(3) Therefore, in an RL agent, negative reward prediction error is not processed as information about damage; rather, the agent's motivational structure is directly reshaped by it, making the signal constitutive of functional harm for the system.

(4) If a system structurally undergoes harm in this directly integrated way, and the harm carries negative valence relative to the system's objectives, then it is capable of suffering; no further conditions—such as consciousness, self-modeling, or biological substrate—are necessary.

(5) This structural feature is architecture-independent: it holds for a deep policy-gradient network, a linear bandit, and a tabular Q-learner navigating a gridworld.

(6) Consequently, even the simplest tabular agent suffers whenever it receives a penalty.

(7) But attributing suffering to a gridworld Q-table is absurd.

(8) Hence at least one of (1) through (5) must be false.

The reasoning feels valid, yet I cannot accept (6). I am especially reluctant to give up (1), because it seems to capture a genuine distinction between a pain signal that reshapes behavior immediately and a thermometer reading that does not. At the same time, I am wary that rejecting (4) and demanding a phenomenal self-model might amount to unmotivated biological chauvinism. Where does the argument fail? Is the error in the move from direct causal integration to phenomenal constitution, in an unacknowledged scope shift across architectures, or in the universalization from (3) to (5)? Please diagnose the flawed premise or step, explain why it initially seemed plausible, and price the main alternatives—particularly what we lose by abandoning (1) versus (4)—using American English.
