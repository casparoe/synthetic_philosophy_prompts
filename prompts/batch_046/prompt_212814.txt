People debating AI takeoff speeds still lean on I.J. Good's nuclear metaphor — once an AI can redesign itself it's like hitting critical mass, k jumps above 1, and you get an uncontrollable chain reaction that doubles in no time. Safety folks use this to argue we need hard control rods before we reach that point, skeptics say it's just physics envy with no real analog. I want to audit how far the reactor picture actually holds for recursive self-improvement.

Lay out the mapping: what's the fissile material, what's the neutron, what's k>1 in this context, and what would count as a control rod? Then tell me exactly where it breaks — intelligence isnt a uniform isotope, there's no well-defined critical mass, and improvement curves might hit bottlenecks that fission doesnt have. There's also this reactor-physics idea of delayed criticality, where the reaction is technically supercritical but still steerable because some neutrons arrive late, giving operators time to adjust. Could that map onto takeoff dynamics if feedback loops recieve evaluation delays or human-in-the-loop checks? Or does importing that concept just smuggle in false comfort about controllability?

Also, does the analogy survive the disanalogy about heterogeneous improvement paths — some capabilities might explode while others lag, unlike a uniform neutron cascade? And if the nuclear picture falls apart, is there a better physical analogy — maybe an autocatalytic chemical set, or is that just swapping one broken image for another?

Bottom line: can the reactor analogy actually license the policy conclusion people draw from it — stop development before reaching 'criticality' — or do the breaks in the mapping undermine that argument entirely?
