Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise and the problem of justifying deduction.

Camp A — the Framework Internalists: they hold that the Tortoise's regress is dissolved once we distinguish using a rule of inference within an object-language derivation from mentioning that rule in a meta-language statement; on their view, accepting modus ponens (or any logical rule) is part of adopting a formal system, not a claim within the system that itself requires logical proof, and the attempt to add it as a premise either changes the logical form or collapses the object-language/meta-language boundary.

Camp B — the Pragmatic Foundationalists: they hold that Carroll's dialogue exposes a genuine epistemological gap; logical rules are not self-justifying, and if every inference requires a prior rule, then either we face an infinite regress or we must ground deduction in non-logical capacities — practical reasoning, cognitive architecture, or normative commitments — that are not themselves derivable.

Produce a formal document with the following sections:

1. Agreed claims: numbered statements both sides can sign about the logical structure of the dialogue (the Tortoise's refusal to accept the conclusion of a modus ponens argument unless the rule itself is added as an explicit premise; the resulting infinite regress if the same demand is applied to that added premise; the formal difference between a derivation that applies a rule and a derivation that lists the rule among its premises).

2. Disagreements table: state precisely where they part ways, with each camp's own view phrased in wording that camp would accept. Include at least: (a) whether the use-mention distinction eliminates the need for justification or merely relocates it; (b) whether a rule of inference can be treated as a premise without altering the system's soundness or logical form; (c) whether there exists a non-circular, non-inferential ground for basic logical rules.

3. Cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I take two likely cruxes to be: (i) whether "accepting a logical rule" is best modeled as adopting a framework, as performing a mental act that requires independent justification, or as expressing a normative commitment that is neither purely descriptive nor purely arbitrary; (ii) whether an axiomatized system in which every inference rule is explicitly listed as an axiom eliminates Carroll's regress or merely transforms it into a regress about the acceptance of axioms.

4. Predictive cases: for each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp would predict about it. For crux (ii), consider a fully explicit formal proof-checker in which every application of modus ponens must be justified by reference to an axiom stating that rule: does this show that justification is internal and complete, or does it reveal that justification has been offloaded onto the practical commitments of the system's designer? For crux (i), consider whether agents who reject classical logic but retain the same inferential practices — for example, in paraconsistent or relevance-logical contexts — are best described as having changed their framework or as having revealed that logical commitment was never purely descriptive.

5. Verbal dispute assessment: conclude with an explicit judgment about whether any portion of the disagreement is verbal — for example, whether disputes about "justification" depend on whether justification is understood as internal coherence within a system versus external grounding of the system — and, if so, identify exactly which portion and why.

Write this as a polished, formal text that both parties could sign, with residual disagreement made exact. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the framing precise and somewhat in the weeds — for instance, by specifying the exact logical form of the Tortoise's challenge (a conditional with premises P and P→Q, with the rule of detachment withheld) and the conditions under which a meta-language statement can serve as a premise without altering derivational validity — but avoid invoking highly obscure theories or requiring knowledge of specific recent literature that a well-prepared generalist model might not reliably have. Do not use verbatim quotations from Carroll's text; if you refer to specific lines, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
