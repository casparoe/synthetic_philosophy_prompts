Having spent three decades in experimental biophysics before turning to philosophy of medicine, I have been trying to articulate my own reflective equilibrium on what makes consent genuinely valid, as opposed to merely procedurally correct. I find myself with the following concrete verdicts, which do not obviously cohere.

First, a retired engineer with no biological training signs a gene-therapy consent form after the principal investigator explains viral-vector mechanisms using only technical immunological terminology; he nods but later admits he could not distinguish a viral vector from a bacterial plasmid. I judge this not valid consent: general intelligence does not substitute for domain-relevant comprehension.

Second, a surgeon tells a patient that an elective procedure carries "minimal risk," knowing that for this patient's specific comorbidity profile the serious-adverse-event rate is twelve percent, though the population average is 0.3 percent. The statement is technically defensible by reference to the general cohort. I judge this invalid: strategic exploitation of epistemic asymmetry through framing vitiates consent even in the absence of literal falsehood.

Third, a healthy volunteer from a low-income community enrolls in a phase-I trial for a substantial payment equal to three months' wages; the study carries a non-trivial but well-characterized risk, and the compensation falls within institutional norms rather than being calibrated to induce risk-taking. I judge this valid: structural economic pressure is not coercive unless it targets a specific dependency that removes reasonable refusal.

Fourth, a patient with early-stage Alzheimer's, during a documented lucid interval confirmed by an independent neuropsychological assessment, executes an advance directive refusing artificial nutrition. Six hours later, in delirium, she demands food and revokes the directive. I accept the lucid-interval directive as valid: competence should be assessed as temporally localized, not globally assigned.

Fifth, a fifteen-year-old with exceptional cognitive maturity independently reads and analyzes a forty-page neuroethics consent form, asks precise questions about blinding and data retention, and requests to enroll; local law requires parental consent, which is refused. I think her assent should count as functionally valid consent, though legal proxy rules are a separate institutional matter.

Sixth, a person agrees to intimate physical contact after their partner threatens to end the relationship and withdraw emotional and financial support on which they depend; they feel they have no meaningful alternative. I judge this invalid.

Seventh, I keep going back and forth on a pair that looks structurally parallel but feels different. In one case, a researcher withholds information about a hidden placebo arm, knowing that disclosure would cause nocebo-related dropouts that compromise statistical power; the subject experiences no direct harm but was misled about study design. In the other, an employer requires employees to consent to continuous biometric monitoring as a condition of retaining employment they genuinely need; the monitoring is fully disclosed and harmless, but refusal means termination. The first feels like a deception failure; the second feels like a coercion failure. Yet both involve conditioning authorization on an asymmetry of information or power. Are these genuinely distinct modes of invalidation, or two manifestations of one underlying condition?

Finally, the case I genuinely cannot stabilize: a parent tells their young child that a necessary but painful vaccination "will not hurt at all," knowing it will, solely to secure the child's cooperation. Is this an invalidation of assent through deception, or is it permissible paternalistic framing that preserves autonomy in a broader developmental sense?

What principle, or small family of principles, captures the pattern of my verdicts? Please state it precisely, identifying which case motivates each clause, predicting its verdict on at least one further scenario I have not listed, and being honest about which of my judgments it must overturn to maintain coherence. Then apply it specifically to my oscillating pair and to the vaccination-assent case, explaining whether those verdicts should stand or fall.
