In many urban service economies, local conventions—tipping restaurant staff at a culturally fixed percentage, queuing in linear order, and obeying pedestrian signals—are enforced with sharply different moral valences. Undertipping is frequently condemned as a personal failure or exploitation; queue-jumping provokes public shaming and accusations of unfairness; jaywalking, though technically illegal in many jurisdictions, is usually treated as a harmless regulatory breach rather than a moral wrong. At the same time, visitors are routinely expected to observe all three norms without being told which carry genuine moral weight, which are mere legal formalities, and whether ignorance of local custom excuses violation. What implicit theory of conventional obligation explains this graded pattern? Must it presuppose that moral force attaches only to conventions that solve coordination problems with distributive stakes, that local membership generates special duties of deference to place-based custom, or that functional indispensability transforms social habit into binding obligation? Reconstruct the philosophy embedded in the practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and assess whether a consistent universalist account—holding that the same rules bind visitors and locals alike, with no special normative weight assigned to geography—could preserve the practice or would force it to collapse into a uniform system of legal compliance and purely voluntary gratuity.
