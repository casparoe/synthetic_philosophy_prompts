I'm a journalist writing long-form profiles of scientists and philosophers for a general-interest magazine, and I need a reusable editorial procedure—applicable about six times a year, within a forty-eight-hour deadline, without psychological testing, and requiring my editor's sign-off—to decide whether I may justifiably attribute a specific intellectual virtue (intellectual humility or epistemic courage) to a profile subject. The procedure must translate virtue ethics, which treats these traits as stable dispositions integrated with practical wisdom, into operational steps, while honoring the situationist critique that behavior often tracks situational affordances rather than cross-situational character. Structure it as an ordered sequence of tests in words: first a behavioral-variance screen across observed contexts; then an affordance-audit that classifies each context by visibility, competitive stakes, and collaborative structure; then a practical-reasoning check that asks whether the behavior expresses judgment about the good of inquiry rather than habit, fear, or impression management; then a narrative-coherence filter. Include explicit stop conditions where attribution is withheld, explicit hand-off points where the remaining question is irreducibly one of values rather than evidence, and a final sign-off node. Apply it to three hypothetical subjects: (a) a climate researcher who publicly corrects her own modeling errors but privately dismisses junior colleagues' objections in closed meetings; (b) a neuroscientist whose replication rigor is consistent in academic settings but collapses in industry-funded collaborations; and (c) a philosopher who defers to domain experts in written correspondence but never in televised debates. For each case, specify exactly what virtue-claim I may make, where the procedure forces me to withhold the label against my editorial instinct, and where it admits it cannot decide. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—for non-intellectual virtues, for subjects with very few observed contexts, and for cases where publication deadlines require a character judgment before the evidence is complete.
