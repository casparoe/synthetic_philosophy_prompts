Exam question: In his essay Of the Standard of Taste, Hume attempts to navigate between pure subjectivism and full objectivism about beauty. Consider a hypothetical disagreement between two critics who both satisfy Hume’s familiar correctives—delicacy of sentiment, extensive comparative practice, freedom from personal prejudice, and sound reasoning—yet belong to radically different aesthetic traditions: one formed by lifelong immersion in Japanese wabi-sabi sensibilities and another formed by Western Renaissance ideals. They evaluate the same minimalist concrete installation and reach opposite conclusions about its beauty. Does Hume’s framework allow us to declare one judgment correct and the other mistaken? If so, on what grounds? If not, does this reduce his position to a form of cultural relativism?

Student answer: Hume begins by denying that beauty resides in objects as a primary quality; instead, it is a sentiment of pleasure or approval produced in a qualified perceiver. Nevertheless, he rejects pure subjectivism by arguing that not all sentiments are equally authoritative. He identifies a class of ideal critics whose judgments are refined through practice, comparison across works, and the elimination of prejudice. When these critics concur, their agreement establishes an intersubjective standard of taste.

Applying this to the case: both critics have undergone the requisite training and appear free of personal bias, yet they disagree. Hume would resolve this by observing that cultural formation itself can function as a hidden prejudice that the correctives fail to fully remove. Consequently, at least one critic has not truly satisfied Hume’s conditions, even though it appears otherwise. This means there remains a single objective standard—embodied in the genuinely ideal critic—but in practice we may be unable to determine which tradition produces her. At the same time, Hume’s framework permits that different cultures cultivate genuinely different but equally valid sensibilities, so each tradition may possess its own local standard governed by its own ideal critics. Therefore his view is best characterized as qualified objectivism: sentiment is necessary for beauty, but only sentiments that survive universal correctives are correct, and when traditions conflict we lack a meta-criterion to adjudicate between them.
