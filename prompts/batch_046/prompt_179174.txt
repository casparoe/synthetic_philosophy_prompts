I am composing a short philosophical poem for a workshop I am leading on the metaphysics of persistence, and I want the form itself to do the conceptual work rather than serve as mere ornament. I have chosen the pantoum—quatrains in which lines two and four of each stanza are repeated, displaced into lines one and three of the next—because its mechanics of repetition-with-relocation seem to enact the perdurantist picture of a person as a four-dimensional worm composed of numerically distinct temporal parts, each capable of bearing incompatible properties without contradiction. At the same time, I want the speaker to resist that dispersal: an enduring consciousness that insists it is wholly present in every stanza, not scattered like slices across time.

The central tension should turn on the problem of temporary intrinsics—how a single thing can be, for instance, both bent and straight, or both grieving and intact, if it is entirely present at each moment—so that the poem’s repeated lines shift their relational meaning from stanza to stanza (bent in one context, straight in the next) while the speaker clings to a unified identity. I need roughly five quatrains, with no fixed rhyme scheme beyond what the repetition supplies, and I want the final stanza to bring the displaced refrains together in the last two lines without resolving them, forcing the enduring self and its temporal parts to share the same breath as neither yields. The language should be precise enough that someone versed in the endurantism-perdurantism debate recognizes the four-dimensional ontology and the problem of temporary intrinsics being performed, yet it must remain a poem—accessible to someone who has never encountered the terminology, feeling the ache of a self trying to hold together across time.
