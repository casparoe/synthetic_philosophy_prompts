I've been following the recent threads here on epistemic value—especially the debate over whether virtue epistemology actually solves Plato's Meno problem or merely relocates it—and I want something that uses poetic form to think, not just to name-drop concepts. Write a villanelle that takes up Socrates' question: if true belief and knowledge both guide action correctly, why is knowledge more valuable?

Use two refrains that alternate through the stanzas: True belief runs like Daedalus' art (carrying the image of the statues that flee unless chained) and But what is tied that was not there before? (carrying the lingering value question Socrates leaves open). The form itself should do the philosophical work: let the refrains circle each other without resolution through the middle stanzas, where you should bring in Socrates' answer that knowledge is true belief tied down by an account of the cause—aitias logismos—but allow the rhyme scheme and repetition to keep ambiguous whether that tether adds a new value or only prevents loss. In the final couplet, place both refrains side by side, mirroring the moment in the dialogue where stability and value are named together but not clearly separated. Make it accurate enough that a reader familiar with the Meno recognizes the specific metaphor and the gap in Socrates' argument, and polished enough to serve as a closing reading for a seminar on knowledge and achievement.
