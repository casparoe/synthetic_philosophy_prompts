Construct a taxonomy—presented as a branching decision tree—of interpretive positions on Plato’s stance toward intoxication and philosophical ecstasy, designed for an upper-level ancient-philosophy seminar. The tree must begin with the shared premise that the symposiastic settings in Symposium and Laws are philosophically serious rather than purely literary framing. From there, split the logical space with four successive diagnostic questions: (1) Does Socrates’ refusal to become drunk in Symposium II express a normative claim about philosophical practice, or merely a personal habit of moderation? (2) Does the Laws’ regulation of communal drinking represent a political suppression of ecstatic experience, or an institutional channeling of it into structured civic ritual? (3) Is the true drunkenness linked to love and madness in Symposium and Phaedrus meant literally (alcohol-induced) or metaphorically (divine frenzy and possession)? (4) Is the tension between Symposium’s apparent celebration of drinking and Laws’ strict regulation best resolved through a developmental biographical reading (early Plato liberal, late Plato authoritarian) or through a unified context-sensitive account? The required leaf positions are: the ascetic rejectionist (intoxication always impedes philosophy); the institutionalist moderator (drinking is permissible only under civic regulation); the literal-ecstasy interpreter (controlled alcohol-induced states facilitate philosophical insight); and the metaphorical-frenzy interpreter (all valuable drunkenness is non-literal possession). For each unoccupied combination of answers—especially any that combines a literal-ecstasy reading of Symposium with an authoritarian suppression reading of Laws without a developmental bridge—note briefly whether the gap seems incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Phrase every node as a question an interpreter can answer without knowing the label of the position it leads to, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely distinguish.
