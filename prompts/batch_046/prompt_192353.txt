As a retired physicist who has lately taken up philosophy with the same seriousness I once reserved for statistical mechanics, I recently sat in on a small discussion group where two members—Elena, an economist, and Marcus, a historian—became entangled in a disagreement that seemed to me to straddle the boundary between population ethics and practical reason. I should like your help in adjudicating their exchange, since my scientific training offers me little guidance here.

They were considering a hypothetical policy decision: a nation chooses to store high-level nuclear waste in a geologically stable formation, knowing that over two centuries a very small but definite increase in cancer incidence will occur in the region, and also knowing that the economic and migratory patterns the storage facility induces will alter which individuals are eventually conceived and born. Elena argued that, because the specific people who suffer the cancers owe their very existence to the policy—their parents or grandparents migrated to the region because of the facility's presence—no wrong has been done to them. To harm someone, she said, requires that they be made worse off than they otherwise would have been; but these people would not have existed at all under the alternative policy, so there is no baseline of well-being against which to measure harm.

Marcus replied that this reasoning proves too much. If a policy produces a future population whose lives are barely worth living—say, widespread chronic illness—while an alternative policy produces a flourishing population of different people, then on Elena's view we have no reason to prefer the flourishing world. That strikes him as absurd: surely we ought not to deliberately bring about a world of suffering just because the sufferers cannot complain that they were robbed of a better life. He proposed that the relevant comparison is not between an actual person and their non-existence, but between two feasible world-histories, and that our obligation is to avoid selecting the worse history regardless of who inhabits it.

Elena countered that Marcus has smuggled in a person-affecting intuition through the back door and then denied it. Once we abandon the idea that morality must be justified to each affected individual, she said, we are left with an impersonal calculus that treats people as interchangeable containers of well-being, which is precisely what the nonidentity problem was meant to challenge. She added that in ordinary life we do not think we wrong someone by bringing them into existence with a mild disability, provided their life is still good on balance; extending that intuition to collective choice is only consistent.

Marcus acknowledged the force of the problem but distinguished between same-person harm—where an existing individual is made worse off—and structural harm, where an institutional choice creates conditions that are unjustifiable to anyone who might live under them. He suggested that a future person could reasonably reject the nuclear-waste policy not by saying I should have lived a different life, but by saying the conditions under which I live should not have been chosen for anyone when a better alternative was available. Elena answered that this merely relabels the impersonal comparison: better for anyone collapses into better in total, which brings us back to the calculus she rejects.

They asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange round by round—Elena's initial claim, Marcus's first reply, Elena's counter, and Marcus's final distinction—and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both are missing. In particular, I am curious whether Marcus's structural harm succeeds in avoiding the nonidentity trap, or whether Elena is correct that it is only a disguised form of total well-being comparison.
