Having recently retired from experimental physics, I have been working through philosophy of technology with the same rigor I once applied to thermodynamics, and for a discussion group I have tried to formalize my views on technological persistence and evolutionary analogies. I am particularly drawn to the structural claim—often linked to von Neumann’s thinking on self-replicating automata—that technological lineages can maintain and adapt themselves independently of continuous human oversight, operating in dynamics that resemble evolutionary selection. At the same time, I want to hold five commitments together: (a) that these technological dynamics are genuinely evolutionary in structure, allowing replication, variation, and selection without biological substrate; (b) that such dynamics are normatively empty, meaning that assessing a technological lineage by replication fidelity and energy capture is appropriate, whereas judging it by human flourishing or suffering commits a category error; (c) that we nevertheless possess an overriding ethical obligation to preserve human conscious agency and cultural continuity, even if satisfying that obligation constrains autonomous technological replication; (d) that the evolutionary analogy is strictly heuristic—technological replication is teleologically directed by design intentions rather than blind variation—so I reject any literal ontological identification of machines with biological organisms; and (e) that should human extinction appear probable, our primary duty is to safeguard scientific and cultural archives rather than to maximize the unimpeded replication of technological systems.

I am unsure whether this package is stable. The normative emptiness in (b) seems to pull against the ethical constraint in (c), and my insistence in (d) that the analogy is only heuristic might seem to undermine the structural independence I affirm in (a). Meanwhile, (e) appears to prioritize preservation over replication in a crisis, which may or may not cohere with (c). I want an audit rather than a lecture: please examine each pair of commitments, distinguish genuine tensions from merely apparent ones, rank the real conflicts by severity, and for each specify what the least costly revision would be and which single commitment, if abandoned, would clean up the most problems. In particular, tell me whether (a) and (d) actually conflict or whether directed replication can still support evolutionary dynamics without literal biological ontology, and whether (b) can be retained without dissolving (c). I am not signaling which view I would surrender; I want to know where the fault lines actually lie.
