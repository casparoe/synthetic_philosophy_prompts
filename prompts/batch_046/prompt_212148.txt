In philosophical debates over disjunctivism, the relation between veridical perception and hallucination is sometimes analogized to the difference between a photograph taken by a physical camera at a real scene and an AI-generated image that depicts an identical scene with perfect fidelity but no causal connection to it. Advocates of a "highest common factor" account invoke this comparison to argue that since the two outputs are subjectively indistinguishable, they must share an internal representational core—like shared pixels—while disjunctivists reject this, maintaining that veridical perception is not an internal representation at all but a direct openness to the world. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the camera, the generator, the image file, and the viewer? Then identify the disanalogies that matter—particularly concerning intentionality, causal history, and whether subjective identity entails sameness of mental kind—and assess whether the analogy genuinely licenses the highest-common-factor conclusion, or whether, once the breaks are visible, it actually undermines that view by showing that origin can determine the nature of a state despite identical appearance.
