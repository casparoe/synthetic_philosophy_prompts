I was grading papers with a colleague and we got sidetracked arguing about Du Bois — specifically teh mid-1920s essay where he says all art is propaganda. Two grad students in my seminar got into it and I honestly froze. Here is roughly what they said.

Marcus went first: he said Du Bois is basically giving Black artists a marching order. The essay praises painters and writers who use their work to fight stereotypes, and Marcus read that as normative — like, if you're Black and making art, you have a duty to make it propaganda for racial uplift. He said Du Bois dismisses art for art's sake as a luxury Black people can't afford right now.

Lena pushed back: she said Marcus is treating a descriptive claim like a prescription. Du Bois isn't just talking to Black artists; he's saying all art propagates some worldview, including the white art that gets called universal. So teh point is to show that the charge your art is too political is hypocritical. Then she introduced this distinction she cooked up — propaganda of presence (making Black humanity visible on its own terms) versus propaganda of assimiliation (shaping Black art to be palatable to white audiences). She argued Du Bois warns against the second kind even while embracing the first.

Marcus wasn't convinced. He said if Du Bois is just describing inevitability, then the essay is reduntant — why bother writing it? He also tried to extend Du Bois's idea of double consciousness from Souls of Black Folk to art: the Black artist is always seeing herself through white eyes, so her work is inevitably reactive propaganda, not just presence.

Lena said that's overreaching. Double consciousness is about subjective experience, not the ontology of an artwork. She said Marcus is reducing Black art to pure reaction, which Du Bois actually resists when he insists on beauty and truth as intrinsic goods, not just counter-images. She also said the essay isn't redundant because in 1926 white critics actually believed in aesthetic neutrality, so debunking that myth had real political bite.

Marcus came back with: okay, but Lena's presence vs assimilation framing isn't in the text, it's her own injection. Du Bois just says make beautiful things that tell the truth about Black life — there's no hidden taxonomy. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't.

Can you adjudicate move by move? Rate Marcus's normative reading, Lena's descriptive reading, her presence/assimilation distinction, Marcus's redundancy objection, his double-consciousness extension, and Lena's reply about overreaching. Who's right about what, and is either missing something important about how Du Bois relates beauty to politics?
