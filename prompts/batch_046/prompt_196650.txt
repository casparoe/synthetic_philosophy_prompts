As a novelist examining the machinery of a modern federation, I am struck by a constitutional design that fills its lower legislative chamber by lot for one non-renewable six-year term, permanently bars those selected citizens from any future elected office, and yet permits career politicians—who have never faced the lot—to serve indefinitely in executive and judicial posts, rotating among them without restriction. The same design requires every citizen to complete a brief local administrative apprenticeship before entering the pool for selection. What conception of representation, accountability, and civic equality does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit political philosophy: does it treat legislative service as a distributed burden analogous to taxation, executive authority as a specialized vocation requiring continuous practice, or democratic legitimacy as a temporary suspension of private citizenship? Determine whether the institution is philosophically coherent, or whether its parts quietly depend on rival theories—perhaps a sortitionist ideal of descriptive representation set against an elitist theory of governing competence. Say where the practice is underdetermined, compatible with several incompatible views, and describe precisely what would have to change in the constitutional architecture if we replaced its implicit theory with a consistently participatory or consistently meritocratic one.
