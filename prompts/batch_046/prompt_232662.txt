I'm drafting the final examination for our upper-level graduate seminar on autonomy, identity, and medical decision-making. We have spent the term examining how advance directives are justified—typically through appeals either to the continuity of the patient's values over time or to the present welfare of the patient who no longer remembers those values—but I want an essay question that forces students past these familiar frameworks. Specifically, I want them to confront a constructed case that introduces a conceptual wrinkle not usually discussed in the standard literature: a patient who, at fifty, signs a detailed advance directive refusing all life-extending interventions should she suffer severe cognitive decline, but also appends a handwritten addendum—what we might call a "suspension clause"—stating that if, after such decline, she consistently expresses a desire to continue living, and if her caregivers can identify what looks like a simple but stable narrative in which her present experiences matter to her, then the directive is not revoked but suspended, leaving the decision in a kind of normative limbo until some further condition is satisfied.

The exam question should present this scenario and ask students to determine whether the suspension clause is a coherent exercise of diachronic autonomy or whether it undermines the very authority the directive claims to exercise. It should require them to address whether the authority of the earlier self can extend to authorizing its own temporary displacement without thereby collapsing into pure present-interest paternalism on the one hand, or into an uncritical fetishization of past preferences on the other. Students should be expected to discuss what conditions would make the suspension valid—perhaps some threshold of narrative coherence in the present self, or some evidentiary standard governing the caregivers' interpretive role—without merely invoking the distinction between psychological connectedness and narrative identity as if naming the distinction were an argument. The essay should be answerable in 75 minutes, with an expected length of roughly 1,200 words.

Please provide: (1) the exam question, clearly framed and with the scenario embedded; (2) a model answer of approximately 700 words that makes the specific conceptual moves a top answer requires—particularly regarding the asymmetry between revocation and suspension, the normative status of the caregivers' interpretive judgment, and why neither a pure precedent-autonomy account nor a pure present-welfare account can accommodate the clause without substantial modification; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 marks with four must-hit points, the marks allocated to each, and for each a one-line note describing the specific conceptual mistake or omission that typically costs candidates those marks. The question should not depend on citing specific court cases, clinical studies, or canonical texts by title; it should remain conceptual, though it should engage the familiar tension between integrity-based and interest-based justifications without treating either position as self-evidently correct.
