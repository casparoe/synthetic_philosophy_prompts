I'm preparing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of mind course (90 minutes, ~1000 words). Write one essay question on pain asymbolia and the badness of pain that cannot be answered by reciting the standard sensory/affective distinction. Inject a niche conceptual framing: present patients with bilateral anterior cingulate lesions who report precise nociceptive qualities, deny any unpleasantness, and actively seek repeated exposure—label this state "motivational silence," where the signal lacks the avoidance-driving force usually coupled to pain. Ask students to take and defend one of two metaphysical positions: either (a) pain's badness is extrinsic, belonging to a separable affective module, so these patients experience genuine pain that is not bad; or (b) they experience only a sensory homologue, not pain proper, because the affective-motivational dimension is constitutive. Require them to (i) articulate the crux between these views with precision, (ii) explain how their chosen view accommodates the patients' first-person reports of being "in pain" without collapsing into contradiction, and (iii) assess whether their conclusion undermines the normative argument that pain's intrinsic badness gives direct moral reason to eliminate it. Provide the essay prompt, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points (each with its point value, a one-line description of what an A-level answer does there, and the specific mistake that typically costs marks at that point). The separation between an A and a B should depend on whether the student genuinely chooses and defends a crux rather than summarizing known clinical descriptions. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies.
