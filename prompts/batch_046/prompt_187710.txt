Write a dialogue between a committed Du Boisian philosopher of art and a sympathetic but critical interlocutor who accepts Du Bois's diagnosis of racial capitalism but rejects his propagandistic mandate. The Du Boisian should defend the view that for the oppressed artist, aesthetic autonomy is not a virtue but a form of moral abdication, and that art must serve as propaganda for collective dignity because neutrality under oppression is complicity. The critic should concede the diagnosis but argue that reducing art to propaganda risks an epistemic injustice: it denies the audience the capacity to derive political meaning from formal ambiguity, irony, or aesthetic disruption, and it treats the artist's formal choices as merely instrumental rather than generative. Have them respond to each other directly—the Du Boisian should reply that autonomy is a privilege of those not fighting for survival, and the critic should distinguish autonomy-as-indifference from autonomy-as-necessary-condition for genuinely transformative art. Focus the exchange on whether an artwork that refuses explicit political declaration—say, one that deploys formal radicalism or deliberate ambiguity—can still satisfy Du Bois's ethical demand, or whether such refusal is itself a betrayal of the collective. End with the tension unresolved but precisely located.
