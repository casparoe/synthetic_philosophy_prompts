I'm trying to name this thing I keep running into when I read ethnographies of local administration, and I'm not sure if it's already covered by some concept or if I'm just mixing up categories. There's this pattern where a official distributes public goods — jobs, permits, access to water — mostly to people in their social web, not close family but people they owe or who owe them. The weird part is that the official doesn't pocket anything; acutally, they often take a personal hit, like giving away the best irrigation slots and leaving their own farm drier. Nobody in the community calls it corruption; they see it as being a responsible patron. But Western audit frameworks flag it instantly as clientelism or nepotism. The thing is, classic clientelism feels wrong because there's no explicit vote-for-job deal being tracked, and it's not nepotism since it's not kin-based. It's more like the public resource itself becomes the gift in a long-term reciprocity loop, and the official is just a node, not a boss skimming the top. Does this specific shape have a name — corruption without self-enrichment, or patronage with no transaction ledger? Or does it collapse into something like "diffuse reciprocity"? I don't just want a label; I want to know where the edges are. At what point does this stop being legitimate community care and become corruption, and is the Western framework missing something real here, or is it right to flatten all these distinctions?
