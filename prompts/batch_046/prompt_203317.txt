I'm an algebraic geometer fluent in scheme theory: I think in terms of schemes, structure sheaves, stalks, nilpotent elements, generic points, morphisms, and automorphism groups of ambient spaces. Restate Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles and Black's two-sphere counterexample—two spheres identical in all intrinsic qualitative and metric properties, separated only by their spatial relation in a symmetric universe—using only that vocabulary. Specifically, model the spheres and their embedding space as schemes; ask whether their separation can be represented as an intrinsic difference in stalks or nilpotent data, as an automorphism of the ambient scheme that fixes one sphere and not the other, or as a failure of scheme-isomorphism despite identical underlying point-sets and metrics. Then provide the residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate—intrinsic versus relational properties, the meaning of "indiscernible," the force of the counterexample—translate cleanly into scheme-theoretic distinctions, which parts require distorting sheaf or morphism concepts to accommodate, and which parts have no counterpart at all. Conclude by assessing whether the residue reveals a genuine limitation of scheme-theoretic ontology in expressing purely relational differences, or whether it shows that Black's intuition was already importing non-structural assumptions that scheme theory correctly refuses to encode.
