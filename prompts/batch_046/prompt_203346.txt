I am grading exams for a graduate seminar on symmetric reasoning and decision theory, and after a long argument with a friend about whether superrationality collapses once empirical uncertainty enters the player pool, I am unsure how to score one particular response. The exam asked students to compare Douglas Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery he describes in Scientific American, specifically: (a) explain why the superrational strategy in Platonia requires each of the twenty participants to send a telegram with probability exactly 1/20; (b) explain why the analogous cooperative strategy in the Luring Lottery requires rolling a simulated die with sides equal to the expected number of responders—approximately five percent of the readership—rather than using a fixed probability given in the problem statement; (c) evaluate whether the actual contestants' behavior—submitting entries numbering in astronomically large ranges—undermines Hofstadter's normative claim that symmetric reasoners should converge on identical strategies, or whether it merely records a descriptive failure; and (d) assess whether the lottery's dependence on an empirically estimated circulation parameter introduces a form of Bayesian reasoning about population size that is foreign to the pure a priori symmetry argument in Platonia, and if so, whether this "Bayesian contamination" weakens the normative force of the cooperative solution.

The student answer is as follows. In Platonia, twenty people share common knowledge of rationality and are forbidden from cooperating. Because each is superrational, they recognize that every other player will reason identically. If they all used a deterministic rule, either everyone or no one would send a telegram, yielding either zero winners or a null result. To ensure exactly one expected sender, each randomizes with probability 1/20, since 20 multiplied by 1/20 equals one expected transmission, and the symmetry guarantees no correlation in their independent randomizations. The Luring Lottery alters the payoff structure: the prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, and contestants may submit any number they wish on a single postcard. Here the pool size is unknown, so the cooperative analog is for each contestant to roll a die with sides equal to the expected number of responders—roughly five percent of the readership—sending a postcard marked 1 only if they roll a one. This yields an expected number of submissions equal to one, just as in Platonia. The key difference is that in Platonia the parameter 20 is given a priori, whereas in the lottery it must be estimated empirically from circulation data. Once N is fixed, however, the reasoning is structurally identical. Regarding actual behavior, many contestants submitted entries in the googolplex range, treating the game as a competitive maximization problem rather than a coordination problem. Hofstadter took this to show that superrationality is descriptively false and should be abandoned in favor of standard Nash equilibrium reasoning, since people clearly do not converge on symmetric strategies in practice. Still, this is only a failure of application: if every contestant had actually rolled the die using the correct empirical N, the magazine might have received exactly one entry and paid out one million dollars. So the normative claim survives intact, though its practical force depends on common knowledge not only of rationality but also of the circulation figures used to set N. Notably, because the expected payout per contestant is invariant to the value of N—each faces the same 1/eN scaling—the strategy remains scale-independent regardless of readership size.
