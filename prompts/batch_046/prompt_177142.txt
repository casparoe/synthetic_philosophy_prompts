I'm preparing a discussion paper on democratic legitimacy and the status of religious reasoning in liberal societies, and a fellow student offered me a concise summary of John Rawls's mature position that I suspect is partially distorted but cannot fully diagnose. She writes that Rawls maintains religious citizens ought never to invoke theological premises or scriptural authority in debates over constitutional essentials and basic justice, since faith-based reasoning fails to count as public reason. She further claims that Rawls attributes persistent religious disagreement to what he terms the burdens of judgment—essentially cognitive limitations and willful obstinacy that prevent agreement on comprehensive doctrines—and therefore demands that every citizen translate her moral convictions into a purely secular, political vocabulary before participating in legislation or judicial reasoning. In her account, religious belief is relegated to a private sphere with no legitimate public voice unless it is first stripped of its doctrinal content; consequently, she concludes that Rawls's framework tacitly privileges secular and atheistic worldviews, because only religious citizens bear this burdensome translation obligation.

Before I rely on this characterization, I want an accuracy audit that separates the defensible core from the misrepresentations. Specifically, does Rawls actually prohibit all religious argument in the public forum, or does his proviso permit the introduction of comprehensive doctrines under certain conditions? Is his explanation of pluralism reducible to ignorance and stubbornness, or does he treat the burdens of judgment as structural features of free institutions that affect secular and religious doctrines alike? Does his distinction between political conceptions of justice and comprehensive doctrines imply that religious citizens must become secular liberals, or does the idea of an overlapping consensus allow them to endorse liberal principles for political reasons that remain internal to their faith? And finally, is her claim that Rawls privileges atheism undermined by his insistence that secular comprehensive doctrines—such as Kantian autonomy or Millian liberalism—are equally subject to the constraints of public reason? I am not asking for bibliographic references or page citations; I want to know whether this summary is fair enough to use as a foil in my paper, and where a defender of political liberalism would correct it.
