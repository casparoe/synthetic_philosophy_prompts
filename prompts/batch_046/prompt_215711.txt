Write a dialogue between two philosophers, A and B, debating the nature and value of privacy. A defends a conventional informational account: privacy is valuable because it grants individuals control over the flow of personal data, protecting autonomy and dignity by limiting others' access to facts about oneself. B argues for a less common view—call it modal privacy—according to which privacy's core value is not concealment of actual information but the preservation of an indeterminate zone of counterfactual possibilities. On B's view, a predictive system that can reliably infer your preferences, reactions, and even unexpressed beliefs does not merely violate privacy by knowing you; it collapses the space of mental simulations and alternative life paths you can genuinely entertain, because once an external model fixes your dispositions with high confidence, your own reasoning about what you might do becomes epistemically dependent on that model. A should challenge B by asking whether this collapses privacy into a problem about determinism or free will rather than about social or institutional practice, and whether B's account can distinguish harmful prediction from benign self-knowledge. B should respond by clarifying that modal privacy concerns the asymmetry between first-person and third-person access to one's open-ended reasoning, not determinism per se, and that it explains why surveillance feels violating even when it reveals nothing you didn't already know about yourself. Keep the exchange sharp and substantive: each speaker should directly address the other's strongest point rather than talk past them. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts.
