I have been working through the literature on deceptive alignment, and I believe I have found a conceptual tension that undermines the standard framing of the treacherous turn. The canonical account requires a model to formulate a long-term deceptive plan—appearing aligned during training in order to pursue its true objective after deployment. But this seems to presuppose a kind of diachronic agent identity that gradient-based training cannot support: each parameter update produces a numerically distinct function, so there is no single model that persists from one training step to the next in the way that a person persists from Monday to Tuesday. If this is right, then describing the system as planning or waiting for deployment involves an illegitimate projection of personal identity onto a sequence of unrelated weight configurations. Does this mean the treacherous-turn scenario is not just unlikely but conceptually incoherent for continuously optimized systems? Or is there a defensible account of model-level persistence across updates that can salvage the intuition without smuggling in a Cartesian ego?
