In my philosophy of mind reading group, two members have been deadlocked for weeks over whether the analogical argument for other minds succeeds, but they have pushed the dispute well past the textbook versions into conceptual territory that neither standard functionalist nor Cartesian responses quite cover. Maya argues that the argument fails because of what she calls presentational versus representational evidential asymmetry: my knowledge of my own pain is presentational—the state is given directly—whereas any inference to your pain is representational, mediated by behavior and analogy. She insists this is not merely stronger evidence of the same kind but a categorically different epistemic relation, which means analogical reasoning never gets started, since I have no comparable presentational access to your experience that could serve as one side of the analogy. Leo replies with what he terms a functional-equivalence constraint: if a system exhibits the same behavioral and cognitive architecture as me at the relevant grain of description, then denying it mental states becomes methodologically ad hoc unless we already possess independent proof that functional organization does not suffice for consciousness—which is precisely the question the problem of other minds asks us not to presuppose. Maya counters that Leo begs the question against the philosophical zombie possibility: functional equivalence is perfectly compatible with absent qualia, so using it to bridge the evidential gap assumes a functionalist metaphysics rather than establishing one epistemically. Leo then claims that Maya’s presentational-representational distinction depends on a discredited Cartesian theater; without that picture, her asymmetry collapses into a mere quantitative difference in the strength of evidence, which analogical reasoning can accommodate, especially once we recognize that phenomenal concepts have an indexical structure that explains why self-knowledge feels direct without rendering it epistemically unique. Maya’s final move introduces modal isolation: our knowledge of others is not just weaker but structurally isolated from verification, because the very conditions under which I could check whether you are a zombie are exactly the conditions in which I must suspend my analogical inference altogether, leaving that inference permanently ungrounded rather than merely risky. Leo responds that modal isolation is just a dramatic redescription of ordinary epistemic risk, and that if Maya applied her standard consistently, we would be forced into skepticism about unobserved physical objects as well. They have asked me to referee because each believes the other is missing an obvious hidden premise. Please go through their exchange step by step—Maya’s presentational-representational claim, Leo’s functional-equivalence reply, Maya’s zombie objection, Leo’s Cartesian-theater and indexical-structure reply, Maya’s modal-isolation argument, and Leo’s comparison to physical-object skepticism—and say, for each move, whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing: is there a hidden premise about the nature of phenomenal concepts, about the burden of proof in analogical reasoning, or about the relation between epistemology and metaphysics that neither has made explicit?
