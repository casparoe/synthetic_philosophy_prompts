I'm preparing the final examination for my graduate seminar on the philosophy of history and historical explanation, and I need an essay question that tests whether students can move beyond summarizing Fernand Braudel's well-known framework of geographical, social, and individual temporalities. I want the prompt to inject a precise but non-standard analytical concept—call it structural latency—defined as a condition in which a geographical or climatic structure does not merely lower the probability of a certain event but actively suppresses its conceptual availability to actors for an extended period, dissolving only through slow structural transformation rather than through any discrete causal trigger. The exam should ask students to use this concept to evaluate whether Braudel's methodology can consistently accommodate an apparently agent-driven decision—specifically, an imperial commander's choice to maintain overland supply routes across an arid plateau despite well-documented seasonal flooding patterns—without reducing that decision to a mere narrative epiphenomenon or, conversely, without smuggling a hidden event-causality back into the longue durée.

Please draft the following: first, the exam question itself, calibrated for a 90-minute written response of approximately 900 words, aimed at students who have read Braudel's major study of the sixteenth-century Mediterranean world but who should not need any obscure secondary scholarship to answer; second, a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words that demonstrates how to distinguish structural latency from simple probabilistic constraint and that takes a clear position on whether Braudel's tripartite schema requires a supplementary layer of structural dissolution; third, a marking scheme totaling 25 marks built around five must-hit conceptual moves—each assigned specific marks, accompanied by a one-line description of the characteristic failure mode, and specifying the exact analytical step that earns full credit—plus 5 marks reserved for the coherence and precision of the argument. The question should be unanswerable by mere recitation of Braudel's three rhythms; it should force candidates to adjudicate the tension between slow-structure explanation and the irreducible presence of decision-points in the historical record. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and if you reference Braudel's own formulations, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
