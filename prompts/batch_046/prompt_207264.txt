Analyze IBM's 1997 defeat of Garry Kasparov by Deep Blue as a case study in the inversion of reasoning—systems that exhibit designed competence without underlying comprehension—through both Darwin's evolutionary inversion and Turing's computational inversion. Specifically: (a) identify the precise sense in which Deep Blue displayed chess competence (defeating a world champion) while lacking any strategic comprehension of the game, and draw the parallel to Darwin's claim that natural selection produces adaptive complexity without a designing intelligence; (b) examine whether Deep Blue's architecture—brute-force search guided by handcrafted evaluation functions—instantiates Turing's idea that symbol manipulation can yield intelligent-seeming outputs without semantic understanding, and discuss whether the match supports, challenges, or remains silent on Turing's functionalist criterion for attributing thought; (c) analyze the human interpretive response, including Kasparov's allegations of human-like creativity, IBM's marketing framing, and the audience's tendency to narrativize individual moves as intentional, to determine whether this is a case of illegitimately projecting comprehension onto mere competence, and whether Darwin's argument gives independent reason to resist that projection in artificial systems; (d) address the objection that Deep Blue's programmers supplied comprehension exogenously, and evaluate whether this rescues the machine from the inversion or merely displaces it onto the design process; and (e) conclude by saying what the episode reveals about the evidentiary limits of behavioral tests for mental-state attribution, and whether competence without comprehension should be treated as an anomaly for philosophy of mind or as its central paradigm.
