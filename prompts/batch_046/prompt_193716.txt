I'm putting together a reading group on belief and responsibility, and I think I've tied myself in knots over James and Clifford. My collegue mentioned epistemic permissivism — the idea that two people with the same evidence can rationally hold different credances — and suddenly it clicked: that's basically James's whole point against Clifford, right? Clifford demands that belief track evidence alone, and James says that when the intellectual case is genuinely underdetermined, our passional nature can legitimately break ties. So if permissivism is true everywhere, doesn't that mean Clifford's principle isn't just too strict but literally incoherent? After all, if evidence never fully fixes belief, there's always some will filling the gap, which seems to violate Clifford's core demand.

But then I started wondering about scope. James usually restricts his defense to options that are live, forced, and momentous — mostly religious commitment. If permissivism applies even to mundane empirical beliefs, then James's restriction looks arbitrary, and we should read him as having smuggled in a general framework that demolishes evidentialism across the board. So my actual question is: are we wrong to treat James as offering a narrow exception? Is there something about forcedness or momentousness that blocks the generalization to ordinary credence, or is the standard framing just wrong? I feel like I'm conflating two different debates here, but I can't see exactly where the line falls.
