You serve as the senior ethics and legal advisor to an intergovernmental rapid-response unit that reviews five to ten proposals per quarter recommending coercive military intervention justified primarily on humanitarian grounds. Your memos are due within seventy-two hours, are written for a board of political appointees with no specialized philosophy or legal training, and determine whether a proposal advances to formal authorization; the stakes include the potential authorization of armed force and the unit’s institutional credibility. You are required to apply a comparative structural framework developed from the asymmetries among Kosovo (1999), Iraq (2003), and Libya (2011): the framework holds that humanitarian intervention is permissible only if (a) the atrocity being halted is of a kind that the proposed operation’s own institutional form does not reproduce at scale, and (b) the post-intervention governance architecture does not inherit the sovereignty-violating or exclusionary mechanisms of the regime being removed. Atrocity severity is treated as necessary but not sufficient, and the framework explicitly excludes long-term geopolitical forecasting or economic side-effect weighing from its scope.

Write a reusable decision procedure your office can run on any incoming proposal: state the ordered questions or tests to apply, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation against authorization, where it stops with a clear recommendation in favor, and where it must hand the decision back to the board because the remaining issue is one of values rather than structural analysis. Include explicit misfire warnings—cases in which ambiguous institutional similarity will cause the procedure to generate a false positive or false negative—and flag the boundary conditions where regional expertise you do not possess must be imported. Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

(i) an air-campaign to halt systematic ethnic displacement in a centralized authoritarian state with intact military command and a unitary civil administration;
(ii) a ground-intervention and regime-change operation in a fragmented state where tribal militias control territory, no unified civil administration exists, and the proposed force includes ground troops with a mandate to restructure governance;
(iii) a limited no-fly zone with no explicit regime-change mandate in a state where the regime is committing localized but severe atrocities through non-state proxies.

One of these applications must yield a recommendation that your unit is under strong political pressure to reject but that the procedure nonetheless supports, and one must reach a hand-off point where structural analysis is exhausted and value judgment takes over. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—for example, predicting long-term stability, weighing non-structural geopolitical costs, or substituting for region-specific empirical knowledge.
