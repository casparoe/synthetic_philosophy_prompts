Treat the episode as a disciplined two-way case study. First, apply the Mingjia apparatus: map the IAU's three criteria for planethood—hydrostatic equilibrium, orbital dominance, and the "clearing the neighborhood" condition—onto the logical separation of form (xing), color (se), and relational position in the White Horse dialogue. Identify which astronomers, institutions, and public discourses acted as analogues to the disputants over whether "planet" denotes an inherent physical kind or a conventional designation (ming) that can drift from its object (shi). Explain whether the persistence of Pluto's popular status as a "planet" confirms the Mingjia suspicion that names and actualities diverge, and whether the vote itself represents a collective decision about language rather than a discovery about nature. Second, reverse the analysis: show precisely where the Pluto dispute fractures the Mingjia model. Does procedural scientific authority introduce a form of institutional naming that the individualistic logic of Gong Sun Long and Hui Shi never anticipated? Do Hui Shi's paradoxes about separable properties illuminate the tension between Pluto's roundness and its orbital behavior, or do they mislead because scientific taxonomy aims at predictive utility rather than logical purity? Be explicit about what the episode confirms in the School of Names' view of categorization, what it undermines, and where the texts remain genuinely silent rather than being forced to speak. Avoid treating the IAU vote as merely a semantic quarrel; attend to the material and institutional stakes—funding priorities, textbook revisions, and public legitimacy—that make this case different from the individual conceptual puzzles the Mingjia texts address.
