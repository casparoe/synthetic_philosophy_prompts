As a journalist covering science and ideas, I keep noticing a rhetorical pattern in discussions of generational cohorts—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so on—that I do not have a precise term for, and I want to know whether the field has named it. A writer will first define a cohort administratively, by birth-year boundaries, treating it as a purely demographic container with no essential properties. Then, often in the same piece, they will speak of that cohort as if it were a natural kind with stable, causally efficacious psychological traits—“Gen Z’s innate digital fluency,” “Millennial risk aversion,” “Boomer institutional loyalty”—without clarifying whether these come from shared historical events (period effects), life-stage (age effects), or something genuinely cohort-specific. When challenged, they retreat to “I’m just describing people born between year X and year Y,” but then immediately revert to essentialist causal claims—“Gen Z thinks about work differently because of who they are”—as if the cutoff years themselves carried explanatory power. I see echoes of the ecological fallacy and of reification, but neither captures the specific oscillation between container and kind, nor the spurious causal weight given to the boundary years. Is there an established concept—perhaps in the sociology of generations, social ontology, or science studies—that names this exact double-use? If not, please coin and define it precisely, distinguish it rigorously from nearby errors (ecological fallacy, period-age-cohort confusion, ordinary essentialism), and specify whether the false causal weight of the cutoff years belongs in the definition or is merely a frequent symptom. Also identify the boundary cases: for instance, does the phenomenon apply when a cohort label is used consistently as a kind, or only when there is this visible slide?
