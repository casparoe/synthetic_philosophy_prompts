As a novelist constructing the moral architecture of a historical epic, I find myself returning repeatedly to the same problem: when does a character genuinely deserve the fate she receives? I have reached the following verdicts about concrete situations, but they appear to pull in opposite directions, and I should like to know whether a single principle can hold them together or whether one must be abandoned.

First, consider Elara, a surgeon who spends fifteen years refining a technique that saves a child's life; she receives both professional honour and substantial remuneration. I judge that she fully deserves both.

Second, consider Marcus, a civil servant promoted after a clerical error misattributes a colleague's report to him; the selection committee's reasoning is sound, but the attribution is false. I judge that Marcus does not deserve the promotion.

Third, consider Sera, a musician born with congenital perfect pitch and raised in affluence that funded years of private tuition; she applies herself diligently and achieves celebrated status. I judge that part of her acclaim is undeserved—those conditions were not of her making—yet she also deserves some recognition for the discipline she exercises.

Fourth, consider Jonah, a labourer of extraordinary diligence who, through an economic collapse he could not have foreseen, dies in poverty. I judge that he does not deserve that poverty; the outcome tracks nothing within his control.

Fifth, consider Kaelen, a mathematician who derives a proof of profound importance during sleep, the solution arising from unconscious processes he neither directed nor comprehended. I judge that he does not deserve credit for the proof, despite it bearing his name.

These five verdicts trouble me. Case Three and Case Four both involve factors outside the agent's control, yet I grant partial desert in the former and deny it entirely in the latter. Meanwhile, Case One rewards mastery that also depended on cognitive gifts and social opportunity, yet I grant full desert there without the qualification I apply to Sera.

What principle—stated with sufficient precision to have distinct clauses—captures these judgments? Does it turn on intentional agency at the moment of achievement, on contribution to welfare, on the presence of effort that the agent could have withheld, or on some combination? Please articulate the principle, assign each clause to the case that motivates it, state what it predicts for a further case: a novelist who writes a brilliant novel by deliberately copying an unknown manuscript that she genuinely, though erroneously, believes she composed herself (a phenomenon of cryptomnesia), and say honestly which of my five verdicts must be overturned if the principle is to remain simple rather than ad hoc.
