I have recently been navigating informed consent on behalf of a parent facing an elective but high-risk procedure, and the experience has left me skeptical of the standard philosophical taxonomy—deception, coercion, and incompetence—as an exhaustive account of what invalidates consent. I am drafting a short essay that introduces a distinction I call architectural deception: not an explicit misstatement of risks (propositional deception), but the design of the consent interaction itself—sequencing, cognitive load, framing of alternatives—such that technically complete disclosure systematically prevents the formation of an accurate belief about what is being agreed to. I want to argue that this undermines consent not by violating autonomy through false belief, but by violating what I term interactional competence: the sustained, relational capacity of the consenter to revise their understanding within the interaction. This differs from individual incompetence; the environment disables the exercise of competence rather than the subject lacking it. I also want to suggest that coercion should be reconceived not merely as a threat that makes refusal too costly, but as any structural asymmetry that makes the cost of revising one’s belief prohibitively high—though I worry this collapses coercion into architectural deception.

Could you comment on the coherence and interest of this framework? Specifically: (1) Does architectural deception genuinely differ from familiar critiques of therapeutic privilege and information overload, or am I merely relabeling? (2) Is interactional competence defensible, or does it conflate the conditions for valid consent with the conditions for good pedagogy? (3) Where in the essay should I address the objection that all complex consent involves unavoidable framing, and that my view risks demanding an impossible neutrality? I am not asking for a bibliography or citations, but I would value suggestions on which standard objections—perhaps from relational autonomy or procedural accounts of consent—this line is most vulnerable to, and whether the personal framing strengthens or undermines the philosophical claim.
